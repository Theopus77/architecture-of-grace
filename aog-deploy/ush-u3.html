A New Nation

1763–1791

In 1763, most colonists were proud to be British. They celebrated the king's birthday and cheered Britain's victory over France. Just 13 years later, many of those same people declared that they no longer belonged to the British Empire. Within 30 years, they had fought a long war, won their independence and written a new plan of government that is still in use today.

This unit tells how that happened. It begins with arguments over taxes and rights, moves through eight years of war, and ends in a hot meeting room in Philadelphia where delegates designed a new kind of government. Along the way you will meet farmers, printers, soldiers, spies and lawmakers, including women, African Americans and Native nations whose choices shaped the outcome.

The founders spoke of liberty and equality, but in 1791 those ideals did not reach everyone. Enslaved people, women and Native Americans were largely left out. As you read, look for both sides of the story: the bold new ideas about rights and self-government, and the people who were still waiting for those ideas to include them.

The big question

How did the American colonies become one nation, and how well did the new nation live up to its promise of liberty?

Big questionHow did loyal British colonists become people ready to fight for independence?
The story

Two Lanterns and a Fast Horse

On a cold spring night in 1775, a warning raced from Boston into the Massachusetts countryside.

Boston, April 18, 1775. The streets were dark and quiet, but the town was nervous. British soldiers, called Regulars, had been living in Boston for years. Now they were getting ready to march. Patriot leaders guessed where they were headed: the town of Concord, about 18 miles away. Colonists had hidden guns and gunpowder there.

Dr. Joseph Warren, a Patriot leader in Boston, sent two riders to spread the alarm. One was Paul Revere, a silversmith. The other was William Dawes, a shoemaker. Dawes slipped out of town by land. Revere had planned a signal ahead of time. Two lanterns hung in the steeple of the Old North Church meant the soldiers were crossing the Charles River by boat. Friends across the river saw the lights.

Revere was rowed quietly across the river past a British warship. On the other side, he borrowed a horse and galloped toward Lexington. He stopped at houses along the way to warn the militia, the local citizen soldiers. He probably did not shout “The British are coming!” Most colonists still thought of themselves as British. He more likely warned that “the Regulars” were coming out.

In Lexington, Revere warned two Patriot leaders, Samuel Adams and John Hancock. Dawes arrived soon after. The two men rode on toward Concord with a third rider, Dr. Samuel Prescott. A British patrol stopped them on the road. Revere was captured for a time. Dawes escaped. Prescott jumped his horse over a wall and carried the warning to Concord.

Revere and Dawes were not alone. Other riders fanned out. Church bells rang. Drums beat. By dawn on April 19, militiamen from many towns were grabbing their muskets. On the green in Lexington, a small group of them faced the British soldiers. Someone fired. No one knows who. The first battle of the American Revolution had begun.

Section 1

An Empire Deep in Debt

5.1

A Line on the Map

Main ideaAfter winning the French and Indian War, Britain tried to stop colonists from settling west of the Appalachian Mountains.

In 1763, Britain won the French and Indian War. France gave up its lands in North America. British colonists were proud. Many hoped to move west into the rich lands of the Ohio River valley. But those lands were not empty. Native nations such as the Shawnee, Delaware and Odawa lived there.

Native nations did not want settlers pouring onto their lands. In 1763, an Odawa leader named Pontiac led many nations in a war against British forts around the Great Lakes. The fighting was costly for everyone. British leaders wanted to avoid another expensive war.

So King George III issued the . It drew a line along the Appalachian Mountains. Colonists were not allowed to settle west of that line. Settlers who were already there were told to leave. Many colonists were angry. They felt they had fought the war to win those lands. Now their own government was blocking them. The line was hard to enforce, and many settlers crossed it anyway.

5.2

Parliament Wants Money

Main ideaTo pay its war debts, Britain's Parliament began taxing the colonies directly, starting with the Sugar Act and the Stamp Act.

The French and Indian War left Britain with a huge debt. Britain also planned to keep about 10,000 soldiers in North America to guard the frontier. Someone had to pay for all this. Leaders in , Britain’s lawmaking body, decided the colonists should pay a share. After all, the war had protected them.

In 1764, Parliament passed the Sugar Act. It actually lowered the tax on molasses, a sweet syrup used to make rum. But this time Britain meant to collect the tax. For years, colonial merchants had avoided such taxes by smuggling. Now smugglers could be tried in special courts without a jury.

In 1765, Parliament passed the . It taxed printed paper. Newspapers, pamphlets, legal documents and even playing cards needed an official stamp to show the tax was paid. This tax touched almost everyone, from lawyers to printers to ordinary shoppers. It was also the first direct tax Parliament placed on the colonies. Many colonists thought it crossed a line.

5.3

No Taxation Without Representation

Main ideaColonists argued that only their own elected assemblies could tax them, and their protests forced Parliament to repeal the Stamp Act.

Colonists did not object to every law from Britain. They objected to being taxed by a body they did not elect. Colonists had no members in Parliament. They believed that, as British subjects, they had the right to be taxed only by their own , such as the elected assemblies in each colony. Their slogan became “No taxation without representation.”

In October 1765, delegates from nine colonies met in New York at the Stamp Act Congress. They sent a respectful message to the king and Parliament. It said the colonists were loyal subjects, but that only their own assemblies could tax them. It was one of the first times several colonies acted together.

Protest also filled the streets. Groups called the Sons of Liberty scared stamp collectors into quitting. Merchants joined a , refusing to buy British goods. British merchants lost money and begged Parliament for help. In 1766, Parliament repealed, or canceled, the Stamp Act. But on the same day it passed the Declaratory Act. That law said Parliament had full power to make laws for the colonies. The argument was not over.

Section 2

Protest Turns to Conflict

5.4

New Taxes, New Boycotts

Main ideaWhen Parliament taxed everyday imports in 1767, colonists, including many women, answered with boycotts and homemade goods.

In 1767, Parliament tried again. The taxed goods the colonies imported from Britain, including glass, lead, paint, paper and tea. Some of the money would pay the salaries of royal governors and judges. That worried colonists. If Britain paid these officials, colonial assemblies would lose power over them.

Colonists returned to boycotts. Merchants in many towns signed agreements not to import British goods. Women played a big part, because they bought much of what families used. Groups called the Daughters of Liberty held spinning bees, where women spun thread together to make homespun cloth. Wearing homemade cloth instead of British fabric became a sign of patriotism.

In 1768, Britain sent soldiers to Boston to keep order and protect tax collectors. Many Bostonians resented having armed troops in their streets. Soldiers and townspeople often insulted each other. Some off-duty soldiers took part-time jobs, which made local workers angry. Tension grew. In 1770, Parliament canceled most of the Townshend taxes. It kept one, the tax on tea, to show it still had the right to tax.

5.5

Blood on King Street

Main ideaIn 1770, British soldiers fired into a crowd in Boston, killing five people, and Patriots used the event to turn opinion against Britain.

On the cold night of March 5, 1770, a crowd gathered around a British guard on King Street in Boston. People shouted insults and threw snowballs and chunks of ice. More soldiers arrived. In the confusion, the soldiers fired into the crowd. Five colonists died. One of them was Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native American ancestry. He is often remembered as one of the first people to die in the struggle for American independence.

Patriots called the shooting the . A massacre is the killing of people who cannot defend themselves. Paul Revere made an engraving of the event. It showed soldiers calmly firing on peaceful townspeople. That was not exactly what happened, but the picture spread quickly. It was , information shaped to persuade people to take a side.

The soldiers were put on trial. Their lawyer was John Adams, a Patriot. He believed every person deserved a fair trial, even an unpopular one. Adams argued that the soldiers had feared for their lives. Most of them were found not guilty. Two were convicted of a lesser crime. The trial showed that colonists valued the rule of law, even when they were angry.

5.6

Tea in the Harbor

Main ideaCommittees linked the colonies, and when Britain passed the Tea Act, Boston Patriots dumped a shipload of taxed tea into the harbor.

Patriots knew they were stronger together. In 1772, Samuel Adams helped start a in Boston. These committees wrote letters to share news and plan protests. Soon committees spread through many towns and colonies. Before phones or the internet, this letter network helped the colonies think and act as one.

In 1773, Parliament passed the Tea Act. It was meant to help the British East India Company, which had too much tea and was losing money. The law let the company sell tea directly to the colonies. The tea was actually cheaper, but it still carried the tax. Patriots saw a trick. If colonists bought the cheap tea, they would be accepting Parliament’s right to tax them.

Colonists in several ports refused to let the tea be unloaded. In Boston, the governor insisted the tea come ashore. On the night of December 16, 1773, a group of men, some dressed as Mohawk people, boarded three ships. They broke open 342 chests of tea and dumped them into Boston Harbor. This protest became known as the . Some colonists cheered. Others thought destroying property went too far.

Section 3

From Punishment to War

5.7

Britain Cracks Down

Main ideaParliament punished Massachusetts with harsh laws in 1774, and the colonies responded by meeting together in the First Continental Congress.

Britain’s leaders were furious about the tea. In 1774, Parliament passed laws to punish Boston and Massachusetts. Colonists called them the , meaning laws that could not be put up with. One law closed Boston’s port until the tea was paid for. Another cut the power of the Massachusetts assembly and limited town meetings. Another let royal officials be tried in Britain instead of in the colony. A new Quartering Act allowed troops to be housed in colonial buildings.

General Thomas Gage, commander of British forces in North America, became governor of Massachusetts. With the port closed, Boston’s trade stopped and many people lost work. Other colonies sent food and supplies to help. Britain had hoped to isolate Boston. Instead, the punishment pulled the colonies closer together.

In September 1774, delegates from 12 colonies met in Philadelphia. Only Georgia did not send anyone. This meeting was the . Delegates included George Washington, John Adams and Samuel Adams. They agreed to stop trade with Britain. They urged colonies to train their militias. They also agreed to meet again in May 1775 if Britain did not change course.

5.8

The Shot Heard Round the World

Main ideaOn April 19, 1775, fighting at Lexington and Concord turned the long argument with Britain into a war.

By 1775, many Massachusetts towns had trained . These were militia members ready to fight at a minute’s notice. They stored guns and gunpowder in towns such as Concord. General Gage decided to seize those supplies. On the night of April 18, about 700 British soldiers set out from Boston.

Riders spread the alarm. At dawn on April 19, the soldiers reached Lexington. About 70 militiamen stood on the town green. Someone fired a shot. No one knows who fired first. The British soldiers then fired, and eight militiamen were killed. The British marched on to Concord.

At Concord, the soldiers found only a few supplies. At the North Bridge, they clashed with hundreds of militiamen, who forced them back. On the long march back to Boston, the British were attacked from behind trees, walls and houses. By the end of the day, the British had lost many more men than the colonists. Thousands of militiamen then surrounded Boston. The , the war for independence, had begun.

5.9

One Last Try for Peace

Main ideaThe Second Continental Congress prepared for war while still offering the king a chance at peace, which he rejected.

In May 1775, the met in Philadelphia. The fighting had already started. Congress now had to act like a government. It created the Continental Army and chose George Washington of Virginia to lead it. Washington had fought in the French and Indian War and was widely respected.

Around the same time, colonial forces led by Ethan Allen and Benedict Arnold captured Fort Ticonderoga in New York. Its cannons would later help the Americans. Still, many delegates did not want to break away from Britain. They hoped the king would take their side against Parliament.

In July 1775, Congress sent the king the . An olive branch is a symbol of peace. The petition said the colonists were loyal and asked the king to help end the fighting. King George III refused to read it. Instead, he declared that the colonies were in rebellion. For many colonists, the king’s answer showed that peace on fair terms was not possible.

Section 4

Declaring Independence

5.10

A Pamphlet Changes Minds

Main ideaThomas Paine's pamphlet Common Sense convinced many colonists in 1776 that independence was the right choice.

Even after a year of fighting, many colonists hoped to stay part of the British Empire. Then, in January 1776, a writer named Thomas Paine published a , a short booklet, called Common Sense. Paine had come to Philadelphia from England only about a year before.

Paine wrote in plain, bold language that ordinary people could understand. He attacked the idea of kings. He said it made no sense for a small island to rule a whole continent across an ocean. He argued that the colonies would be better off as an independent , a nation where people choose their leaders.

Common Sense sold widely and was read aloud in homes, taverns and army camps. People who could not read heard its arguments. It did not change every mind, but it helped turn many colonists toward independence. Within months, colony after colony told its delegates in Congress to support a break with Britain.

5.11

Jefferson Writes the Declaration

Main ideaIn July 1776, Congress approved the Declaration of Independence, which explained why the colonies were breaking from Britain.

In June 1776, Richard Henry Lee of Virginia asked Congress to declare the colonies independent. Congress chose a committee of five to write a statement explaining the decision. The group included Thomas Jefferson, John Adams and Benjamin Franklin. Jefferson, a 33-year-old Virginian, wrote the first draft. The others suggested changes.

On July 2, 1776, Congress voted for independence. On July 4, it approved the final wording of the . The document has four main parts. It opens by explaining why a statement is needed. Next it describes people’s natural rights. Then it lists many complaints, or , against the king. Finally, it declares the colonies free and independent states.

The most famous part is about rights. It says all people have , rights that cannot be taken away, including life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. It says governments get their power from the consent of the governed. If a government destroys people’s rights, the people may change it or replace it. These ideas came partly from Enlightenment thinkers such as John Locke.

5.12

Who Was Left Out?

Main ideaThe Declaration said all men are created equal, but in 1776 those words did not apply to enslaved people, women or Native Americans.

The Declaration’s words were bold, but they did not match life in 1776. About one in five people in the colonies was enslaved. Jefferson himself enslaved hundreds of people over his lifetime. His first draft blamed the king for the slave trade, but Congress removed that section. Some delegates’ home colonies depended on slavery.

Women were not mentioned. They could not vote, and married women usually could not own property in their own names. Native Americans appear in the Declaration only in a complaint that the king had turned them against the colonists. Native nations were fighting to protect their own lands and freedom.

Still, the Declaration’s promise lasted. Over time, many groups used its words to demand their rights. Enslaved people in Massachusetts cited the ideas of liberty when they asked for freedom. Later, abolitionists, women’s rights leaders and civil rights leaders quoted it. The gap between the Declaration’s and reality became a challenge that Americans have worked on ever since.

The story

A Winter of Huts and Hunger

In the winter of 1777, the Continental Army nearly fell apart at Valley Forge, and came out of it stronger.

December 19, 1777. About 12,000 tired soldiers marched into a place called Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, about 20 miles from Philadelphia. The British had captured Philadelphia that fall. Congress had fled the city. George Washington chose Valley Forge because it was close enough to watch the British, and high ground made it easier to defend.

There were no buildings for the army. The soldiers had to build their own shelter. They cut down trees and built rows of log huts. Each hut held about 12 men, packed around a small fireplace. Smoke filled the air. Many soldiers had no shoes and wrapped their feet in rags. Blankets were scarce. Some days there was little to eat except “firecake,” a flat bread of flour and water cooked on hot stones.

The biggest danger was not the cold. It was disease. Illnesses such as typhus and dysentery spread quickly through the crowded camp. Nearly 2,000 soldiers died during the months at Valley Forge, most of them from sickness. Washington begged Congress and the states for food and clothing. Supplies came slowly, partly because the new government had little money and poor ways to move goods.

In February 1778, a Prussian officer named Friedrich von Steuben arrived. He spoke little English, but he knew how to train soldiers. Main ideaBritain shifted the war to the South, where early British victories gave way to hard-fought American comebacks.

The story

A Hot Summer Behind Closed Doors

In 1787, a group of delegates locked themselves in a room in Philadelphia and wrote a plan that still governs the nation.

Philadelphia, May 1787. Delegates arrived at the Pennsylvania State House, the same building where the Declaration of Independence had been approved 11 years earlier. Today we call it Independence Hall. They came from 12 states. Rhode Island refused to send anyone. Their official job was to fix the Articles of Confederation, the nation's first plan of government. Many of them planned to do much more.

Some of the most famous people in America were there. George Washington was chosen to lead the meeting. Benjamin Franklin, 81 years old, was the oldest delegate. James Madison of Virginia, a short, quiet man who had studied governments of the past, came with a plan already written. Over the summer, 55 delegates took part, though not all at the same time.

The delegates agreed to keep their talks secret. They wanted to speak freely and change their minds without newspapers reporting every word. Guards stood at the doors. Day after day, the delegates argued through a hot, sticky Philadelphia summer. Much of what we know comes from Madison, who sat near the front and took careful notes every day.

The arguments were fierce. Big states and small states fought over power. Northern and southern delegates clashed over slavery. More than once, the meeting almost fell apart. Some delegates left early. But again and again, the delegates found compromises, deals in which each side gave up something to reach an agreement.

On September 17, 1787, 39 delegates signed the finished Constitution. As the last men signed, Franklin pointed to the back of Washington's chair, which had a sun painted on it. According to Madison's notes, Franklin said he had often wondered whether the sun was rising or setting. Now, he said, he was happy to know it was a rising sun. The new plan still had to be approved by the states.

Main ideaAfter declaring independence, each state wrote a constitution that limited government power and protected certain rights.

The Articles of Confederation

Main ideaThe Articles of Confederation created a weak national government because Americans feared a strong central power.

The Continental Congress also wrote a plan for a national government. It was called the . A confederation is a loose union of independent states. Congress approved the Articles in 1777, but they did not take effect until 1781. Maryland refused to approve them until states such as Virginia gave up their claims to western lands.

Under the Articles, the national government was just one body: Congress. Each state had one vote, no matter how many people lived there. There was no president to carry out laws and no national courts to settle disputes. Congress could declare war, make treaties, borrow money and run a post office.

But Congress had very little power. It could not collect taxes. It could only ask the states for money, and the states often said no. It could not control trade between states. Important laws needed the votes of 9 of the 13 states. Changing the Articles required all 13 to agree. Americans had designed a weak government on purpose. They did not want to replace one with another.

The Land Ordinance of 1785 set up a system to survey, or measure, the land. It was divided into squares called s, each six miles on a side. Each township had 36 sections of one square mile. One section in every township was set aside to support public schools. You can still see this grid today in the square roads and farm fields of Illinois.

The of 1787 set rules for governing the region. When an area had 60,000 free people, it could apply to become a state, equal to the original 13. Settlers were promised freedom of religion and trial by jury. The law also banned slavery in the territory, although in practice some people in Illinois were held in slavery or forced service for years afterward. The ordinance promised good faith toward Native nations, but settlers kept pushing onto their lands.

Section 2

Signs of Trouble

7.4

A Government Without Money

Main ideaWithout the power to tax or control trade, the Confederation government could not pay its debts or earn respect from other nations.

The war left the nation deeply in debt. Congress owed money to soldiers, to citizens and to countries such as France. But Congress could not tax. When it asked the states for money, they sent only a small part of what was needed. Many soldiers went home without the pay they were promised.

During the war, Congress had printed paper money called Continentals. So much was printed that it lost almost all its value. People began saying something useless was “not worth a Continental.” States printed their own money, too. Some states taxed goods from other states. Trade became confusing and unfair.

Other nations saw the weakness. Britain kept soldiers in forts in the Northwest Territory, even though the peace treaty said it would leave. In 1784, Spain closed the port of New Orleans to American trade. Western farmers needed that port to ship their crops down the Mississippi River. Congress had no army or money to push back. Many leaders began to fear the new nation might fall apart.

7.5

Farmers in Revolt

Main ideaShays's Rebellion, an armed protest by Massachusetts farmers, convinced many leaders that the national government needed to be stronger.

In the 1780s, many farmers in western Massachusetts were in trouble. They owed money but had little cash. The state raised taxes to pay its war debts and demanded payment in hard money, such as gold or silver. Courts began taking farms from people who could not pay. Some farmers were even sent to jail for debt.

In 1786, angry farmers, many of them war veterans, took action. They gathered in armed groups and shut down courts so judges could not take their farms. One of their leaders was Daniel Shays, a former captain in the Continental Army. In January 1787, rebels marched on the federal , a storehouse for weapons, in Springfield. The state militia fired on them, and several rebels were killed. The uprising, called Shays&#x27;s Rebellion, soon ended.

The rebellion shocked leaders across the country. Congress had no army to help Massachusetts. George Washington worried that the nation was falling into disorder. The year before, delegates from five states had met in Annapolis, Maryland, to discuss trade problems. They had called for a larger meeting in Philadelphia. After Shays’s Rebellion, more states agreed to send delegates.

Section 3

The Great Bargains of 1787

7.6

Two Plans for a New Government

Main ideaAt the Constitutional Convention, large states backed the Virginia Plan and small states backed the New Jersey Plan.

Soon after the began, delegates made a bold decision. Instead of fixing the Articles, they would write a new plan. Not everyone agreed, but the idea moved forward.

The first plan came from Virginia. It was mostly the work of James Madison. The called for three branches of government: one to make laws, one to carry them out and one to judge them. Congress would have two houses. In both, the number of representatives would depend on a state’s population. That meant large states such as Virginia would have much more power.

Small states objected. They feared the big states would outvote them on everything. William Paterson of New Jersey offered another plan. The kept a Congress with one house, where each state had one equal vote, as under the Articles. It gave Congress some new powers, such as taxing trade. For weeks, the two sides argued, and neither would give in.

7.7

The Great Compromise

Main ideaThe Great Compromise created a Congress with two houses: one based on population and one with equal votes for each state.

By early July 1787, the convention was stuck. Large and small states would not budge. Some delegates feared the meeting would fail. Roger Sherman and Oliver Ellsworth of Connecticut helped push a middle path.

Under their plan, Congress would have two houses. In the House of Representatives, each state’s number of seats would depend on its population. In the Senate, every state would have two senators, no matter its size. Bills about raising money would have to start in the House. On July 16, the delegates narrowly approved this plan. It became known as the , or the Connecticut Compromise.

Each side got something it wanted, and each gave something up. That is what a is. The Great Compromise saved the convention. Today, Congress still works this way. Illinois has 2 senators, the same as every other state, but its number of House members depends on its population.

7.8

Bargains Over Slavery

Main ideaTo keep southern states in the union, delegates made compromises that protected slavery in the new Constitution.

Slavery divided the delegates. Southern states had large numbers of enslaved people. They wanted them counted when deciding how many House seats a state got, even though enslaved people could not vote. Many northern delegates objected. The convention agreed that three of every five enslaved people would be counted. This was the . It gave southern states extra power in Congress and in choosing presidents.

Delegates also argued about the , the business of bringing captured Africans to America to be sold. Some delegates wanted to end it. Delegates from Georgia and South Carolina threatened to leave. The compromise said Congress could not ban the trade until 1808. Congress did ban it starting in 1808, but slavery itself continued inside the country.

Another clause required that people who escaped slavery into another state be returned. The Constitution never uses the word slavery. It speaks instead of “other persons” and people “held to service.” Many delegates knew slavery clashed with the ideals of the Revolution. They chose union over ending slavery, a choice that would shape the nation’s future.

7.9

Choosing a President

Main ideaDelegates created the office of president and the Electoral College as a compromise over how the nation's leader would be chosen.

The Articles had no leader to carry out laws. The delegates decided to create one: a . The president would enforce laws, command the military and deal with other nations. But how much power should one person have? After a war against a king, the delegates did not want to create another one.

They argued about how to choose the president. Some wanted Congress to pick. Others feared that would make the president too dependent on Congress. A few wanted the people to vote directly. Others worried that voters in a huge country would not know candidates from far-off states. There was also a slavery question. The South had many people who could not vote.

The answer was the . Each state chooses electors equal to its number of senators plus representatives. The electors vote for president. The president serves a four-year term. Everyone expected George Washington to be the first president. His honesty and his willingness to give up power after the war made many delegates comfortable creating the office.

Section 4

A New Plan Takes Hold

7.10

Dividing and Checking Power

Main ideaThe Constitution divides power among three branches and between the national and state governments so that no one part becomes too strong.

The Constitution begins with the Preamble, which explains its goals. Then it sets up three branches. Congress, the legislative branch, makes laws. The president leads the executive branch, which carries out laws. The Supreme Court and other courts form the judicial branch, which decides what laws mean. Splitting government this way is called .

Each branch can limit the others. This is called . The president can veto, or reject, a bill passed by Congress. Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in both houses. The Senate must approve the president’s choices for judges and top officials. Congress can impeach, or charge, and remove a president for serious wrongdoing.

The Constitution also divides power between the national government and the states. This system is called . Only the national government can print money, declare war or make treaties. States run elections and set up schools and local governments. Both can collect taxes. The Constitution can also be changed through amendments, but the process is hard on purpose.

7.11

The Fight Over Ratification

Main ideaFederalists and Antifederalists debated the Constitution in every state before it was ratified in 1788.

The Constitution would take effect only after 9 of the 13 states approved it. To means to approve officially. Each state held a special convention to decide. A great debate broke out in newspapers, taverns and meeting halls.

Supporters were called . They argued that the Articles had failed and that the nation needed a stronger government to survive. Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay wrote 85 essays in New York newspapers explaining the Constitution. They signed them with the pen name Publius. Today these essays are known as The Federalist Papers.

Opponents were called . They included Patrick Henry and George Mason of Virginia. They feared a strong national government would crush the states and threaten liberty. Their biggest complaint was that the Constitution had no bill of rights. Delaware ratified first, in December 1787. In June 1788, New Hampshire became the ninth state. Votes in Massachusetts, Virginia and New York were close. Federalists won support partly by promising to add a bill of rights.

7.12

Adding a Bill of Rights

Main ideaThe first ten amendments, the Bill of Rights, were added in 1791 to protect individual freedoms from the national government.

The new government began in 1789, with George Washington as the first president. Congress met in New York City, the first capital. James Madison, now a member of the House of Representatives, kept the Federalists’ promise. He drafted a list of amendments to protect people’s rights.

Congress sent 12 amendments to the states. By December 1791, enough states had ratified 10 of them. These first ten s are known as the . The First Amendment protects freedom of religion, speech, the press and assembly, and the right to petition the government. Other amendments protect the right to a fair and speedy trial, protect against unreasonable searches, and ban cruel and unusual punishments.

The Tenth Amendment says that powers not given to the national government are kept by the states or the people. At first, the Bill of Rights limited only the national government, and it did not protect everyone equally. Enslaved people, for example, did not enjoy these rights. Over time, Americans have used the Bill of Rights to demand fair treatment for more and more people.
