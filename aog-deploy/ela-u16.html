The Language of Persuasion

Rhetoric

In November 1863 the most admired speaker in America talked for two hours at Gettysburg, and the president who followed him spoke for about two minutes. One speech is a footnote. The other is carved in marble. Sixty-six years earlier, an Irish clergyman had calmly proposed that the poor sell their babies as food, and readers who understood him went cold with shame. And this week, somewhere near you, a poster, a column and a four-minute speech all argued the same claim, and the one with the least evidence changed the most minds. Words move people. This unit is about how.

You will start with rhetoric, the old art of choosing words to persuade: the three appeals of trust, feeling and reason; the situation every speech lives in; the devices of repetition, parallelism, antithesis and the question that answers itself. You will read Lincoln, Douglass, Sojourner Truth and the Declaration of Independence as arguments built by people who knew exactly what they were doing. Then you will study tone and voice, from Swift's straight-faced horror to Twain's deadpan, and learn how a reader hears the difference between what a text says and what its writer means. Finally you will take those tools into the world: ads, editorials, speeches and your own feed, where the same moves appear with less honesty and more speed.

By the end you will be able to name what a speaker is doing to you while it is happening, judge whether an argument has earned its conclusion, spot the five most common ways reasoning goes wrong, and write an argument that states the other side's best point and survives it. None of this is about being suspicious of everything. It is about being moved by the right things, for reasons you can explain.

The big question

How do words move people, and how do you tell an argument that has earned your agreement from one that has only won it?

The story

Two Minutes at Gettysburg

On a November afternoon in 1863, the famous speaker talked for two hours. Then the president stood up and spoke for about two minutes.

The battle of Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, was fought over three days in July 1863. When it ended, more than seven thousand soldiers were dead and tens of thousands were wounded. The bodies were buried in a hurry, in shallow graves, some in fields where farmers needed to plant. By fall the town had bought land for a proper cemetery, and a ceremony was planned to dedicate it on November 19.

The main speaker was Edward Everett, the most admired orator in the country. He had been president of Harvard, a senator, a secretary of state. He spoke from memory for about two hours. His speech described the battle in detail, named the generals, traced the movements of the armies, and compared the Union cause to the great causes of ancient Greece. The crowd listened. Newspapers printed every word.

Then Abraham Lincoln stood up. He had been invited almost as an afterthought, to give a few appropriate remarks. He spoke around 270 words. He did not name a single general or describe a single charge. He did not say the word Gettysburg. He talked about a nation founded on an idea, a war that was testing that idea, and a task the living still had to finish. Then he sat down. Some people in the crowd were not sure he had finished.

The next day Everett wrote Lincoln a note. He said he would be glad if he could believe that he had come as near to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as Lincoln had in two minutes. He meant it. Everett's speech is a historical document now. Lincoln's is carved in stone in Washington, and students memorize it in classrooms across Illinois, where Lincoln practiced law and where he is buried.

This chapter is about what those 270 words did, and how. Rhetoric is the art of choosing words to move an audience. It is an old word for something you already do every day, when you ask a coach for more playing time or talk a friend out of a bad decision. Lincoln did it better than almost anyone. By the end of this chapter you will be able to see his tools, and then pick them up yourself.

Talk about itEverett gave the crowd facts, names and details. Lincoln gave them almost none. Why might the speech with fewer facts have been the one that lasted?

The Three Appeals

37.1

Ethos: Why Should I Trust You?

Main ideaEthos is the trust an audience places in a speaker, and writers build it on purpose.

Two people tell you the same thing: the bridge on Ninth Street is unsafe. One is a stranger yelling from a car window. The other is a civil engineer who inspected the bridge that morning and is holding a clipboard. You believe the engineer. Nothing about the words changed. What changed is the speaker. Ancient Greek teachers of speaking called this , the character of the speaker as the audience sees it. When we trust the person, we lean toward trusting the message.

Speakers do not simply have ethos; they build it. They mention their experience. They admit a weakness before someone else can point it out. They use the right kind of language for the room, so that they sound like someone who belongs there. They show that they share the audience’s values. Frederick Douglass, speaking in 1852, opened by calling himself a humble speaker who felt his own limits. That modesty was a move. It told a room of white listeners that he was not there to scold them, which made it possible for him to scold them very hard a few minutes later.

Ethos can also be lost in one sentence. A speaker who gets a simple fact wrong, or who mocks the audience, or who clearly wants something for himself, loses the room. This is why the strongest ethos often comes from showing that you have something at stake, and that your interest lines up with the audience’s. Lincoln at Gettysburg never said I. He said we, eleven times. He placed himself in the crowd, one of the living with a job left to do. That is ethos built by standing next to your listeners instead of above them.

37.2

Pathos: Moving the Heart

Main ideaPathos is an appeal to the audience's emotions, and it works through pictures, stories and chosen words.

A charity could tell you that 14 percent of children in a county do not have enough to eat. Or it could show you a photograph of one child’s lunch: a slice of bread and half an apple. The number is true and the picture is true, but the picture makes you reach for your wallet. That pull is , the appeal to emotion. Pathos is not a trick. Human beings decide with their feelings as well as their reasons, and a speaker who ignores that is talking to half a person.

Pathos is built from concrete details. Abstract words like suffering and sacrifice slide past. A specific image stops you. Lincoln did not say the war had a high cost. He said the men had given the last full measure of devotion, a phrase that makes you picture a cup filled all the way. Douglass did not say slavery was cruel. He described the sound of the slave trader’s whip and the chains of a coffle of people marched through the streets, so that his listeners could hear it. Pathos lives in the noun you can see and the verb you can feel.

Word choice does the rest. Consider the difference between a house and a home, between a crowd and a mob, between passed away and died. Each pair means nearly the same thing, but each word carries a different feeling. Writers call this , the emotional charge of a word beyond its plain meaning. A speaker who wants you to feel warmth chooses home. A speaker who wants you to feel fear chooses mob. Learning to notice connotation is the first step to noticing when someone is steering your feelings.

Pathos has a limit. When an audience senses that it is being played, the feeling turns to resentment. A speech that is nothing but sad stories, with no reasons behind them, starts to feel like manipulation. The best speakers use pathos to make a reasoned point matter, not to replace the reasoning. Ask of any emotional appeal: is the feeling earned by the facts, or is it being used instead of them?

37.3

Logos: The Chain of Reasons

Main ideaLogos is the appeal to reason: claims backed by evidence and linked by logic the audience can follow.

A student wants a later curfew. She could say please a hundred times, or she could say: my grades went up this semester, I have never once come home late, and the bus from work does not arrive until 10:15. Now her parents have something to answer. That is , the appeal to reason. Logos gives the audience a claim, evidence for it, and the reasoning that connects the two. It is the part of an argument that would still stand if a stranger read it in a flat voice.

Evidence comes in kinds. There are facts and statistics, which can be checked. There are examples, which make a general claim concrete. There is testimony, what a witness or an expert says. And there is comparison, showing that a similar case turned out a certain way. Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address is short on evidence in the usual sense, but it has a clear logical spine. The nation was founded on the idea that all men are created equal. The war tests whether such a nation can last. Therefore the living must finish the work, so that the idea survives. Each step depends on the one before it.

The word therefore is the sound of logos. So are because, since, if, then and as a result. When you read a speech, circle those words and you will find its skeleton. Then ask the two questions that test any chain of reasons. Is each piece of evidence true? And does the conclusion really follow from it? Many arguments fail the second test while passing the first. The facts are right but they do not lead where the speaker says they lead.

Logos is the appeal that respects the audience most, because it treats listeners as people who can think. But logos alone rarely moves anyone. A perfect argument delivered by a speaker no one trusts, about a topic no one cares about, changes nothing. The Greek philosopher Aristotle, who first sorted the appeals into these three kinds, taught that a persuasive speech needs all three working together. Ethos opens the door, pathos makes the audience care, and logos gives them a reason to act.

Audience, Purpose and Occasion

37.4

The Rhetorical Situation

Main ideaEvery act of persuasion happens in a situation: a speaker, an audience, a purpose and an occasion that shape every choice.

Imagine the same message delivered three times. A coach tells the team at halftime, down by twelve, that they can still win. A doctor tells a patient that he can still recover. A friend texts you at midnight that you can still pass the class. The words are almost identical. Everything else is different: who is talking, who is listening, why, and when. Rhetoricians call this whole frame the . You cannot judge a speech without it, any more than you can judge a move in chess without seeing the board.

The is whoever the speaker is trying to move. It is rarely everyone. Lincoln at Gettysburg was speaking to a crowd of maybe fifteen thousand people, but he was also speaking past them to newspaper readers across the North, and beyond them to the future. Douglass in 1852 spoke to an antislavery society in Rochester, New York, mostly white, mostly already on his side. He needed them not to agree but to act. Knowing the audience tells you why a speaker chose a gentle tone or a harsh one, a simple word or a grand one.

The is what the speaker wants to happen. To inform, to persuade, to comfort, to warn, to unite, to shame. Most speeches have one main purpose and several smaller ones. The is the event and moment that called for the speech: a dedication, a trial, a holiday, a crisis. The occasion sets rules. A eulogy that cracks jokes and a wedding toast that lists the couple’s faults both break them. Lincoln’s occasion was a cemetery dedication, and so his speech honors the dead before it asks anything of the living. Patrick Henry’s occasion in March 1775 was a Virginia convention deciding whether to arm the colony, and so his speech is all urgency.

When you analyze a speech, start by writing these four things in one sentence: who is speaking, to whom, why, and when. Douglass, a formerly enslaved man, spoke to an antislavery society on July 5, 1852, to make them feel the distance between the nation’s promise and its practice. Once you have that sentence, every choice in the speech becomes a question you can answer. Why did he wait until the fifth of July instead of the fourth? Because the day after the celebration was the right moment to ask what the celebration meant.

37.5

Structure That Serves a Purpose

Main ideaThe order of a speech is a choice, and a strong structure carries the audience from where they are to where the speaker wants them.

Look at the shape of the Gettysburg Address, not the words. It has three movements. The first sentence is about the past: eighty-seven years ago, a nation was founded. The middle is about the present: now we are in a war, and we are standing on a battlefield. The last long sentence is about the future: what the living must do so that the nation shall not perish. Past, present, future. The itself is an argument. It says that this moment is a link in a chain, and that the chain can be broken or held.

Douglass’s Fourth of July speech has a different shape, chosen for a different purpose. He spends the first part praising the founders. He calls them brave and great, and he means it. Then he turns. He asks what the Fourth of July can mean to a person in chains, and the rest of the speech answers that question with a force that would have been impossible without the praise before it. The structure sets a trap the audience walks into willingly. They nod along at the founders, and then they discover that the founders’ words condemn them.

Lincoln’s Second Inaugural Address, given in March 1865 as the war was ending, uses a third shape. It opens by refusing to do what an inaugural speech usually does. There is no need for a long address, he says, and no need to predict the outcome. Then it looks back at how the war began and how both sides prayed to the same God. It ends with the famous call to act with malice toward none and charity for all. The structure moves from what will not be said, to what must be understood, to what must be done. Each stage prepares the next.

When you analyze structure, draw it. Divide the speech into its movements and give each one a label: praise, turn, demand; past, present, future; refusal, memory, call. Then ask why this order and not another. What would be lost if Douglass had begun with the attack? What if Lincoln had begun with the future? Usually you will find that the order does something that the words alone cannot. It manages the audience’s feelings over time, which is the deepest work a speech can do.

37.6

Word Choice Under Pressure

Main ideaIn a great speech, single words carry the argument, and swapping one for a near synonym changes what the speech does.

Lincoln began, Four score and seven years ago. He could have said eighty-seven years ago. The meaning is identical. But four score and seven echoes the language of the King James Bible, which most of his audience heard every Sunday. The phrase makes the founding sound ancient and sacred before he says a word about it. Word choice, which scholars call , is where a speaker’s purpose becomes visible in the smallest unit. Change the word and you change the room.

Douglass built his most famous passage on one pronoun. He did not say our Fourth of July. He said your Fourth of July, your national independence, your celebration. Every your is a wall. The audience, who thought of him as one of them, is suddenly on the other side of it. Then he answers his own question with a list of nouns: a sham, a fraud, a hollow mockery. Each is harsher than the one before. The diction climbs, and the audience climbs with it.

Notice what strong speakers avoid as much as what they use. Lincoln does not say Confederate, rebel, enemy or slavery in the Gettysburg Address. He speaks of the nation, the brave men, the great task. The absence keeps the speech from becoming an argument about blame and lets it be an argument about purpose. In the Second Inaugural he does say slavery, plainly, and calls it the cause of the war. By then the purpose had changed, and the diction changed with it.

To analyze diction, pick the three or four words in a passage that seem to be doing the most work. For each, write a near synonym and ask what would be different. Nation or country? Perish or end? Devotion or effort? Then look for patterns: are the words simple or grand, concrete or abstract, warm or cold? Lincoln’s are mostly short and plain, with a few grand ones placed like stones in a stream. That pattern is his voice, and it is a choice.

37.7

Repetition and Parallelism

Main ideaRepeating a word or a grammatical shape makes an idea easier to hear, remember and feel.

Say government of the people, by the people, for the people. Now say government that belongs to the people, is run by them and serves their interests. The second version is clearer in one sense. The first is the one carved in marble. What makes it work is , the same word people hammered three times, and , three phrases built on the same grammatical pattern: preposition plus the people. The ear hears the pattern before the mind sorts the meaning, and the pattern says: these three things belong together and they are complete.

Repetition works because the human ear is built for rhythm. A word said once is information. A word said three times is a drumbeat. Lincoln used we cannot three times in a row: we cannot dedicate, we cannot consecrate, we cannot hallow this ground. Each verb is grander than the last, and the repeated we cannot ties them into one rising line. Preachers, poets and coaches all know this trick. So do advertisers, which is a reason to notice it.

Parallelism is repetition of shape rather than word. When Patrick Henry asked whether life was so dear, or peace so sweet, as to be purchased at the price of chains and slavery, the two questions match each other: noun, so, adjective. Matched shapes feel balanced and finished. Broken parallelism feels clumsy, which is why a slogan like fast, cheap, and it works well does not stick. Your ear notices that the third item has a different shape, even if you cannot say why.

In your own writing, use parallelism when you have a list of ideas that you want the reader to weigh as equals. Give each the same grammatical form. Use repetition when one idea is the heart of the paragraph and you want it to ring. Then stop. A device used once is powerful. Used on every line, it becomes noise, and the audience stops hearing it.

37.8

Antithesis and the Rhetorical Question

Main ideaSetting opposites side by side, or asking a question the audience must answer in their heads, pulls listeners into the argument.

The world will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here. Read that sentence twice. It is built on a hinge: what we say against what they did, little note against never forget. This is , placing two opposite ideas in matched grammatical shapes so that the contrast snaps into focus. Antithesis is parallelism with a fight inside it. The ear hears the balance; the mind feels the clash.

Antithesis is everywhere in great speeches because a contrast is easier to grasp than a single idea. Patrick Henry ended his speech to the Virginia convention with a choice between liberty and death. Douglass set the founders’ greatness against the nation’s shame. Lincoln’s Second Inaugural balances both sides of the war in one line: both read the same Bible and pray to the same God, and each invokes His aid against the other. In every case the speaker does not explain the contrast. He places the two halves and lets the audience feel the gap.

A is a question asked for effect, with no answer expected because the answer is obvious. Douglass asks whether he is expected to argue that a man is entitled to liberty, that he is the rightful owner of his own body. He does not wait for a reply. The question makes the audience answer yes in their own heads, which means they have just made his argument for him. That is the power of the device. A statement is something the speaker owns. A rhetorical question is something the listener helps build.

Both devices carry a risk. Antithesis can make a complicated matter look like a simple choice between two things, when there are really five. Rhetorical questions can sound like a speaker avoiding the work of proving a point. When you find one in a text, ask: is the contrast fair, and is the answer to the question really as obvious as the speaker pretends? Sometimes it is. Sometimes the question is doing the work that evidence should have done.

Reading and Writing Rhetoric

37.9

Two Voices for Freedom

Main ideaDouglass and Sojourner Truth used very different tools to argue for the same thing, and the difference came from their situations.

Frederick Douglass escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838 and became the most famous Black orator in America. His 1852 speech in Rochester is long, learned and carefully built. He quotes the Declaration of Independence and the Bible. He uses the vocabulary of lawyers and preachers. His ethos depends on showing a mostly white audience that a formerly enslaved man could out-argue any of them on their own ground. Every long sentence is part of that proof.

Sojourner Truth was born enslaved in New York and freed by state law in the 1820s. She could not read or write. She spoke at a women’s rights convention in Akron, Ohio, in 1851. What she said was written down by others, in more than one version. The versions do not fully agree. The best-known version was published years later. It put her words into a Southern dialect she almost certainly did not use, since she grew up speaking Dutch. So we know her argument better than her exact words. She argued from her own body and her own life. She had worked as hard as any man. She had borne children and seen them sold. She asked whether she was not a woman too.

Set the two side by side. Douglass builds logos from documents; Truth builds it from lived experience. Douglass borrows the authority of the founders; Truth’s authority is her own arms and back. Douglass’s audience needed to be shamed into action; Truth’s audience, arguing about whether women were too weak for rights, needed to be shown a woman who was not. Neither approach is better. Each fits its rhetorical situation. That is the lesson: the right tool depends on the speaker, the room and the job.

Truth’s case also teaches something about sources. When a speech survives only in other people’s notes, you are reading their choices as well as hers. Founding Documents as Arguments

Main ideaThe Declaration of Independence is a structured argument, and later speakers borrowed its ethos by quoting it.

The Declaration of Independence, adopted July 4, 1776, is often read as a list of famous phrases. It is better read as an argument with a shape. It opens by saying why an explanation is owed: when a people separates from its government, decent respect for the opinions of mankind requires them to state their reasons. Then it gives a general principle: all men are created equal and have rights that government exists to protect. Then it gives evidence, a long list of specific complaints against the king. Then it draws the conclusion: these colonies are, and ought to be, free.

That shape is logos in its classic form. Principle, evidence, conclusion. The principle is stated as self-evident, which is a rhetorical move: it tells the reader that no proof is needed and that anyone who disagrees is being unreasonable. The list of complaints is pathos as much as logos. Twenty-seven grievances in a row, each beginning He has, build an anger that a summary could never produce. That is repetition doing emotional work inside a legal document.

Once a document has this kind of standing, other speakers borrow it. Lincoln’s four score and seven years counted back to 1776, not to the Constitution of 1787. The Declaration held the phrase he needed: created equal. Douglass quoted the Declaration to his audience and then asked whether its principles extended to him. The women who met at Seneca Falls in 1848 rewrote it as a Declaration of Sentiments, changing all men are created equal to all men and women are created equal. Each of them was claiming the founders’ ethos for a cause the founders had not included.

This is one of the most useful patterns in rhetoric. Find a text your audience already honors, and show that your claim follows from it. The audience cannot reject your claim without rejecting the text they honor. It is also a pattern to watch for, because a speaker can quote a famous document while ignoring what it actually says. When someone appeals to the founders, or to the Constitution, or to the Bible, go and read the passage. Ask whether it says what the speaker claims.

Writing a Rhetorical Analysis

Main ideaA rhetorical analysis explains how a text persuades, not whether you agree with it, and it does so with quoted evidence.

A answers one question: how does this text try to move its audience? Not is it right, not do I like it. How. That shift is hard for many writers, because it means holding your own opinion to one side. You can analyze a speech you despise and find its methods brilliant. You can admire a speech and find its logic thin. The essay is about the tools, and about how well they fit the job.

Start with the rhetorical situation in one sentence: speaker, audience, purpose, occasion. Then state a thesis that names the main strategies and what they accomplish. Not: Lincoln uses ethos, pathos and logos. That is true of every speech ever given. Instead: Lincoln uses a past-present-future structure, repeated first-person plural pronouns, and Biblical diction to turn a cemetery dedication into a call to finish the war. A thesis like that can be proved, because it makes specific claims you can point to in the text.

Each body paragraph then follows one pattern. Name a choice the writer made. Quote the words that show it, briefly. Explain what the choice does to the audience and how it serves the purpose. The middle step, the quoting, is where weak analyses fall apart. Do not summarize the speech. Do not tell the reader that Lincoln was a great speaker. Put the actual words on the page and then look closely at them. One well-examined sentence beats ten paragraphs of praise.

Here is a model paragraph, written for this lesson. Lincoln never says I in the Gettysburg Address. In its place he uses we and us fifteen times, as in it is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here. The pronoun places the president in the crowd, one mourner among many, and gives the closing charge the weight of a shared vow rather than an order. For an audience that had just buried its sons, an order from Washington might have stung. A vow made together could be kept. That paragraph names a choice, quotes it, and explains its effect on a specific audience. That is the whole job.

A Perfectly Reasonable Suggestion

In 1729 a respected Irish clergyman published a calm, well-organized essay proposing that poor families sell their babies as food.

Jonathan Swift was the dean of St. Patrick's Cathedral in Dublin, a famous writer, and an angry man. Ireland in the 1720s was ruled from London, and its poor were desperately poor. Beggars crowded the roads with children they could not feed. Swift had written serious pamphlets about it, with serious proposals. Almost nobody in power had listened.

So in 1729 he published a short essay with a long title. It called itself a modest proposal for preventing the children of poor people from being a burden to their parents or country. The voice in the essay is calm and helpful. It sounds like an economist. It begins by describing the sad sight of beggars and their children, and says that anyone who could find a cheap and easy way to make those children useful would deserve a statue.

Then it makes its proposal. The writer has been assured, he says, by a knowledgeable American, that a healthy one-year-old child is a delicious and nourishing food, whether stewed, roasted, baked or boiled. He goes on to work out the numbers. He estimates how many children could be sold each year, at what price, and how much a family would earn. He notes that the skin could be made into gloves. He answers objections. He lists the advantages, one by one, in a perfectly organized paragraph.

Readers at the time were horrified, and then, a beat later, most of them understood. Swift did not want anyone to eat a child. He wanted his readers to notice that England's policies and Ireland's landlords were already treating the poor as things to be consumed. By saying the monstrous thing in the voice of a reasonable man, he made the reasonable men in his audience hear themselves. Near the end, he even slips in the real solutions he had proposed before, and has his narrator dismiss them as hopeless.

The essay works because of tone. Every word is calm; every idea is monstrous. The gap between the two is where the meaning lives. This chapter is about that gap: how writers control the feeling of their words, how a reader hears the difference between what a text says and what its writer means, and how you can build a voice of your own that sounds like nobody else.

Tone Is Not Mood

Main ideaTone is the writer's attitude toward the subject; mood is the feeling the text creates in the reader, and the two can differ.

Your phone buzzes: Fine. Do whatever you want. Those five words carry an attitude, and you can hear it even though no one spoke aloud. The attitude is not fine at all. That attitude, the writer’s feeling toward the subject or the reader, is . Every text has one, from a textbook to a text message. In speech, tone lives in the voice. On the page, it has to be built out of words, and a reader who cannot hear it will misread almost everything.

Tone is different from . Mood is the feeling the text creates in the reader. The two often match: a warm tone makes a warm mood. But they can pull apart. Swift’s tone in A Modest Proposal is calm, patient and practical. The mood in the reader is horror. That gap is on purpose. A horror story might be told in a cheerful tone to make the reader more uneasy, not less. When you describe a text, keep the two words separate. Tone belongs to the writer’s attitude. Mood belongs to the reader’s experience.

Naming tone takes precise words. Happy and sad are not enough. Is the tone bitter, playful, tender, mocking, weary, urgent, cold, admiring, sarcastic, matter-of-fact? Try to find a word that fits and one that almost fits, and explain the difference. A student who says the tone of the Gettysburg Address is serious is right but has said little. Tone is usually the first thing a reader feels and the last thing a reader can explain, and the skill of this chapter is closing that gap.

Diction Builds Tone

Main ideaTone comes mostly from word choice: connotation, level of formality and how concrete or abstract the words are.

Here are two sentences about the same dog. The animal was removed from the residence. And: They dragged the poor old dog out of his home. Same event. The first is cold, official, a little evasive; nobody in it seems responsible. The second is angry and sad. Nothing changed but the words. Removed became dragged; animal became poor old dog; residence became home. This is at work, and it is the main engine of tone.

Three features of diction do most of the work. First, : the emotional charge a word carries. Slender and skinny describe the same body with opposite attitudes. Second, formality: whether the words are the kind you would use in a courtroom or a kitchen. Residence is formal; home is not. Formal diction tends to sound distant, careful, sometimes cold. Informal diction sounds close and personal, sometimes careless. Third, whether words are concrete or abstract. A thing you can see, dog, has more feeling in it than a category, animal.

Swift’s diction is the secret of A Modest Proposal. He uses the words of trade and farming for human children: he speaks of breeders, of stock, of yield, of a child as a dish. Every one of those words is ordinary in a report about cattle. Put next to the word child, each becomes a small shock. The reader feels the tone go wrong before the argument does, and that feeling is the point. Swift is showing how the language of profit sounds when it is turned on people.

To analyze diction for tone, underline the five words in a passage that carry the most feeling. For each, write the plain, neutral version. Then describe what the writer’s choice adds. If you cannot find a neutral version, that word may be doing more than you thought. This method also works in reverse for your own writing. If a paragraph you wrote sounds flat, find the neutral words and ask which ones deserve a charge.

Main ideaSentence length and shape, called syntax, give a writer's voice its rhythm and can change tone as much as words do.

Irony: Saying the Opposite

Main ideaVerbal irony means the opposite of what the words say, and readers detect it through context and tone clues.

It is pouring rain, your bus is late and your shoes are soaked. Someone says, Great day. You do not correct them. You know they mean the reverse, and you know it instantly, from the situation and their face. That is : saying one thing to mean its opposite. Sarcasm is verbal irony with an edge, aimed at someone. On the page there is no face, so a writer has to leave other clues, and a reader has to look for them.

The clues are usually a gap. The words say one thing; the facts around them say another. Swift’s narrator calls his proposal modest and then proposes cannibalism. The word modest is ironic because the plan is the least modest thing imaginable. Praise that is far too big for its object is a clue: a writer who calls a broken pencil a magnificent instrument does not mean it. So is calm about something that should cause alarm. So is a narrator who reveals more than he realizes, as when Swift’s narrator mentions that he cannot profit from his own plan because his children are too old.

There are two other kinds of irony worth knowing. is when what happens is the opposite of what was expected, such as a fire station burning down. is when the audience knows something a character does not, so that the character’s words mean more than the character intends. Neither is about tone exactly, but both create it. A story full of dramatic irony feels tense or sad, because the reader is watching someone walk toward a door they cannot see.

Irony is the device most often missed by readers, and missing it flips the meaning of a text. A reader who takes Swift literally thinks he is a monster. A reader who misses the irony in a friend’s text starts a fight. When something in a text seems too strange, too calm or too extreme to be meant, stop and ask whether the writer is saying the opposite. That pause is the whole skill. Irony does not announce itself; it waits for a reader who is paying attention.

Satire: Laughing to Wound

Main ideaSatire uses irony, exaggeration and mockery to expose a real fault, and it always has a target and a purpose.

Swift’s essay is satire. Its target is not the poor, and not really the narrator. Its target is a society that talked about poverty in the cold language of profit and did nothing. The laughter, if you can call it that, is meant to shame.

Satire has tools. Irony is the main one: the writer pretends to hold a view in order to expose it. is another: taking a real tendency and pushing it past the point of sanity, so that its shape becomes visible. Society was already treating the poor as economic units; Swift pushed that one step further, to eating them. A third tool is the , an invented speaker who is not the author. The persona is a solemn old moralist; the target is the empty way adults lecture the young. The reader laughs, then notices that Twain has a point.

That is worth remembering online, where a satirical post gets shared without its context and becomes, for many readers, a sincere one. When you meet a text that seems outrageous, look for the target. If there is one, and the exaggeration exposes something true about it, you are probably reading satire. If the outrage is the whole point, you are probably not.

Main ideaSaying less than the truth or far more than it are both tone tools, and both depend on the reader knowing the real size of the thing.

The gap between the small words and the large fact is where the effect lives. Understatement can be funny, brave or bitter, depending on the situation, but it always sends the same signal: the speaker is in control of feelings that could have overflowed.

Overstatement, also called , runs the other way. I have told you a million times. That backpack weighs a ton. Nobody takes the number literally, and nobody is meant to. Hyperbole is the natural language of anger, joy and exhaustion, and it is everywhere in advertising, which is a reason to notice it there. That means writers must give the facts before or after the small or huge words, and readers must check the facts against the words. When you find a claim that seems far too small or far too big, ask which it is, and then ask what tone it creates. The answer is often the key to the whole passage.

38.7

Formal and Informal Registers

Main ideaA register is the level of language a situation calls for, and choosing the wrong one is a tone mistake even when every word is correct.

You do not talk to your grandmother the way you talk to your teammates, and you do not text a friend the way you write a college application. Each situation has a : a level of language, from very formal to very casual, that fits it. Formal register uses complete sentences, standard grammar, precise vocabulary and no slang. Informal register allows contractions, sentence fragments, slang and inside jokes. Neither is better. A wedding toast in legal language and a court filing in text-speak are both failures.

Register is a tone decision that most people make without noticing. That is fine until the situation is new. A student emailing a teacher for the first time often reaches for the register of a group chat, hey can u send the hw, and the teacher hears disrespect that the student did not intend. The words were not wrong. The register was. The fix is not to sound fancy; it is to match the room: Hello Ms. Ortiz, could you send the homework from Tuesday? Thank you.

Writers also switch registers on purpose, for effect. A formal essay that drops one plain, blunt sentence, such as This is wrong, gains force from the drop. A casual voice that suddenly goes formal can sound ironic, as when a friend says I regret to inform you that we are out of chips. Twain is a master of this. His narrators speak plainly and then produce a grand phrase, and the mismatch is the joke. Swift does the reverse: a formal register the whole way, applied to something no formal register should touch.

When you read, notice the register and ask whether it fits. A politician who speaks to a crowd of farmers in the register of a law lecture has made a rhetorical error, and a reader who notices can say exactly what went wrong.

Tone in Letters and Editorials

Main ideaPersuasive nonfiction, from an editorial to a letter, chooses a tone to fit its reader, and the tone is part of the argument.

An is an opinion piece in a newspaper, written to persuade readers about a public question. A letter to the editor is a reader’s reply. Both are arguments, but their tone is a choice as important as their evidence. A calm, measured editorial invites the reader to reason alongside it. A furious one invites the reader to feel with it or reject it. Neither is automatically right. The tone must fit the subject, the reader and the paper.

Read the opening of an editorial and ask what it assumes about you. A sentence like Anyone who has waited for a Chicago bus in January knows the problem assumes you are a neighbor and a fellow sufferer; the tone is companionable. A sentence like The council’s decision is indefensible assumes you are a judge who will weigh the case; the tone is prosecutorial. A sentence like It is time we stopped pretending assumes you share the writer’s frustration; the tone is impatient. Each opening picks a relationship with the reader and holds it.

Letters, public and private, work the same way. Lincoln wrote in 1862 to Horace Greeley, a newspaper editor who had attacked him in print. Lincoln’s reply was published. Its tone is patient and almost gentle, even though the subject was the most explosive question of the war. He says that if he could save the Union without freeing any slave he would, and if he could save it by freeing all of them he would, and if he could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone he would do that. The measured tone was aimed past Greeley at a divided public, and it was itself an argument: that the president was thinking, not raging.

When you analyze a persuasive letter or editorial, name the tone in a precise word or two, find three phrases that create it, and then ask the important question: how does this tone help the argument reach this reader? A furious tone can be exactly right for readers who are already angry and need to be organized. A gentle tone can be exactly right for readers who expect an attack. The tone is never decoration. It is a decision about who the reader is.

When the Tone Shifts

Main ideaMany texts change tone partway through, and the moment of the shift is usually where the meaning turns.

Douglass spends the first part of his Fourth of July speech in a tone of respect, even admiration, for the founders. Then, in a sentence, he changes. Pardon me, he asks, why am I called to speak here today? The tone becomes sharp, then furious, then, near the end, hopeful. A reader who marks only one tone for the speech has missed its engine. The is where the argument happens.

Tone shifts are usually marked. Look for a but, a yet, a question, a one-line paragraph, a change of pronoun, a sudden short sentence after long ones. Look for a change in diction, from grand words to plain ones or the reverse. Lincoln’s Second Inaugural moves from a flat, almost bored tone about how little needs to be said, to a solemn tone about God’s judgment, to the tender last sentence. Each turn is signaled by a change in sentence length and word choice, and each turn does something to the audience.

Swift’s essay has a shift so quiet that many readers miss it. Near the end, the narrator lists other remedies, the ones Swift had seriously proposed in earlier writing: taxing absentee landlords, buying Irish goods, learning to love the country. The narrator says he does not want to hear of these, since there is no hope anyone will try them. For one paragraph the mask slips. The tone under the calm is exhausted and bitter, and a reader who catches it has found Swift’s actual face.

When you analyze a text, draw a line where the tone changes and label the tone on each side. Then ask two questions. What signals the shift? And what does the shift do to the reader? Often the answer to the second question is the thesis of your whole essay. A text that moves from praise to attack is making its audience feel the fall. A text that moves from attack to hope is offering a way out. The shape of the tone is the shape of the argument.

38.10

Reading Twain for Voice

Main ideaTwain's voice comes from plain diction, talking syntax and a deadpan turn, and studying it shows how a voice is made of choices.

Samuel Clemens grew up in Hannibal, Missouri, on the Mississippi River. He worked as a riverboat pilot, and he took the name Mark Twain from the leadsman’s call for two fathoms of water. He wrote The Adventures of Tom Sawyer in 1876 and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn in 1884. In the second book he did something new in American literature. He let a poor, uneducated boy tell the whole story in his own words. The voice was the point. Readers had never heard a book sound like that.

Three things make Twain’s voice. First, plain diction: short common words, the ones people actually say, with the occasional grand word dropped in for comic effect. Second, a talking syntax: long sentences strung together with and, the way a person tells a story out loud, with a short sentence when the story needs to stop. Third, the deadpan turn: a sentence that sets up something serious and ends somewhere absurd, delivered with a straight face. The reader does the laughing; the narrator never does.

Twain also wrote about voice directly. In a letter of 1888 he said that the difference between the almost right word and the right word is a large matter, as large as the difference between the lightning bug and the lightning. That is the plainest statement of a stylist’s creed ever written, and it is itself an example. He could have said precise diction matters. Instead he gave you a bug and a bolt of lightning.

To learn from a voice, imitate it. Take a paragraph of your own and rewrite it the way Twain would tell it: plain words, one long talking sentence, one deadpan turn at the end. Then rewrite it as Poe would, nervous and full of dashes. You will not write like either of them for long. But you will discover that your own voice is also made of choices, and that you can make them on purpose.

Developing a Voice of Your Own

Main ideaYour writing voice is built from choices you already make in speech, and you strengthen it by writing plainly, reading aloud and cutting what is not yours.

Many students believe they have no writing voice. They do. It shows up in the story they tell at lunch, in the text they send when something goes wrong, in the way they argue with a sibling. Those are all voices, made of diction and syntax and attitude. The problem is not that the voice is missing. The problem is that school writing often trains students to hide it behind words they would never say: utilize, in conclusion, the aforementioned. A voice cannot be heard through a costume.

The first step, then, is subtraction. Write a draft. Read it aloud. Every time you hit a phrase you would never say out loud to a friend, mark it. Some of those phrases belong; formal writing has its register. But most of them are borrowed, and borrowed words sound borrowed. Replace them with the word you would actually use, and see if the sentence gets stronger. It usually does. Twain’s lightning-bug rule applies here: the almost-right word is often the one you reached for because it sounded like writing.

The second step is addition, and it is harder. A voice needs a point of view: something you notice that others do not, an attitude toward the subject, a willingness to say a plain thing plainly. It also needs rhythm. Vary your sentence lengths. Put the short sentence where the stop belongs. Let one long sentence run when the thought runs. Read writers whose voices you like, and notice what they do, and steal the moves without stealing the words.

The third step is honesty about register. A strong voice is not the same as an informal one. Lincoln’s voice at Gettysburg is formal, and it is unmistakably his. What makes a voice is not slang but control: the writer chose every word, and you can feel it. So choose. Decide your tone before you begin. Hold it. Break it once if the break is earned. And read your writing aloud, always, because the ear catches what the eye forgives.

39.1

Ads, Editorials and Speeches

Main ideaDifferent kinds of persuasive text follow different rules, and a reader judges each by what its form can and cannot do.

An has seconds. It cannot build a chain of reasons, so it does not try. It works by pairing a claim with a feeling: a picture, a face, a song, three words. Judge an ad by asking what it wants you to feel and what it wants you to do, and then asking whether the feeling has anything to do with the doing. A soda ad full of laughing friends is offering friendship, not soda. Once you name the trade, the ad loses most of its power, and you can decide about the soda on its own.

An or opinion column has room. It can cite a study, quote an expert, admit a weakness and answer it. That is why it is the form where you should expect evidence, and where its absence is a warning. A column that runs eight hundred words and gives no checkable fact is an ad in a longer coat. Judge an editorial by the quality of its evidence and by whether it deals honestly with the strongest point on the other side. The pediatrician’s column admitted the bus problem. That admission made the rest of it more believable.

A speech has a body in the room. It can use the speaker’s presence, the audience’s mood and the moment. Dani’s alarm clock was evidence a column could not offer, because you could see her. But a speech is also gone the moment it ends, so speakers rely on repetition, short claims and pathos more than writers do. Judge a speech by what would survive if you wrote it down. The skill is to hold each text to the standard of its form, and then to notice what the form leaves out. Ads leave out reasons. Speeches leave out detail. Editorials leave out the face. How Good Is the Evidence?

Main ideaEvidence is not all equal: ask where it came from, how much of it there is, how recent it is, and whether it actually supports the claim.

Two people tell you a new phone is terrible. One tried it for a day. The other runs a repair shop and has seen five hundred of them. Both are giving you evidence. It is not the same evidence. The first job of a reader is to sort what is offered by its weight, and there are four questions that do most of the sorting. Where did this come from? How much of it is there? How recent is it? And does it actually support the claim it is attached to?

Source first. A statistic from a national study is not the same as a statistic from a company selling the product. An expert in sleep is not an expert in bus schedules. When a text says studies show, ask which studies, and whether you could find them. Then quantity. One story is an : vivid, human, and proof of almost nothing on its own, because one case can always be an exception. Dani’s alarm clock was an anecdote. It moved the room, and it was honest, but a thousand students with different alarms would tell you more. The pediatrician’s two districts were a small sample too. Good readers notice the number.

Recency matters when the world changes. A study of teenage sleep from twenty years ago may still hold; a statistic about phone use from five years ago probably does not. And then the hardest question: . Evidence can be true, plentiful and fresh and still not prove the point. Suppose a column shows that students in a later-starting district earn higher grades. That is real evidence. But if the district is also wealthier, the grades may come from the money, not the clock. The evidence is true; the link to the claim is weak.

Make this a habit with a simple mark. When you read an argument, put a small mark by every piece of evidence and rate it quickly: strong, fair or weak, on those four questions. Then look at what the argument rests on. Some arguments turn out to rest on one strong study and a lot of noise; that is fine, if the study holds. Some rest on nothing but anecdotes and confidence. Those are the ones to be careful with, especially when you agree with them.

Finding the Assumption

Main ideaEvery argument rests on something unstated, and finding that assumption is often the fastest way to test the argument.

Here is a complete argument in two sentences. Teenagers are tired in first period. Therefore school should start later. It sounds finished, but there is a gap between the evidence and the conclusion, and something invisible is bridging it. The bridge is an : a belief the argument needs but never states. Here the assumption is that the tiredness comes from the start time and not from something else, such as phones at midnight. If that assumption fails, the evidence stays true and the conclusion falls anyway.

Every argument has assumptions. That is not a flaw; it is how arguments work. Nobody can state everything. The question is whether the assumption is one the audience would accept if it were said aloud. Let Us Sleep assumes that sleep is the school’s responsibility. Many people would agree. Some would not, and they would say a student who wants sleep should go to bed. The poster never argues that point because it never sees it. Its readers who disagree are not being stubborn; they are rejecting a premise the poster did not know it had.

To find an assumption, write the evidence on one line and the conclusion on another, and ask what would have to be true for the first to lead to the second. Then say it out loud in the plainest words you can. Often it sounds reasonable, and you move on. Sometimes it sounds like this: all the people in this group think alike. Or: what happened once will happen again. Or: because I want this, it must be true. Said aloud, those collapse. That is why speakers leave them unsaid, sometimes on purpose and often without noticing.

Lincoln in 1858, running for the Senate in Illinois, said that a house divided against itself cannot stand, and that the nation could not remain permanently half slave and half free. His stated evidence was the history of the slavery fight. His assumption, which he made plain, was that the nation would eventually become all one thing or all the other. His opponent Stephen Douglas rejected exactly that assumption, arguing that the states could go on differing forever. The whole campaign was a fight over an unstated premise brought into the open. That is what a good argument does.

How Arguments Go Wrong

39.4

Slippery Slope and Straw Man

Main ideaA slippery slope predicts disaster without showing each step; a straw man attacks a weaker version of the other side's argument.

A is a pattern of reasoning that looks like an argument but does not do the work of one. Fallacies are worth naming because they repeat. Once you know the shape, you see it in a board meeting, a comment thread and your own first draft. This lesson and the next two name five of the most common. Learn them as shapes, not as insults; calling someone’s point a fallacy without showing where the reasoning breaks is itself a lazy move.

The says that one step will lead, unstoppably, to a terrible end. If we let students use phones at lunch, next they will use them in class, then during tests, and then no one will learn anything. Each step might follow. But the argument never shows that it must. It skips from the first step to the cliff and lets fear do the rest. The test is simple: ask for the reasons at each step. A real argument can give them. A slippery slope has nothing to say when you ask why the second step follows from the first.

The is a different move. Instead of answering what the other side said, the speaker answers something weaker that sounds similar, knocks it down, and takes a bow. The pediatrician asked for a one-year trial of a later start time. A board member replied that we cannot let students sleep until noon. Nobody had asked for noon. The board member built a figure of straw, easy to topple, and toppled it. The test is to go back to the original words. If the reply answers something the other side did not say, it has answered nothing.

Both fallacies are common because both feel like winning. The slippery slope makes the speaker sound like the only one who sees danger; the straw man makes the other side sound foolish. Neither one moves a careful listener, and both leave the real question untouched. When you catch one in your own writing, the fix is the same: go back to the actual claim on the other side, state it in words its holders would accept, and answer that. It is harder. It is also the only thing that works on people who do not already agree with you.

Main ideaJumping from too few cases to a rule, or from one thing following another to one thing causing another, are two of the most natural mistakes in reasoning.

You meet two people from a town and both are rude, so the town is rude. A friend fails one quiz and decides she is bad at math. A takes a small number of cases and stretches them into a rule about everyone. The mind does this on its own, because patterns are useful and quick. The fallacy is not in noticing a pattern; it is in stopping there. Two cases are a hunch. A rule needs enough cases, chosen fairly, to rule out the chance that you just happened to meet the wrong two.

Watch for the words that hide it: everyone, always, no one, teenagers, people like that. Then look for the sample. In a school debate on start times, a student said that everyone she knew was exhausted by second period. Her sample was her friends, who stayed up together on the same group chat. That is a real observation about a real group. It says nothing about the freshman who goes to bed at nine. Fixing the fallacy does not mean abandoning the claim. It means shrinking it to the size of the evidence: many students I know, not everyone.

is a cousin. Because one thing happened after another, the argument says the first caused the second. The district moved its start time and test scores rose; therefore the start time raised the scores. Maybe. But the same year the district hired new teachers and cut class sizes. The order of events proves nothing by itself. The classic case: ice cream sales and drownings both rise in July, and neither one causes the other; summer causes both. Whenever you see after, therefore because of, look for the third thing.

Both fallacies are the raw material of most bad arguments about people. Almost every prejudice is a hasty generalization, and almost every superstition is a false cause. That is why they matter beyond essays. The habit that guards against them is a single question, asked before you believe and before you write: what else could explain this? If you can think of one other cause or one exception, you do not have a proof yet. You have a lead, and a lead is worth following, honestly.

39.6

Appeal to Authority and Other Shortcuts

Main ideaCiting an authority is fine when the authority is real and relevant; it becomes a fallacy when the name replaces the reasoning.

A doctor says the start time should change. A famous athlete says the same. Which one counts? Both are appeals to authority, and one of them is legitimate. The doctor is an expert on the thing being argued, sleep and adolescent health, and her expertise is a reason to weigh her view. The athlete is an expert on running, which is not the question. The becomes a fallacy when the authority is outside the field, when the authority is unnamed, or when a name is used to end the discussion instead of to add to it.

The test has two parts. Is this person actually an expert on this exact question? And does the argument still show its reasoning, or does it stop at the name? Studies show, experts agree and everyone knows are the sound of the second failure. A real expert can be cited and then checked. A vague one cannot. Notice, too, that even a true expert can be wrong; the appeal gives you a reason to listen, not a reason to stop thinking. Aristotle was a genuine authority, and he thought heavy objects fall faster than light ones.

A few other shortcuts are worth naming because they appear so often. The attacks the person instead of the argument: of course she wants a later start, she is a teenager. The says a thing is true because many people believe it. The offers only two choices when there are more: either we change the start time or we accept failing students. Each of these can be answered the same way. Bring the conversation back to the claim and the evidence, which is where it should have been.

One warning. Learning the names of fallacies can make a reader feel clever and go hunting, and a hunt for fallacies is not the same as a search for the truth. An argument with a fallacy in it may still be right; the fallacy just means that particular step did not prove it. The right response is not to declare victory but to ask what evidence would prove the point, and then to look for it. Fallacies are places where the work was skipped. The work still has to be done, by someone.

39.7

The Rhetorical Situation of a Feed

Main ideaSocial media is a rhetorical situation with its own audience, purpose and pressures, and those pressures reward the weakest kinds of argument.

Apply the four questions from the last chapter to a post in your feed. Who is speaking? Often you cannot tell, and the name might be invented. To whom? To everyone and no one, since the writer does not know who will see it. Why? Frequently to be seen, shared and liked, which is a different purpose from persuading. When? In a stream that moves so fast that a post has a few seconds to earn a second look. That is the rhetorical situation of social media, and it explains a great deal about what appears there.

The pressures of that situation reward certain moves. Short claims beat long reasoning, because reasoning takes room. Strong feeling beats careful evidence, because feeling travels faster than checking. Certainty beats doubt, because no one shares a post that says it might be complicated. A straw man is easy to write and easy to cheer. A slippery slope fits in one line. The feed is not full of fallacies because people online are foolish. It is full of them because the form rewards them, the way a poster rewards a slogan.

That has consequences for readers. The post that reaches you has already been sorted by how much feeling it produced in other people, not by how true it was. Your own feed is also shaped by what you have clicked, so it learns to show you the arguments you already like. That is a hasty generalization machine: you see the same kind of case a hundred times and conclude that the world is like that. The fix is not to leave the feed. It is to read it knowing what it is, the way you read an ad knowing what it is.

The most useful habit is to take a post you feel strongly about and treat it as a text. Write down the claim. Write down the evidence, if any. Name the appeal it leans on. Look for the assumption. Ask what form it is, an ad, an anecdote, an argument, and hold it to that form’s standard. Three minutes of this on a post that made you angry will usually show you that the post was built to make you angry, which is a different thing from being right.

39.8

Checking Before You Share

Main ideaSharing a claim is making it, so check the source, the date and the original context before passing it on.

When you share a post, you become its author for everyone who sees it through you. That is the plain rhetorical fact. If it is wrong, you said it. So the question before sharing is the same as the question before writing: can I stand behind this? A few checks take less time than typing a comment. They are the checks a good editor makes before printing a letter, and they are worth learning as a habit rather than a rule.

First, find the source. A screenshot of a headline is not a source; the article is. A post that says a study found is not a source; the study is. Click through, and if there is nothing to click through to, that silence is the answer. Second, check the date. Old news gets shared as new all the time, and a true statistic from 2015 can be a false one now. Third, check the : the words around the words. A quotation cut out of a speech can mean the opposite of what the speech meant, and irony, as the last chapter showed, dies the moment it leaves its context.

Fourth, look sideways. Instead of reading more of the post, open a new tab and see what other, independent sources say about the same claim. If a startling fact appears in only one place, that is a reason for doubt, not excitement. If the same claim is being made by sources that do not usually agree, it is more likely to be true. This is the habit of a reporter, and it is faster than it sounds once it is a habit.

Finally, notice your own feeling. The posts you most want to share are the ones that made you feel something strongly, and that feeling is exactly what a weak argument counts on. This does not mean distrusting everything that moves you. It means that a strong feeling is a signal to check, the way a car’s warning light is a signal to look under the hood. Douglass’s speech moves people to this day, and every fact in it holds. Being moved and being right are not enemies. They just have to be checked separately.

39.9

Anticipating the Objection

Main ideaA strong written argument states the best objection to itself fairly and answers it, which is called a counterclaim and rebuttal.

Picture the reader who disagrees with you, sitting across the table, arms folded. Every argument you write is really addressed to that person; the ones who already agree do not need convincing. So the most important paragraph in an argument is the one where you say what that reader is thinking. Writers call it the : the strongest point on the other side, stated in words its holders would accept. Then comes the : your answer to it. An argument without those two is a speech to an empty room.

The counterclaim must be the strong version. The pediatrician’s column did this well. It did not say some people worry about small problems like buses. It said that a later start would push the end of the day later, that buses serve three schools on one schedule, that after-school jobs and practices would be squeezed, and that these were real costs to real families. Only after that did it answer: a one-year trial would show whether the costs were as high as feared, and other districts had solved the bus problem by staggering routes. A reader with folded arms unfolds them a little when she hears her own point stated better than she would have stated it.

There are three honest ways to rebut. You can show the objection rests on a mistake of fact. You can concede that it is true but show that your point outweighs it. Or you can concede it fully and narrow your claim. That last one feels like losing and is often winning: a smaller claim that survives is worth more than a large one that does not. What you cannot do is rebut a straw man, ignore the objection, or bury it in the last sentence with a quick but. Readers notice, and they stop trusting everything above it.

Here is the shape of an argument that survives contact. A claim, stated exactly. Two or three pieces of evidence, each rated strong by the four questions. The reasoning that links them, with the assumption stated when it matters. The counterclaim in its strongest form. The rebuttal. And a conclusion that says what should happen now. Write the counterclaim paragraph first, before the rest of the draft. If you cannot state it fairly, you do not understand the question well enough to argue about it yet.

39.10

Disagreeing Well

Main ideaCivil disagreement is not softness; it is a set of moves that keep the argument on the claim and give the other person a reason to keep listening.

Two students disagree about a book. One says the ending was a cheat. The other says the ending was the only honest one. Ten minutes later they are talking about who always interrupts. The argument has left the book and moved to the people, and from there it can only get worse. is the skill of keeping an argument on the claim. It is not about being polite for its own sake. It is about the fact that ad hominem, once it starts, ends every chance of anyone changing their mind.

The first move is to say back what the other person said before answering it. I think you are saying the ending was honest because the character had been lying to himself the whole book, is that right? This does three things. It proves you listened. It catches misunderstandings before they grow. And it forces you to build the steel version of their point instead of the straw one. Most disagreements that go bad go bad because each side is answering something the other did not say.

The second move is to separate the claim from the person. She is wrong about the ending, not she always thinks she is right. The third move is to name what you agree on. Two people who agree that the character was lying to himself, and disagree only about whether the ending should have punished him, have shrunk the fight to something that can be settled. The fourth move is to change your mind out loud when you should. It is the rarest thing in an argument, and the most persuasive, because it shows the other person that changing their mind would not be a defeat either.

The philosopher John Stuart Mill argued in 1859 that a person who knows only their own side of a case knows little even of that side, because the reasons for a position only become clear when you have heard the strongest case against it. That is the deepest reason for civil disagreement. The person across the table is not only an opponent. They are the only source of the objections your own argument needs. Treat them like that, and the argument becomes something two people do together rather than something one does to the other.

39.11

A Structured Debate

Main ideaA formal debate gives each side equal time, a fixed order and a duty to answer, which forces the moves this chapter has taught.

In 1858 Abraham Lincoln and Stephen Douglas held seven debates across Illinois, in towns from Ottawa to Alton, before crowds of thousands. The format was strict. The first speaker had an hour. The second had an hour and a half. Then the first had thirty minutes to reply. Nobody could dodge, because the other man would be standing there in a few minutes with the crowd listening. The format did not make the two men honest. It made dishonesty expensive, which is the most a format can do.

A classroom borrows that idea at a smaller scale. A is stated, a sentence that can be argued for or against: this school should start at 8:30. One side affirms it, the other opposes. Each side gives an opening with its claim and best evidence. Then comes the part that matters, the round, where each side must answer what the other actually said. Then closings. A judge or the class scores not who was loudest but who answered the other side’s strongest point and who left it standing.

Preparing for a debate is the best practice in this chapter, because you do not know which side you will get. You have to build the strongest case for both. That means finding the best evidence for each and rating it with the four questions. It means locating the assumptions. It means predicting the other side’s counterclaims, so that your rebuttal is ready before they speak. Students who prepare only their own side lose to students who prepared both, every time. The second group has already heard every point the first group will make.

Rules for the room, written for this lesson. Speak to the claim, never to the person. Say back the point before you answer it. Cite evidence by its source, and expect to be asked where it came from. Concede what is true; the judge will notice, and it costs you nothing. Keep time. And when it ends, shake hands, because the person across from you just gave you the objections your argument needed, and next week you may be arguing their side.
