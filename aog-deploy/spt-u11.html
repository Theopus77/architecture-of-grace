Other Institutions, Same State

History

Sport needs more than players. It needs a body that writes rules, a room to play in and someone who holds the key. In this unit you will meet two kinds of institutions. The first is disability sport: deaf games from 1924, wheelchair basketball from 1949 and the Paralympic movement. The second is the public sport of Illinois: school land, park districts and the Illinois High School Association.

Both stories are about who was let in. Deaf and disabled athletes built their own federations when others left them out. Chicago families fought over who could use a beach or a park. Girls in Illinois waited decades for some state titles. Laws, lawsuits and votes opened many of these doors.

You will be able to name the bodies that run these sports and when they began. You will read laws, reports and records. You will explain how a rule, a tax or a building decided who could play. You will also tell legends apart from records.

The big question

How do public bodies, from federations to park districts, decide who gets to play and where?

Disability Sport as Institution

Disability Sport
Big questionHow did disabled athletes build their own games, federations and rule books, and win a place beside the Olympics?
The story

A Card Beside a Racing Chair

Before a wheelchair race, a small card says which class the racer belongs to.

A racing wheelchair rests near the start of a track. It has three wheels, a long low frame and no armrests. Next to it lies a card from a classification panel. The card names the athlete's sport class. In wheelchair track racing, one class is written T54. The letter T stands for track.

The card works a little like a ticket. It lets the athlete enter races against others whose impairments affect the sport in a similar way. A panel of trained classifiers gave the card. The panel may include a doctor or a physical therapist and an expert in the sport. The athlete can ask for a review if the class seems wrong.

That card is the product of more than a century of institution building. Deaf athletes held their first international games in Paris in 1924. Doctors and veterans started wheelchair games after World War II. Coaches in Illinois founded a national wheelchair basketball association in 1949. The International Paralympic Committee was founded in 1989.

Each step made a new office, a new rule book or a new championship. Disabled athletes and their allies ran many of these bodies themselves. The racing chair and the card together show sport, not charity. Records are kept, results are official and medals are won.

Section 1

Deaf Athletes Organize First

21.1

A College Team in Washington

Main ideaGallaudet, founded in 1864, gave deaf students a college with its own teams and a sports life run in sign language.

In 1864 President Abraham Lincoln signed a law that let a school in Washington, D.C., grant college degrees. The school became University, named for Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet. He had helped open the first lasting American school for deaf students, in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817.

By the late 1800s, many state schools for the deaf had teams. So did Gallaudet. Players and coaches talked in sign language. Deaf schools played hearing schools in their towns, so their teams were part of local sport from early on.

A well known story says the football huddle began at Gallaudet in the 1890s. In it, quarterback Paul Hubbard pulled his team into a circle so other deaf teams could not read their signs. The story is told by the university itself, but written records from the time are thin. Historians treat it as a tradition, not a proven first.

Which claim would you call a record and which a tradition? What kind of paper would make Claim B stronger?

21.2

The Silent Games of 1924

Main ideaIn 1924 deaf leaders in Paris held the first international games for deaf athletes and founded a federation to run them.

In August 1924 athletes from several European countries met in Paris for the International Silent Games. The organizer was , a deaf Frenchman who led a French deaf sports federation. The Summer Olympics had been held in Paris a few weeks earlier that same summer.

At the end of the games, leaders founded a governing body. Its French name was the Comité International des Sports des Sourds, or . Today it is called the International Committee of Sports for the Deaf. Deaf people have led it from the start, which makes it one of the oldest sport bodies run by disabled people themselves.

The games have been held about every four years since, apart from the World War II years. Winter games were added in 1949. In 2001 the name was adopted. To enter, an athlete must have a hearing loss of at least 55 decibels in the better ear, and hearing aids are taken off during competition.

21.3

Dummy Hoy and the Umpire Story

Main ideaWilliam Hoy, a deaf outfielder, played major league baseball from 1888 to 1902; the claim that he caused umpire hand signals is a legend.

William lost his hearing as a young child in Ohio. He attended the Ohio School for the Deaf. He played in the major leagues from 1888 to 1902 for several teams. In 1901 he played for the Chicago White Stockings of the new American League, the team that became the White Sox.

In his time, newspapers called him Dummy Hoy. The word was a common, hurtful label for deaf people then. Many writers today use his given name, William, or his nickname Hoy. He was known as a fast runner and a steady outfielder. He communicated with teammates through signs.

A famous story says umpires began using hand signals for balls and strikes because of Hoy. Historians of baseball find no clear record to prove it, and umpire signals may have spread from several sources. The story is best labeled a . The record that is certain is his long career in the majors.

Wheelchairs After the War

21.4

Stoke Mandeville, 1948

Main ideaLudwig Guttmann used sport in a British spinal injury hospital and held the first Stoke Mandeville Games on the day the 1948 London Olympics opened.

was a Jewish neurologist, a doctor of the brain and nerves, in Germany. He fled Nazi Germany in 1939 and settled in Britain. In 1944 he opened a spinal injuries center at Stoke Mandeville Hospital, northwest of London. Many patients were soldiers with injuries to the spinal cord.

At the time, many people with spinal cord injuries died within a few years. Guttmann changed care with turning, skin care and early activity. He used sport as part of rehabilitation, which means rebuilding strength and skills after an injury. Patients played wheelchair polo, archery and basketball.

On July 29, 1948, the London Olympics opened. The same day, Guttmann held the first , an archery contest for 16 wheelchair athletes, 14 men and 2 women. In 1952 a team of Dutch veterans came, and the event became international. Guttmann hoped such games would one day stand beside the Olympics.

21.5

Veterans and a Tournament in Galesburg

Main ideaAmerican veterans played wheelchair basketball after 1945, and in 1949 teams founded the National Wheelchair Basketball Association in Galesburg, Illinois.

After World War II, thousands of American veterans came home with spinal cord injuries. In veterans’ hospitals, some began playing basketball from wheelchairs around 1946. Teams formed in California, New England and the Midwest. One California team, the Flying Wheels, toured the country and drew crowds.

directed a new program for students with disabilities at the University of Illinois. It started in 1948 at a branch campus in Galesburg. In April 1949 Nugent hosted the first National Wheelchair Basketball Tournament there. Teams from several states took part.

At that tournament, the teams founded the , or NWBA. It wrote rules, kept standings and held a national championship every year. It was a governing body like any other sport league. It made wheelchair basketball one of the oldest organized disability team sports in the United States.

21.6

A Campus Built for Access

Main ideaNugent's program at the University of Illinois grew into an early model of an accessible campus with its own wheelchair sports teams.

In 1949 Nugent’s program moved from Galesburg to the main University of Illinois campus in Urbana-Champaign. Students who used wheelchairs lived, studied and played sports there. Nugent pushed the university to add ramps and curb cuts, the sloped cuts in a curb that let a wheelchair roll from sidewalk to street.

The program’s teams became famous. The Gizz Kids played wheelchair basketball and later other wheelchair sports. The university also ran buses that could carry wheelchair users around campus. Students with disabilities formed their own groups and helped run the programs.

The Illinois program showed that is a matter of buildings and rules, not only of individual bodies. A student cannot play in a gym he cannot enter. Later national laws on access, such as the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, required the kinds of changes Illinois had tried decades before.

Paralympic Federations

21.7

Rome, 1960

Main ideaThe first Paralympic Games were held in Rome in 1960, in the same city as the Olympics that year.

In September 1960, the Summer Olympics were held in Rome. Right after them, Guttmann’s international wheelchair games were held in Rome too. About 400 athletes from 23 countries took part. Today the International Paralympic Committee counts Rome 1960 as the first .

All athletes in Rome used wheelchairs. Events included archery, swimming, fencing, table tennis and wheelchair basketball. Athletes with other impairments, such as blind athletes and amputees, were added to the games in 1976. The first Winter Paralympics were held in Örnsköldsvik, Sweden, in 1976.

The word Paralympic was first used for the wheelchair games. Later it was explained as meaning beside, or parallel to, the Olympics. That meaning fits Guttmann’s hope from 1948. Tokyo hosted both the Olympics and the Paralympics in 1964, and many later hosts did the same.

21.8

One Committee, One Host City

Main ideaThe International Paralympic Committee, founded in 1989, now governs the Paralympics, and since the 2000s one city hosts both Games.

By the 1980s, several groups ran disability sport. There were separate bodies for wheelchair athletes, blind athletes, amputees and athletes with cerebral palsy. Each had its own rules. In 1988 Seoul held the Paralympics in the same venues it had used for the Olympics.

On September 22, 1989, the , or IPC, was founded in Düsseldorf, Germany. It became the single governing body for the Paralympic Games. Its headquarters is in Bonn. It works with national Paralympic committees in each country.

In 2001 the IPC and the International Olympic Committee signed an . It said a city chosen for the Olympics would also host the Paralympics. Beijing in 2008 was the first host picked under that rule. In the United States, the Olympic committee added Paralympic to its name in 2019.

21.9

Classes on a Card

Main ideaClassification groups athletes by how their impairment affects a sport, and its rules have changed from medical labels to sport-based tests.

Early wheelchair games grouped athletes by the level of their spinal cord injury. Doctors did the sorting. That worked for one kind of injury. It did not work well once blind athletes, amputees and athletes with cerebral palsy joined.

Over the 1980s and 1990s, many sports moved to . That means grouping athletes by what their bodies can do in that sport, not only by a diagnosis. A swimmer and a sprinter with the same impairment may be placed in different classes in their sports.

In 2007 the IPC adopted an Athlete Classification Code, and it has been revised since. The code says who can be a classifier, how athletes are tested and how they can appeal. Each has a short label, like T54 in wheelchair track racing. Arguments about fairness in classes are still part of the Paralympic movement.

Laws, Records, Not Posters

21.10

Special Olympics at Soldier Field

Main ideaThe first International Special Olympics Summer Games, for athletes with intellectual disabilities, were held at Soldier Field in Chicago in July 1968.

In 1962 Eunice Kennedy Shriver held a summer day camp at her home in Maryland. It was for children and adults with intellectual disabilities, which means differences in learning and thinking skills. It showed that campers could take part in sports and games when given the chance.

In Chicago, a Park District teacher named Anne McGlone Burke planned a citywide track meet for athletes with intellectual disabilities. The Chicago Park District worked with the Kennedy Foundation. On July 20, 1968, the first International Summer Games opened at Soldier Field. About 1,000 athletes from the United States and Canada came.

Special Olympics grew into a worldwide organization. It uses divisioning, which groups athletes by ability, age and sex so that contests are close. It also holds year-round training, not only one event. Anne Burke later became a justice of the Illinois Supreme Court.

21.11

Laws That Opened the Gym

Main ideaFederal laws from 1973 to 1990, and a 2013 letter from the Education Department, required schools to give students with disabilities a fair chance at sports.

In 1973 Congress passed the Rehabilitation Act. Its says programs that receive federal money may not shut people out because of disability. Public schools receive federal money, so the rule covers school sports too. In 1975 a federal law guaranteed a free public education for children with disabilities.

In 1990 the , or ADA, reached further. It covered public places and many private businesses, including stadiums and gyms. New buildings had to be accessible. Arenas had to offer wheelchair seating spread through the building.

In January 2013 the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights sent a letter to schools. It said students with disabilities must get an equal chance to try out for teams. Schools should make reasonable changes when they do not change the nature of the sport. Where needed, schools should consider separate or allied teams.

21.12

Athletes, Not Poster Children

Main ideaDisability rights activists pushed back on charity images and asked that disabled athletes be seen as competitors with records and rights.

For much of the 1900s, many charities raised money with a . The poster showed a disabled child and asked the public for pity and gifts. Television telethons did the same, often for many hours. The money helped research and care, but the pictures showed disabled people as people to feel sorry for.

In the 1970s and 1980s, the disability rights movement pushed back. Activists held protests and sit-ins for laws like Section 504. They used the slogan Nothing About Us Without Us. It means disabled people should help make the decisions that affect them.

Disability sport bodies share that idea. Athletes sit on athlete councils in the IPC and in national committees. Results are kept as official , like any other sport. United States law brought Paralympic athletes into the national Olympic committee’s work in 1998, through the Ted Stevens Olympic and Amateur Sports Act.

Illinois Rooms

Illinois
Big questionWho built the public fields, gyms and school leagues of Illinois, and who decided which neighborhoods and students could use them?
The story

A Key on a School Night

A park-district worker unlocks a gym door, and a neighborhood walks in.

It is seven o'clock on a winter school night. A park-district worker turns a key in a steel door. The lights hum on over a wooden floor. Soon teenagers arrive for open gym, and a group of adults sets up for a league game.

The key belongs to a public body. In Illinois, a park district is its own unit of local government. It can collect property taxes, own land and hire staff. In Chicago, one park district has run the city's parks since 1934. In hundreds of other Illinois towns, voters elect park board members.

The same building may be shared with a school. On weekday mornings the gym holds physical education classes. On Friday nights it may hold a high school game played under the rules of the Illinois High School Association, founded in 1900. Two public institutions use one room.

Every such room has a history. Someone set aside the land. Someone voted for the tax. Someone decided which neighborhoods got a field house first. Sometimes people were kept out of a beach or a park by threats and custom. The history of Illinois sport is partly a history of keys and who held them.

Land Set Aside

22.1

Section Sixteen

Main ideaThe Land Ordinance of 1785 set aside one section of every township for public schools, tying land and schooling together in Illinois.

In 1785 Congress passed the . It set a plan to survey land west of the original states into square , each six miles on a side. Each township was cut into 36 sections of one square mile. Illinois land was later surveyed on this grid.

The ordinance reserved section 16 of every township for public schools. Towns could sell or rent that land and use the money for schools. Illinois became a state in 1818. Its early school funds came in part from these school lands.

This choice linked public land to public schooling from the start. Many later school buildings, playgrounds and athletic fields stand on land a public body owns for the public. When a school district builds a field, it follows that old idea that land can serve every child.

22.2

A Playground at Hull House

Main ideaIn the 1890s Hull House, a settlement house on Chicago's West Side, opened a playground and gym for crowded immigrant neighborhoods.

In 1889 Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr opened on Halsted Street in Chicago. It was a , a center where reformers lived among working families and offered classes and services. The neighborhood was crowded with immigrants from Italy, Greece, Eastern Europe and other places.

Children there had few safe places to play. Streets were busy with wagons, and yards were small. In the 1890s Hull House opened a playground and a gymnasium. Its leaders argued that play space was a public need, like clean water or schools.

Reformers in Chicago and other cities formed a . They asked city governments, not only charities, to pay for play spaces. In 1906 the Playground Association of America was founded, with Jane Addams among its leaders. Their argument helped move play from private gifts to public budgets.

22.3

Field Houses of 1905

Main ideaIn 1905 Chicago's South Park Commission opened neighborhood parks with field houses, giving working families public gyms, pools and meeting rooms.

Chicago once had many separate park boards. One was the South Park Commission. In the early 1900s, voters approved money for small neighborhood parks in crowded areas. The commission hired the Olmsted Brothers to design the parks. D. H. Burnham and Company designed the buildings.

In 1905 the first ten of these parks opened. Sherman Park, Davis Square and Armour Square were among them. Each had a . A field house held a gym, locker rooms and showers, a library branch and meeting rooms. Outdoors there were ball fields, a swimming pool and playgrounds.

The field house was a new kind of public room. It mixed sport, bathing, reading and clubs in one building. City leaders and visitors from other cities came to study the plan. President Theodore Roosevelt praised the small parks as a notable civic achievement.

Section 2

Who Got a Field

22.4

Twenty-Two Boards Become One

Main ideaIn 1934 Chicago's separate park boards were combined into the Chicago Park District, whose board the mayor appoints.

By the early 1930s, Chicago had 22 separate park districts. Each had its own board, staff and taxes. The Great Depression left many of them short of money. Some could not pay their debts or keep parks open.

In 1934 voters approved combining them into one body, the . The mayor appoints its board, with the approval of the City Council. The new district took over parks, beaches, field houses and pools across the city. It also received federal help through New Deal work programs, which built and repaired many park buildings.

The Park District also owns Soldier Field, the lakefront stadium that opened in 1924. It has hosted college football, the first Special Olympics games in 1968 and, since 1971, the Chicago Bears. A stadium owned by a park district is a public room, even when a private team uses it most.

Park Boards Across Illinois

Main ideaOutside Chicago, Illinois towns can form park districts with elected boards that tax, own land and run gyms and pools.

Illinois law lets communities form . A park district is a separate unit of local government, apart from the city and the school district. Voters in the area approve it. They then elect its board of .

A park district can levy a property tax, borrow money for buildings and buy land. It runs parks, pools, ice rinks, golf courses and recreation centers. Illinois has hundreds of park districts. Many suburbs and small towns have one.

Park districts and school districts often sign . The school may use a district pool for swim meets. The park district may use a school gym for evening leagues. The agreement says who pays for staff, cleaning and repairs. Keys, schedules and insurance are all written down.

22.6

The 1919 Beach

Main ideaIn July 1919 a Black teenager drowned after being stoned at an informally segregated Chicago beach, setting off a week of deadly violence.

During and after World War I, many Black families moved from the South to Chicago. This was part of the . They found jobs, but housing and public spaces were often split by race. There was no written law for Chicago beaches, but custom and threats drew lines.

On July 27, 1919, Eugene Williams, a Black teenager, was swimming off the beach near 29th Street. His raft drifted across the unmarked line. A white man threw rocks at him, and he drowned. A police officer refused to arrest the man. Fights broke out on the beach.

The violence spread across the city for about a week. Thirty-eight people died, 23 Black and 15 white, and hundreds were hurt. A state commission later studied the causes. Its 1922 report, The Negro in Chicago, pointed to housing, jobs and the unequal use of parks and beaches.

The commission found that fights over the use of parks, playgrounds and beaches had been a steady source of racial conflict in Chicago before the 1919 riot.

22.7

Wade-Ins and a Court Decree

Main ideaProtests at Rainbow Beach in 1960 and 1961 and a 1983 federal consent decree challenged unequal park use and spending in Chicago.

Decades after 1919, some Chicago beaches still felt closed to Black residents. In 1960 and 1961, groups of Black and white protesters held at Rainbow Beach on the South Side. They walked into the water together to claim the beach for everyone. They met angry crowds, and police were called to protect them.

Other gaps were about money. Parks in some neighborhoods got newer buildings, more programs and more staff than others. In 1983 the U.S. Department of Justice sued the Chicago Park District, saying Black and Latino neighborhoods received fewer services.

The case ended in a , a court-approved agreement. The Park District agreed to spread programs, staff and repairs more fairly across the city. A federal court watched over the agreement for years. Fairness now had to be measured and reported, not only promised.

Section 3

The IHSA as a School Body

22.8

Principals Meet in 1900

Main ideaThe Illinois High School Association, founded in 1900, is an association of member schools that sets eligibility rules and runs state tournaments.

In the late 1800s, high school teams often used players who were not really students. Some were older men or paid ringers. Schools argued over rules from game to game. Principals wanted schools, not outside clubs, to control school sport.

In 1900 Illinois principals founded the , or IHSA. Member schools agreed to follow shared rules on who could play. A player had to be an enrolled student making progress in classes. The IHSA’s office is in Bloomington.

The IHSA is not a state agency. It is a private, not-for-profit association of member schools, both public and private. Today more than 800 high schools belong. It runs state series and championships in many boys and girls sports and in activities such as speech and music.

Think it through
Why Principals, Not Coaches?

Before 1900 a team captain or coach might recruit any strong player in town. After 1900 the IHSA required that players be enrolled students passing their classes, and member principals enforced the rule.

22.9

How a Bylaw Changes

Main ideaIHSA member schools write and change their own bylaws through a yearly process that ends in a vote of member principals.

The IHSA’s rules are written in its and . The constitution sets up the association and its board. Bylaws are the detailed rules, such as on eligibility, transfers and the number of contests allowed in a season.

Anyone at a member school can propose a change through the principal. Proposals go to town hall meetings held around the state each fall. A Legislative Commission of school representatives reviews them. Proposals it advances go on a ballot, and member school principals vote.

A separate , elected from school leaders, runs the association between votes. It hears appeals and hires the executive director. This design makes the IHSA a kind of small government. Its members are schools, and each school has a vote.

Think it through
A Proposal's Path

Fall: a principal submits a proposal. Town hall meetings: schools discuss it in regions around the state. Winter: the Legislative Commission reviews it and sends some proposals to a ballot. Late winter: member principals vote. A rule that passes goes into the handbook.

22.10

Championships Added Over Time

Main ideaIHSA state championships were added one sport at a time, and the dates show when each group of students got a state title to play for.

The IHSA did not start with every sport. It added state one by one. Boys tennis crowned its first state champion in 1912. Boys wrestling held its first state finals in 1937. Each new tournament meant new rules, officials and a place in the handbook.

Girls had to wait much longer. Most IHSA girls championships began in the 1970s, after Title IX became federal law in 1972. Some came much later. Girls wrestling held its first IHSA state finals in 2022, at the end of the 2021–22 season.

A championship is an , not just a game. It needs a bracket, seeding rules, host sites and paid officials. When the IHSA adds a sport, schools start teams because a state title now exists. The dates of first championships trace who was invited into school sport, and when.

22.11

Required Gym Class

Main ideaIllinois law has long required physical education in public schools, which is why nearly every school has a gym.

Illinois law requires public schools to offer . For many years the School Code called for daily physical education for students in kindergarten through grade 12. Illinois was known as one of the few states with such a broad daily rule. Later laws let some students be excused, such as students in certain sports or classes.

A rule for daily classes needs a room. That helps explain why Illinois school buildings from the 1900s onward nearly always include a gymnasium. Districts paid for gyms with local taxes and bonds approved by voters.

The gym built for classes then became the home of school teams and community events. Graduations, elections and assemblies were often held there. So one law about classes shaped the buildings in almost every Illinois town. It also gave park districts rooms to share after school.
