Religion in Our Community

Religion in Society

Look around your city. You will see churches, synagogues, mosques, temples and gurdwaras. You will meet classmates who pray in many ways, and some who do not follow a religion at all. Chicago is a place where many faiths live side by side.

In this unit you will visit houses of worship, learn about the 1893 Parliament of Religions, and see what the First Amendment says about freedom of religion. You will also hear how some faiths are kept by telling stories, and how faiths traveled with people to Chicago.

By the end, you will be able to name houses of worship, explain freedom of religion in plain words, ask kind questions, and tell how faiths change and grow when people move.

The big question

How do people of many faiths share one community, and how do their faiths travel and grow?

Neighbors of Many Faiths

Community
Big questionHow can people of many faiths, and people of no faith, live and work together as good neighbors?
The story

Three Doors, One Food Drive

A church, a mosque and a synagogue on one block fill the same truck with food.

It is a cold Saturday morning in Chicago. On one block stand three buildings. One is a church with a tall steeple. One is a mosque with a green dome. One is a synagogue with a Star of David over the door.

Today the three groups are running a food drive together. Volunteers carry boxes of rice, beans, soup and pasta out to a big white truck. A sign on the truck says: Neighbors Feeding Neighbors.

Amira, age nine, tapes labels on boxes. Her family prays at the mosque. Next to her, Eli stacks cans. He goes to the synagogue. Grace, who goes to the church, counts the boxes and writes the total on a clipboard.

Eli picks up a can of soup and frowns. "Does this one have pork in it?" he asks. "My family keeps kosher, and so does the pantry at our synagogue." Amira nods. "Muslims who eat halal skip pork too." Grace reads the label. "Chicken noodle," she says. "No pork."

By noon the truck is full. The three leaders shake hands. Each group believes different things about God and prayer. Today they found one thing they could do together: make sure their neighbors have enough to eat.

13.1

Many Buildings, Many Faiths

Main ideaChicago has churches, synagogues, mosques, temples and gurdwaras, where people of many faiths gather.

Take a bus ride across Chicago and look out the window. You will pass churches with steeples and crosses. You may see a synagogue, a mosque with a dome, a Hindu temple, a Buddhist temple and a Sikh gurdwara.

Each building is a . That means a place where people gather to pray, sing, read holy writings and learn. Christians often meet in churches. Jews often meet in synagogues. Muslims often pray in mosques.

Many Hindus and Buddhists visit temples. Sikhs gather in a gurdwara, which means "door of the Guru." Chicago has all of these because people from all over the world have made their homes here.

Inside a Church, Synagogue and Mosque

Main ideaEach house of worship has special things inside that show what matters to the people who gather there.

Walk into many churches and you may see a cross, rows of seats called pews, and an altar at the front. Christians believe Jesus is the Son of God. The cross reminds them of his death and, they believe, his rising again.

In a synagogue, the most special place is the , a cabinet on the front wall. Inside are Torah scrolls. The Torah holds the first five books of the Hebrew Bible. Jews read from it aloud in services.

A mosque has an open prayer hall, often with rugs. A small curved space in the wall, the mihrab, shows the direction of Mecca. Muslims face that way when they pray. Many mosques have no chairs, so people can bow and kneel.

13.3

A Temple Open to All

Main ideaThe Bahá'í House of Worship in Wilmette has nine sides and nine doors and welcomes people of every faith.

North of Chicago, in the town of Wilmette, stands a white building covered in lace-like patterns. It is the Bahá&#x27;í House of Worship. It was finished and opened for worship in 1953, after many years of building.

The building has nine sides and nine doors. Bahá’ís believe that God has sent many teachers, and that people of all religions are one human family. The nine doors are often said to welcome people from every direction.

Inside there are no sermons. People read or sing passages from Bahá’í writings and from the holy books of other faiths. Visitors of any religion, or none, are welcome to sit quietly and listen.

Main ideaIn 1893 Chicago hosted the World's Parliament of Religions, where leaders of many faiths spoke to one another.

In 1893 Chicago held a giant world’s fair. People came to see new inventions, like the first Ferris wheel. At the same time, the city held a meeting called the .

A parliament is a meeting where people speak and listen. Leaders came from many faiths: Christians, Jews, Hindus, Buddhists, Muslims and others. It was one of the first times so many faiths met in one room.

13.5

The Parliament Returns

Main ideaStarting in 1993, the Parliament of the World's Religions began meeting again, including more times in Chicago.

One hundred years later, in 1993, people held a new Parliament of the World’s Religions in Chicago. This time thousands of people came from many more faiths than in 1893.

Since then, the Parliament has met in cities around the world. It came back to Chicago in 2023. People share music, prayers, food and ideas. They talk about problems all faiths care about, like hunger and caring for the earth.

The Parliament does not try to make everyone believe the same thing. Its goal is understanding. That means people of different faiths learning about each other and working together on shared goals.

Freedom and Kindness

13.7

The First Amendment

Main ideaThe First Amendment says the government may not pick a religion, and people may follow any faith or none.

The United States has a set of rules called the Constitution. In 1791, ten changes were added, called the Bill of Rights. The very first one is the . It protects freedom of religion.

In plain words, it says two things. First, the government may not set up an official religion or pick one faith over others. Second, the government may not stop people from following their faith.

This means a person may be Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh, Bahá’í, or of any faith. A person may also follow no religion at all. Everyone has the same rights either way.

13.8

Religion at a Public School

Main ideaPublic schools teach about religions without telling students what to believe.

A public school is paid for by the government. Because of the First Amendment, a public school may not lead students in prayer or tell them which faith is true.

But public schools may teach about religions. That is what this course does. You learn what each faith’s followers believe and do, the way you might learn about any big part of history and culture.

Students keep their own freedom too. A student may pray quietly on their own, wear a headscarf or turban, or skip foods their faith does not allow. Teachers try to make room for these needs.

Big questionHow do people keep a faith alive by telling it, and how do faiths travel with people to new places?
The story

Remember It, Don't Write It

A visiting elder shares a story and asks the class to hold it in their memory.

Snow covers the playground. Inside Room 12, the class sits in a circle on the rug. A guest has come to visit. She is an Anishinaabe elder, which means a respected older person in her Native nation.

Before she starts, she smiles. "Please put your pencils down," she says. "Today I want you to remember with your ears and your heart, not your paper. That is how my grandmother taught me."

She tells a story of a time long ago when water covered the world. The animals tried to dive down and bring up a little mud. Many could not reach the bottom. At last the small muskrat came up with mud in its paw.

The mud was placed on a turtle's back, and it grew into land. "That is why many of our people call this land Turtle Island," she says. "Our stories also tell how the lakes and the land are gifts to care for."

When she finishes, the room is quiet. "Now you carry a piece of this story," she says. "Tell it gently. And remember that some of our stories are only for our own people. I shared this one because it is okay to share."

Faiths Kept by Telling

14.1

What Is an Oral Tradition?

Main ideaAn oral tradition is knowledge passed down by speaking, singing and remembering, not only by writing.

Some faiths have one main holy book. Christians read the Bible. Muslims read the Qur’an. But many faiths around the world have been kept mainly by telling, from older people to younger people.

This is called an . Oral means spoken. Stories, songs, prayers and teachings are learned by listening and remembering. They are passed on at family gatherings, ceremonies and quiet evenings.

Even faiths with holy books use telling. The Torah tells parents to teach its words to their children at home and on the road. Many Jewish families still say prayers from memory each day.

14.2

Memory and Respect

Main ideaIn oral traditions, people remember stories carefully and tell them with respect.

How can a story last hundreds of years with no book? People listen many times. They hear it at the same season each year. They learn the words, the pauses and even the way to sit while listening.

Tellers often use , saying key parts again and again. Songs, drumming and rhythm help too. It is much easier to remember words that you sing than words you only read once.

In many Native communities, a story is more than fun. Adherents see it as a teaching about how to live. Listening quietly and remembering well are ways of showing respect to the teller and the story.

14.3

Stories With Permission

Main ideaSome stories are shared with everyone, and some are only for members of a community or for certain times.

Not every story is meant for everyone. In many Native nations, some stories are sacred and kept within the community. Outsiders are asked not to retell them or write them down.

Some stories also have a right time. In many Anishinaabe families, certain sacred stories are told only in winter, when snow is on the ground. Other stories may be shared at any time.

So a respectful learner asks . You might ask, "Is this a story I may share?" If the answer is no, you honor it. Learning about a faith includes respecting what its people choose to keep.

Across the Ocean

Main ideaWhen people move, they carry their faith with them, and it can grow in new places.

Faiths travel with people. When Yoruba people were taken to the Americas as enslaved people, many kept their beliefs alive in secret. Their faith helped form new traditions in Cuba and Brazil.

In Cuba, one tradition is called Santería or Lucumí. In Brazil, one is called Candomblé. Followers honor the orishas with drumming, songs and dance. Today these traditions have followers in Chicago too.

This shows how faiths . They keep old teachings and take on new forms in new places. Moving can bring hard times, and faith often helps people stay connected to home.

The Great Migration

Main ideaIn the Great Migration, millions of Black Americans moved north, and their churches helped build Chicago.

From about 1916 to 1970, around six million Black Americans left the South. They moved to northern and western cities to find jobs and escape unfair laws. This was the .

Hundreds of thousands came to Chicago. They brought their faith, mostly Baptist and Methodist Christianity. They filled old churches and started many new ones on the South Side.

Chicago churches became famous for . Thomas A. Dorsey, often called the father of gospel music, led a choir at Pilgrim Baptist Church. Singer Mahalia Jackson made Chicago gospel known around the world.

New Neighbors, New Temples

Main ideaImmigrants from around the world brought churches, synagogues, temples, mosques and gurdwaras to the Chicago area.

Chicago has always been a city of . In the 1800s and early 1900s, people came from Ireland, Germany, Poland, Italy and Eastern Europe. They built Catholic and Protestant churches and Jewish synagogues.

Later, many families came from Mexico and Latin America, bringing Catholic parishes full of Spanish-language masses. After 1965, new U.S. laws let more people come from Asia and Africa.

Those newcomers built Hindu and Buddhist temples, mosques and Sikh gurdwaras across the Chicago area. Walk down Devon Avenue on the North Side and you can see many cultures and faiths on one street.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Where do Muslims often gather to pray?

Why: Muslims often pray together in a mosque.

2. What does the First Amendment protect?

Why: The First Amendment keeps the government from picking a religion and lets people follow any faith or none.

3. What was special about 1893 in Chicago?

Why: The World's Parliament of Religions met in Chicago in 1893.

4. Which building in Wilmette has nine doors?

Why: The Bahá'í House of Worship has nine sides and nine doors and welcomes all.

5. What is an oral tradition?

Why: An oral tradition is passed down by telling, singing and remembering.

6. Why might an elder ask you not to retell a story?

Why: Some stories are sacred and meant only for the community, so learners ask permission.

7. In many Anishinaabe stories, what is Turtle Island?

Why: Many Native people call North America Turtle Island, from a creation story.

8. Which faith honors the orishas?

Why: Many followers of Yoruba religion honor the orishas as powerful spirits.

9. Which word is Jewish giving to people in need?

Why: Tzedakah is the Jewish word for giving as a matter of justice. Zakat is the Muslim word.

10. What did the Great Migration bring to Chicago?

Why: Black families from the South built churches, and gospel music grew there.

11. Which is a respectful question about a classmate's holiday?

Why: Asking how a family celebrates is kind and open. The others tease.

12. Which tradition grew in Brazil from Yoruba roots?

Why: Candomblé grew in Brazil from Yoruba roots. Santería grew in Cuba.

13. What may a public school do about religion?

Why: Public schools may teach about religions but may not tell students what to believe.

14. What change in 1965 helped bring more temples and mosques to Chicago?

Why: A 1965 law let more people come from Asia and Africa, who built new houses of worship.

15. Which three nations form the Council of Three Fires?

Why: The Ojibwe, Odawa and Potawatomi call themselves Anishinaabe and form the Council of Three Fires.
