The Subjunctive

Mood

Listen to a Spanish-speaking parent for one evening and count the sentences that are not statements of fact. "Quiero que comas." (I want you to eat.) "Espero que te vaya bien." (I hope it goes well for you.) "No creo que llueva." (I do not think it will rain.) "Si tuviera tiempo, te llevaría." (If I had time, I would take you.) Wishes, hopes, doubts and imagined worlds fill ordinary talk, and Spanish marks every one of them with a change in the verb. That change is the subjunctive mood, and this unit is about learning to hear it, form it, and use it on purpose.

The first chapter starts with a letter of advice from an older sister at college and uses it to answer three questions: what a mood is, how to build the present subjunctive from the yo form, and which words trigger it. You will learn the six irregulars (sea, esté, vaya, haya, sepa, dé), the two-clause pattern with que and a change of subject, and the WEIRDO triggers: wishes, emotions, impersonal expressions, recommendations, doubt and ojalá. The second chapter follows a class writing about one thing they would change in their town. It adds doubt versus certainty, conjunctions like para que and cuando, the imperfect subjunctive built from the preterite, the conditional, and the if-sentence that puts them together: Si tuviera dinero, viajaría.

Many heritage speakers already say these forms without thinking. This unit shows why they work, so you can check your own writing, fix a sentence that sounds off, and explain the rule to someone else. By the end you will write a letter of advice and a paragraph about a change you would make, with every wish, doubt and imagined condition in the right mood.

The big question

How does Spanish use the shape of a verb to separate what is from what someone wants, doubts, feels or imagines?

The story

A Letter from Lucía

An older sister writes home from her first month of college, and every piece of advice she gives uses a verb form her brother has never studied.

Mateo is a junior at a high school on the Southwest Side of Chicago. His sister Lucía left in August for her first year at a university downstate. She promised to write a real letter, on paper, once she had settled in. In October it finally arrives. Mateo reads it at the kitchen table while their mother makes coffee, and he notices something strange about the verbs.

"Quiero que estudies un poco cada día, no todo la noche antes del examen," she writes. (I want you to study a little every day, not all of it the night before the exam.) "Es importante que duermas. Te recomiendo que hables con tus maestros cuando no entiendas algo." (It is important that you sleep. I recommend that you talk with your teachers when you do not understand something.) Mateo knows the verb *estudiar*. He knows *tú estudias*. So why does Lucía write *estudies*?

He keeps reading. "Ojalá que vengas a visitarme en noviembre. Espero que mamá esté bien y que no trabaje tanto." (I hope you come visit me in November. I hope Mom is well and does not work so much.) Every hope, every wish, every piece of advice has the same twist: an -ar verb ends in -e, and an -er or -ir verb ends in -a. Lucía is not making mistakes. She is using a form that Spanish saves for exactly this kind of sentence.

That evening Mateo shows the letter to his mother. She reads it twice and smiles. "Así hablo yo contigo," she says. (That is how I talk to you.) "Quiero que comas. Necesito que me ayudes." (I want you to eat. I need you to help me.) He has heard those sentences his whole life. He just never noticed that the verb was doing something different. This chapter is about that difference: the subjunctive mood.

By the end of the chapter, Mateo will be able to write back. He will tell Lucía what he hopes for her, what worries him, and what he recommends, and every verb will land in the right form. So will yours.

Section 1

Mood, Not Tense

35.1

Two Moods for One Verb

Main ideaTense tells when something happens; mood tells how the speaker sees it. The indicative reports, and the subjunctive wishes, doubts or reacts.

You already know several tenses. Hablo (I speak) is present. Hablé (I spoke) is preterite. Hablaba (I used to speak) is imperfect. A tense answers the question when. Spanish verbs carry a second kind of information that English mostly hides: mood. Mood answers a different question: is the speaker reporting a fact, or wishing, doubting, or reacting to something? That second job belongs to the (subjunctive).

Compare two sentences from Lucía’s letter. "Mateo estudia cada día" (Mateo studies every day) is a report. The speaker is stating what happens. That is the (indicative), the mood you have used in every chapter so far. "Quiero que Mateo estudie cada día" (I want Mateo to study every day) is not a report. Nobody is saying that Mateo studies. Lucía is saying what she wants. The verb changes from estudia to estudie to mark that shift.

English used to do this too, and a few traces remain. "I suggest that he be on time" uses be instead of is. "If I were you" uses were instead of was. Most English speakers never think about those forms, which is why the Spanish subjunctive feels new. It is not new. It is a whole set of endings that Spanish uses every day, in every conversation where someone hopes, asks, doubts or feels something about what another person does.

Here is the rule to carry through the chapter. The indicative states what is. The subjunctive frames what someone wants, doubts, or feels about what might be. The same verb can appear in either mood, and the ending tells you which one you are hearing.

2. What does mood tell you about a verb?

35.2

The Two-Clause Pattern

Main ideaThe subjunctive almost always appears in a second clause after que, when the subject of that clause is different from the subject of the first.

Look at the shape of a subjunctive sentence. "Quiero que estudies." It has two parts. The first, quiero (I want), is a main clause in the indicative. Then comes (that). Then a second clause with a different subject, tú, and the verb in the subjunctive: estudies. A (clause) is a group of words with its own subject and verb. Most subjunctive sentences are built from exactly two of them, joined by que.

The change of subject is the key. In "Quiero estudiar" (I want to study), I am the one wanting and I am the one studying. There is one subject, so Spanish uses the (infinitive), the plain -ar, -er or -ir form. In "Quiero que tú estudies" (I want you to study), I want but you study. Two subjects, two clauses, and the second verb goes into the subjunctive. English hides this with "I want you to study," which is why English speakers often say the wrong thing: "Quiero tú estudiar" is not Spanish.

Try it with Lucía’s other sentences. "Espero visitar a Lucía" (I hope to visit Lucía): one subject, infinitive. "Espero que Lucía me visite" (I hope Lucía visits me): two subjects, que, subjunctive. "Necesito dormir" (I need to sleep): one subject. "Necesito que duermas" (I need you to sleep): two subjects. The pattern is the same every time. Main clause, que, new subject, subjunctive verb.

One more thing to notice. The que is not optional in Spanish. English can say "I hope she comes" with no that. Spanish must say "Espero que venga." If you see a verb of wanting or hoping followed by que and a new subject, expect the subjunctive to follow.

Section 2

Forming the Present Subjunctive

35.3

Start from the Yo Form

Main ideaTo form the present subjunctive, take the present-tense yo form, drop the -o, and add the opposite vowel endings: -e for -ar verbs, -a for -er and -ir verbs.

The present subjunctive is built in three steps. Step one: take the present indicative yo form. For hablar (to speak) that is hablo. Step two: drop the -o. That leaves habl-. Step three: add the subjunctive endings. For -ar verbs they are -e, -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en. So hablar gives hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen. Notice the trade: -ar verbs, whose indicative endings use the vowel a, switch to e in the subjunctive.

For -er and -ir verbs the trade runs the other way. Comer (to eat): yo como, drop the -o, com-, add -a, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an. That gives coma, comas, coma, comamos, comáis, coman. Vivir (to live): vivo, viv-, viva, vivas, viva, vivamos, viváis, vivan. Learners sometimes call this the opposite vowel rule. An -ar verb takes e endings; an -er or -ir verb takes a endings. If you can conjugate the present indicative, you already have every piece you need.

Why start from the yo form and not the infinitive? Because many verbs change in the yo form, and the subjunctive keeps that change in all six persons. Tener (to have) has the yo form tengo, so its subjunctive is tenga, tengas, tenga, tengamos, tengáis, tengan. Hacer (to do, make) has hago, so haga, hagas, haga. Decir (to say) has digo, so diga. Conocer (to know a person) has conozco, so conozca. Salir (to leave) has salgo, so salga. Start from the infinitive and you would get the wrong stem every time.

Two common mistakes. First, learners forget that the yo and él/ella/usted forms are identical in the subjunctive: hable can mean I speak or he speaks, so context or a subject pronoun tells you which. Second, learners add -o to the subjunctive by habit and write hablo where they mean hable. Say the sentence with the trigger in front of it, Quiero que hable, and the o will sound wrong.

1. What is the tú subjunctive form of hablar?

Why: Drop the -o from hablo and add the -ar subjunctive ending -es: hables. Hablas is indicative, hablarás is future, hablaste is preterite.

2. Why is the subjunctive of tener "tenga" and not "tena"?

35.4

Stem Changes and Spelling Changes

Main ideaStem-changing verbs keep their change in the subjunctive wherever the indicative has it, -ir verbs add a second change in nosotros and vosotros, and -car, -gar and -zar verbs adjust their spelling to keep the sound.

Stem-changing verbs follow the yo-form rule with one extra wrinkle. Pensar (to think, e→ie) has the yo form pienso, so the subjunctive is piense, pienses, piense, pensemos, penséis, piensen. The nosotros and vosotros forms drop the change, just as they do in the indicative (pensamos, not piensamos). Volver (to return, o→ue) works the same way: vuelva, vuelvas, vuelva, volvamos, volváis, vuelvan. Picture the boot shape you learned for the present tense. It still holds.

Verbs ending in -ir do one more thing. In the nosotros and vosotros forms, where the main change disappears, a smaller change appears: e becomes i, and o becomes u. (to sleep, o→ue) gives duerma, duermas, duerma, durmamos, durmáis, duerman. Sentir (to feel, e→ie) gives sienta, sientas, sienta, sintamos, sintáis, sientan. Pedir (to ask for, e→i) has the change in every form: pida, pidas, pida, pidamos, pidáis, pidan. Lucía’s advice "Es importante que duermas" uses the first one.

The last group is about spelling, not sound. In Spanish, c before e sounds soft, like s, and g before e sounds like the j in jalapeño. When an -ar verb whose stem ends in c, g or z takes the e endings, the spelling has to change to keep the hard sound you hear in the infinitive. (to look for) becomes busque, not busce. (to arrive) becomes llegue, with a silent u to keep the g hard. (to begin, e→ie) becomes empiece, because Spanish does not write z before e.

These are the same three spelling changes you met in the yo form of the preterite: busqué, llegué, empecé. If you remember those, you already know which verbs to watch. A quick test: say the subjunctive form out loud. If the c or g sound has changed from the infinitive, the spelling needs fixing.

35.5

The Six Irregulars

Main ideaSix common verbs do not follow the yo-form rule in the present subjunctive: sea, esté, vaya, haya, sepa and dé.

The yo-form rule covers almost every verb in Spanish, because it is really a rule about the stem. But six verbs have yo forms that give no usable stem. (to be) has soy. (to be) has estoy. (to go) has voy. (there is, there are) has hay, and its true yo form is he. (to know a fact) has sé. (to give) has doy. Drop the -o from soy or doy and nothing helpful is left. So these six use special stems.

Here they are. Ser: sea, seas, sea, seamos, seáis, sean. Estar: esté, estés, esté, estemos, estéis, estén. Ir: vaya, vayas, vaya, vayamos, vayáis, vayan. Haber: haya (used mostly as the there-is form, "Espero que haya tiempo," I hope there is time). Saber: sepa, sepas, sepa, sepamos, sepáis, sepan. Dar: dé, des, dé, demos, deis, den. Two of these carry accents for a reason. Esté is stressed on the last syllable, so it needs the mark. Dé takes an accent to look different from de (of), even though both are one syllable.

You have already met these six as the stems of the usted commands: sea, esté, vaya, sepa, dé, plus haya. The command and the subjunctive are the same form, which is one reason the subjunctive feels familiar to heritage speakers. Lucía’s letter uses two of them: "Espero que mamá esté bien" (I hope Mom is well) and "Ojalá que vayas a la biblioteca" (I hope you go to the library). A grandparent’s "Que Dios te dé salud" (May God give you health) uses a third.

A memory trick many students use is the first letters: sea, esté, vaya, haya, sepa, dé. Learn them as a set, say them aloud until they come without effort, and watch for the two accents. The most common mistake is writing este for esté, which turns I hope she is into this.

Section 3

What Triggers It

35.6

Wishes and Wants

Main ideaVerbs of wanting, hoping and needing, such as querer, esperar, desear and necesitar, trigger the subjunctive when a que clause has a new subject.

Teachers often use the letters WEIRDO to remember what triggers the subjunctive: Wishes, Emotions, Impersonal expressions, Recommendations, Doubt, and Ojalá. This lesson takes the W. A wish is any verb that expresses what the speaker wants to happen, especially when another person has to do it. (to want that), (to hope that), desear que (to wish that), (to need that), and (to prefer that) all belong here.

Watch the pattern in Lucía’s own words. "Quiero que estudies un poco cada día." Querer, que, new subject, subjunctive. "Espero que mamá esté bien." Esperar, que, new subject, subjunctive. "Necesito que me llames el domingo" (I need you to call me on Sunday). Necesitar, que, new subject, subjunctive. Every one of these sentences describes something that has not happened yet and may not happen. Lucía cannot make Mateo study. She can only want it. The subjunctive marks that gap between wanting and being.

Now the contrast that catches English speakers. "I want to study" and "I want you to study" look almost the same in English. In Spanish they are built differently. "Quiero estudiar" keeps one subject and uses the infinitive. "Quiero que estudies" has two subjects and needs que plus the subjunctive. The mistake to avoid is dragging the English pattern across: "Quiero tú estudiar" and "Quiero que estudias" are both wrong, one for word order and one for mood.

A short set of examples you can copy. Mis padres quieren que yo vaya a la universidad (My parents want me to go to college). Prefiero que tú manejes (I prefer that you drive). Esperamos que ustedes vengan a la fiesta (We hope you come to the party). Deseo que tengas un buen día (I wish you a good day). Notice that the subjunctive verb is always the second one, never the verb of wanting itself.

35.7

Emotions and Impersonal Expressions

Main ideaReactions such as me alegra que and me molesta que, and judgments such as es importante que and es necesario que, trigger the subjunctive in the que clause.

The E in WEIRDO stands for emotions. When the main clause reports a feeling about something someone else does, the que clause takes the subjunctive. (I am glad that), (it bothers me that), me sorprende que (it surprises me that), siento que (I am sorry that), temo que (I fear that). "Me alegra que Lucía esté contenta" (I am glad Lucía is happy). Lucía’s happiness may be a fact, but the sentence is about my reaction to it, and Spanish marks that with the mood.

The I stands for impersonal expressions. These begin with es (it is) plus an adjective and are called impersonal because no person is the subject. (it is important that), es necesario que (it is necessary that), es bueno que (it is good that), es mejor que (it is better that), es posible que (it is possible that), (it is a shame that). Lucía wrote "Es importante que duermas." She is not reporting that Mateo sleeps; she is judging that sleeping matters.

A useful test: does the main clause tell you what is true, or how someone feels or judges? "Es verdad que Lucía estudia mucho" (It is true that Lucía studies a lot) states a fact, so the verb stays indicative. "Es bueno que Lucía estudie mucho" (It is good that Lucía studies a lot) judges, so the verb turns subjunctive. Es verdad que, es cierto que, es obvio que and es evidente que all report certainty and keep the indicative. Almost every other es plus adjective phrase triggers the subjunctive.

Main ideaExpressions of doubt or denial, and the word ojalá, trigger the subjunctive; expressions of belief and certainty keep the indicative.

The speaker is holding the idea at a distance, and the subjunctive marks that distance.

The flip side matters just as much. (to believe that), pensar que (to think that), estar seguro de que (to be sure that), es cierto que (it is true that) express confidence, so their que clause stays indicative. The O in WEIRDO is , a word Spanish took from Arabic during the centuries when Arabic was spoken across much of the Iberian Peninsula. 35.10

When Not to Use It

Main ideaNo change of subject means an infinitive, and a main clause that reports or believes means the indicative; the subjunctive is only for a que clause that wishes, doubts or reacts about a different subject.

Once students learn the subjunctive, many start using it everywhere, so this lesson is about restraint. Rule one: no change of subject, no subjunctive. "Quiero ir" (I want to go), "Espero terminar" (I hope to finish), "Prefiero manejar" (I prefer to drive). One person wants and does, so the second verb is an infinitive. Writing "Quiero que yo vaya" is a common learner error; it is grammatical but unnatural, like saying I want that I go.

Rule two: reporting and believing take the indicative. "Sé que Lucía estudia" (I know Lucía studies). "Creo que la comida es buena" (I think the food is good). "Es cierto que hace frío" (It is true that it is cold). "Lucía dice que tiene mucha tarea" (Lucía says she has a lot of homework). Each main clause treats the que clause as a fact. Learners who have just memorized WEIRDO sometimes write "Sé que Lucía estudie." Saber, creer without no, es verdad and decir when it reports are not triggers.

Rule three: the trigger verb itself is never subjunctive. In "Quiero que vengas," only vengas changes. Writing "Quiera que vengas" is a mistake that comes from over-applying the endings. The main clause stays in whatever indicative tense fits, and only the verb after que takes the new mood. A quick check is to cover everything before que; the remaining verb is the only candidate.

Put the three rules together as questions. Is there a que with a new subject? If not, use an infinitive. Does the main clause wish, feel, judge, advise or doubt? If not, use the indicative. If both answers are yes, use the subjunctive, and only on the second verb. Mateo runs each of Lucía’s sentences through those questions and every one comes out right.

36.1

Dudo que and Creo que

Main ideaDoubt and denial trigger the subjunctive, while belief and certainty keep the indicative, so the same sentence can flip moods when no is added.

Main ideaQuestions with creer can take either mood depending on what the speaker expects, and quizás and tal vez take the subjunctive when the speaker is unsure.

The words and , both meaning maybe or perhaps, work the same way. "Quizás llueve mañana" (Maybe it rains tomorrow) with the indicative sounds fairly confident. Notice that none of these sentences has two clauses. Quizás and tal vez sit in front of a single verb, the way ojalá does. That is a reminder that the subjunctive is not really about que; it is about the speaker’s stance. Que is simply the most common place to see the stance change, because a main clause of wanting or doubting is usually there.

36.3

Main ideaVerbs of emotion such as alegrarse de que, sentir que and temer que take the subjunctive in the que clause, even when the event is real.

Emotion verbs trigger the subjunctive even when the thing you feel about is a plain fact. "Me alegro de que el parque tenga luces nuevas" (I am glad the park has new lights). The lights are real. The sentence is still about my reaction, so tenga is subjunctive. This is the trigger English speakers resist the most, because in English the fact seems to matter more than the feeling. In Spanish, once the main clause reacts, the mood follows.

The common emotion triggers: (to be glad that), (to be sorry that), (to fear that), tener miedo de que (to be afraid that), sorprenderse de que (to be surprised that), esperar que (to hope that), (it is sad that), (I like that), me molesta que (it bothers me that). 36.4

So That, Before, Unless

Main ideaSome connecting words always take the subjunctive because what follows them has not happened yet: para que, antes de que, sin que, a menos que, con tal de que and en caso de que.

A (conjunction) is a word that joins two clauses, like because, so that, or before. Some Spanish conjunctions point to something that has not happened and may never happen, and those always take the subjunctive. (so that): "Arreglan el parque para que los niños jueguen" (They are fixing the park so that the kids will play). The playing is the goal, not yet a fact. (before): "Terminan antes de que empiece el verano" (They finish before summer begins). Summer has not begun.

The full set that always takes the subjunctive: (so that), (before), sin que (without), (unless), con tal de que (provided that), and (in case). Teachers sometimes group them by their first letters. "No cambian nada a menos que la gente hable" (They do not change anything unless people speak up). "Salgo sin que mis padres me vean" (I leave without my parents seeing me). "Llevo paraguas en caso de que llueva" (I bring an umbrella in case it rains).

Contrast these with conjunctions that report a reason or a result. (because) takes the indicative: "Arreglan el parque porque los vecinos se quejaron" (They are fixing the park because the neighbors complained). The complaining happened. Así que (so, therefore) also takes the indicative. The difference between para que and porque is the difference between a purpose and a cause. Purpose looks forward, so it takes the subjunctive; cause looks back, so it takes the indicative.

When there is no change of subject, several of these drop que and take an infinitive: "Trabajo para ganar dinero" (I work in order to earn money), "Antes de salir, cierro la puerta" (Before leaving, I close the door). Add a new subject and the que returns with the subjunctive: "Trabajo para que mi hermano gane dinero" (I work so that my brother earns money).

36.5

When, Until and As Soon As

Main ideaTime conjunctions such as cuando, hasta que and en cuanto take the subjunctive when the event is still in the future, and the indicative when it is habitual or already happened.

(when) is the most common conjunction in Spanish, and its mood depends on time. "Cuando llego a casa, hago la tarea" (When I get home, I do my homework) describes a habit, so it takes the indicative. "Cuando llegué a casa, hice la tarea" (When I got home, I did my homework) reports the past, also indicative. But "Cuando llegue a casa, haré la tarea" (When I get home, I will do my homework) points to a moment that has not arrived, and that pushes llegar into the subjunctive.

The same rule covers (until), (as soon as), (as soon as), después de que (after) and mientras (while). Future and not yet real: subjunctive. Habit or past: indicative. "Esperaré hasta que termine la reunión" (I will wait until the meeting ends) uses termine. "Siempre espero hasta que termina la reunión" (I always wait until the meeting ends) uses termina. "En cuanto abran el parque, vamos a ir" (As soon as they open the park, we are going to go) uses abran.

English gives no help here. "When I get home" is the same in all three sentences, so English speakers must ask a question the language never made them ask: has this already happened, does it happen regularly, or is it still ahead? A future or a command in the main clause is the strongest clue. "Llámame cuando llegues" (Call me when you arrive) is a command about a moment still to come, so llegues is subjunctive.

Señora Reyes’s class made a chart. Past: cuando llegué. Habit: cuando llego. Future: cuando llegue. The first two are indicative in different tenses. Only the third is subjunctive, and only because the arrival is still ahead. Daniela’s paragraph ends with one: "Cuando yo sea alcaldesa, los autobuses van a pasar toda la noche" (When I am mayor, the buses are going to run all night).

The Imperfect Subjunctive

36.6

Building the -ra Forms

Main ideaThe imperfect subjunctive comes from the preterite ellos form: drop -ron, add -ra, -ras, -ra, -ramos, -rais, -ran, with an accent on the nosotros form.

The present subjunctive handles wishes and doubts about now and the future. When the main clause is in the past, or when the wish is only imagined, Spanish uses the (imperfect subjunctive). It has one starting point for every verb in the language: the ellos form of the preterite. That is the good news. There are no separate irregulars to learn, because every irregularity already lives in the preterite you know.

Three steps. Take the preterite ellos form of hablar: hablaron. Drop -ron: habla-. Add -ra, -ras, -ra, -ramos, -rais, -ran: hablara, hablaras, hablara, habláramos, hablarais, hablaran. Comer: comieron, comie-, comiera, comieras, comiera, comiéramos, comierais, comieran. Vivir: vivieron, viviera, vivieras, viviera, viviéramos, vivierais, vivieran. The only accent falls on the nosotros form, on the vowel just before -ramos.

Now the verbs that are irregular in the preterite. Ser and ir share fueron, so both become fuera, fueras, fuera, fuéramos, fuerais, fueran. Tener: tuvieron, tuviera. Estar: estuvieron, estuviera. Poder: pudieron, pudiera. Hacer: hicieron, hiciera. Decir: dijeron, dijera. Querer: quisieron, quisiera. Saber: supieron, supiera. Poner: pusieron, pusiera. Haber: hubieron, hubiera. Dar: dieron, diera. Ver: vieron, viera. Dormir: durmieron, durmiera. Pedir: pidieron, pidiera. If you know the preterite, you have them all.

There is a second set of endings, -se, -ses, -se, -semos, -seis, -sen (hablase, comiese), used more in Spain and in older writing. You will see it in books and hear it from some speakers. It means the same thing. This course teaches the -ra forms, which are the everyday choice across Latin America and the ones Daniela’s grandmother uses.

36.7

Past Triggers, Past Subjunctive

Main ideaWhen the trigger verb is in a past tense, the subjunctive after que shifts to the imperfect subjunctive: quiero que vengas becomes quería que vinieras.

Every trigger from the last chapter still works in the past. The only change is that the subjunctive after que shifts back with it. Present: "Quiero que vengas" (I want you to come). Past: "Quería que vinieras" (I wanted you to come). Present: "Es importante que duermas." Past: "Era importante que durmieras." Present: "Dudo que llegue." Past: "Dudaba que llegara." This matching of tenses is called the (sequence of tenses). A present trigger takes the present subjunctive; a past trigger takes the imperfect subjunctive.

The past trigger can be preterite or imperfect. "Mi abuela quería que yo estudiara medicina" (My grandmother wanted me to study medicine). "El maestro nos pidió que escribiéramos un párrafo" (The teacher asked us to write a paragraph). "Me alegré de que vinieran" (I was glad they came). "No creía que fuera posible" (I did not think it was possible). In each case, cover the main clause and you will find an -ra form after que.

"Yo quería que todos me escucharan" (I wanted everyone to listen to me). "Era necesario que alguien dijera la verdad sobre el parque" (It was necessary for someone to tell the truth about the park). "Me sorprendió que tantos vecinos vinieran" (It surprised me that so many neighbors came).

The mistake to watch is mixing the tenses: "Quería que vengas" pairs a past trigger with a present subjunctive, and it sounds wrong to native ears. Match the times, and the sentence will hold together. If the wanting was in the past, the coming is in the imperfect subjunctive too.

36.8

The Conditional

Main ideaThe conditional, formed by adding -ía endings to the whole infinitive, means would, and it shares its irregular stems with the future tense.

Before building the if-sentence from the story, you need its second half. The (conditional) is the tense for would. "Cambiaría el parque" means I would change the park. It is one of the easiest tenses to form: keep the whole infinitive and add -ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían. Hablar: hablaría, hablarías, hablaría, hablaríamos, hablaríais, hablarían. Comer: comería. Vivir: viviría. Every ending carries an accent on the í, and the endings are identical for -ar, -er and -ir verbs.

The conditional uses the same irregular stems as the future tense. Tener becomes tendría, poner pondría, salir saldría, venir vendría, poder podría, saber sabría, haber habría, querer querría, hacer haría, decir diría. If you learned "tendré" for I will have, then "tendría" for I would have is the same stem with a different ending. Daniela wrote "pondría más autobuses" (I would put on more buses). That is poner with its irregular stem.

The conditional does three jobs. It says what would happen: "Con más dinero, la ciudad arreglaría las calles" (With more money, the city would fix the streets). It softens a request: "¿Podría hablar con el alcalde?" (Could I speak with the mayor?). And it reports what someone said would happen: "Dijo que vendría" (She said she would come). In this chapter it does the first job, as the partner of the imperfect subjunctive.

Be careful not to confuse the conditional with the imperfect indicative. Comería (I would eat) and comía (I used to eat) look alike, but the conditional keeps the whole infinitive, comer-, in front of the ending. The imperfect drops it: com-ía. Say them side by side and the extra syllable in the conditional stands out.

36.9

Si Tuviera, Viajaría

Main ideaA contrary-to-fact if-sentence uses si plus the imperfect subjunctive for the condition and the conditional for the result: Si tuviera dinero, viajaría.

Here is the sentence from the board, taken apart. "Si pudieras cambiar una cosa, ¿qué cambiarías?" The si clause holds the condition, and the verb is pudieras, imperfect subjunctive, because the condition is imagined. The other clause holds the result, and the verb is cambiarías, conditional, because it is what would follow. Si plus imperfect subjunctive, then conditional. That is the whole recipe, and it works for every contrary-to-fact sentence in Spanish.

"Si tuviera dinero, viajaría" (If I had money, I would travel). "Si yo fuera alcaldesa, pondría más luces" (If I were mayor, I would put in more lights). "Si la ciudad arreglara el parque, los niños jugarían allí" (If the city fixed the park, the kids would play there). "Si tuviéramos más autobuses, la gente no manejaría tanto" (If we had more buses, people would not drive so much). The order of the two halves can flip: "Viajaría si tuviera dinero." The forms do not change.

Two mistakes to avoid. First, English speakers sometimes put the conditional in both halves, "Si tendría dinero, viajaría," because both feel like would. The si clause never takes the conditional. Second, some learners use the present subjunctive after si, "Si tenga dinero." Si never takes the present subjunctive either. After si, the choice is between the present indicative, for a real possibility, and the imperfect subjunctive, for something imagined.

Daniela’s grandmother uses these every day: "Si yo fuera joven, aprendería a manejar" (If I were young, I would learn to drive). "Si tuviera tu edad, estudiaría más" (If I were your age, I would study more). Notice that English also flips to were in "if I were," the one place English still marks this mood. Spanish marks it every time.

36.10

Real If and Imagined If

Main ideaSi with the present indicative describes a real possibility and pairs with the present or future; si with the imperfect subjunctive describes something imagined and pairs with the conditional.

Omar’s first draft, "Si puedo cambiar una cosa, cambio el parque," was not wrong Spanish. It was the wrong kind of if. Si with the present indicative describes a real, open possibility, and the result goes in the present or the future. "Si llueve, no vamos al parque" (If it rains, we are not going to the park). "Si estudias, vas a aprobar" (If you study, you will pass). "Si tengo tiempo, te llamaré" (If I have time, I will call you). These are conditions that might come true today.

Si with the imperfect subjunctive describes something the speaker treats as unreal, and the result goes in the conditional. "Si lloviera, no iríamos al parque" (If it rained, we would not go to the park), said on a sunny day. "Si estudiaras, aprobarías" (If you studied, you would pass), said to someone who does not. "Si tuviera tiempo, te llamaría" (If I had time, I would call you), meaning I do not. Same si, different mood, different world.

Put the two side by side with the assignment. Real: "Si el alcalde escucha, la ciudad va a cambiar" (If the mayor listens, the city is going to change). Imagined: "Si el alcalde escuchara, la ciudad cambiaría" (If the mayor listened, the city would change). The first speaker thinks it might happen. The second speaker is sure it will not, or is dreaming. Señora Reyes wanted the second kind, because the assignment asks what you would do with a power you do not have.

Omar’s final paragraph shows the whole unit at work. "Si pudiera cambiar una cosa, arreglaría el parque para que los niños jugaran sin miedo. Why: Ser is irregular: sea, seas, sea, seamos. Somos is indicative, fuéramos is imperfect subjunctive, seremos is future.

5. Which sentence should use the infinitive?

Why: With no change of subject, Spanish uses the infinitive: Quiero comer. The others switch to a new subject after que.

6. Which expression keeps the indicative?

Why: Es cierto que reports certainty: Es cierto que hace frío. The other three trigger the subjunctive.

7. Which sentence means "Mom tells me to sleep more"?

Why: Decir with a subjunctive clause gives an instruction: me dice que duerma. With duermo it would only report that I sleep more.

8. No creo que el autobús ___ (pasar) después de las nueve.

Why: No creer que expresses doubt, so the verb is the subjunctive pase.

9. Arreglan las luces para que la gente ___ (caminar) segura de noche.

Why: Para que always takes the subjunctive: camine.

10. Which cuando sentence describes a habit?

Why: Cuando hace calor, me siento describes what happens regularly, so both verbs are present indicative. The others point to the future and take the subjunctive.

11. What is the yo imperfect subjunctive of ir?

Why: From the preterite fueron, drop -ron and add -ra: fuera. Vaya is present subjunctive, iba is imperfect, iría is conditional.

12. El maestro pidió que nosotros ___ (escribir) un párrafo.

Why: A past trigger, pidió, takes the imperfect subjunctive: escribiéramos, with the accent before -ramos.

13. What is the yo conditional of poner?

Why: Poner uses the irregular stem pondr- in the conditional: pondría. Ponía is imperfect, pondré is future, ponga is present subjunctive.

14. Si yo ___ (poder) cambiar una cosa, arreglaría el parque.

Why: An imagined condition after si takes the imperfect subjunctive: pudiera. The conditional belongs in the result clause.

15. What does "Si tuviéramos más autobuses, la gente no manejaría tanto" mean?

Why: Si plus the imperfect subjunctive tuviéramos with the conditional manejaría is a contrary-to-fact if-sentence: if we had, people would not.
