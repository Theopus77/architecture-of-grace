Culture, Art and Who Skates

Culture

Skateboarding is a sport, but it is also a culture with its own art, photography, film, music and ways of welcoming people. Deck graphics by artists such as Jim Phillips and Vernon Courtland Johnson hang in shops and homes. Magazines such as SkateBoarder, Thrasher and TransWorld, and videos from The Bones Brigade Video Show to today's online clips, taught people how to skate and what skating could mean.

This unit also asks who got to be part of that culture and who was left out of the pictures. Women such as Patti McGee and Peggy Oki skated from the early years. Skaters of color such as Ray Barbee, Stevie Williams and Nyjah Huston, skaters in Brazil and Japan, and wheelchair riders such as Aaron Fotheringham pushed the doors wider. Groups like Skateistan and The Skatepark Project work on purpose to bring new people in.

By the end you will be able to name key artists, magazines and videos and place them in time, explain how photos and video changed the way people learn tricks, and describe the people and groups who made skateboarding more open. You will also be able to name practical habits that help a skatepark welcome everyone, whether you are watching, starting out or coming back.

How did skaters build an art world of their own, and how did that world open its doors to more kinds of people?

The story

Forty Years on a Shop Wall

A row of old decks in a small skate shop reads like a history book with pictures.

On a Tuesday afternoon a woman in her fifties walks into a neighborhood skate shop to buy wheels for her nephew. Above the counter, the owner has screwed about thirty old decks to the wall in a long row. The first one is a narrow plank of plain wood with no picture at all. The last one is wide, with a curved nose and tail and a bright painted scene across the bottom.

She stops in the middle of the row. A deck with a cartoon hand screaming in pain catches her eye, then one covered in skulls and swords. "I remember these," she says. "My brother had that one taped to his bedroom wall. He never rode it. He said it was too nice to scratch." The owner laughs and tells her that plenty of people did the same thing in the 1980s.

Next to the decks hang a few things that are not boards. There is a faded magazine cover from the 1980s, a hand-stapled photocopied booklet with smudged pictures, and a VHS tape case with a skeleton on it. Under them, a small screen plays a skate clip on a loop with a fast punk song behind it.

The owner points at the row. "People think skating is just tricks," he says. "But look. Somebody drew these. Somebody shot these photos and cut these videos together. Somebody picked the music." The woman takes a picture of the wall with her phone before she leaves with the wheels.

Art Under Your Feet

31.1

From Plain Planks to Painted Decks

Main ideaEarly decks were mostly plain, and graphics grew into a major art form once companies, teams and pro models gave artists a reason to draw.

The first store-made skateboards, sold around 1959 and through the early 1960s, looked a lot like small surfboards. Many were plain wood, sometimes painted one color, with a company name stamped or printed on top. Their job was to be ridden and sold, not to be looked at. A is the art printed on a skateboard deck, usually on the bottom, and in those years there was very little of it.

That changed in the 1970s and especially the 1980s. Companies built teams of sponsored skaters, and each top skater could get a , a deck sold with that skater’s name and their own graphic. A skater’s board became a kind of flag. Young riders bought the deck of the skater they admired, so the picture on the bottom had to be strong enough to be seen across a skatepark or in a magazine photo.

The bottom of the deck became the canvas for a practical reason. The top is covered with , a sandpaper-like sheet that keeps shoes from slipping, so art there disappears. The bottom shows when a skater flies out of a ramp, flips the board or carries it. The bottom also gets scraped by rails, ledges and curbs, so every graphic slowly wears away. Many skaters see that wear as part of the board’s story.

The second is from the late 1980s: wider, with a kicked-up tail, and its whole bottom is covered in a bold drawing in three or four colors. Turn both over. The top of the first is bare wood. The top of the second is covered in black grip tape.

31.2

Jim Phillips at Santa Cruz

Main ideaJim Phillips, an artist for Santa Cruz, drew bold cartoon graphics such as the Screaming Hand that became some of the best-known images in skating.

Jim Phillips was an artist from Santa Cruz, California, who drew for surf culture before he drew for skateboards. He became the main graphic artist for Santa Cruz, a skateboard company, from the 1970s into the 1990s. His style used thick black outlines, bright flat colors and wild cartoon energy. You can recognize it from across a room.

His most famous image is the , a blue hand with an open, yelling mouth in its palm. It first appeared in the mid-1980s and has been printed on decks, shirts, stickers and wheels ever since. A graphic that a company uses again and again, until people know the company from the picture alone, works like a . The Screaming Hand became exactly that for Santa Cruz.

Phillips also designed graphics for individual pro models, matching each picture to a skater’s name or personality. His work shows that deck art was not decoration added at the end. It was planned art made for a long, narrow shape with rounded ends, a hard space to fill well. Later artists studied how he used that shape, placing the main image so it still read clearly when the board was moving.

Think it through
Filling a Long, Narrow Space

Imagine you are handed a blank deck and a pencil. The space is about four times as long as it is wide, with rounded ends that curve up. A square drawing would leave big empty bands at the nose and tail. A drawing that runs the full length, with the strongest shape near the middle, fills it and still shows when the board is upside down in the air.

31.3

Vernon Courtland Johnson and the Skulls

Main ideaVernon Courtland Johnson drew Powell Peralta's skulls, skeletons and the Bones Brigade images that defined how 1980s skating looked.

Powell Peralta was a California skateboard company started by George Powell and Stacy Peralta in the late 1970s. Its team, the , included young skaters such as Tony Hawk, Steve Caballero, Lance Mountain, Mike McGill and Rodney Mullen. The company needed a look as strong as the team, and the artist who gave it one was Vernon Courtland Johnson, often called VCJ.

Johnson drew detailed skulls, skeletons, snakes and dragons in pen and ink, full of fine lines and shading. His skull-and-sword design became a symbol of the company and the team. He also drew pro model graphics fitted to each rider, such as a dragon for Steve Caballero and a hawk-like skull for Tony Hawk. The images looked a little dangerous, which fit the way skating felt to many teenagers in the 1980s.

Johnson’s graphics did more than sell boards. They tied together the decks, the team, the magazine ads and the company’s videos into one recognizable world. Placing Jim Phillips’s bold cartoons next to Johnson’s fine-lined skulls shows two very different answers to the same problem. Both artists made a narrow wooden board into something people wanted to keep, frame and remember decades later.

Think it through

The Johnson board has a skull drawn with hundreds of fine pen lines and careful shading, almost like an old engraving. Step back three meters. Now step close.

Photographs and Magazines

31.4

SkateBoarder and the Dogtown Stories

Main ideaSkateBoarder magazine spread skating's look across the country, and its 1970s Dogtown articles and photos made the Zephyr skaters famous.

A magazine called The Quarterly Skateboarder first appeared in 1964, during the first boom. It soon became SkateBoarder. When the boom faded around 1965 and 1966, the magazine stopped. It came back in the mid-1970s, after urethane wheels brought skating back to life, and for the rest of the decade it was the main way most skaters outside California saw what was happening.

In the mid-1970s SkateBoarder ran a series of articles and photos about the Zephyr team from Santa Monica and Venice, California, an area some locals called . Craig Stecyk wrote and photographed many of them, and Glen E. Friedman, a teenager at the time, took photos of the same skaters. Their pictures showed Tony Alva, Jay Adams, Peggy Oki, Stacy Peralta and others riding low and aggressive, like surfers on concrete.

These were not posed portraits. They were : pictures taken in the middle of a move, often in empty backyard swimming pools during the 1976 to 1977 drought. A reader in another state could study a photo and copy a skater’s crouch, arm position and line. In 2001 Stacy Peralta made Dogtown and Z-Boys, a documentary film that retold this story using many of those photos.

31.5

Thrasher and TransWorld

Main ideaThrasher (1981) and TransWorld SKATEboarding (1983) carried skating through the 1980s, each with its own voice and look.

By the early 1980s many skateparks had closed over insurance costs, and SkateBoarder magazine had changed direction. Skaters moved to backyard ramps, empty lots and city streets. Two new magazines followed them. started in San Francisco in 1981. started in Southern California in 1983.

Thrasher had a rough, loud voice. It covered backyard ramps, street skating and punk bands, and its slogan, "Skate and Destroy," matched that attitude. TransWorld tended toward cleaner layouts and a slightly calmer tone, with long photo features and interviews. Skaters often argued about which one they liked better. Having both meant more photographers, writers and skaters got paid and seen.

Both magazines relied on the , a row of photos taken a fraction of a second apart that shows a trick from start to finish. A sequence lets a reader see where the feet go at each moment, which a single photo cannot do. Thrasher later began naming a Skater of the Year, a title many skaters still treat as one of the highest honors in the sport. Many of the best-known skate photographers built their careers shooting for these two magazines.

31.6

How a Skate Photo Is Made

Main ideaSkate photographers use wide lenses, flashes and careful timing to freeze a trick at the exact moment that shows it best.

A good skate photo usually catches the peak of a trick: the highest point of an ollie, the middle of a grind, the instant a flipped board lines up under the feet. That moment lasts a tiny fraction of a second. The photographer has to know the trick well enough to press the shutter just before it, because there is always a small delay between seeing and pressing.

Many skate photographers use a , a very wide lens that bends straight lines into curves and lets the camera get close to the skater while still showing the whole obstacle. Others stand far back with a long lens to show the size of a spot. Portable units, small bright lights fired for a split second, freeze motion and light a skater who is in shadow. Photographers often place two or three flashes around a spot before the skater tries anything.

Shooting a trick can take many tries. A skater may need dozens of attempts to land it, and the photographer needs one try where the landing, the angle and the light all work. That shared work builds trust between skater and photographer. Today a phone can shoot bursts of many frames per second, so anyone can try this at a local park, as long as they stand well out of the skater’s path.

Think it through
Setting Up One Shot

A photographer arrives at a low ledge in a skatepark. First, she watches the skater try the trick a few times without shooting. Next, she sets one flash on a small stand to the side, out of the skater's path. Then she kneels low with a fisheye lens about two meters from the ledge, so the ledge looks long and the skater looks tall. On each try she presses the shutter a moment before the board reaches the ledge.

Why does she watch a few tries before shooting, and why does she press a moment early?

Skate Video as an Art Form

31.7

The Bones Brigade on VHS

Main ideaThe Bones Brigade Video Show (1984) used home video tape to show skating in motion, and videos soon became how skaters learned and shared tricks.

In 1984 Powell Peralta released The Bones Brigade Video Show, directed by Stacy Peralta. It came out on , the home videotape format of the 1980s, so a skater could watch it on a family TV and rewind a trick again and again. Magazines could show a sequence of a few photos. A video showed the whole run, with speed, sound and timing.

Powell Peralta followed with Future Primitive in 1985 and The Search for Animal Chin in 1987. These videos mixed real skating with short scenes and jokes, and they made the Bones Brigade skaters famous far from California. Skaters copied tapes for friends, and shops played them on TVs. A teenager in a small town could now see Tony Hawk or Steve Caballero ride a ramp in real time.

Video changed how people learned. A skater could pause a tape on the frame where the back foot snapped the tail, then play it in slow motion. Some skaters say they learned their first tricks from a paused VHS tape. It also changed what a professional skater was. A pro now had to be filmed well, not just win contests, and the , a skater’s own section of a video, became a major event.

31.8

Street Videos of the Early 1990s

Main ideaVideos such as Blind's Video Days (1991) and Plan B's Questionable (1992) made street skating and the filmmaker's style central to skate culture.

By the early 1990s many skaters rode city streets: curbs, ledges, benches, stairs and handrails. Video followed them. Blind’s Video Days came out in 1991, directed by Spike Jonze, who later became a well-known film director. It followed a small group of skaters, including Mark Gonzales, Jason Lee and Guy Mariano, through their city. Its loose, personal style felt like hanging out with friends, and many skaters still name it among the most important skate videos.

Plan B’s Questionable came out in 1992. It showed highly technical street skating, with flip tricks and long grinds done on everyday city objects. Watching it, viewers could see how far flat-ground tricks had come since Rodney Mullen’s early-1980s work. Each skater’s part was edited to a chosen song, so the rhythm of the music and the rhythm of the tricks matched.

These videos show skate filmmaking as an art. The filmer picks the angle, follows the skater while rolling on a board, and decides what to keep. The editor chooses the order of tricks, the song and the , the last and usually hardest trick in a part. Many famous street spots seen in these videos were private or not meant for skating, and rules there varied; skaters learning today build skills at skateparks and on small, low obstacles first.

Think it through
Building a Video Part

An editor has forty clips of one skater and a three-minute song. First, she drops the clips that are blurry or badly framed. Next, she groups tricks by spot so the part moves from place to place. Then she places small tricks during the quiet start of the song and harder tricks as it builds. She saves the most difficult landing for the final beat: the ender.

Why might an editor put the hardest trick last instead of first?

31.9

From Camcorders to the Internet

Main ideaBetter cameras in the 1990s and the internet in the 2000s moved skate video from mailed tapes to clips anyone can post.

In the mid-1990s Sony released the VX1000, a digital (a handheld video camera). Paired with a fisheye lens, it became a standard tool for skate filmers for years. Its look, sharp and close with curved edges, is still copied on purpose today. Full-length videos moved from VHS to DVD around the early 2000s, often with extra footage and behind-the-scenes clips.

The internet changed the speed of everything. YouTube launched in 2005, and Instagram in 2010. A trick filmed in the morning could be seen around the world by night. Short clips, often under a minute, became as important as long videos. Skaters in Brazil, Japan, South Africa or Illinois could build an audience without a magazine or a company choosing them first.

Something was gained and something changed. More people can share their skating, including girls, older skaters, adaptive riders and skaters far from California, who once rarely appeared in magazines. At the same time, a constant stream of clips can make a full video part, planned and edited over a year or more, feel rare. Many skaters still treat a full part as the highest form of the art.

31.10

Punk, Hip-Hop and the Soundtrack

Main ideaPunk in the 1980s and hip-hop in the 1990s shaped skate culture, and the songs in videos and games introduced many people to that music.

In the early 1980s, as skating moved to backyards and streets, many skaters listened to : short, fast, loud songs, often made cheaply by young bands. Punk’s attitude of making things yourself, without asking permission from big companies, fit skating at that time. Some skaters played in bands, and Thrasher covered punk music alongside skating and put out compilation tapes of what it called skate rock.

In the 1990s became just as important. Street skating and hip-hop both grew in cities and both treated everyday places, a plaza or a corner, as a stage. Video parts were set to rap, soul, jazz and rock, and the song a skater chose said something about who they were. A part’s music could make a run feel smooth and stylish or wild and fast.

Music also carried skating to new people. When the video game Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater came out in 1999, its soundtrack mixed punk and hip-hop. Many players heard those bands and rappers for the first time while playing. Today the song choice in a video part is still treated as part of the art, and filmers sometimes edit each trick to land on a beat.

Think it through
Same Tricks, Different Song

The second time it runs with a slow soul song: the same pushes and carves feel smooth and relaxed, and you notice the skater's style more than the danger.

31.11

Zines and DIY Culture

Main ideaSkaters have a long habit of making things themselves: photocopied zines, backyard ramps and skater-built parks like Burnside.

A (say "zeen") is a small, homemade magazine, often typed or handwritten, cut and pasted together, photocopied and stapled. In the 1980s and 1990s skaters made zines about their local crews, spots and bands, and traded or mailed them to each other. A zine needed no publisher and no advertisers. It needed a copy machine, some photos and someone who cared.

This habit is called , short for do it yourself. When many skateparks closed in the early 1980s, skaters built wooden half-pipes and mini ramps in backyards, sometimes from plans printed in magazines. In 1990 skaters in Portland, Oregon, began pouring concrete under the Burnside Bridge without permission. The city later accepted Burnside skatepark, and it became famous around the world. In London, skaters have used the Southbank undercroft since the 1970s, and the Long Live Southbank campaign kept it open with an agreement in 2014.

DIY is a way of seeing a place and asking what it could become. It also comes with real responsibilities. Building on land you do not own can be illegal and unsafe, and good DIY projects today often work with a city or landowner, as Burnside eventually did. The same spirit drives skaters who make their own videos, photos, shirts and art, and who teach each other at the local park.

Over the years more walls appear. Much later, city leaders who once saw a problem start to see a public park that people travel from other countries to ride.

Women in the Early Years

32.1

Patti McGee in 1965

Main ideaPatti McGee won the women's title at the first national championships in 1965 and appeared on the cover of Life magazine that year.

The first National Skateboard Championships were held in Anaheim, California, in 1965, and ABC’s Wide World of Sports showed them on television. Patti McGee won the women’s national title. At a time when many people thought of skating as something only boys did, a young woman was the champion on national TV.

That same year Life magazine, one of the most widely read magazines in the United States, put McGee on its cover. The photo showed her doing a on a moving skateboard, barefoot, with her body straight above the board. A handstand on a rolling board takes strength, balance and practice. The cover carried skateboarding, and a woman skater, into millions of homes.

The first boom faded around 1965 and 1966, and many contests stopped. McGee’s place in the story stayed, though. Later women skaters have named her as proof that girls were part of skateboarding from the start. Her story is a reminder that the record of who skated depends partly on who was photographed and written about, and that being seen can open a door for others.

Peggy Oki and the Zephyr Team

Main ideaPeggy Oki was the only woman on the original Zephyr team, and she skated in the same low, surf-style way as her teammates.

In the mid-1970s the Zephyr team came out of a surf shop in Santa Monica, California, near Venice. Its skaters, including Tony Alva, Jay Adams and Stacy Peralta, rode low to the ground, dragging a hand on the concrete the way a surfer touches a wave. At the Del Mar Nationals in 1975, the Zephyr skaters surprised the crowd and the judges. Most competitors did upright freestyle routines. The Zephyr team crouched, carved hard and slid their boards in long, surf-like turns. Oki competed there with her teammates. Her skating showed that this new, aggressive style was not something only boys could do.

Oki later turned much of her energy to art, working as a painter. For later women who discovered the Zephyr story through magazines and the 2001 documentary Dogtown and Z-Boys, she became an important figure. Seeing one woman in a famous team photo showed many girls a place for themselves in the story.

Contests, Careers and Equal Pay

32.3

Women's Pro Careers Grow

Main ideaWomen skaters rode professionally from the 1970s on, but they had fewer contests, pro models and photos until skaters organized for change.

In the late 1970s a few women, such as Laura Thornhill and Ellen O’Neal, rode for companies and appeared in magazines, mostly in : tricks done on flat ground, often in a planned routine. When many skateparks closed in the early 1980s, contests for everyone shrank, and women’s events often disappeared first. For years many girls who skated rarely saw another woman at the park or in a magazine.

In the 1990s and 2000s more women turned pro in street and vert skating. Elissa Steamer, a street skater, became the first woman included in the video game Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater in 1999. Millions of players saw her listed beside men. Women’s events were added at more contests, but they often paid much less, had less TV time and were sometimes cut with little warning.

Women skaters answered by organizing. In the late 2000s a group of women pros pushed the X Games to pay women and men the same prize money, and the contest agreed. When skateboarding entered the Olympics, women and men had the same number of events, street and park for each. That structure was the result of decades of women showing up, skating and asking.

Being Seen Changes Who Skates

Main ideaWhen people see skaters who look like them in photos, videos and contests, more of them try skating, so coverage itself opens doors.

For much of skating’s history, magazines and videos mostly showed young men, often white and often from California. That did not mean only those people skated. Girls, older people, skaters of color and skaters in other countries were at parks and on streets, but they appeared in print much less. A is a person others look up to and learn from, and many skaters went years without seeing one who looked like them.

Coverage tends to feed itself. Companies sponsored the skaters who appeared in magazines, magazines photographed sponsored skaters, and young people copied what they saw. Breaking that loop took skaters, photographers and editors who chose to show more kinds of people. Online video helped too, because skaters could post their own clips without waiting to be chosen.

In media, means who is shown and how. Today it is common to see women, adaptive riders and skaters from many countries in contests, videos and ads. Skate groups often say that seeing someone like you on a board makes the first visit to a park feel possible. For a beginner, a single friendly face at the park can do the same thing.

Adaptive Skating and Belonging

32.9

WCMX and Aaron Fotheringham

Main ideaWCMX is riding a skatepark in a wheelchair, and Aaron Fotheringham showed what is possible when he landed the first wheelchair backflip in the mid-2000s.

, short for wheelchair motocross, means riding a skatepark’s ramps, bowls and ledges in a wheelchair. Riders use strong, specially built chairs, often with extra wheels or suspension, and they wear helmets and pads like any skater. The same ideas apply as on a board: lean into a ramp, keep your weight centered, and learn to fall safely before trying bigger moves.

Aaron Fotheringham, from Las Vegas, was born with spina bifida and has used a wheelchair for most of his life. As a boy he rode at a skatepark with his brother, who rode a BMX bike. In the mid-2000s he landed the first wheelchair backflip, rolling up a ramp and turning a full circle backward in the air before landing. Clips of it spread widely online, and he later performed in live action-sports shows around the world.

Adaptive skating is a wider family. It includes wheelchair riders, skaters with prosthetic legs, skaters who are blind or have low vision, and people who ride sitting or kneeling on a board. The word means changing the equipment or the way a skill is done so it fits a person’s body. Many parks now hold adaptive sessions, and riders teach each other new ways to drop in, turn and fall.

Main ideaGroups such as Skateistan and The Skatepark Project, plus local girls' and adaptive sessions, work on purpose to bring new people into skating.

Skateistan began in Kabul, Afghanistan, in 2007, founded by Oliver Percovich, an Australian skater. In the United States, the Tony Hawk Foundation began in 2002 to help communities build public skateparks, especially in places with few resources. It was renamed The Skatepark Project in 2020. These groups use a few simple moves that any park can copy. They lend helmets and pads so cost is not a barrier. They set times when beginners will not feel rushed. They train volunteers to teach, starting on grass or flat ground. Most of all, they make sure a new person is greeted by name. A is anything that keeps someone from starting, such as cost, fear, distance or not seeing anyone like you.

A Place Where Many Belong

Main ideaSkateparks work best for everyone when riders share space with simple habits: take turns, watch your line and welcome newcomers.

A busy skatepark has no referee, so riders follow unwritten rules called . Wait at the edge and take turns dropping in. Look before you roll so you do not cut across someone’s path. Do not sit on obstacles or stand in landing zones. If you fall, move off quickly. These habits keep a park safe for a nine-year-old, a sixty-year-old and a wheelchair rider at the same time.

Belonging also comes from small social habits. Experienced skaters often cheer when someone lands a first trick, even a very small one, and that cheer means a lot to a beginner. Tapping a board on the ground is a common way to applaud. Older skaters and adults starting late are part of this too; many parks now see people in their forties, fifties and older learning for the first time or coming back.

Skating has always mixed many kinds of people, even when photos did not show it. The doors opened by Patti McGee, Peggy Oki, Ray Barbee, Elissa Steamer, Aaron Fotheringham, the teenage Olympic medalists and countless local volunteers make today’s parks more open than ever. A is a group of people who share a place or an interest and look out for each other. Every rider can help build one.
