Three Rebellions

Genesis 3, 6 and 11

The first chapters of Genesis tell very old stories. A garden is lost. Heavenly beings cross into the human world. A great flood comes, and one family is kept safe. People have read these stories for thousands of years. Some scholars, like Michael Heiser, call them rebellions, times when someone went against God's plan.

In this unit you read two of those stories closely: the serpent in Eden (Genesis 3) and the sons of God before the Flood (Genesis 6). You start with the text itself. Then you meet old books that retold it, like the Septuagint, 1 Enoch, Jubilees and Josephus. Last, you see how scholars read it today.

By the end, you will be able to retell both stories in order. You will name the verses and old books each reading uses. You will explain words like nachash, Nephilim, Septuagint and covenant. And you will explain each reading fairly, the way its own readers would.

The big question

What do the first rebellion stories in Genesis say, and why have readers from ancient times to today read them in different ways?

The story

The Snake on the Kitchen Table

Two cousins disagree about a snake in a very old story, so they open the book and read it together.

Maya and her cousin Leo sat at the kitchen table after dinner. Leo had a picture book of Bible stories. On one page, a green snake hung from a tree in a garden. "It is just a snake," Leo said. "A snake that could talk." Maya shook her head. "My teacher said it might be something else."

Leo's grandmother heard them. She did not pick a side. Instead she brought a real Bible to the table. "Let's see what the words say," she said. "Then you can see why people read them in different ways." She turned to the book of Genesis, chapter 3.

They read the first verse slowly. The serpent was clever, more than any wild animal. It spoke to the woman. It asked her a tricky question about what God had said. Leo pointed at the page. "See? It says animal." Maya pointed too. "It says it was more clever than the animals. And snakes do not talk."

Grandma smiled. "You both noticed something true," she said. "People have asked about this snake for over two thousand years. Some old books say animals could talk back then. Some scholars today think it was a shining spirit being. Let's read the old words and see why." Leo leaned in. "What old words?" Grandma opened her notebook.

Talk about itMaya and Leo each noticed something real in the words. What is a good way to talk with someone who reads a story differently than you do?

Old Stories, Old Words

9.1

Three Stories Go Wrong

Main ideaGenesis 3, 6 and 11 tell three early stories where things go wrong; the scholar Michael Heiser called them three rebellions.

Genesis is the first book of the Bible. Its first eleven chapters tell stories of beginnings. Three of these stories show someone going against God’s word. They are in Genesis 3, Genesis 6 and Genesis 11.

Genesis 3 tells of a serpent in the garden of Eden. Genesis 6 tells of the sons of God coming to people on earth. Genesis 11 tells of the tower of Babel. In each story, someone refuses God’s plan. That kind of refusal is called a .

Many readers have noticed links between these stories. The Bible scholar Michael Heiser, in The Unseen Realm (2015), called them three rebellions. He thought they help explain why the world in the Bible is so troubled. Other readers see them as separate stories. This unit reads the first two closely.

More than two thousand years ago, Jewish scholars in Egypt put Genesis into Greek. This Greek translation is called the . It is one of the oldest windows into how early readers understood Genesis. For the serpent, it uses ophis, the usual Greek word for a snake.

9.4

The Garden and the Mountain

Main ideaEzekiel 28 calls Eden both the garden of God and the holy mountain of God.

Genesis is not the only book that mentions Eden. The prophet wrote about it too. In Ezekiel 28, he speaks a poem to the king of Tyre, a rich city by the sea. The poem uses pictures from Eden to describe the king.

Ezekiel 28:13 says, "Thou hast been in Eden the garden of God." Verse 14 speaks of "the holy mountain of God." In this poem, Eden is a garden and also a mountain. Many scholars notice this. In the Bible, a holy mountain is often where God meets people. So they read Eden as more than a farm.

Verse 14 also calls the one in the poem an anointed . A cherub is a heavenly guard. Many readers say the poem is about a human king and pictures his pride. Others think it also looks back to a proud being in Eden. Readers still discuss this.

9.5

Main ideaIn Genesis 3:22 God says the man is "as one of us," and readers explain the "us" in different ways.

Genesis 3:8 says the man and woman heard God walking in the garden in the cool of the day. In this story, God is close by. People and God share the same place. Many readers find this a warm and peaceful picture.

Genesis 3:22 has a surprise. God says, "Behold, the man is become as one of us." Who is us? Readers give different answers. Many Christians hear a hint of the Trinity. Some Jewish teachers said God was speaking with the angels. Others read it as a royal way of speaking.

Some scholars read us as God’s . That means God’s heavenly court of spirit beings. E. Theodore Mullen studied councils like this in old Canaanite and Hebrew texts (1980). Michael Heiser thought Eden was where this council met with people. Many readers see only God and the first people in Eden.

The Serpent

9.6

The Word Nachash

Main ideaThe Hebrew word for serpent, nachash, sounds like words for giving omens and for shining bronze, and Heiser read these as clues.

In Genesis 3, the Hebrew word for the serpent is . Most English Bibles translate it as serpent or snake. That is its usual meaning. Some readers also look at other Hebrew words that sound the same or share the same letters.

One related word means to give . An omen is a sign people once used to try to tell the future. Another related word means bronze, a shiny metal. Heiser wrote that these links could point to a being that shines and gives secret messages.

Word links like these are clues, not proof. Hebrew has many words that look alike. Many scholars think nachash here simply means snake. Heiser read the clues together with Ezekiel 28. He called the serpent a , a bright spirit being, not an ordinary snake.

9.7

Old Books Retell the Serpent

Main ideaJubilees and Josephus retell Genesis 3 and treat the serpent as an animal that could speak in those early days.

Long ago, Jewish writers retold the Genesis stories in their own books. One is the Book of , from about 160 BCE. Jubilees says that in Eden all the animals spoke one language. It says their mouths were closed on the day the people left the garden.

Another is , a Jewish historian who wrote in Greek about 94 CE. His book Antiquities of the Jews tells the Eden story. He says the serpent lived near Adam and Eve and could talk. He says God took away its speech because it meant harm.

Both writers read the serpent as a real animal in a very different world. Many Jewish and Christian readers still read it that way. Some scholars read Genesis 3 as a about choices. Others, as you saw, see a spirit being. The same verses lead to different readings.

9.8

Later Readers Name the Serpent

Main ideaGenesis 3 never names the serpent, but later books, like the Wisdom of Solomon and Revelation, link it to the devil.

Read Genesis 3 closely, and you will notice something. The serpent is never given a name. It is only called the serpent. The name does not appear in the Eden story at all.

Later writers began to link Eden with the . The Wisdom of Solomon, a Jewish book written in Greek about two thousand years ago, says death came into the world through the devil’s envy. Later came , the last book of the Christian Bible. Revelation 12:9 calls a great dragon "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan."

Because of verses like these, many Christians see the Eden serpent as Satan. Jewish readers explain it in more than one way. Some keep the serpent and Satan apart. Others read it simply as a snake. Knowing which book says what keeps the readings clear.

10.3

The Nephilim

Main ideaThe Nephilim are named in Genesis 6:4 and Numbers 13:33; many translations call them giants.

Genesis 6:4 names a group called the . The Septuagint, the old Greek translation, calls them gigantes, giants. The King James Version also says giants. Many newer Bibles keep the Hebrew name, Nephilim. Genesis does not describe what they looked like.

The name appears again in Numbers 13:33. Spies sent into Canaan came back with a report. They said they saw the Nephilim there. They said they felt as small as grasshoppers next to them. So the Bible links the name with very tall people.

Readers disagree about where the name comes from. Many connect it to the Hebrew word naphal, to fall. Some scholars, like Michael Heiser, think it comes from an word meaning giant. Aramaic is a language close to Hebrew. Either way, the story stays short and calm.

Three Ways to Read It

10.4

Reading One: Heavenly Beings

Main ideaThe oldest Jewish readings, in 1 Enoch, Jubilees and Josephus, say the sons of God were heavenly beings.

The oldest known writings that explain Genesis 6 are Jewish books from before the time of Jesus. One is . Its first part, the Book of the Watchers, was likely written about 300 to 200 BCE. It calls the sons of God , heavenly beings who came down to earth.

1 Enoch 6 says two hundred Watchers came down on Mount Hermon. In the 1950s, pieces of 1 Enoch in Aramaic were found among the . J. T. Milik published them in 1976. They show that Jews were reading 1 Enoch before the time of Jesus.

Jubilees and the historian Josephus also say angels took wives. Today 1 Enoch is in the Bible only of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches. George Nickelsburg wrote a long study of it (2001). Michael Heiser held this reading too, in Reversing Hermon (2017).

10.5

Reading Two: The Line of Seth

Main ideaAnother reading says the sons of God were Seth's faithful family marrying into Cain's family.

Genesis 4 and 5 tell of two families. One comes from , the son of Adam who killed his brother Abel. The other comes from , a son born to Adam and Eve later. Genesis 4:26 says people began to call on the name of the LORD in Seth’s time.

In the early 200s CE, a Christian writer named Julius Africanus read Genesis 6 this way. The sons of God were Seth’s faithful family. The daughters of men came from Cain’s family. Around 420 CE, Augustine, a famous church leader, taught this reading in his book City of God.

This became the most common Christian reading for centuries. Many still hold it. They point out that Jesus says angels do not marry, in Matthew 22:30. Readers who disagree note that Genesis never calls Seth’s family sons of God.

10.6

Reading Three: Powerful Kings

Main ideaA third reading says the sons of God were powerful human rulers who took whatever wives they wanted.

In the ancient world, some said they were children of gods. In Egypt, the pharaoh was called a son of the sun god Ra. So some readers think the sons of God in Genesis 6 were proud rulers.

Some Jewish teachers of the early centuries CE read it this way. An old Aramaic translation of Genesis, the Targum Onqelos, calls them the sons of great men. In this reading, the sin was . Rulers took any women they chose.

Some modern scholars also hold this view. They note that verse 4 calls the children mighty men and famous men, words often used for warriors. Readers who favor the heavenly beings reading answer that Job and 1 Enoch point the other way.

Think it through
A Ruler's Title

Imagine an ancient ruler carving his name in stone: Son of the Sun God, Mighty King. Now read Genesis 6:2 again: the sons of God took wives of all which they chose. The kings reading asks whether these could be the same kind of people.

10.7

Clues From the New Testament

Main idea2 Peter 2 and Jude 6 speak of angels who sinned, near Noah and Enoch, and many scholars link them to the Watchers.

The New Testament also speaks of angels who sinned. 2 Peter 2:4 says God put them in a dark place to wait for judgment. The Greek word used there for that place is .

The next verse, 2 Peter 2:5, talks about Noah and the Flood. Jude 6 tells of angels who left their proper home. A few verses later, Jude 14 names Enoch and quotes words found in 1 Enoch 1:9. Scholars agree on that quote.

Richard Bauckham, in his study Jude, 2 Peter (1983), read these verses in light of the Watchers story in 1 Enoch. Readers who hold the line of Seth reading answer differently. They say these verses could be about a different fall of angels.

10.8

Putting the Clues Together

Main ideaReaders weigh several clues together: Job, 1 Enoch, the New Testament and the mighty children.

No careful reader rests a view on one verse. Michael Heiser, for example, put several together. First, sons of God means heavenly beings in Job. Second, the oldest Jewish readers said so. Third, 2 Peter and Jude seem to agree.

Fourth, he pointed to the children. Genesis 6:4 calls them mighty men of old. Numbers 13:33 links the Nephilim with very tall people. Heiser thought ordinary human marriages would not explain this. He saw a heavenly being crossed.

Readers who disagree weigh the clues differently. Some give most weight to Matthew 22:30, about angels not marrying. Some trust later church teaching. Studying this debate shows how scholars build a case from many clues.

The Flood and Noah

10.9

Why the Flood Came

Main ideaGenesis 6:5–7 says people's hearts had turned to wrong, and God decided to send a flood.

Next comes Genesis 6:5. It says the LORD saw that people’s was great. Every thought of their hearts was only evil all the time. Genesis 6:6 says this grieved God in his heart.

So God decided to send a flood. Many readers see the reason in verse 5, the wrong that people did. 1 Enoch tells a longer story. It says the Watchers taught people harmful skills, and the earth cried out. Some scholars think Genesis placed 6:1–4 before the Flood on purpose.

Genesis 6:8 then says something hopeful. "But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD." means kindness that someone did not earn. In the middle of a sad chapter, the story turns to one family.

10.10

Noah and the Ark

Main ideaGod told Noah to build a huge ark, and Noah's family and the animals were kept safe inside.

Genesis 6:9 calls Noah a just man who walked with God. God told Noah to build an . An ark is a large boat shaped like a box. Genesis 6:15 gives its size: 300 cubits long, 50 cubits wide and 30 cubits high.

A was the length from an elbow to the tip of the middle finger. That is about 18 inches. So the ark was about 450 feet long. That is longer than a football field.

Noah, his wife, his three sons Shem, Ham and Japheth, and their wives went inside. Animals went in too, by pairs. Genesis 7:12 says rain fell for forty days and forty nights. The story says everyone inside the ark was kept safe.

10.11

The Rainbow Promise

Main ideaAfter the Flood, God made a covenant with Noah and set the rainbow as its sign.

After the waters went down, Noah and his family came out of the ark. Genesis 9 says God made a with Noah. A covenant is a serious promise that binds two sides together. God promised never again to send a flood to destroy the earth.

God gave a sign of this promise. Genesis 9:13 says, "I do set my bow in the cloud." The bow is a . The story says God will see the rainbow and remember the promise to every living thing.

Genesis 9:1 repeats words from Genesis 1:28: be fruitful and multiply. So many readers see the Flood as a fresh start, like a new creation. Many see the rainbow as a sign of God’s patience. Genesis 11 tells of Babel next.
