A ratio is a group you repeat

Two blue and three gold. That set is the ratio 2 to 3.

A ratio is a set you keep taking. 2:3 and 6:9 are the SAME ratio, because 6:9 is that same set three times. To keep a ratio the same you MULTIPLY both parts by the same number — never add.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

A ratio of 2:3 means there are 3 more gold than blue.

A ratio compares by GROUPS, not by difference. 2:3 means two blue for every three gold — which could be 2 and 3, or 20 and 30. The gap changes; the ratio does not.

Groups, not the gap between them.

⚠️ People often think…

To keep a ratio the same, add the same number to both sides.

Adding changes it. 2:3 plus one each is 3:4, which is a different mix entirely — more blue per gold than before. Multiplying preserves it: 2:3 doubled is 4:6, the same mix.

Multiply to keep it. Adding changes it.

⚠️ People often think…

For a unit rate, divide the bigger number by the smaller one.

Divide by whatever you want ONE of. “Miles per hour” means one hour, so divide miles by hours — even if hours is the bigger number. The word “per” tells you the denominator.

The word “per” names the denominator.

⚠️ People often think…

If blue to gold is 2:3, then 2/3 of them are blue.

A ratio compares the two PARTS; a fraction compares a part to the WHOLE. The whole is 2 + 3 = 5, so blue is 2/5 of them. Add the parts before you write a fraction.

Add the parts to get the whole.

1 Watch one

A recipe uses 2 cups flour to 3 cups milk. You want 9 cups of milk. How much flour?

  1. Find how many times bigger: 9 ÷ 3 = 3.
  2. Whatever you did to one part, do to the other — multiply, not add.
  3. 2 × 3 = 6 cups of flour.
  4. Check: 6:9 is the same mix as 2:3. Adding 6 to each would have given 8:9, which tastes wrong. ✓

Helpful Hints

🪜 From a comparison to a unit rate — the ladder

Ratio (for every) → equivalent ratios (multiply both) → ratio table → rate (two different units) → unit rate (for ONE) → use it: multiply to go up, divide to come back.

Compare, scale, divide to one, multiply to many.

🤝 The whole topic in one idea

Whatever you do to one number of a ratio, do the same MULTIPLYING or DIVIDING to the other. A table, a tape diagram and a double number line are just three ways of keeping track of that one move.

⚠️ Traps the test loves

  • Order. “Dogs to cats” and “cats to dogs” are different ratios. Match the words.
  • Part or whole? “Red to blue” is part to part. “Red to all” needs the total — add first.
  • Adding is not scaling. 2:3 plus 1 each is 3:4, a different ratio.
  • Unit price is dollars ÷ units. Flipping it gives units per dollar — then HIGHER is better.
  • The cheaper package is not always the better buy. Compare unit prices, not totals.
  • “30% of what is 12?” is not “30% of 12.” Decide which number is the whole.

🎯 How the test will ask

  • A picture of objects — “write the ratio of ___ to ___” (watch the order, and part vs whole).
  • A table with a blank — find the multiplier from a full row, then use it.
  • Two packages — “which is the better buy?” (two divisions, then compare).
  • A trip — distance, rate, time: two are given, find the third.
  • A percent — find the part (30% of 50) or the whole (30% of what is 15?).
  • A unit change — feet to inches, minutes to hours. Smaller unit, bigger number.

✅ Before the test, can you…

  • Write a ratio three ways, in the order the words ask for?
  • Fill in a ratio table and spot the row that does not belong?
  • Solve a “ratio and a total” problem with a tape diagram?
  • Find a unit rate and use it to go up to any amount?
  • Pick the better buy and show the two unit prices?
  • Find a percent of a number, and the whole when you know a part and its percent?
