The Classroom: Nouns and Articles

Nouns

Walk into any classroom and you are surrounded by nouns: *el libro* (the book), *la silla* (the chair), el reloj (the clock), la pizarra (the board). In Spanish, every one of those nouns carries a gender, masculine or feminine, and a number, one or more than one. The little words in front of them, el, la, los and las, change to match. This unit shows you the patterns that let you pick the right one most of the time.

You will also learn the Spanish that gets you through a real school week. You will use hay to say what is in a room, necesito and tengo to talk about what you need and have, and the names of your classes. You will hear class commands like Abran los libros (Open your books) and learn the questions that rescue you when you are lost, like ¿Puede repetir, por favor? (Can you repeat, please?).

By the end, you will be able to label a whole classroom correctly, make nouns plural, fill out a supply list and ask for help without switching to English. Heritage speakers will find names for patterns they already hear every day. New learners will get the tools to sound clear and correct from the first day of class.

The big question

How do small words like el, la, hay and tengo help you describe and survive your school world in Spanish?

Gender and Number

Gender
Big questionHow can a word's ending tell you whether to say el or la, and how do you make it plural?
The story

A Room Full of Sticky Notes

Mateo's class labels every object in the room, and one tiny word keeps causing arguments.

On the second day of seventh grade, Señora Ortiz handed Mateo a stack of yellow sticky notes. "Label everything in this room in Spanish," she said. "Every label needs a little word in front of the noun." Mateo looked around at the desks, the windows, the map and the clock. He grabbed a marker and got to work.

His first note was easy. He stuck *la mesa* (the table) on the teacher's table. His friend Lupe wrote *el libro* (the book) and stuck it on a dictionary. Then Mateo wrote "la mapa" and put it on the map of Mexico. Lupe frowned. "My abuela says *el mapa* (the map)," she said. "It ends in -a, but it takes el."

Soon the whole class was arguing in whispers. Is it el or la for *la pared* (the wall)? What about *el lápiz* (the pencil)? Andrés counted the pencils in the cup and asked, "If there are twelve, is it los lápizes?" Señora Ortiz smiled. "Almost. Watch what happens to the z. It turns into a c: *los lápices*."

By lunch, the room was covered in yellow. There were 28 notes on 28 chairs, each one saying *la silla* (the chair). The clock said *el reloj*, and the door said *la puerta*. Señora Ortiz walked around with a red pen and fixed only three labels. "Spanish nouns follow patterns," she said. "Learn the patterns, and you can label almost anything. Learn the few exceptions, and you will sound like you grew up with the language."

Section 1

Every Noun Has a Gender

21.1

Masculine and Feminine Nouns

Main ideaEvery Spanish noun is either masculine or feminine, even nouns for objects like tables and books.

Picture the sticky notes in Mateo’s classroom. One says (the book). Another says (the table). A noun is a word for a person, place, thing or idea. In Spanish, every noun belongs to one of two groups. Grammar calls these groups masculine and feminine. This label is called the noun’s gender.

Gender in grammar is not about boys and girls. A table is not female, and a book is not male. Gender is just a family name for words. Masculine nouns team up with el, and feminine nouns team up with la. The group decides which small words and endings go with the noun.

English speakers often forget gender because English has only one word, the. Spanish has several. The best habit is to learn every noun with its article, like a first and last name. Do not learn mesa. Learn la mesa. Do not learn libro. Learn el libro. Then you never have to guess later.

Think it through
Two Families of Words

Masculine family: el libro (the book), el cuaderno (the notebook), el escritorio (the desk). Feminine family: la mesa (the table), la silla (the chair), la puerta (the door). Notice the little word in front. It tells you the family.

Say each noun out loud with its article three times. Which family has more nouns ending in -o?

21.2

The Four Words for The

Main ideaSpanish has four words for the: el, la, los and las, chosen by the noun's gender and number.

Mateo labeled one chair (the chair). Then Señora Ortiz asked him to label the whole row of chairs. He wrote las sillas (the chairs). The word for the changed with the noun. Words like el and la are called definite articles. They point to a specific thing, just like the in English.

Spanish picks the article by two facts. First, is the noun masculine or feminine? Second, is it one thing or more than one? That second fact is called number. One is singular, and more than one is plural. Put the two facts together and you get four choices: el for masculine singular, la for feminine singular, los for masculine plural and las for feminine plural.

Look at the pattern with real classroom nouns. El cuaderno becomes los cuadernos. La ventana (the window) becomes las ventanas. The article and the noun both change. A common mistake is to change only the noun, as in "el cuadernos." Spanish wants every word in the group to match.

Think it through
Sorting the Articles

masculine + one = el: el cuaderno. feminine + one = la: la ventana. masculine + more than one = los: los cuadernos. feminine + more than one = las: las ventanas. Two questions, four answers.

Make the plural with the right article: el escritorio, la puerta.

Words to know
el artículo definido
the definite article — el, la, los or las; it means the and points to a specific thing
la silla
the chair — a feminine noun; plural las sillas
la ventana
the window — a feminine noun; plural las ventanas
Check yourself

1. Which article goes with ventanas (windows)?

Why: Ventana is feminine, and ventanas is plural, so the article is las.

2. Which label has a mistake?

Why: Cuadernos is plural, so it needs los, not el. The article must match the noun's number.

3. What two facts decide which word for the you use?

Why: Spanish articles match the noun's gender (masculine or feminine) and number (singular or plural).
21.3

A, An and Some

Main ideaUn and una mean a or an, and unos and unas mean some; they match gender and number too.

Sometimes you do not mean a specific thing. Lupe said, "I need a pencil," not the pencil. Spanish uses (a, an) for masculine nouns and (a, an) for feminine nouns: un lápiz, una silla. These are called indefinite articles because they do not point to one exact item.

The plural forms are unos and unas. They mean some or a few. Unos libros means some books. Unas sillas means some chairs. So Spanish has four indefinite articles, the same way it has four definite ones. The same two questions decide which one you use: which gender, and how many?

English speakers sometimes use una with every noun because it sounds like the English word a. That makes "una libro," which is wrong. Check the gender first. Another mistake is "un sillas." Plural nouns need a plural article, so it must be unas sillas.

Think it through

Find every indefinite article in the dialogue. Is each noun masculine or feminine?

un

una
a or an — the indefinite article for a feminine singular noun: una silla

some — the plural indefinite articles: unos libros, unas sillas

Section 2

Endings That Predict Gender

21.4

The -o and -a Pattern

Main ideaMost nouns ending in -o are masculine, and most nouns ending in -a are feminine.

Look at the labels on the classroom wall: el cuaderno, el escritorio, el techo (the ceiling). All end in -o, and all take el. Now look at la puerta, la ventana, la pizarra (the board). All end in -a, and all take la. This is the biggest pattern in Spanish gender.

The rule is simple: a noun ending in -o is usually masculine, and a noun ending in -a is usually feminine. Usually is the key word. The pattern works for most nouns you will meet in class, so it is a safe first guess when you see a new word.

What about nouns that end in other letters, like (the clock) or (the wall)? The ending does not give a clear signal, so you must learn those with the article. That is why labeling with el or la is so useful. The sticky note teaches the gender every time you look at it.

Think it through
Guess the Gender

Here are new nouns from the room: basurero (trash can), carpeta (folder), piso (floor), goma (eraser). Step 1: look at the last letter. Step 2: -o means el, -a means la. So: el basurero, la carpeta, el piso, la goma.

Use the pattern: el or la for bandera (flag) and armario (cabinet)?

21.5

Endings in -ción and -dad

Main ideaNouns ending in -ción, -sión, -dad and -tad are feminine.

Señora Ortiz wrote two words on the board: (the lesson) and (the university). Both are feminine, and neither ends in -a. Some endings are even more reliable than -a. Nouns ending in -ción or -sión are feminine: la canción (the song), la televisión.

Nouns ending in -dad or -tad are feminine too: la ciudad (the city), la verdad (the truth), la libertad (freedom). Many of these words look like English words ending in -tion or -ty. Education is la educación. University is la universidad. That link helps you guess both meaning and gender.

Watch the accent when these words become plural. La lección has an accent on the ó. In las lecciones, the accent disappears, because the stress now falls in the normal place. The same thing happens with la canción and las canciones. The -dad words keep their spelling and add -es: las ciudades.

Think it through
English Cousins

21.6

El Problema and El Mapa

Main ideaMany nouns ending in -ma that come from Greek, plus el mapa, are masculine even though they end in -a.

Remember Mateo’s label "la mapa"? Lupe was right: it is (the map). A small group of nouns ends in -a but is masculine. Many of them end in -ma and came into Spanish from ancient Greek. The most common are (the problem), el tema (the topic), el sistema (the system), el idioma (the language), el programa and el clima (the climate).

You do not need to know Greek to spot these. Many look like English words ending in -m or -em: problem, system, program, theme. When a Spanish -ma word looks like one of those, use el. Their plurals use los: los problemas, los idiomas.

Be careful not to overuse this rule. Not every -ma word is masculine. La cama (the bed) and la forma (the shape) are feminine. The masculine -ma words are mostly longer, school-sounding words. When in doubt, learn the word with its article from a dictionary or a teacher.

Write labels with el or los for: programa, sistemas, clima.

Exceptions Worth Knowing

21.7

El Día and La Mano

Main ideaTwo common nouns break the pattern: el día is masculine, and la mano is feminine.

You already say buenos días (good morning) and (the day). Día ends in -a, but it is masculine. That is why it is buenos días, not buenas días. Every word that describes día must be masculine too: un día bonito (a nice day).

The opposite surprise is (the hand). It ends in -o, but it is feminine. When Señora Ortiz says "Levanten la mano" (Raise your hand), she uses la. The plural is las manos. You wash las manos, not los manos.

These two words are used every day, so mistakes with them stand out. A good trick is to memorize a whole phrase. Say buenos días and levanten la mano until they feel natural. The phrase carries the gender for you, so you do not have to think about the rule.

21.8

Nouns for People

Main ideaNouns for people often change their ending to match the person: el alumno, la alumna.

For people, gender often does match the person. A boy student is , and a girl student is la alumna. A male teacher is el maestro, and a female teacher is la maestra. The ending changes from -o to -a, and the article changes with it.

Some people nouns do not change their ending at all. Instead, only the article changes. is a male student, and la estudiante is a female student. The same is true for el artista and la artista. Words ending in -ista or -ante often work this way.

Nouns ending in a consonant, like profesor, usually add -a for a woman: el profesor, la profesora. When a group has both boys and girls, Spanish traditionally uses the masculine plural: los alumnos can mean a mixed class. You may also hear speakers say los alumnos y las alumnas to include everyone clearly.

Section 4

One Thing, Many Things

21.9

Add -s or -es

Main ideaAdd -s to nouns ending in a vowel and -es to nouns ending in a consonant.

Andrés counted the objects in the room to make plural labels. For el libro, he added -s: los libros. For la silla, he added -s: las sillas. When a noun ends in a vowel (a, e, i, o, u), you usually add -s. El estudiante becomes los estudiantes.

When a noun ends in a consonant, add -es. El papel (the paper) becomes los papeles. La pared becomes las paredes. El reloj becomes los relojes. The extra e makes the word easier to say. Try saying "papels" out loud in Spanish. It sounds wrong because Spanish words rarely end in two consonants like -ls.

Adding -es can move an accent. El examen (the test) has no written accent, but los exámenes needs one to keep the stress on the same syllable. Words that lose an accent, like lección, go the other way: las lecciones. When you make a plural, say it out loud and check where the stress falls.

21.10

When Z Turns to C

Main ideaA noun ending in -z changes the z to c before adding -es: el lápiz, los lápices.

Andrés wanted to write "los lápizes." Señora Ortiz showed him the fix. When a noun ends in -z, change the z to c, then add -es. becomes los lápices. (the light) becomes las luces. La vez (the time, as in one time) becomes las veces.

Why the change? Spanish spelling has a habit: it almost never writes z before e or i. It uses c instead, because ce and ci already make the same sound as z. You see the same habit in words like cena (dinner) and cien (one hundred).

Notice that lápiz keeps its accent in the plural: lápices. The stress stays on the first syllable, and the word now has three syllables, so the written accent still needs to be there. Many students drop it by mistake. Say LÁ-pi-ces out loud to hear where the stress falls.

Think it through
Three Steps to the Plural

Start: el lápiz. Step 1: the word ends in -z. Step 2: change z to c: lápic-. Step 3: add -es: lápices. Step 4: change the article: los lápices. Try it with la luz: luc- → luces → las luces.

Make the plural: el pez (the fish) and la nuez (the nut).

21.11

Making Every Word Match

Main ideaIn Spanish, the article, the noun and any describing word all match in gender and number.

At the end of the day, Señora Ortiz checked the labels. She wanted every word in a label to match. (the red backpack) is all feminine and singular. Los cuadernos rojos (the red notebooks) is all masculine and plural. This matching is called agreement.

Agreement is like a team jersey. If the noun is feminine plural, the article and the adjective wear the same colors: las mochilas rojas. Change one word, and the others must change too. Adjectives ending in -e, like grande (big), only change for number: la mesa grande, las mesas grandes.

English speakers often forget to change the adjective, writing "las mochilas rojo." Heritage speakers usually hear the right form, but may spell it the way it sounds. A simple checking habit helps: point to each word in the phrase and ask, masculine or feminine? One or more? Every answer should be the same.

Big questionWhat Spanish do you need to get through the first week of school, from your supply list to asking for help?
The story

The First Week at a New School

Sofía starts at a bilingual middle school and has one weekend to get ready.

Sofía's family had just moved from Aurora to Little Village in Chicago. On Friday, her new school sent home a supply list written in English and Spanish. Her grandmother, who speaks mostly Spanish, read it out loud at the kitchen table. "Necesitas tres cuadernos, una calculadora y dos carpetas," she said. (You need three notebooks, a calculator and two folders.)

On Saturday they went shopping. Sofía looked at the shelves and tried to name everything. "Hay muchas mochilas," she said. (There are a lot of backpacks.) Her grandmother laughed. "Sí, pero ya tienes una mochila." (Yes, but you already have a backpack.) Sofía checked the list again. She had a backpack and pencils. She still needed the calculator.

On Monday, her homeroom teacher, el señor Fuentes, spoke Spanish most of the morning. "Abran los libros en la página diez," he said. (Open your books to page ten.) Sofía understood abran and libros, but she missed the number. She took a deep breath and raised her hand. "¿Puede repetir, por favor?" (Can you repeat, please?)

El señor Fuentes smiled and said it again, more slowly. After class, he told Sofía, "Asking is the most important skill in any language." By Friday, Sofía knew the names of all her classes, the words on her supply list and five ways to ask for help. She also knew that her question had helped three other students who were too shy to ask.

There Is, There Are

22.1

One Word: Hay

Main ideaHay means both there is and there are, and it never changes form.

Walk into a classroom and look around. (There is a board). (There are twenty student desks). The same small word, hay, works for one thing or for many. It is pronounced like the English word eye. The h is silent.

English needs two forms, there is and there are. Spanish needs only one. That makes hay easy, but English speakers sometimes try to make it plural. They say "han" or "hayn" for there are. Those forms are wrong here. Hay stays hay: hay un reloj, hay tres relojes.

To say there is not, put no in front: No hay tiza (There is no chalk). Spanish does not need a word for any or no before the noun in this sentence. No hay papel means There is no paper or There isn’t any paper. The word order stays simple: no, then hay, then the thing.

Think it through
Sofía's Homeroom

Hay una pizarra blanca. (There is a whiteboard.) Hay veinticinco pupitres. (There are twenty-five desks.) Hay dos ventanas grandes. (There are two big windows.) No hay reloj. (There is no clock.) Sofía needs to ask what time it is!

Describe your own classroom in three sentences that start with Hay or No hay.

Words to know
hay
there is or there are — one form for one thing or many: hay un libro, hay dos libros
el pupitre
the student desk — plural los pupitres
no hay
there is not, there are not — no hay tiza, there is no chalk
Check yourself

1. How do you say There are three windows?

Why: Hay means both there is and there are, so it does not change for plural nouns.

2. What does No hay papel mean?

Why: No hay means there is not or there isn't any, so No hay papel means there is no paper.

3. How is hay pronounced?

Why: The h in Spanish is silent, and ay sounds like the English word eye.
22.2

Hay With Un, Una or Numbers

Main ideaAfter hay, use un, una, a number or no article, but not el or la.

Hay introduces something new. You are telling someone that a thing exists in a place. That is why hay goes with un or una, a number, or words like muchos (many): hay una computadora, hay muchos libros. These words introduce things the listener has not heard about yet.

A very common mistake is "Hay el libro en la mesa." That sentence mixes two ideas. If you mean a book, say Hay un libro en la mesa (There is a book on the table). If you mean the specific book you were talking about, Spanish uses a different verb, estar: El libro está en la mesa (The book is on the table).

Plural nouns after hay can also stand with no article at all. Hay lápices en la caja means There are pencils in the box. This is like English, where you can also say there are pencils without some. Just remember the rule of thumb: hay plus el or la is almost never right.

Think it through
Right or Wrong?

Hay una computadora en la mesa. ✓ (a computer) Hay muchas sillas. ✓ (many chairs) Hay lápices en la caja. ✓ (pencils, no article) Hay la computadora en la mesa. ✗ → La computadora está en la mesa. (The computer is on the table.)

Fix this sentence two ways: Hay el mapa en la pared.

Words to know
muchos, muchas
many, a lot of — hay muchos libros, hay muchas sillas
la computadora
the computer — feminine; some countries also say el ordenador
la caja
the box — feminine; hay lápices en la caja
Check yourself

1. Which sentence is correct?

Why: Hay introduces something new, so it goes with un, una or a number, not el or la.

2. You want to say the specific map is on the wall. Which sentence works?

Why: For a specific thing that is in a place, Spanish uses estar: El mapa está en la pared.

3. What does Hay muchas mochilas mean?

Why: Hay means there are, and muchas means many or a lot of.
22.3

Asking ¿Qué hay en…?

Main ideaAsk ¿Qué hay en…? to find out what is in a place, and ¿Cuántos hay? to ask how many.

Sofía’s grandmother opened the new backpack and asked, " la mochila?" (What is in the backpack?) Sofía answered, "Hay un cuaderno y dos lápices." The question uses qué (what) plus hay plus en (in or on) and a place. The answer starts with hay too.

To ask how many, use ¿Cuántos hay? for masculine nouns and ¿Cuántas hay? for feminine ones. ¿Cuántos libros hay? means How many books are there? ¿Cuántas sillas hay? means How many chairs are there? Notice the question word matches the noun, just like an adjective does.

Remember the upside-down question mark at the start: ¿. English speakers often forget it. Also remember the accent on qué and cuántos in questions. Without the accent, que and cuantos are different words. The accent tells the reader this is a real question word.

1. How do you ask How many chairs are there?

Why: Silla is feminine, so the question word is cuántas, and there are is hay.

Section 2

What I Need and What I Have

22.4

Necesito: I Need

Main ideaNecesito means I need; it can come before a noun or before a verb in its base form.

Sofía read her supply list and said, "Necesito una calculadora" (I need a calculator). Necesito comes from necesitar (to need), a regular -ar verb. Drop -ar and add endings: necesito (I need), necesitas (you need), necesita (he, she or you formal need). You can use these three forms right away.

Necesito can come before a noun: necesito un lápiz. It can also come before another verb in its base form, the infinitive, which ends in -ar, -er or -ir: Necesito estudiar (I need to study). Necesito ir al baño (I need to go to the bathroom). English adds to. Spanish does not.

A common mistake is "Necesito a estudiar," with an extra a, copied from English to. Leave it out. Another mistake is using the -o ending for everyone: "Tú necesito." Match the ending to the person. Tú necesitas, Sofía necesita.

Think it through
The Supply List Walk-Through

necesitar → drop -ar → necesit- → add the ending. yo necesito una calculadora (I need a calculator). tú necesitas dos carpetas (you need two folders). ella necesita estudiar (she needs to study). No extra word for to: necesito estudiar, not necesito a estudiar.

Tell a partner two things you need for school and one thing you need to do tonight.

Words to know
necesitar
to need — a regular -ar verb: necesito, necesitas, necesita
la calculadora
the calculator — feminine
el infinitivo
the infinitive — the base form of a verb, ending in -ar, -er or -ir: estudiar
Check yourself

1. How do you say I need to study?

Why: Necesito goes straight to the infinitive. Spanish does not add a word for to.

2. Which form fits: Tú ___ un bolígrafo.

Why: With tú, a regular -ar verb takes -as: tú necesitas.

3. What does Sofía necesita dos carpetas mean?

Why: Necesita means he or she needs, so the sentence means Sofía needs two folders.
22.5

Tengo: I Have

Main ideaTengo means I have; tener is irregular: tengo, tienes, tiene.

Sofía’s grandmother pointed at the backpack. "Ya una mochila." (You already have a backpack.) Sofía answered, "Sí, una mochila." (Yes, I have a backpack.) These forms come from tener (to have). Tener is irregular, which means it does not follow the normal pattern.

Look at the forms: tengo (I have), tienes (you have), tiene (he, she or you formal have), tenemos (we have), tienen (they or you all have). The yo form has a surprise g. In three forms the e becomes ie. Only tenemos keeps the plain e from tener. Spain also uses tenéis for you all.

Learners often say "teno" or "tieno" for I have. Both are wrong. Memorize tengo as a word by itself. You can also say what you do not have: No tengo calculadora (I don’t have a calculator). In negative sentences like this, speakers often drop un or una.

Think it through
Borrowing a Pen

Sofía: ¿Tienes un bolígrafo? (Do you have a pen?) Diego: Sí, tengo dos. (Yes, I have two.) Sofía: Gracias. Yo no tengo bolígrafo hoy. (Thanks. I don't have a pen today.) Diego: No hay problema. (No problem.)

Ask a classmate if they have three different school supplies, using ¿Tienes…?

Words to know
tener
to have — an irregular verb: tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tienen
tengo
I have — the yo form of tener, with a g: tengo un lápiz
tienes
you have (tú) — ¿Tienes un bolígrafo? Do you have a pen?
Check yourself

1. How do you say I have a notebook?

Why: Tener is irregular. The yo form is tengo, with a g.

2. Which form fits: Nosotros ___ clase de ciencias.

Why: The nosotros form keeps the plain e from tener: tenemos.

3. Diego says, Sí, tengo dos. What does he mean?

Why: Tengo means I have, so tengo dos means I have two.
22.6

The Supply List

Main ideaLearn school supplies with their articles so you can ask for, buy and borrow them.

Sofía’s supply list had eight items. Each one is a noun you can use with hay, necesito and tengo. (the notebook), la carpeta (the folder), (the pen), el lápiz (the pencil), la regla (the ruler), la goma (the eraser), las tijeras (the scissors) and la calculadora (the calculator).

Some supplies have more than one name across the Spanish-speaking world. A pen is el bolígrafo in many places and la pluma in Mexico. An eraser can be la goma or el borrador. None of these is wrong. Heritage speakers can use the word their family uses. In class, just know that several words may point to the same object.

Notice las tijeras. English says scissors, a plural word for one tool. Spanish also uses a plural here: Necesito las tijeras. When you shop, you might see a price tag. Prices below are only examples. If a notebook costs two dollars, three notebooks cost six dollars: tres cuadernos, seis dólares.

Section 3

Your Classes

22.7

School Subjects

Main ideaSchool subjects are nouns with articles, and many look like their English names.

On Monday, Sofía got her schedule. It listed her classes: (math), (history), (science), el inglés (English), el español (Spanish), el arte (art) and la educación física (P.E.). Many look like the English words, which helps you guess.

Look at the articles. Matemáticas and ciencias are plural in Spanish, even though math and science are singular in English. So you say las matemáticas and las ciencias. Languages like inglés and español are masculine and are not capitalized in Spanish. English capitalizes English. Spanish writes inglés with a small i.

To name a class, you can say la clase de plus the subject: la clase de historia (history class). Tengo clase de ciencias means I have science class. In that phrase, Spanish does not use an article after de. It is clase de arte, not clase de el arte.

las ciencias
science — plural in Spanish: la clase de ciencias
el inglés

Check yourself

1. Which is the correct way to write Spanish class in Spanish?

Why: Clase is feminine, and names of languages are not capitalized in Spanish: la clase de español.

22.8

When Is the Class?

Main ideaSay a la una or a las plus a number to tell what time a class starts.

Sofía’s friend Diego asked, "¿A qué hora es la clase de arte?" (What time is art class?) Sofía looked at her schedule. " dos." (At two.) To say at a time, use a las and the number: a las nueve (at nine), a las once (at eleven).

One o’clock is different, because una is singular. Say (at one), not a las una. This is the same agreement rule you learned for articles. Una is one hour, so the article is la. Every other hour is more than one, so the article is las.

You can add a subject to make a full answer: Tengo matemáticas a las ocho (I have math at eight). If a class starts at 8:30, add y media (and a half): a las ocho y media. A class from 8:30 to 9:15 lasts 45 minutes, so it is shorter than one hour.

Think it through
Comparing Schedules

Diego: ¿A qué hora es tu clase de historia? (What time is your history class?) Sofía: A la una. ¿Y tu clase de arte? Diego: A las dos y media. (At two thirty.) Sofía: ¡Yo también tengo arte a las dos y media! (I also have art at two thirty!)

Ask a partner what time two of their classes are, and answer about yours.

Words to know
¿A qué hora?
At what time? — ¿A qué hora es la clase?
a las
at (a time, two o'clock or later) — a las dos, a las once
a la una
at one o'clock — la because una is singular
Check yourself

1. How do you say at one o'clock?

Why: Una is singular, so you use la: a la una.

2. Sofía says, Tengo matemáticas a las ocho y media. When is her math class?

Why: A las ocho means at eight, and y media adds half an hour: 8:30.

3. Which question asks what time a class is?

Why: ¿A qué hora…? asks at what time something happens.
Section 4

Talking in Class

22.9

Commands You Will Hear

Main ideaTeachers give class commands in the ustedes form, which often ends in -en or -an.

El señor Fuentes said, " los libros" (Open your books), to the whole class. A command tells someone to do something. When a teacher talks to the whole group, the command uses the ustedes (you all) form. For -ar verbs, it usually ends in -en: (Listen), Miren (Look). For -er and -ir verbs, it ends in -an: Abran (Open), Escriban (Write), Lean (Read).

The endings look flipped. Escuchar is an -ar verb, but its command ends in -en. Abrir is an -ir verb, but its command ends in -an. This swap is a real pattern, not a mistake. You will learn why in later units. For now, learn these commands by ear and by action.

Some commands you will hear every day are Siéntense (Sit down), Levanten la mano (Raise your hand), Saquen una hoja de papel (Take out a sheet of paper) and Repitan (Repeat). The teacher may also speak to one student with a tú command: Abre el libro, Escucha. Both forms ask for the same action.

Think it through
Simon Says, Spanish Style

El señor Fuentes: Levanten la mano. (Everyone raises a hand.) El señor Fuentes: Siéntense. (Everyone sits.) El señor Fuentes: Saquen un lápiz. (Everyone takes out a pencil.) El señor Fuentes: Escriban su nombre. (Everyone writes their name.) Listen for the verb, then act it out.

Take turns giving three class commands to your group. Can they act each one out?

Words to know
abran
open (you all) — a class command from abrir: Abran los libros
escuchen
listen (you all) — a class command from escuchar
siéntense
sit down (you all) — a class command; the accent keeps the stress on sién
saquen
take out (you all) — from sacar: Saquen una hoja de papel
Check yourself

1. The teacher says Escuchen. What should the class do?

Why: Escuchen comes from escuchar, to listen. It is the ustedes command form.

2. Which command means Open the books, said to the whole class?

Why: Abran is the ustedes command of abrir, used for a whole group.

3. Why does escuchar give Escuchen, with an e?

Why: For ustedes commands, -ar verbs take -en and -er/-ir verbs take -an.
22.10

Asking for Help

Main ideaUse short, polite questions like ¿Puede repetir, por favor? to keep learning when you are lost.

When Sofía missed the page number, she asked, "" (Can you repeat, please?) Puede is the usted form of poder (to be able to), the polite way to talk to a teacher. You can also say Más despacio, por favor (More slowly, please) or No entiendo (I don’t understand).

When you know the English word but not the Spanish one, ask (How do you say…?). ¿Cómo se dice scissors en español? When you hear a Spanish word you do not know, ask ¿Qué significa…? (What does … mean?). ¿Qué significa tijeras?

Use usted, not tú, with most teachers. Saying "¿Puedes repetir?" to a teacher is understood, but it can sound too casual in many schools. Some students worry that asking questions makes them look lost. In fact, these questions show you are working in Spanish. They keep the conversation going instead of switching to English.

Think it through
Five Lifelines

1. ¿Puede repetir, por favor? (Can you repeat, please?) 2. Más despacio, por favor. (More slowly, please.) 3. No entiendo. (I don't understand.) 4. ¿Cómo se dice ___ en español? (How do you say ___ in Spanish?) 5. ¿Qué significa ___? (What does ___ mean?)

Pick the lifeline you would use if you know the word ruler in English but not in Spanish. Say the full question.

1. You know the word ruler in English but not in Spanish. What do you ask?

Why: ¿Cómo se dice…? asks how to say an English word in Spanish.
