Fair Start

Fairness

Every match has a start. In a judo room, players bow. In a wrestling room, they shake hands. In a boxing ring, a bell rings. In a school gym, a whistle blows.

Each signal has a history. It comes from a place and a people. Old paintings, books and records help us learn these stories.

In this unit, you will name the signals different rooms use. You will learn where they come from. You will tell a legend apart from a record.

The big question

How do people start a match fairly, and how do we learn where those ways came from?

Bow, Handshake, Whistle

Fairness
Big questionHow do different rooms show that a match is about to start?
The story

Two Rooms, One Minute

Two classes start at the same time, with two different signals.

It is nine o'clock. In the school gym, a PE teacher lifts a whistle. She blows one short sound. The children stop and look up. Class has begun.

Across town, a judo class starts at nine o'clock too. There is no whistle. The students kneel in a line on the mat. They bow to their teacher, and the teacher bows back.

Both rooms began at the same minute. One used a sound. One used a bow. Each signal tells people the same thing: now we start, and the rules are on.

People around the world start matches in many ways. Fencers salute. Wrestlers shake hands. Boxers wait for a bell. Each room has its own signal and its own history.

Talk about itWhat signal tells you that your class has started?

The Judo Bow

Main ideaIn judo, people bow when they enter the room and before a match.

Judo began in Tokyo, Japan. A teacher named Kano Jigoro started his school in 1882. He called the school the Kodokan.

In many judo schools, students when they step onto the mat. Two players bow to each other before a match. They bow again at the end.

In Japan, a bow is a common way to say hello and thank you. Judo schools kept this everyday sign of respect.

5.2

Sumo Starts Together

Main ideaIn sumo, there is no whistle; the two wrestlers start at the same moment.

Before a sumo match, wrestlers stand in the ring. Many toss a handful of salt. This custom comes from Shinto, an old religion of Japan. In Shinto, salt stands for making a place clean.

A in a bright robe watches. He holds a wooden fan. The two wrestlers crouch and face each other. They choose the moment to start together. This start is called the .

The Boxing Bell

Main ideaIn boxing, a bell starts and ends each round.

Boxing matches are cut into parts called . The Queensberry Rules of 1867 made each round three minutes long. A rest of one minute came between rounds.

A person called the timekeeper watches the clock. The timekeeper rings a . One ring starts the round. Another ring ends it. Everyone in the room can hear it.

From the source

The rounds are to be three minutes long, with one minute of rest between rounds.
The Queensberry Rules, rule 3, England, 1867 (paraphrased)

The story

A New Book on the Table

A teacher opens a book full of old pictures and old rules.

It is reading time. A teacher opens a big book on the rug. The first page shows a painting on a stone wall. Two wrestlers reach for each other.

The painting is about four thousand years old. It comes from a tomb in Egypt. The children lean in close. How can a picture last so long?

The next page shows an old rule book. Then a photo of a museum in Tokyo. Each page holds a clue about the past. The children count three kinds of clues.

History is like a puzzle. Pictures, books, records and places are the pieces. People who study history put the pieces together.

Kano Jigoro started judo in 1882. He was also a school teacher. He wrote many talks and articles about judo.

He wrote about two big ideas. One means best use of energy. The other means helping each other so all do well. Judo schools still teach these .

Kano named two ideas for judo: use your energy in the best way, and help each other so that everyone does well.
