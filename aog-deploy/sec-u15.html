Forgeries and Fakes

Forgeries

Some of the most famous secret society documents in the world were never secret and never real. A satire about a French emperor was rewritten into a hate forgery. Typed pages were slipped into a national library to invent a medieval order. An anonymous roll of film offered a top secret committee no agency had ever formed. A British television spoof about a plan to leave Earth was later repeated as a real exposé.

These fakes matter because people believed them, and some did great harm. The Protocols of the Elders of Zion was used to spread hatred of Jews for decades and was promoted by the Nazi state. In this unit you will put the people who were harmed, and the people who exposed the lies, at the center of the story.

You will also learn the historian's tools. You will compare texts in parallel columns, trace claims back to their origin, and test documents by provenance, material, anachronism and who gains. You will study one real find, the Chinon Parchment, to see that a surprising document can be genuine. By the end, you will be able to test a document yourself and explain your verdict with evidence.

The big question

How do we prove a document is forged or genuine, and why do some proven fakes keep spreading?

The Protocols: Anatomy of a Forgery

The Protocols
Big questionHow can a careful reader prove that a document is a forgery, and why can a proven lie keep doing harm?
The story

An Old Book in Constantinople

In 1921 a British reporter is handed a worn French book and finds the famous secret plan already inside it.

It is the summer of 1921 in Constantinople, the great city on the Bosporus now called Istanbul. Philip Graves is the correspondent there for The Times of London. The city is crowded with people who fled the Russian Revolution and civil war. Some of them bring stories. Some bring papers. One of them brings Graves a book.

The man is a Russian exile. In his articles Graves calls him only Mr. X. Historians later identified him as Mikhail Raslovlev. He hands over an old French book, worn from use, and says something strange. Read this, he tells Graves, and then read the Protocols of the Elders of Zion.

Graves knows the Protocols. It is a pamphlet that claims to be the secret minutes of Jewish leaders plotting to rule the world. It has spread across Europe and America since the Russian Revolution. Many readers treat it as proof of a hidden plot. Even in Britain, some serious people have asked whether it might be real.

Graves reads the French book. It is a satire from 1864, written by a lawyer named Maurice Joly to mock a French emperor. It does not mention Jews at all. Yet page after page, its ideas and images match the Protocols, often in the same order. Graves begins to set the two texts side by side. In August 1921 The Times prints what he found.

Talk about itGraves had a strong hunch, but he did not just announce it. Why was printing the two texts side by side more convincing than simply saying the Protocols were fake?
Section 1

From Satire to Forgery

29.1

Joly's Satire of 1864

Main ideaThe Protocols copied a French satire from 1864 that attacked Emperor Napoleon III and never mentioned Jews.

In 1864 a French lawyer named Maurice Joly published a book in Brussels, Belgium. He did not put his name on it. The title page said only that it was written by a contemporary, meaning someone living at the time. Its title in English is Dialogue in Hell Between Machiavelli and Montesquieu.

The book is a . A satire uses humor, irony or exaggeration to criticize real people or real power. Joly’s target was Napoleon III, the emperor of France. Joly believed the emperor kept a free-looking government on the surface while controlling it underneath. He could not safely say that openly, because France had strict , meaning government control over what could be printed.

So Joly wrote a , a text written as a conversation. Two dead thinkers argue in the afterlife. Montesquieu defends laws, liberty and a free press. Machiavelli explains, coldly and cleverly, how a modern ruler could control newspapers, courts and voters while pretending to respect them. Readers were meant to recognize Napoleon III in Machiavelli’s plans.

The French government did recognize him. Joly was identified, tried and sentenced in April 1865 to 18 months in prison. His book was seized and soon forgotten. Keep one fact in mind for the rest of this chapter: Joly’s satire is about a French emperor. It does not mention Jews at all.

29.2

A Forgery Appears in Russia

Main ideaThe Protocols was first printed in a St. Petersburg newspaper in 1903 and in a fuller book version in 1905.

Nearly forty years after Joly’s book, a new text appeared in the Russian Empire. In August and September of 1903, a St. Petersburg newspaper called Znamya, meaning The Banner, printed it in parts. The paper’s editor was Pavel Krushevan, a writer known for his hostility toward Jews. The text later became known as the Protocols of the Elders of Zion.

A here means the official minutes of a meeting. The text claims to be the secret minutes of Jewish leaders planning to take over the world. It is a : a document made to look like something it is not, in order to deceive. No such meeting took place, and no such leaders existed.

In 1905 a religious writer named Sergei Nilus printed a fuller version as the last chapter of a book of his. Nilus’s edition became the one most often translated. The timing matters. The Russian Empire was a place of deep , meaning hatred of or prejudice against Jews. In those years mobs carried out deadly attacks on Jewish communities, called pogroms. A forgery like this did not create hatred from nothing. It fed on fears that already existed and gave them the look of proof. That is why historians study not only who wrote a false document, but also who wanted to believe it.

29.3

Who Made It? An Open Question

Main ideaHistorians agree the Protocols is a forgery, but they still debate exactly who made it.

Students often ask who wrote the Protocols. The honest answer is that historians are not sure. There is a difference between two questions. Is the text a forgery? That question is settled. Who forged it? That question is still open, and historians argue about it using evidence.

The traditional view points to the Okhrana, the secret police of the Russian tsar, and its office in Paris. The historian Norman Cohn, in his 1967 book Warrant for Genocide, followed this view, which rests partly on witnesses who spoke at a trial in the 1930s. In 1999 a Russian scholar, Mikhail Lepekhin, named a writer called Mathieu Golovinski as the author. That claim is disputed.

Other historians look elsewhere. Cesare De Michelis, in a 2004 study, argued that the text came from Russian nationalist circles around 1902 and 1903. Scholars also agree that the forger borrowed from more than one source. One was an 1868 German novel by Hermann Goedsche, which included an invented scene of a secret meeting in a Prague cemetery.

This is a good example of how a works. A historian studies the past using evidence and says how sure each claim is. We can hold two ideas at once. We know the Protocols is fake because of what it copies. We do not yet know for certain whose hands did the copying. An open question about the author does not reopen the question of the forgery.

Section 2

Proving the Copying

29.4

The Times Prints the Proof

Main ideaIn August 1921 Philip Graves showed in The Times that the Protocols had been copied from Joly's satire.

Philip Graves was a careful British journalist based in Constantinople. After Mr. X gave him Joly’s book, Graves compared it with the Protocols line by line. He found that the Protocols had borrowed Joly’s arguments, images and order of ideas. The forger had changed the speaker. In Joly, it is Machiavelli advising a ruler. In the Protocols, it is the supposed Jewish elders.

The Times printed Graves’s findings in three articles on 16, 17 and 18 August 1921. The paper then reissued them as a pamphlet with a plain title: The Truth About the Protocols: A Literary Forgery. A is a fake that is built by copying and changing someone else’s writing.

This mattered because of who was speaking. The Times was one of the most respected newspapers in the world. The year before, some voices in Britain had asked whether the Protocols might be genuine. Now the same paper laid out the for readers to judge. Evidence is the facts or documents that show whether a claim is true. Graves did not ask readers to trust him. He showed them the two texts together. That choice turned a debate about opinions into a test anyone could check with their own eyes.

29.5

Reading in Parallel Columns

Main ideaSetting two texts side by side lets any reader see copied structure, wording and order.

Graves used a simple tool: . He printed a passage from Joly on one side and the matching passage from the Protocols on the other. Readers could move their eyes back and forth. They did not need to know French or Russian history. They only needed to compare.

What should a reader look for? First, the order of ideas. Two authors writing on their own might share one idea, but they will rarely share twenty ideas in the same order. Second, distinctive images, such as an unusual comparison or example. Third, small leftovers: details that made sense in the original but look odd in the copy.

This lesson uses an invented, harmless example so the method is clear. In the practice below, one text is an original school newsletter and the other copies it. You will not read the Protocols itself. That text was written to spread hatred. Historians who teach it show only short matching lines, and only to prove the copying. When a match is long, detailed and in the same order, chance is not a reasonable explanation. The newer text depends on the older one. That is called . It is one of the strongest kinds of proof a historian can offer.

Copy (a flyer found in April): The club is a small country. Its members are its citizens, its rules are its border, and the hose is its only road.

List three things the flyer kept from the newsletter. Which leftover detail gives the copy away, and why does it look odd in the new text?

29.6

What the Copying Proves

Main ideaBecause the Protocols copies a satire about a French emperor, it cannot be the real record of a Jewish meeting.

Proof of copying leads to a clear conclusion. Suppose the Protocols were real minutes of a secret meeting. Then the speakers’ words would be their own. But the words are Joly’s, written in 1864 to mock Napoleon III. A real meeting could not have produced a speech that already existed, word for word in places, in a French satire.

The copy also carries odd leftovers. Some are an , something placed in the wrong time. Scholars have noted passages that fit France under Napoleon III better than the world of 1903, because that is what Joly was writing about. A forger working fast did not clean every trace.

The deeper point is about blame. Joly’s satire was a warning about how any ruler could abuse power. The forger took that warning and pinned it on a whole people who had nothing to do with it. That is what made the forgery so dangerous. It turned a general fear of power into hatred of a specific group. Notice what the proof does not need. It does not depend on who the forger was or on anyone’s opinion of Jews. It rests on the texts themselves. Anyone can check it. That is why historians call the question closed.

Step 1: Joly's satire was printed in 1864 and attacks Napoleon III. Step 2: The Protocols first appeared in 1903. Step 3: Long parts of the Protocols match Joly in wording, images and order. Step 4: Therefore the Protocols depends on Joly.

Add a Step 5 that explains why the Protocols cannot be the real minutes of a secret meeting. Which step would a defender of the Protocols have to prove wrong?

29.7

The Bern Trial

Main ideaIn 1935 a Swiss court in Bern ruled that the Protocols were forgeries, and a 1937 appeal did not change that finding.

In the 1930s, pro-Nazi groups in Switzerland handed out copies of the Protocols. Swiss Jewish organizations went to court in Bern, the Swiss capital. They argued that the pamphlets broke a Bern law against trashy literature. The trial ran from October 1934 to May 1935, and witnesses testified about where the text came from.

On 14 May 1935, Judge Walter Meyer gave his ruling. He found the Protocols to be forgeries and called them trashy literature. Two of the five men charged, named Fischer and Schnell, were convicted. The other three were , meaning found not guilty.

The defendants made an , asking a higher court to review the case. On 1 November 1937 the appeals court overturned the convictions. Its reason was a point of law: it held that the trashy literature statute did not fit a political text like this one. The appeals court did not rule that the Protocols were genuine. Later promoters of the Protocols sometimes say the Bern verdict was overturned, as if that proved the text real. That is a misreading. A can be reversed because a law does not apply, while the facts found about a document still stand.

Section 3

How the Lie Spread

29.8

Henry Ford's Newspaper

Main ideaBeginning in 1920, Henry Ford's Dearborn Independent spread the Protocols to a huge audience in America and abroad.

Henry Ford was one of the most admired men in America. His Model T put millions of families on the road. He also owned a weekly newspaper, the Dearborn Independent, based in Dearborn, Michigan. On 22 May 1920 the paper began a series of attacks on Jews, and it kept them up in 91 more issues.

The series leaned heavily on the Protocols. It presented the forgery as a key to world events. The articles were then collected into books under the title The International Jew. The first volume appeared in November 1920. Ford’s fame gave the old Russian forgery an American voice and a huge new audience.

This is a lesson about , meaning information spread on purpose to shape what people believe, often by misleading them. Propaganda works better when a trusted figure spreads it. Many readers reasoned that a man as successful as Ford must know what he was talking about. His success in business had nothing to do with whether the Protocols were true. The International Jew was translated into at least 16 languages, according to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum. It traveled far beyond Michigan, including to Germany, where Nazi leaders praised it.

29.9

Ford's Apology of 1927

Main ideaA libel suit pushed Ford to sign a public apology in 1927, but the books his paper made kept spreading.

In the 1920s the Dearborn Independent also attacked Aaron Sapiro, a Jewish lawyer who organized farmers’ cooperatives. Sapiro sued Ford for , meaning printing false statements that damage someone’s reputation. The trial in 1927 ended in a mistrial. Before a new trial could begin, Ford decided to settle the matter.

Ford signed a public statement dated 30 June 1927. It was drafted by Louis Marshall, a leader of the American Jewish Committee, and Ford accepted it without changes. In it Ford apologized to Jewish people, said he withdrew the attacks, and said he had not known the details of what his paper printed. At the end of 1927 the paper stopped publishing.

Historians debate how sincere the apology was. Ford owned the paper, and many doubt he did not know what it said. Also, copies of The International Jew kept circulating abroad, especially in Germany. In 1938, on his 75th birthday, Ford accepted a high honor from Nazi Germany, the Grand Cross of the German Eagle.

A is a public statement taking back something said or printed. Ford’s retraction shows both the power and the limits of correcting the record. It was real and public. It did not recall the books already printed or the ideas already planted.

29.10

The Forgery in Nazi Germany

Main ideaNazi leaders used the Protocols as propaganda, helping build the hatred that led to the Holocaust.

In Germany after World War I, the Protocols found eager readers among people looking for someone to blame for defeat and hard times. According to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, the Nazi writer Alfred Rosenberg introduced Adolf Hitler to the text in the early 1920s. The Nazi party published at least 23 editions of it between 1919 and 1939.

Some German newspapers fought back. In 1924 the Frankfurter Zeitung, a respected paper, exposed the Protocols as a fraud. But after the Nazis took power in 1933, independent voices were silenced. The Protocols was even used in some German schools. Once they took power, they could print it with the full weight of the state behind it.

The forgery gave Nazi a false story of a hidden enemy. An ideology is a set of ideas that a movement uses to explain the world and justify its actions. The Nazi state used that story to justify taking away Jewish people’s rights, property and safety.

The end of that road was the , the murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during World War II. A forged document did not cause the Holocaust by itself. But historians see it as one of the lies that prepared many people to accept persecution. The people harmed, not the forgers, are the center of this history.

Section 4

Why It Still Matters

29.11

A Lie That Keeps Returning

Main ideaThough proven false for over a century, the Protocols still circulates, sometimes with the villain renamed.

In 1964 a subcommittee of the United States Senate Judiciary Committee printed a report on the Protocols. Its title called the text a fabricated historic document. Even at the height of the Cold War, members of Congress put on record that the Protocols was fake and traced how it had spread.

Yet the text never went away. It is still printed and still shared online in many languages today. Human rights groups and Holocaust museums continue to track it. A lie that has been disproved can keep moving if people pass it on without checking where it came from. That is called when it is shared by mistake, and when it is spread on purpose.

Sometimes the Protocols returns in a new costume. Some writers keep the text but say it is really about a different secret group, such as the Illuminati. The writer Bill Cooper reprinted it in his 1991 book Behold a Pale Horse, a case this course studies in Unit 17. Many of Cooper’s readers care about real government secrets, and some of those secrets are documented.

But renaming the villain does not change the text’s history. It is still Joly’s satire, rewritten by a forger to target Jews, and still the document the Nazis used. A reader can take real questions about secrecy seriously and still refuse to pass on a known forgery.

29.12

Answering a Forgery With Care

Main ideaThe best answers to a hate forgery are checking its source, showing the evidence, and remembering the people it harmed.

Graves, the Bern witnesses and the Frankfurter Zeitung reporters all did the same basic thing. They asked where the text came from and showed the answer. That is the heart of , the historian’s habit of asking who made a document, when, where, how and why, before trusting it.

Careful people also avoid spreading the harm. Teachers who study the Protocols show only short matching lines next to Joly, and only to prove the copying. They do not hand out the full text. Repeating a hateful claim, even to argue with it, can plant it in new minds. Pointing to the evidence is safer than repeating the lie.

Center the people who were harmed. Jewish families in Russia, Germany and elsewhere lived with the fear and violence this text helped stir up. Honor also the people who stood up: Graves and his source, the Swiss Jewish groups who went to court, Louis Marshall and Aaron Sapiro in America, and the German reporters of 1924.

Finally, learn the pattern. Many forgeries follow the same shape: a secret meeting, a hidden group, a plan to rule the world, and no way to check the source. When you meet that shape, you can slow down, ask the source questions, and look for the original. That is a skill, and it grows with practice.

Pages Planted in a Library

Main ideaThe Dossiers Secrets were typed pages deposited in France's national library in 1967 to make an invented order look historic.

How Historians Test a Document

30.9

Four Tests for Any Document

Main ideaHistorians test a document by its provenance, its physical materials, its anachronisms, and who gains from it.

Historians use a set of tests on any document that seems too good to be true. The first is provenance. Where did this come from, and who has held it? A letter kept in a family’s papers for 150 years, then given to an archive, has strong provenance. A roll of film from an unknown sender has almost none.

The second test is the material itself. Paper, parchment, ink and typewriter type all change over time. Experts can often date a page by what it is made of and how its letters were printed. Checking whether a document is genuine is called . The MJ-12 papers could not be tested this way, because only photographs existed.

The third test looks for an , something placed in the wrong time. A word, a title, a date format or an object that did not yet exist can expose a forgery at once. Forgers know the big facts of the past, but they often slip on the small ones.

The fourth test asks who gains. Who benefits if people believe this? Plantard gained a royal family tree. Promoters gained sales and attention. A benefit does not prove a document false, but it tells you where to look harder. Together, these tests turn a feeling of doubt into evidence.

12. Why did many viewers miss that Alternative 3 was a spoof?

Why: A labor dispute moved it from 1 April to 20 June 1977, so the April Fools' context was lost.

13. A reader finds ten books about the Priory of Sion, and all trace back to the 1967 papers. What is this called?

Why: Many sources that all rest on one original claim are an example of circular sourcing.

14. How does the Chinon Parchment show that a surprising document can be real?

Why: Unlike the planted papers, the parchment passes provenance, material and consistency tests.

15. A post shares a passage from the Protocols but says it is about the Illuminati. What should a careful reader conclude?

Why: Renaming the villain does not change where the text came from or what it was made to do.
