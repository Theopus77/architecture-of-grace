America on the Move

1860–1920

In 1860, most Americans lived on farms, and much of the West belonged to Native nations. By 1920, railroads crossed the continent, more Americans lived in cities than on farms, and the United States ruled lands across the Pacific and the Caribbean. Few times in the nation's history brought so much change so fast.

This unit follows the people who lived through that change. You will meet cowboys, homesteaders and Exodusters, and Plains nations fighting to keep their homelands. You will meet inventors, business giants, factory workers and millions of immigrants. You will see Chicago burn and rise again, and visit Jane Addams at Hull House. Then you will meet the reformers who tried to fix what fast growth had broken, from muckraking reporters to the women who won the vote.

As you read, notice who gained from all this growth and who paid the price. Some people found land, jobs and freedom. Others lost their homes, their rights or their lives. Americans in this era argued hard about what government should do to make the country fair, and those arguments still shape our lives today.

The big question

How did growth in the West, in industry and overseas change who Americans were, and who paid the price for that growth?

Big questionWho gained and who lost as the United States spread across the West after the Civil War?
The story

The Buffalo Nearly Vanish

Tens of millions of bison once roamed the Plains. By 1890, only a few hundred were left in the wild.

Picture a sea of brown backs moving across the grass. The herd is so big that it takes days to pass by. The ground shakes under their hooves. For thousands of years, scenes like this were normal on the Great Plains. Tens of millions of American bison, often called buffalo, lived there. No one counted them, but they were one of the largest herds of animals on Earth.

For Plains nations such as the Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche and Kiowa, the buffalo meant life. People ate the meat fresh or dried it for winter. Hides became tipi covers, robes and moccasins. Bones became tools. Horns became cups and spoons. Nothing was wasted. The buffalo also held a sacred place in songs, stories and ceremonies.

Then, in just a few decades, the herds almost disappeared. Railroads cut across the Plains in the 1860s and 1870s. Hunters rode the trains west and shot bison by the thousands. In the 1870s, factories in the East learned to turn buffalo hides into tough leather, so a single hide could be sold for cash. Hunters often skinned the animals and left the meat to rot. Some army leaders were glad to see the herds go. They knew that without the buffalo, Plains nations could not keep their way of life.

By about 1890, only a few hundred wild bison were left. Piles of bleached bones lay across the prairie. Settlers gathered them and sold them by the ton to be ground into fertilizer. A few people began to worry. William Hornaday, a zoo scientist, counted the survivors and warned that the animal could soon be gone forever. A small wild herd hung on in Yellowstone National Park, and a handful of ranchers kept small herds of their own.

Slowly, people worked to bring the bison back. In 1905, a group called the American Bison Society formed, with Theodore Roosevelt as its honorary president. In 1907, bison raised at the Bronx Zoo in New York were sent by train to a preserve in Oklahoma. Over the next century, herds grew on ranches, in parks and on Native lands. Many tribes now raise buffalo again as part of their culture. In 2014, bison returned to Nachusa Grasslands in northern Illinois.

Today, the United States has hundreds of thousands of bison, and in 2016 the bison became the national mammal. Still, they are not the endless herds of the past. The story of the buffalo is the story of the West in small: great wealth and growth for some, and terrible loss for others.

Gold, Rails and Cattle

18.1

Mining Booms and Boomtowns

Main ideaDiscoveries of gold and silver drew thousands of people west and created towns that grew fast and often died just as fast.

The California Gold Rush of 1849 was only the beginning. In 1858 and 1859, gold was found near Pikes Peak in Colorado. In 1859, miners in Nevada struck a huge deposit of silver and gold called the . News spread fast. Thousands of fortune seekers rushed to the mountains, hoping to strike it rich.

Near each strike, a popped up almost overnight. Virginia City, Nevada, grew from a few tents into a busy city with hotels, theaters and newspapers. Merchants, cooks, laundry workers and saloon owners often made more money than the miners did. Women ran boardinghouses and restaurants. Chinese, Mexican, Irish and Cornish miners all worked in the camps, though Chinese and Mexican miners often faced unfair taxes and violence.

Most single miners never got rich. The easy gold near the surface ran out quickly. Digging deep into rock took costly machines, so big mining companies took over. When the ore was gone, many towns emptied out and became . Mining also brought settlers onto Native lands, which led to conflict across the West.

18.2

Rails Across the Continent

Main ideaThe first transcontinental railroad, finished in 1869, tied East and West together and was built largely by Chinese and Irish workers.

In 1862, during the Civil War, Congress passed the Pacific Railway Act. It gave two companies land and loans to build a railroad across the country. The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built east. The Union Pacific started in Omaha, Nebraska, and built west. This line would be the first railroad, meaning it crossed the whole continent.

The Central Pacific had to cut through the steep Sierra Nevada mountains. It hired thousands of Chinese , who soon made up most of its workers. They blasted tunnels through solid granite and worked through deep snow. They were paid less than white workers and had to buy their own food. Many died in accidents and avalanches. On the Union Pacific, many workers were Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans.

On May 10, 1869, the two lines met at Promontory Summit in Utah. Leaders tapped in a ceremonial golden spike, and a telegraph message told the nation the job was done. A trip across the country that once took months by wagon now took about a week. More railroads followed. They brought settlers, carried cattle and crops to market, and changed the West forever.

18.3

Cattle Drives and Cowboys

Main ideaAfter the Civil War, cowboys of many backgrounds drove Texas cattle north to railroad towns, until fences and hard winters ended the open range.

After the Civil War, millions of longhorn cattle roamed Texas. A steer worth a few dollars in Texas could sell for much more in the North, where cities wanted beef. The answer was the . Cowboys herded cattle hundreds of miles north to railroad towns in Kansas, such as Abilene. From there, trains carried the cattle to places like the Union Stock Yards in Chicago.

The cowboy’s skills came from Mexican , who had herded cattle for generations. Words like lasso, rodeo and chaps come from Spanish. Cowboys were a mixed group. Many were Mexican or Mexican American. Historians estimate that perhaps one in four was Black, many of them formerly enslaved. Nat Love, a Black cowboy, later wrote a book about his life on the trail.

A drive could take two or three months. Cowboys faced storms, stampedes and river crossings for low pay. The era of long drives lasted only about twenty years. Railroads reached farther into Texas. Farmers fenced their land with , a cheap fence invented by Joseph Glidden of DeKalb, Illinois, in 1874. Then the terrible winter of 1886–1887 killed huge numbers of cattle, and the open range came to an end.

Section 2

Farming the Great Plains

18.4

Homesteads on the Prairie

Main ideaThe Homestead Act offered free land, but families who farmed the Plains faced a hard, lonely life with few trees and harsh weather.

In 1862, Congress passed the . It offered 160 acres of public land to a settler who would live on it and farm it for five years. The settler paid only a small filing fee. Men, women who headed households, and immigrants who planned to become citizens could all apply. Hundreds of thousands of families, including many from Germany, Norway and Sweden, moved onto the Plains.

The Plains had few trees, so there was little wood for houses. Many settlers cut blocks of thick, grassy soil called sod and stacked them like bricks. These stayed warm in winter and cool in summer, but dirt fell from the ceiling, and snakes and mice sometimes came through the walls. Families burned dried buffalo or cow chips for fuel.

Life was hard. Blizzards, droughts, prairie fires and swarms of grasshoppers could ruin a year’s work. Women cooked, sewed, tended gardens, cared for animals and nursed the sick, often miles from the nearest neighbor. Children hauled water and herded animals. New tools helped: steel plows cut the tough sod, and windmills pumped water from deep underground. Still, many homesteaders gave up and moved away.

18.5

The Exodusters

Main ideaThousands of Black Southerners moved to Kansas in the late 1870s, seeking land, safety and freedom after Reconstruction ended.

When Reconstruction ended in 1877, life grew more dangerous for Black families in the South. Many were trapped in debt as sharecroppers. White groups used violence to keep them from voting. Many people began to look west for a fresh start. Kansas stood out. It had been a free state, and it had land for homesteading.

In 1879, thousands of Black Southerners traveled to Kansas. They called themselves , after the Book of Exodus in the Bible, which tells how the Israelites escaped slavery in Egypt. Some came by steamboat up the Mississippi River. Others walked. Benjamin "Pap" Singleton, a formerly enslaved man from Tennessee, encouraged the move and helped start Black settlements.

One all-Black town, Nicodemus, Kansas, was founded in 1877. Its settlers lived in dugouts and sod houses at first. Farming was hard, and many arrived with almost nothing. Still, the Exodusters built churches, schools and farms. Their showed that freedpeople were willing to risk everything to own land and live with dignity.

18.6

Farmers Fight Back

Main ideaFarmers facing debt, low prices and high railroad rates joined together in the Grange and later the Populist Party.

By the 1870s, many farmers were in trouble. They grew more crops than ever, so prices fell. Many had borrowed money for land and machines and could not pay it back. Railroads charged high rates to ship their crops, and farmers often had only one railroad to choose from. Farmers felt that banks and railroads held all the power.

Farmers began to organize. The , founded in 1867, started as a social club for farm families. It soon pushed states to pass laws limiting railroad and grain storage rates. In the case Munn v. Illinois in 1877, the Supreme Court ruled that a state could regulate businesses that served the public, such as grain warehouses in Chicago.

In 1892, farmers and workers formed the People’s Party, also called the . Populists wanted the government to own the railroads, create an income tax, let voters elect senators directly, and coin more silver money so that farmers could pay off debts more easily. In 1896, Democrat William Jennings Bryan, who was born in Salem, Illinois, ran for president with Populist support. He lost to William McKinley, but many Populist ideas later became law.

Section 3

Plains Nations Defend Their Homelands

18.7

The People of the Plains

Main ideaPlains nations built their lives around the horse and the buffalo, and the loss of the herds and their land broke their way of life.

Many nations lived on the Great Plains, including the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche, Kiowa, Crow and Pawnee. Some farmed along rivers. Others moved with the seasons, following the buffalo. After Spanish horses spread north in the 1600s and 1700s, many Plains peoples became skilled riders and hunters.

The buffalo gave Plains families food, clothing, shelter and tools. Plains nations also had their own governments, religions and ways of sharing land. As settlers, miners and railroads moved west, the U.S. government signed treaties with many nations. Treaties promised land that would be theirs forever. Again and again, the promises were broken when settlers wanted the land.

The government pushed Plains nations onto , areas of land set aside for them. Reservations were often small and poor for farming. Government food and supplies often came late or not at all. When the buffalo herds were destroyed in the 1870s and 1880s, Plains nations lost their main source of food. Many felt they had no choice but to fight or to move onto reservations.

18.8

Sand Creek and Little Bighorn

Main ideaBroken treaties led to bloody wars, including the Sand Creek Massacre in 1864 and the Lakota and Cheyenne victory at Little Bighorn in 1876.

In November 1864, a camp of Cheyenne and Arapaho people was resting at Sand Creek in Colorado Territory. Their leaders believed they were at peace with the United States. One chief, Black Kettle, flew an American flag over his lodge. Colorado soldiers led by John Chivington attacked anyway. They killed more than 150 people, most of them women, children and elders. A later government investigation condemned the attack. It is now known as the Sand Creek .

In 1868, the Treaty of Fort Laramie promised the Black Hills to the Lakota. The Black Hills were sacred to them. But in 1874, an army expedition led by George Armstrong Custer reported gold there. Miners poured in, and the government pressured the Lakota to give up the land. Lakota and Cheyenne people who refused to move to reservations gathered around leaders such as Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse.

On June 25, 1876, Custer’s soldiers attacked a large village near the Little Bighorn River in Montana. Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors fought back and killed Custer and all the soldiers with him. It was a great victory for the Plains nations, but it did not last. The army sent more troops, and within about a year most of the fighters had been forced onto reservations. The government took the Black Hills.

18.9

Chief Joseph and the Nez Perce

Main ideaIn 1877, the Nez Perce tried to escape to Canada rather than lose their homeland, but they were stopped just short of the border.

The Nez Perce lived in the valleys of what are now Oregon, Idaho and Washington. For years they were at peace with Americans. They even helped Lewis and Clark in 1805. But settlers wanted their land. In 1877, the government ordered one band of Nez Perce, led in part by a young chief named Joseph, to leave the Wallowa Valley and move to a small reservation.

Fighting broke out after a few young Nez Perce men killed some settlers. Fearing punishment for the whole band, about 800 Nez Perce fled. For more than three months, they traveled over 1,000 miles through mountains, heading for Canada. Families, elders and children made the journey, along with their horses. Again and again they held off the soldiers who chased them.

In October 1877, the army caught up with them in Montana, only about 40 miles from the Canadian border. Cold, hungry and exhausted, Joseph and his people surrendered. They had been promised they could go home, but instead they were sent to Kansas and then to Indian Territory, where many died of disease. Joseph spent the rest of his life asking the government to let his people return to their homeland.

The Frontier Closes

18.10

Schools and Land Allotment

Main ideaBoarding schools and the Dawes Act tried to erase Native cultures and took away most of the land Native nations still held.

By the 1880s, many reformers in the East said they wanted to help Native Americans. But their plan was , which meant making Native people give up their own ways and live like white Americans. The government and churches built boarding schools, often far from reservations. Children were sent there, sometimes by force.

The most famous was the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, opened in 1879. Students had their hair cut and their clothes replaced with uniforms. They were given English names and punished for speaking their own languages. Many were deeply lonely, and some died of disease far from home. Still, many students held on to their identity, and some later became leaders for their nations.

In 1887, Congress passed the . It broke up reservation land into separate plots, usually 160 acres for each head of a family. Land left over was sold to white settlers. Supporters said owning farms would help Native families. Instead, much of the land ended up in outside hands. Native landholdings fell from about 138 million acres in 1887 to about 48 million acres by 1934.

18.11

Wounded Knee and the End of the Frontier

Main ideaThe killing of Lakota people at Wounded Knee in 1890 ended the wars on the Plains, the same year officials said the frontier was gone.

By 1890, life on the reservations was hard. Food was short, and the old ways were forbidden. Many Lakota joined a new religious movement called the . Believers danced and prayed for a time when the buffalo would return and the settlers would leave. Government agents feared the Ghost Dance would lead to an uprising.

In December 1890, reservation police killed Sitting Bull while trying to arrest him. Soon after, soldiers stopped a band of Lakota at Wounded Knee Creek in South Dakota. On December 29, as soldiers took their weapons, a shot went off. The soldiers opened fire. Hundreds of Lakota men, women and children were killed. The Wounded Knee Massacre marked the end of the armed fight for the Plains.

That same year, the U.S. Census reported that there was no longer a clear line, a border between settled and unsettled land. In 1893, at a meeting in Chicago, historian Frederick Jackson Turner argued that the frontier had shaped the American character. He said it made Americans independent and democratic. Later historians pointed out that his idea left out the Native nations who had lived on that "free land" all along.

Industrialization and Immigration

1860–1914
Big questionHow did factories, big business and millions of newcomers change the lives of ordinary Americans?
The story

A House on Halsted Street

In 1889, two young women moved into an old mansion in one of Chicago's poorest neighborhoods and opened its doors to everyone.

In 1889, the neighborhood around Halsted Street on Chicago's Near West Side was crowded and noisy. Families from Italy, Greece, Russia, Poland, Ireland, Germany and many other places squeezed into wooden buildings. Streets were muddy, and garbage piled up in the alleys. Many parents worked long hours in factories and sweatshops. Their children often worked too.

That year, Jane Addams and her friend Ellen Gates Starr rented part of an old mansion in the middle of this neighborhood. It had been built years before by a man named Charles Hull, so they called it Hull House. Addams had grown up in Cedarville, Illinois, in a comfortable family. She wanted to do more than give money to the poor. She wanted to live among her neighbors and learn what they needed.

Hull House grew quickly. It opened a kindergarten and a day nursery, so working mothers had a safe place for their young children. It offered classes in English, cooking, art and music. It had clubs for boys and girls, a gym, a library and public baths. It helped build one of Chicago's early public playgrounds. Neighbors came to lectures, plays and concerts. Immigrants could keep their own traditions while learning about their new country.

Saving the Wild Places

A nation that had spent a century taming the land began to ask a new question: which places should be kept wild forever?

In 1870, a group of explorers rode into a strange land in the Rocky Mountains. They saw hot springs in bright colors, boiling mud pots and a geyser that shot water high into the air, again and again, like clockwork. They named that geyser Old Faithful. The explorers worried that private owners would fence in these wonders and charge people to see them.

In 1872, Congress set aside Yellowstone, in the Rocky Mountains of Wyoming Territory, as a public park "for the benefit and enjoyment of the people." President Ulysses S. Grant signed the law. Yellowstone became the first national park in the world. The land was not empty, though. Native peoples, including the Shoshone, Crow and others, had hunted and gathered there for thousands of years. Over time they were pushed out of the park.

Out in California, a Scottish-born writer named John Muir fell in love with the Sierra Nevada mountains and Yosemite Valley. He wrote about giant sequoia trees and granite cliffs as if they were holy places. His writing helped convince Congress to create Yosemite National Park in 1890. In 1892, Muir helped start the Sierra Club to protect wild lands. Around 1900, Black soldiers of the U.S. Army, known as Buffalo Soldiers, patrolled Yosemite and Sequoia to stop illegal grazing and logging. In 1903, Captain Charles Young became the first Black leader of a national park.

That same year, President Theodore Roosevelt went camping with Muir in Yosemite. They slept under the stars and talked about saving forests. Roosevelt had loved the outdoors since he was a sickly boy. As president, he protected about 230 million acres of public land as national forests, parks, wildlife refuges and monuments. In 1906, he used a new law, the Antiquities Act, to make Devils Tower in Wyoming the first national monument.

By 1916, the country had many national parks and monuments, but no one agency ran them. That year, President Woodrow Wilson signed a law creating the National Park Service. Its job was to protect the parks and let people enjoy them, now and in the future. Today, there are more than 60 national parks. Their story raises a question people still debate: how should we balance using the land with saving it?

Main ideaProgressives changed state and national government so that ordinary voters, not party bosses, had more control.

Progressives believed that party bosses and big businesses had too much control over government. Their answer was to give voters more direct power. One step was the secret ballot, so voters could choose in private without being pressured. Another was the , an election in which voters, not party leaders, choose a party’s candidates.

Wisconsin, led by Governor Robert La Follette, became a model of Progressive reform. Other states, such as Oregon, adopted three new tools. The lets citizens propose a law by gathering signatures. The lets voters approve or reject a law directly. The lets voters remove an elected official before the term ends.

Before 1913, U.S. senators were chosen by state legislatures, which party bosses and wealthy interests could sway. The , ratified in 1913, let voters in each state elect their senators directly. Progressives also worked to improve city government by hiring trained experts to run services like water and garbage collection.

20.4

Roosevelt's Square Deal

Main ideaTheodore Roosevelt used the power of the presidency to control big business and give workers and consumers what he called a Square Deal.

In 1901, President William McKinley was shot and killed. Vice President Theodore Roosevelt, only 42 years old, became the youngest president in the nation’s history. Roosevelt was full of energy. He believed the president should act boldly for the public good. He promised Americans a , meaning fair treatment for business owners, workers and consumers alike.

Roosevelt did not think all big businesses were bad. But he believed "bad trusts" that harmed the public should be broken up. In 1904, the Supreme Court agreed with his government that a giant railroad company, Northern Securities, broke the Sherman Antitrust Act. Roosevelt’s government brought dozens of lawsuits against trusts. People called him a .

In 1902, coal miners in Pennsylvania went on strike. As winter came, people feared they would have no coal to heat their homes. Past presidents had sent troops to break strikes. Roosevelt instead brought both sides to the White House and pushed them to accept a settlement. Miners won higher pay and shorter hours. It was the first time a president stepped in as a neutral judge in a labor dispute.

Main ideaRoosevelt and other conservationists protected millions of acres, but Americans disagreed about whether to use natural resources wisely or leave wild places untouched.

By 1900, loggers had cut down huge forests, and hunters had wiped out or nearly wiped out several kinds of animals. Roosevelt believed natural resources belonged to the whole nation, including future generations. This idea is called . In 1905, the U.S. Forest Service was created, led by Gifford Pinchot.

Pinchot and John Muir were both conservationists, but they disagreed. Pinchot believed in "wise use." Forests, rivers and grasslands should be used for timber, water and grazing, but carefully, so they would last. Muir believed in . Some wild places should be left untouched, protected for their beauty and for nature itself.

Their biggest fight was over Hetch Hetchy, a valley inside Yosemite National Park. The growing city of San Francisco wanted to dam the valley to supply water. Muir fought hard to save it. Pinchot supported the dam. In 1913, Congress approved it, and the valley was flooded. The debate between using land and preserving it continues today.

Taft, Wilson and New Laws

Main ideaPresidents Taft and Wilson continued Progressive reform, adding an income tax, the Federal Reserve and stronger rules for business.

William Howard Taft, Roosevelt’s chosen successor, became president in 1909. Taft actually brought more antitrust lawsuits than Roosevelt did. In 1911, the Supreme Court broke up Standard Oil. But Taft sided with more conservative Republicans on some issues, and Roosevelt grew angry. In 1912, Roosevelt ran against Taft as the candidate of a new Progressive Party, nicknamed the Bull Moose Party.

The split among Republicans helped Democrat Woodrow Wilson win. Wilson called his plan the New Freedom. In 1913, the allowed Congress to collect an income tax. That same year, Congress created the , a system of banks that manages the nation’s money supply. In 1914, the Clayton Antitrust Act strengthened the rules against monopolies and protected unions’ right to strike, and the Federal Trade Commission was set up to stop unfair business practices.

Progressive reform had limits. Many Progressives cared little about the rights of Black Americans. Wilson allowed several federal departments to segregate their Black and white workers. Black leaders who had hoped for fair treatment protested strongly.

Votes for Women

20.7

A Long Road to the Ballot

Main ideaWomen fought for the right to vote for more than 70 years, winning first in western states and in Illinois before the national victory.

The fight for woman , or women’s right to vote, began at Seneca Falls, New York, in 1848. After the Civil War, many suffragists were angry that the 15th Amendment protected Black men’s voting rights but said nothing about women. In 1869, Susan B. Anthony and Elizabeth Cady Stanton formed a group to push for a constitutional amendment.

In 1872, Susan B. Anthony voted in the presidential election in Rochester, New York. She was arrested, tried and fined $100. She refused to pay. Anthony traveled the country giving speeches for decades. She died in 1906, before women won the vote nationally, but the 19th Amendment would later be nicknamed the Susan B. Anthony Amendment.

Change came first in the West. Wyoming Territory gave women the vote in 1869. By 1914, most western states had followed. In 1913, Illinois let women vote for president and in many local elections, the first state east of the Mississippi River to do so. That year, Ida B. Wells founded the Alpha Suffrage Club in Chicago to help Black women use their new votes.

20.8

Winning the 19th Amendment

Main ideaUsing both quiet persuasion and bold protest, suffragists won the 19th Amendment in 1920, though many women of color still faced barriers to voting.

By the 1910s, suffragists used two different plans. Carrie Chapman Catt led the National American Woman Suffrage Association. Her "Winning Plan" worked state by state while also pressing Congress. She believed in patient, polite persuasion. Alice Paul, a younger leader, chose bolder action. Her group, later called the National Woman’s Party, held parades and to get attention.

In 1917, Alice Paul’s group began picketing outside the White House. The women, called the Silent Sentinels, held signs asking President Wilson how he could fight for democracy abroad while women at home could not vote. Many were arrested and jailed. When some went on a hunger strike, guards force-fed them. News of this treatment won the suffragists public sympathy.

Women’s work during World War I also strengthened their case. Wilson came to support suffrage. Congress passed the in 1919, and it was ratified in August 1920, when Tennessee became the 36th state to approve it. Still, many Black women in the South were kept from voting by Jim Crow laws. Many Native American women could not vote because they were not yet treated as citizens.

Reaching Across the Oceans

20.9

Why America Looked Outward

Main ideaIn the late 1800s, the United States began to gain lands overseas, including Alaska and Hawaii, in a push called imperialism.

European nations were racing to build by taking control of lands in Africa and Asia. This practice is called . Many American leaders wanted the United States to join the race. Businesses wanted new markets for their goods. Naval officer Alfred Thayer Mahan argued that a great nation needed a strong navy and bases around the world. Some Americans also believed, wrongly, that their culture was better than others and should be spread.

In 1867, Secretary of State William Seward bought Alaska from Russia for $7.2 million. Critics mocked the deal as "Seward’s Folly." Their view changed when gold and other riches were found there. Alaska later became a state in 1959.

Hawaii was an independent kingdom. American planters grew rich there growing sugar. In 1893, the planters overthrew Queen Liliuokalani with help from U.S. Marines. The queen gave up her power under protest to avoid bloodshed. The United States annexed Hawaii in 1898. Many Native Hawaiians opposed the takeover, and in 1993 Congress formally apologized for the overthrow.

War with Spain

Main ideaIn 1898, the United States fought a short war with Spain that began over Cuba and ended with the U.S. gaining Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines.

Cuba, an island just 90 miles from Florida, was a Spanish colony. In 1895, Cubans rose up to fight for independence. Spain fought back harshly, forcing many Cubans into crowded camps where many died. American newspapers printed shocking and sometimes exaggerated stories. This style of sensational reporting was called .

In February 1898, the U.S. battleship Maine exploded in the harbor of Havana, Cuba, and more than 250 sailors died. The cause was never proven, but many Americans blamed Spain. "Remember the Maine!" became a rallying cry. In April 1898, the United States declared war.

The war lasted only a few months. Commodore George Dewey’s ships destroyed a Spanish fleet in Manila Bay in the Philippines. In Cuba, Theodore Roosevelt’s volunteer Rough Riders and Black soldiers of the 9th and 10th Cavalry fought at San Juan Hill. In the peace treaty, Spain gave the United States Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines. Cuba became independent, but the U.S. kept the right to step into its affairs.

The Philippines and the Debate over Empire

Main ideaWhen the U.S. took the Philippines, Filipinos fought for independence, and Americans argued over whether their country should rule other peoples.

Filipinos had been fighting Spain for their independence. Their leader, Emilio Aguinaldo, expected freedom when Spain lost. Instead, the United States took control of the islands. In 1899, war broke out between Filipino fighters and American troops. The lasted about three years of major fighting and cost many more lives than the war with Spain, most of them Filipino.

At home, Americans argued. Supporters of the new empire said the islands would help trade and the navy. Some said Americans had a duty to govern the Filipinos. The strongly disagreed. Its members included writer Mark Twain and Jane Addams of Hull House. They said ruling people without their consent went against the Declaration of Independence.

The United States ruled the Philippines for decades. It built schools and roads, but Filipinos were not free to govern themselves. The Philippines finally became independent in 1946. Puerto Rico and Guam remain U.S. territories today. People born in Puerto Rico have been U.S. citizens since 1917.

A Canal and a Big Stick

Main ideaTheodore Roosevelt helped Panama break from Colombia, built the Panama Canal, and claimed the right for the U.S. to police Latin America.

Ships going from New York to San Francisco had to sail all the way around South America. A canal across Central America would save weeks. The best route crossed Panama, which was then part of Colombia. When Colombia refused Roosevelt’s offer, Panamanian rebels declared independence in 1903. U.S. warships helped keep Colombia from stopping them. The new nation quickly gave the United States the right to build a canal.

Building the Panama Canal took ten years, from 1904 to 1914. Workers dug through mountains and jungle. Diseases spread by mosquitoes, yellow fever and malaria, had killed thousands during an earlier French attempt. Dr. William Gorgas led a campaign to drain swamps and kill mosquitoes, which saved many lives. Tens of thousands of workers, many from Barbados, Jamaica and other Caribbean islands, did the hardest and most dangerous jobs. Thousands died.

Roosevelt believed the United States should be a strong power in its own region. In 1904, he announced the to the Monroe Doctrine. It said the U.S. could step in to Latin American countries that could not keep order or pay their debts. The U.S. later sent troops to several Caribbean and Central American countries. Many Latin Americans resented this .
