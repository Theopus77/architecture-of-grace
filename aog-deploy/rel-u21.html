Traditions

A free meal served on the floor of a gurdwara in Palatine. A Jain monk sweeping the road before each step. A babalawo in Nigeria reciting verses he learned over many years. A crowd in white on a beach in Brazil, sending flowers out to sea. An Ojibwe grandmother in Chicago telling a story only in winter. This unit studies traditions that are smaller in numbers than the ones earlier in the course, but that shape the lives of millions of people and have left clear marks on Illinois and the wider world.

The first chapter studies Sikhism and Jainism, two traditions born in India. Each turns a central conviction into daily practice: for Sikhs, one God and the equality of all people, seen in the langar and the five Ks; for Jains, ahimsa, non-harm to every living being. The second chapter turns to traditions that keep what is sacred without a single book: Yoruba religion in West Africa, its descendants Santeria and Candomble in the Americas, and Native American traditions, including those of the Anishinaabe peoples of the Great Lakes.

By the end of the unit you will be able to explain the key terms and practices of each tradition, attribute beliefs to the people who hold them, compare how different communities answer the same question, and describe how laws in the United States have both suppressed and protected religious practice. You will also practice a skill this course keeps returning to: studying a tradition respectfully, including knowing when some knowledge is not yours to take.

The big question

How do communities without a large following, or without a single sacred book, carry their deepest convictions into daily life and pass them to the next generation?

The story

The Kitchen With No Head of the Table

In a gurdwara kitchen northwest of Chicago, a retired engineer and a first-year student peel the same pile of onions, and no one asks who is more important.

It is a Sunday morning in Palatine, Illinois. Inside a large white building topped with domes, a kitchen is already loud. Steam rises from pots of lentils big enough to bathe in. A retired engineer in a blue turban stirs one pot with a paddle. Beside him a first-year college student, her head covered with a scarf, peels onions and blinks back tears. Neither of them was assigned this job. They came early because the work needed doing.

Down the hall, in a wide room with a carpeted floor, people are taking their places for the meal. There are no chairs. Everyone sits on the floor in long rows, shoulder to shoulder: a doctor next to a bus driver, a grandmother next to a teenager in a hoodie, a visitor who has never been inside a gurdwara next to a man who has come every week for forty years. Volunteers walk the rows with buckets and ladles, serving flatbread, lentils, vegetables and rice pudding to anyone who holds out a plate.

This meal is called the *langar*, and it has been served this way for about five hundred years. Sikh tradition traces it to Guru Nanak, who taught that a person's worth does not depend on caste, wealth, gender or religion. Sitting in a row on the floor to eat the same food makes that teaching visible. In Sikh understanding, you cannot claim to be above the person beside you while sharing a plate of lentils on the same carpet.

The Golden Temple in Amritsar, India, serves this meal to tens of thousands of people every day, and no one is charged and no one is turned away. Smaller gurdwaras across the Chicago area do the same on a smaller scale. The food is plain and vegetarian so that people of every faith and diet can eat it. In this unit, that kitchen is a good place to begin, because it shows a belief becoming a practice that anyone can watch, join or study.

Later the chapter moves to a very different community, the Jains of India, whose central conviction is *ahimsa*, non-harm toward every living thing. Jains also turn a belief into daily choices, about what to eat, what to wear, and even how to walk. Two traditions, two convictions, and in each case a practice you could recognize from across a room.

Talk about itThe langar puts a teaching about equality into a seating arrangement. What is one other practice, from any tradition or none, where an idea about people is built into how a room or a meal is set up?

Guru Nanak and the Ten Gurus

41.1

Nanak's Journey and Message

Main ideaSikh tradition begins with Guru Nanak, who taught devotion to one God and rejected divisions between people.

Sikh tradition tells that Nanak was born in 1469 in the village of Talwandi in the Punjab, a region that today lies on both sides of the India–Pakistan border. His family was Hindu, and his neighbors included many Muslims. According to the traditional accounts called the , at about age thirty Nanak went to bathe in a river and disappeared for three days. When he returned, tradition says, his first words were that there is no Hindu and no Muslim. Sikhs understand this to mean that God is not the property of any one group and that the labels people fight over do not matter to God.

After that experience Nanak spent roughly two decades traveling. Sikh sources describe journeys east to Bengal, south toward Sri Lanka, north into the Himalayas and west to Mecca and Baghdad. On these trips, tradition says, he sang his teachings and debated with religious leaders. A Muslim musician named Mardana traveled with him and played a stringed instrument called the rabab while Nanak sang. This pairing of a Hindu-born teacher and a Muslim companion has remained an important image for Sikhs.

Around 1520 Nanak settled at Kartarpur, on the banks of the Ravi River, and founded a community. Followers farmed, worked and worshiped together, and they came to be called , a Punjabi word meaning learners or disciples. Nanak is called , which in Sikh usage means the teacher who carries God’s word to people. Sikhs believe that the Guru’s role is not to be worshiped but to point the learner toward the one God.

Scholars note that Nanak lived at a time when devotional movements were flourishing in North India. Poets such as Kabir taught love of God beyond caste and ritual, and Sufi Muslim teachers sang of God in the local languages. Sikhs hold that Nanak’s message was a direct revelation, not a blend of other faiths, and the academic study of religion describes both the setting and the community’s own understanding without choosing between them.

41.2

Ten Gurus, One Light

Main ideaSikhs believe the same divine light passed through ten human Gurus, from Nanak in 1469 to Gobind Singh, who died in 1708.

Before his death in 1539, Guru Nanak chose a follower named Lehna as his successor and renamed him Angad, a word Sikhs connect to the idea of a limb of one’s own body. Sikhs believe that the Guruship then passed from person to person, ten Gurus in all, and that each carried the same light. A common Sikh image is one candle lighting the next: the flame is the same even though the candle changes. For this reason Sikhs speak of the Gurus with the same reverence and often refer to them together as a single Guru.

Several Gurus shaped the community’s institutions. The third Guru, Amar Das, made the langar meal a fixed practice and asked visitors to eat in it before meeting him. The fourth, Ram Das, founded the city of Amritsar. The fifth, Arjan, built the Harmandir Sahib, known in English as the Golden Temple, at the center of a pool in Amritsar and compiled the community’s scripture in 1604. In 1606 Guru Arjan died in the custody of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, and Sikhs remember him as the first Sikh martyr.

After Arjan’s death, the sixth Guru, Hargobind, began to wear two swords, which Sikhs interpret as and , worldly authority and spiritual authority. The ninth Guru, Tegh Bahadur, was executed in Delhi in 1675 on the orders of the emperor Aurangzeb. Sikh tradition holds that he died defending the right of Hindus in Kashmir to practice their religion. Sikhs point to this as an example of standing up for the freedom of others.

The tenth Guru, Gobind Singh, was Tegh Bahadur’s son and became Guru at age nine. In 1699 he founded the Khalsa, a community of initiated Sikhs, which a later lesson describes. Before he died in 1708, Sikh tradition says he declared that there would be no more human Gurus and that the scripture itself would serve as Guru from then on. Historians also note that all four of Gobind Singh’s sons died in the conflicts of his lifetime, a loss Sikhs remember in prayer.

41.3

The Scripture That Is the Guru

Main ideaSikhs treat the Guru Granth Sahib as their living Guru, a book of hymns that is honored, sung and consulted rather than simply read.

Walk into any gurdwara and you will see the same thing at the front of the main hall: a large book resting on cushions under a canopy, with an attendant waving a whisk over it. That book is the , and for Sikhs it is not a symbol of the Guru but the Guru itself. Sikhs believe that in 1708 Guru Gobind Singh ended the line of human Gurus and gave that role to the scripture. People bow before it, sit lower than it, and carry it to a bed each night in a ceremony of rest.

The standard printed edition has 1,430 pages, and every copy uses the same page layout so that a hymn is on the same page everywhere in the world. The text is written in , a script the second Guru is credited with shaping, though the languages inside include Punjabi, Hindi, Persian and others. Almost the whole book is poetry set to music. It is organized by , the musical modes of Indian tradition, rather than by topic, because it was meant to be sung.

The Guru Granth Sahib contains the hymns of six of the Sikh Gurus. It also includes the compositions of Hindu and Muslim poets, such as Kabir, Namdev and the Sufi teacher Farid. Sikhs call these poets the . Sikhs see this as a sign that truth is not confined to one community. The book opens with the Mul Mantar, a short statement of who God is. It names God as one, true, creator, without fear or enmity, beyond time, not born, self-existent, and known by the Guru’s grace.

Because the scripture is the Guru, Sikhs do not have priests in the sense of a separate class with special authority over the text. Any Sikh, man or woman, who can read Gurmukhi may lead a service. A reader called a cares for the book and reads from it, and a continuous reading of the whole text from start to finish, called an Akhand Path, takes about forty-eight hours and marks important occasions. At the end of a service, the book is opened at random and the hymn on that page is read as the day’s guidance.

One God, Equal People

41.4

Ik Onkar and the Three Duties

Main ideaSikh teaching centers on remembering the one God while working honestly and sharing with others.

The first symbol in the Guru Granth Sahib, and the one you will see on Sikh flags and doorways, is . It combines the numeral one with a letter and is usually translated as There is one God, or One Reality is. For Sikhs it states the heart of the faith: God is one, without form, present everywhere, the same for every people. Sikhs use many names for God, including Waheguru, Wonderful Teacher, but hold that no image can represent God and that no place or people has an exclusive claim.

According to the Gurus, the main obstacle between a person and God is , a Punjabi word for self-centeredness, the habit of putting I and mine at the center of everything. The remedy Sikhs practice is , remembering God’s name. This can mean repeating a name of God quietly, singing the hymns, or holding God in mind during ordinary work. Sikhs believe that a life shaped by this remembrance slowly loosens the grip of ego and brings a person into harmony with God’s will, called .

Sikhs commonly summarize the Gurus’ teaching in three duties, often given in Punjabi: naam japna, remembering God; kirat karni, earning an honest living; and , sharing what you earn with those in need. Notice that two of the three are about ordinary economic life. Sikh tradition praises the householder who works and raises a family over the ascetic who withdraws from the world. A story told about Guru Nanak has him choosing a poor carpenter’s plain bread over a rich man’s feast because the carpenter’s bread was earned honestly.

Sikhs, like Hindus and Buddhists, speak of karma and rebirth, and they believe the human birth is a rare chance to unite with God. But the Sikh account of liberation is centered on grace. Sikhs hold that a person cannot earn union with God by effort alone; the Guru’s grace opens the way. Compare this with the Christian language of grace, or with the Jain emphasis on individual effort in a later lesson. That comparison is one of the useful exercises of this unit.

41.5

Seva and the Langar

Main ideaSikhs express the equality of all people through seva, selfless service, most visibly in the free langar meal.

Return to the kitchen from the opening story. The people cooking, cleaning and serving there are performing , selfless service done without expecting reward. Sikhs regard seva as a form of worship, not a chore attached to it. Sweeping the floor of a gurdwara, polishing the shoes of visitors, or washing the enormous pots after the meal are all seva. Many Sikhs describe the experience as a way of wearing down haumai, the ego, by putting oneself at the service of others.

The is the free community kitchen and the meal it serves. Sikh tradition credits Guru Nanak with beginning it and the third Guru, Amar Das, with making it a firm rule. He is said to have required that everyone, including the Mughal emperor Akbar when he visited, sit in the rows and eat before meeting him. The rows themselves, called , are the point. In sixteenth-century India, people of different castes did not eat together, and a high-caste person could be considered polluted by sharing food with a low-caste one. The langar deliberately broke that rule.

The Gurus also addressed the position of women. Guru Nanak wrote a hymn asking why anyone should call woman inferior when kings are born of women. Sikh teaching holds that women and men are equally able to read the scripture, lead worship, and receive initiation into the Khalsa. Historians point out that practice in Sikh communities has not always matched this ideal, as in every tradition, and Sikh reformers today often quote these hymns when calling for change.

The scale of the langar can be startling. The Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar serves free meals to tens of thousands of people each day, prepared largely by volunteers. During disasters, Sikh organizations have set up mobile langars for flood victims and, in 2020, for people affected by the pandemic in cities around the world, including Chicago. Studying the langar is a way to see how a teaching about God’s view of people becomes a rule about seating, a menu, and a schedule of volunteers.

The Khalsa and the Five Ks

Main ideaIn 1699 Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa, whose initiated members wear five visible signs of their commitment.

Sikh tradition tells that on the spring festival of Vaisakhi in 1699, at Anandpur in the Punjab hills, Guru Gobind Singh stood before a large gathering with a drawn sword and asked who was willing to give his head for the faith. One man stepped forward and was led into a tent. The Guru returned alone with a bloodied sword and asked again. Four more came forward in turn. Then, the story says, all five walked out of the tent alive and dressed in new robes. Sikhs call them the , the Five Beloved Ones. They came from different castes and different parts of India.

The Guru then prepared , sweetened water stirred with a double-edged sword, gave it to the five, and then asked them to give it to him. Sikhs point to this moment as the founding of the , a word meaning pure or belonging directly to God. Members took a new name: men became Singh, lion, and women became Kaur, princess. Because these names replaced caste surnames, they made it harder to tell a person’s social rank, which Sikhs understand as part of the point. Today many Sikhs carry these names whether or not they have been initiated.

Initiated Sikhs, called , maintain five articles of faith whose Punjabi names all begin with K: , uncut hair, usually kept under a turban by men and by some women; kangha, a small wooden comb that keeps the hair neat; kara, a steel bracelet worn on the wrist; kachera, cotton undershorts; and , a small sword. Sikhs explain them in various ways. The kara, for instance, is often described as a reminder of God’s oneness and of the wearer’s duty to act rightly with the hand that wears it. The kirpan represents the duty to defend the weak.

Not every Sikh is initiated, and Sikhs disagree among themselves about how strictly the five Ks must be kept. A student in a Chicago classroom might wear a turban and a kara, only a kara, or no visible sign at all, and still identify as Sikh. In the United States the kirpan has raised questions in schools and airports, and courts and school districts have often allowed small, secured kirpans as an exercise of religious freedom. Studying the five Ks is a case of the general question this course asks: how visible signs carry a community’s memory and commitments.

The Gurdwara and Sikhs in America

41.7

Inside a Gurdwara

Main ideaA gurdwara is any place where the Guru Granth Sahib is installed, and its layout teaches openness and equality.

The Sikh house of worship is a , a word meaning door of the Guru. Any building where the Guru Granth Sahib is properly installed can be one, from a room in a house to the Harmandir Sahib. Most gurdwaras fly a tall flag called the , usually saffron or blue, marked with the khanda, a symbol of a double-edged sword, a circle and two curved swords. The Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar famously has four doors, one on each side, which Sikhs describe as a welcome to people from every direction and every background.

Visitors of any faith may enter a gurdwara. The protocol is the same for everyone: remove your shoes, cover your head, and do not bring tobacco or alcohol. In the main hall, called the diwan hall, worshipers walk to the front, bow to the Guru Granth Sahib, often leave an offering, and sit on the carpeted floor. Men and women usually sit on opposite sides, though this is custom rather than scripture. There are no statues or images of God, in keeping with the Sikh belief that God has no form.

The heart of a Sikh service is , the singing of hymns from the scripture, usually to harmonium and tabla drums. A speaker may explain a hymn, and the service closes with a standing prayer called the Ardas, which recalls the Gurus and the martyrs and asks for the good of all humanity. Then the book is opened at random and a hymn is read aloud as guidance. Everyone receives , a warm sweet made of flour, clarified butter and sugar, handed out from a single bowl so that no one receives anything different from anyone else.

The gurdwara is also a school, a community center and a kitchen. Children learn Punjabi and Gurmukhi there on weekends. Weddings, called Anand Karaj, take place in front of the Guru Granth Sahib, and the couple walks around the scripture four times while a hymn is sung. Sikh calendars mark the birthdays of Guru Nanak and Guru Gobind Singh, Vaisakhi in April, and the martyrdom days of Guru Arjan and Guru Tegh Bahadur, all observed with kirtan and langar.

Mahavira, the Conqueror

Main ideaJains follow the teaching of Mahavira, the last of twenty-four Tirthankaras, who taught a path of self-conquest and non-harm.

In the same century and the same region of northeastern India where tradition places the Buddha, another teacher left home to seek liberation. Jains call him , Great Hero. His given name was Vardhamana, and tradition says he was born to a ruling family near the city of Vaishali, in what is now the Indian state of Bihar. Traditional Jain dates place his life at 599 to 527 BCE; many historians put him somewhat later, in the fifth century BCE. Jains do not regard him as the founder of their tradition but as its most recent great teacher.

According to Jain tradition, Mahavira was the twenty-fourth , a word meaning ford-maker, one who builds a crossing over the river of birth and death so others can follow. The twenty-third, Parshvanatha, is placed by tradition about 250 years earlier, and most historians accept that he was a real teacher. Jains hold that the tradition itself is eternal and that Tirthankaras appear in every age to restore it. This is why a Jain would object to the sentence Jainism was founded by Mahavira.

Tradition says that at age thirty Mahavira gave up his home, his wealth and eventually his clothing and spent about twelve years wandering, fasting and meditating, often enduring abuse without responding. At the end of that time Jains believe he attained , complete knowledge, becoming a , a conqueror. The word Jain means a follower of the Jina. What Mahavira conquered, in Jain understanding, was not an enemy but his own attachments and passions. He taught for about thirty years and organized a community of monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen, and Jains note that nuns outnumbered monks from the beginning.

Jains believe Mahavira died at Pavapuri, in Bihar, and attained final liberation. The Buddhist scriptures mention him under the name Nigantha Nataputta as a rival teacher, which gives historians an outside witness to his existence. His teaching survives in scriptures called the , written in an ancient language called Ardhamagadhi, though Jains disagree about which texts are authentic, as a later lesson explains.

Ahimsa and the Five Vows

Main ideaJain life is organized around ahimsa, non-harm toward every living being, expressed in five vows kept strictly by monastics and more moderately by laypeople.

A Jain monk in western India walks slowly along a road at dawn, sweeping the ground ahead of him with a soft brush of wool before each step. He wears a cloth over his mouth. He will not eat after sunset, and he will not eat root vegetables at all. Each of these habits follows from one principle: , non-harm. The brush moves insects out of his path. The cloth keeps him from swallowing tiny creatures. Cooking after dark might burn insects drawn to the flame. Pulling up a root kills the whole plant and the small lives in the soil around it.

Jains take ahimsa further than any other major tradition because of how they understand life. According to Jain teaching, every living being has a , a soul, and souls dwell not only in animals but in plants, and in the tiniest single-sensed beings of earth, water, fire and air. Harm to any of them attaches karma to the soul, as the next lesson explains. A Jain scripture, the Acharanga Sutra, teaches that no living creature should be killed, treated with violence, abused, tormented or driven away, and calls this the pure and eternal law taught by those who know.

The first of the five Jain vows is ahimsa. The other four are satya, truthfulness; asteya, not taking what is not given; brahmacharya, chastity; and , non-possession or non-attachment. Monks and nuns take these as the , the great vows, in their full form: they own almost nothing, do not travel by vehicle, and depend on food offered by laypeople. Laypeople take the same five as anuvratas, lesser vows, adapted to family and working life: a strict vegetarian diet, honesty in business, faithfulness in marriage, and limits on what they accumulate.

Jains are divided into two main branches that agree on these vows but differ in practice. monks, whose name means sky-clad, wear no clothing as the fullest expression of non-possession, and this branch holds that women must be reborn as men before final liberation. monks and nuns, white-clad, wear simple white robes, and this branch holds that women can reach liberation and that the nineteenth Tirthankara was a woman. The two branches also disagree about which scriptures are authentic. Both are ancient, and both are active today.

Karma as Matter, and Many Viewpoints

Main ideaJains understand karma as fine matter that clings to the soul, and they teach that no single viewpoint captures the whole truth.

Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs all use the word karma, but Jains mean something unusually specific by it. In Jain teaching, is a kind of extremely fine physical matter that fills the universe. Every action driven by passion, especially anger, pride, deceit and greed, draws karmic particles to the soul, where they stick like dust to a wet cloth. The karma clouds the soul’s natural qualities, which Jains describe as infinite knowledge, perception, bliss and energy, and it determines the soul’s next birth.

The Jain path is therefore a two-part program. First, stop new karma from coming in, which Jains call ; the five vows do this by removing the passions that attract it. Second, burn off the karma already there, which Jains call ; fasting, meditation and patient endurance of hardship do this. When all karma is gone, Jains believe the soul rises to the top of the universe. There it dwells forever in perfect knowledge and bliss as a , a liberated being. Jains do not believe in a creator God. The universe, in Jain teaching, has always existed. The Tirthankaras and siddhas are revered as examples, not as gods who grant favors.

One consequence of this teaching is a strong emphasis on individual effort. Where Sikhs speak of the Guru’s grace, and Christians of God’s grace, Jains hold that no one else can remove your karma for you. Jain temples are full of images of Tirthankaras seated in meditation, and worshipers offer rice, flowers and lamps before them, but Jain teachers explain that the Tirthankara does not respond; the worship shapes the worshiper. The annual festival of Paryushana, lasting eight or ten days, ends with each Jain asking forgiveness from everyone they may have harmed, with the words micchami dukkadam, may my wrongdoing be forgiven.

Jain philosophy is also known for , the doctrine of many-sidedness: reality is complex, and any single statement about it captures only part of the truth. Jain teachers illustrate this with the story of blind men describing an elephant, each touching one part and each partly right. From this follows a habit of saying in some respect before a claim. Modern Jains often present anekantavada as intellectual ahimsa, non-violence toward other viewpoints. It is a useful idea for a course that asks students to compare traditions without ranking them.

Then she tells the story of how the land was made after a great flood. She tells it slowly, with pauses and with small jokes that make the students laugh. Written down, it becomes something else, a thing that can be copied, sold or quoted without the relationship that gave it meaning. You may write down what you learned from it, she says, but not the story itself. Come back next winter, and I will tell it again.

42.1

Story, Song and Ceremony as Scripture

Main ideaIn many Indigenous traditions, sacred knowledge is carried by story, song, ceremony and land rather than by one written book.

Most of this course has studied traditions through their texts: the Torah, the Gospels, the Qur’an, the Vedas, the Guru Granth Sahib. That method works well when a community has a written canon. It fits less well for many traditions, the religious ways of peoples who lived in a place before colonizers arrived and who maintain a distinct relationship with that land. For most of their history, the Yoruba of West Africa and the Native nations of North America kept sacred knowledge without a single book.

In an , teaching is passed from person to person by speaking, singing and doing. A creation story is told by an elder. A song is learned by singing it beside someone who knows it. A ceremony is learned by attending it for years, first watching and then helping. Oral does not mean vague or careless. Yoruba diviners memorize hundreds of long verses, and Native singers learn song cycles that must be performed exactly. Scholars who study oral cultures note that communities often build in checks, such as correction by other elders, that keep the core stable.

Because the knowledge lives in practice, scholars often use the word where they might use scripture elsewhere. A ceremony is a formal religious action, with set words, objects, times and roles, that renews a relationship with the sacred. For many Native nations, the land itself also carries meaning. A mountain, a river or a lake can be the place where a story happened, where ancestors are buried, or where a ceremony must be held. Moving the people away from that land breaks more than a home.

Two cautions guide this chapter. First, there is no single Indigenous religion. There are more than 570 federally recognized tribes in the United States alone, and many hundreds of African peoples, each with its own language and ways. Second, many members of these communities are also Christian or Muslim, or combine practices. The chapter describes selected traditions carefully and attributes each belief to the people who hold it.

Main ideaSome sacred knowledge belongs to particular people, seasons or ceremonies, and outsiders who study it are expected to follow the community's protocol.

The elder in the opening story was following , the rules a community keeps about how sacred things are shared. Protocol can govern when a story is told, as with Anishinaabe winter stories. It can govern who may tell it, such as a member of a certain clan or someone who has been taught and given permission. It can govern who may see a ceremony, whether photographs may be taken, and which objects may be touched. Visitors to a gurdwara cover their heads; visitors to a Native ceremony may be asked to leave their phones outside.

Some knowledge is , meant only for those who have been initiated or who hold a particular role. This is not unique to Indigenous traditions. Many religions have teachings or rites that only the initiated take part in. But restricted Indigenous knowledge has often been taken without permission. Researchers in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries recorded ceremonies, published sacred songs and collected sacred objects for museums, sometimes by purchase and sometimes by theft or from graves.

The United States addressed part of this history in the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990, known as . The law requires museums and federal agencies that receive federal money to inventory Native human remains, burial objects and sacred objects, and to return them to the tribes they came from when a claim is established. Museums in Illinois, like museums across the country, have been working through these returns for decades, and the process continues.

For a student, the lesson is practical. 42.6

Faith Carried Across the Atlantic

Main ideaEnslaved Africans carried their religious traditions to the Americas, where they survived by adapting under harsh conditions.

Between the 1500s and the 1860s, the transatlantic slave trade carried roughly 12.5 million Africans onto ships bound for the Americas, and about 10.7 million survived the crossing, according to the major historical database of slave voyages. Most went to Brazil and the Caribbean, far fewer to North America. Among them were many Yoruba, especially in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when wars in the Yoruba region sent large numbers of captives to Cuba and to the Brazilian state of Bahia.

Enslaved people could not bring shrines or books, but they carried songs, drum rhythms, stories, herbal knowledge and the names of the orisha in memory. A is a people scattered from its homeland, and the African diaspora in the Americas rebuilt religious life under slavery. Colonial authorities and churches usually required baptism and banned or punished African practices. Communities met in secret, in mutual-aid societies called cabildos in Cuba and in the brotherhoods and houses of Brazil.

One well-known result is the pairing of orisha with Catholic saints. In Cuba, Shango was linked with Saint Barbara, Babalu Aye with Saint Lazarus, and Oshun with Our Lady of Charity, Cuba’s patron. Scholars have long called this , the blending of elements from different religions. Some scholars and many practitioners question the term. They argue that the saints often served as a cover or a parallel, and that devotees knew the orisha and the saint were distinct. Others hold that real blending took place. The debate itself is part of the history.

Today these diaspora traditions are practiced across the Americas and have spread to cities such as Miami, New York and Chicago. They are not simply African religion preserved unchanged, nor simply Catholicism with African decoration. They are living traditions shaped by the Middle Passage, by the Americas, and by generations who kept faith under conditions designed to erase it.

Santeria in Cuba and the United States

Main ideaSanteria, also called Regla de Ocha or Lucumi, is a Cuban tradition of orisha devotion that won an important religious freedom case in 1993.

Many practitioners prefer , the rule of the orisha, or . The tradition has no central authority or single text. Knowledge passes through houses, family-like communities led by a priest, and each house traces its lineage back through the priests who initiated its leaders.

A person who is fully initiated as a priest or priestess is called a santero or santera. Initiation, often called making the saint, joins the person to a particular orisha who is understood to rule their head. Many Nations, Many Creation Stories

Main ideaNative American nations each have their own creation stories, and many connect the sacred to specific lands and seasons.

There is no single Native American religion. Each nation has its own language, stories, ceremonies and sacred places. Still, scholars and Native writers point to some common threads. Many Native traditions describe the world as a web of relationships among people, animals, plants, waters and spirit beings, all of whom are kin. The Lakota phrase , all my relatives, spoken at the end of prayers, expresses this sense that a person is never alone and always owes respect to others.

Creation stories differ widely, and many have been published by members of the nations themselves. The Haudenosaunee, or Iroquois, of New York tell of Sky Woman, who fell from a world above and was set down on a turtle’s back, where the earth grew. For this reason many Native people call North America . The Dine, or Navajo, tell of an emergence through a series of worlds into this one. The Anishinaabe tell of a great flood after which a small animal brought up a bit of earth from which the land was remade.

For adherents, such a story teaches how to live in a place, what one owes to the animals and the land, and who one’s relatives are.

Suppression, Survival and Revival

Main ideaU.S. policy suppressed Native religions for decades until laws such as the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 began to protect them.

For much of American history, federal policy aimed to replace Native religions. In 1883 the Department of the Interior issued rules, later called the Code of Indian Offenses, that made the Sun Dance and other ceremonies punishable on reservations. At government and church , beginning with the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania in 1879, Native children were separated from their families, given new names, and punished for speaking their languages or practicing their traditions. The founder of Carlisle, Richard Henry Pratt, summed up his aim as ending the Indian in order to save the person.

In 1890, fear of the Ghost Dance, a new movement that promised renewal to Native peoples, contributed to the army’s massacre of Lakota men, women and children at Wounded Knee in South Dakota. Ceremonies went underground, were held in secret, or were kept alive by a few families. In 1934 the federal commissioner of Indian affairs, John Collier, issued a policy supporting Native religious freedom, but pressure and restrictions continued in many places.

In 1978 Congress passed the , or AIRFA. It declared that the United States would protect and preserve the right of Native Americans to believe, express and practice their traditional religions, including access to sacred sites, use of sacred objects and freedom to worship through ceremonies. But the law set out a policy without strong ways to enforce it. In 1988 the Supreme Court allowed a logging road through sacred lands in California, and in (1990) it ruled that Oregon could deny unemployment benefits to two members of the Native American Church fired for using peyote in a ceremony.

Congress responded with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 and with 1994 amendments to AIRFA that protect the ceremonial use of peyote by members of Native nations. Meanwhile, Native communities have led a : language classes, restored ceremonies, returned sacred objects under NAGPRA, and new generations learning songs from elders. government and church schools, beginning with Carlisle in 1879, that separated Native children from their families and suppressed their languages and religions
American Indian Religious Freedom Act
the 1978 law declaring a U.S. policy to protect Native Americans' right to practice their traditional religions
Employment Division v. Smith
the 1990 Supreme Court case that ruled against Native American Church members fired for ceremonial peyote use
revival
the renewal of practices, languages and ceremonies that had been suppressed or nearly lost
Check yourself

1. What did the Code of Indian Offenses of 1883 do?

Why: The 1883 rules punished Native ceremonies such as the Sun Dance, part of a policy of replacing Native religions.

2. What did the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 declare?

Why: AIRFA stated a policy of protecting Native religious practice, including sacred sites, objects and ceremonies.

3. How did Congress respond to Employment Division v. Smith (1990)?

Why: RFRA (1993) and the 1994 AIRFA amendments answered Smith, the latter protecting peyote use by Native American Church members.

Oral Traditions That Keep the World in Story

1. Why did the elder in the opening story tell her sacred story only in winter?

Why: Anishinaabe protocol reserves certain sacred stories for winter, out of respect and because families once gathered indoors then.

2. In Yoruba tradition, what role do the orisha play?

Why: The orisha, such as Ogun and Oshun, carry out the will of the supreme God, Olodumare.

3. Which practice belongs to Ifa?

Why: In Ifa, a babalawo casts the opele or palm nuts and recites verses of the odu that appears.

4. Which pairing of tradition and country is correct?

Why: Candomble took shape in Bahia, Brazil, and Santeria, or Regla de Ocha, in Cuba; both descend in part from Yoruba religion.

5. What principle did the Supreme Court apply in the 1993 Hialeah case?

Why: The Court struck down ordinances targeting Santeria's ritual sacrifice because they were not neutral.

6. What is the difference between a powwow and a closed ceremony such as many Sun Dances?

Why: Powwows are often public and intertribal, while many sacred ceremonies are closed to outsiders and cameras.

7. Which Great Lakes nation lived around the southern end of Lake Michigan when Chicago was founded?

Why: The Potawatomi, one of the Anishinaabe peoples, lived around the southern end of Lake Michigan and signed the 1833 Treaty of Chicago.

8. Why was the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 later strengthened?

Why: AIRFA lacked enforcement; after cases such as Smith (1990), Congress passed RFRA and the 1994 peyote amendments.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Who founded the Sikh tradition, according to Sikh belief?

Why: Sikh tradition begins with Guru Nanak, born in 1469; Gobind Singh was the tenth Guru and Kabir a bhagat whose hymns are in the scripture.

2. Why do Sikhs treat the Guru Granth Sahib as a living Guru?

Why: Sikhs believe the tenth Guru ended the line of human Gurus and named the scripture as Guru.

3. Which practice best expresses the Sikh teaching that all people are equal?

Why: In the langar, everyone sits in pangat and eats the same free meal, whatever their caste, wealth or religion.

4. Which of the five Ks is uncut hair?

Why: Kesh is uncut hair, usually covered by a turban; the kangha is the comb that keeps it neat.

5. Why does a Jain monk avoid eating root vegetables?

Why: Jains extend ahimsa to plants and tiny beings; uprooting a plant harms many lives.

6. How does the Jain idea of karma differ from the Buddhist idea?

Why: Jain teaching treats karma as physical matter stuck to the jiva; Buddhist teaching treats it as intention and denies a permanent self.

7. Which statement about the two main branches of Jainism is correct?

Why: Digambara means sky-clad and Svetambara white-clad; both keep the five vows.

8. How did Jainism influence Mohandas Gandhi?

Why: Gandhi grew up among Jains in Gujarat and credited Rajchandra as a major influence, though he remained a Hindu.

9. In Yoruba tradition, what is ase?

Why: Ase is the power behind action and change; Olodumare is its source.

10. What makes Ifa an example of an oral tradition?

Why: Babalawos train for years to memorize and recite the verses linked to the 256 odu.

11. Which diaspora tradition took shape in Bahia, Brazil?

Why: Candomble developed in Bahia in the nineteenth century; Santeria developed in Cuba.

12. Why did the Supreme Court strike down Hialeah's laws in 1993?

Why: In the Lukumi case, the Court found the ordinances were not neutral toward religion.

13. What does protocol ask of an outsider studying a closed Native ceremony?

Why: Respect for restricted knowledge means using what the community itself shares and leaving the rest alone.

14. Which statement about Chicago's Native history is accurate?

Why: The 1833 treaty forced most Anishinaabe west, but relocation in the 1950s built a large urban Native community in Chicago.

15. What weakness of the American Indian Religious Freedom Act did later laws address?

Why: AIRFA set a policy but lacked enforcement; RFRA (1993) and the 1994 peyote amendments followed court losses.

1. (Unit 20) Why do many scholars hesitate to call Confucianism simply a religion or simply a philosophy?

Why: It has religious features and lacks others, and the Chinese word for religion, zongjiao, took on that meaning only in the late 1800s.

2. (Unit 19) According to Buddhist tradition, what four sights led Siddhartha to leave the palace?

Why: The traditional story says Siddhartha saw age, sickness, death and a renouncer, which set him on his search.

3. (Unit 20) A family in Chicago keeps ancestor tablets, visits a Buddhist temple at New Year and practices tai chi. What does this best show?

Why: Chinese life has long drawn on Confucian, Buddhist and Daoist practices together, a blending scholars call syncretism.

4. (Unit 19) Right speech, right action and right livelihood belong to which group of the Eightfold Path?

Why: Teachers group these three as sila, ethical conduct; view and intention are wisdom, and effort, mindfulness and concentration are mental discipline.

5. (Unit 20) What rule did Confucius give as one word to guide a whole life in Analects 15.23?

Why: In Legge's translation the word is Reciprocity, stated in the negative form.
