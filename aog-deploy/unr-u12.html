Reversing Hermon: The Forgotten Mission

Hermon in the Gospels

Matthew 16 sets one of the Gospel's key scenes at Caesarea Philippi, at the foot of Mount Hermon. Acts 2 sets another in Jerusalem, where people from many nations hear one message, each in their own language. Older texts stand behind both. 1 Enoch 6 says rebel Watchers came down on Hermon. Genesis 11 and Deuteronomy 32 tell how the nations were scattered and divided.

Researchers read these texts together in different ways. Michael Heiser, in Reversing Hermon (2017), argued that Jesus' mission was meant to undo both the Watchers' rebellion and the scattering at Babel. This unit takes its title from his book. George Nickelsburg, Mark S. Smith, Clinton Arnold, Walter Wink and others read the same passages with other questions. At each stop you will read the text first, then meet the readings.

By the end, you will be able to explain what 1 Enoch, Matthew and Acts say about Hermon, Babel and the nations. You will be able to say what Heiser meant by reversing Hermon, and weigh it beside other readings, using manuscripts, places and passages. The aim is not to decide what to believe. It is to read closely and fairly.

The big question

What do the Gospels and Acts say about Hermon, Babel and the nations, and how well do the texts support Heiser's reading and others?

Big questionWhat does Matthew say happened at Caesarea Philippi and on the high mountain, and why do readers think the setting matters?
The story

Water from the Rock at Banias

At the foot of Mount Hermon, a spring pours out below a cliff that once held statues of a Greek god.

Early on a summer morning, a group of students walks down a stone path in the far north of the land of Israel. Snow still streaks the top of Mount Hermon above them. At the bottom of a tall reddish cliff, cold water bubbles out of the ground and runs off into a stream. That stream is one of the sources of the Jordan River. The place is called Banias today. In ancient times it was called Panias, after the Greek god Pan.

Their guide points up at the cliff. Carved into the rock are flat-backed niches, like empty shelves. Long ago, statues stood in them. Next to the niches is the mouth of a wide cave. Pan was a Greek god of shepherds, flocks and wild places. People came here to honor him with gifts. The first-century Jewish historian Josephus described the cave as very deep, with still water far down inside it.

One student opens her Bible to Matthew 16. She reads that Jesus came with his followers to the region of Caesarea Philippi. That was the Roman city built right here, beside this spring. Here, the story says, Jesus asked his followers who they thought he was. Here Peter answered him. And here Jesus spoke of a rock and of the gates of Hades.

The guide reads them two old texts. The first is 1 Enoch 6, which says rebel heavenly beings called Watchers came down on this very mountain. The second is Josephus, who described the deep cave beside them. Then she names some researchers. George Nickelsburg noted in 1981 that this corner of the land keeps appearing in Jewish stories of heavenly visions. Michael Heiser argued in 2017 that the setting of Peter's words was no accident. Other readers see the place as simply a stop on the road. This chapter starts from the texts and the place, then weighs the readings.

Section 1

The Book and Its Mountain

23.1

Genesis 6 and the Watchers

Main ideaGenesis 6:1–4 is four short verses; 1 Enoch retells them at length, and some New Testament letters show their writers knew that retelling.

Genesis 6:1–4 is one of the shortest and strangest stories in the Hebrew Bible. When people began to multiply, the saw that the daughters of men were beautiful. They took wives from among them. The were on the earth in those days. The children born were the mighty men of old, men of renown. Then the story moves straight on to the Flood.

tells the same story at far greater length. It is a Jewish book, and its oldest part, the Book of the Watchers (chapters 1–36), dates to about the 200s BCE. There the sons of God are , heavenly beings who come down on Mount Hermon. They take human wives, and their children are giants. They also teach people harmful skills. 1 Enoch survives whole only in Ge’ez, the old church language of Ethiopia. It is scripture in the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches.

Some early Christians knew this book. Jude 14–15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9, and scholars agree on that. 2 Peter 2:4 speaks of angels who sinned and are held in . George Nickelsburg, in his commentary 1 Enoch 1 (2001), suggested that the giants’ violence may mirror the wars of Alexander’s successors, which the first readers lived through. Annette Yoshiko Reed, in Fallen Angels and the History of Judaism and Christianity (2005), traced how Jews and Christians first treasured the story and later set it aside.

Michael Heiser went further. In Reversing Hermon: Enoch, the Watchers, and the Forgotten Mission of Jesus Christ (2017), he argued that the Gospel writers also knew the story, and that Jesus’ mission was meant to undo it. Many scholars accept that 1 Enoch shaped Jude and 2 Peter but doubt it shaped the Gospels so much. Others read the sons of God in Genesis 6 as human rulers, or as the family line of Seth. This unit sets these readings side by side.

23.2

Hermon in 1 Enoch

Main idea1 Enoch 6 says the Watchers came down on Mount Hermon and swore an oath there, and it explains the mountain's name by that oath.

Mount Hermon is the tallest mountain in the region, about 2,800 meters (over 9,000 feet) high. Its peak is snowy for much of the year. It stands near where modern Israel, Lebanon and Syria meet. Deuteronomy 3:9 says other peoples had their own names for it. The Sidonians called it Sirion, and the Amorites called it Senir.

1 Enoch 6 tells how two hundred Watchers came down in the days of Jared on the summit of Hermon. Jared is Enoch’s father in Genesis 5. Their leader, Semjaza as Charles spells the name, fears he alone will be blamed. So they all bind themselves with an , a solemn promise with a curse on anyone who breaks it. The text then says the mountain was named Hermon because of that oath.

That line is a , a pun that works in Hebrew and Aramaic. The name Hermon sounds like the root h-r-m, which has to do with setting something apart, often under a ban or curse. The Hebrew noun comes from that root. The writer of 1 Enoch used the sound of the name to tell what happened on the mountain.

1 Enoch comes back to this corner of the land in chapter 13. There Enoch sits by the waters of Dan, south-west of Hermon, reads out the Watchers’ plea, and falls asleep and sees visions. George Nickelsburg, in a 1981 article in the Journal of Biblical Literature, noted that several Jewish texts set heavenly visions in this part of upper Galilee. Other scholars read the Hermon scene mainly as a story that explains an old name. All agree on the key fact: 1 Enoch placed the Watchers’ descent on this mountain.

23.3

Bashan, Og and the Rephaim

Main ideaThe Hebrew Bible links the Hermon region with Og of Bashan and the Rephaim, and tablets from Ugarit tie the same towns to the dead.

South of Hermon lay , a high plain famous for strong cattle and oak trees. Deuteronomy 3:11 says Og, king of Bashan, was the last of the . It adds that his iron bed was nine cubits long, about four meters. Joshua 12:4–5 says Og lived at Ashtaroth and Edrei and ruled over Mount Hermon and all Bashan.

In several Hebrew Bible books, the Rephaim are a people of great size remembered from before Israel came into the land. In other passages, such as Isaiah 14:9 and Psalm 88:10, the same word names the spirits of the dead. Clay tablets from Ugarit, a city on the Syrian coast destroyed about 1200 BCE, help here. They speak of the rapiuma, the honored dead kings. One tablet names a god who sits enthroned in Ashtaroth and rules in Edrei, the same two towns as Og’s. Many scholars think the two uses of Rephaim grew from this old picture.

Psalm 68:15–16 adds a picture. It speaks of the many-peaked mountain of Bashan looking with envy at the mountain God chose for his home. Many commentators identify that mountain of Bashan with Hermon or the peaks near it. The psalm sets two mountains side by side: the great peak of the north, and , the hill of Jerusalem’s temple.

Readers take this in different ways. Some read Psalm 68 as simple poetry about a tall mountain that seems proud next to a small hill. Some see the Rephaim mainly as a memory of an old population. Others, drawing on the tablets from Ugarit, argue that ancient listeners would have heard rival powers in the contrast. Each reading starts from the same verses.

Section 2

A Shrine at the Spring

23.4

Panias and the Cave of Pan

Main ideaAt the foot of Hermon, a spring and cave called Panias held a shrine to the Greek god Pan long before Jesus' time.

After Alexander the Great, Greek culture spread across the eastern Mediterranean. Greek-speaking people honored at a cave on the southern slope of Hermon. Pan was a god of shepherds, flocks and wild country. The place took his name: Panias, or Paneas. Today it is called , because Arabic has no p sound.

The spring at Panias is one of the main sources of the Jordan River. In ancient times the water rose from the cave itself. Later, niches were cut into the cliff beside it to hold statues. Greek inscriptions found there honor Pan and the nymphs, spirits of springs and trees in Greek belief. Archaeologists have also found remains of courtyards and temples in front of the cliff.

Josephus, a Jewish historian writing in the first century CE, describes the cave in his book Jewish War. He says it opens into a deep hollow, with still water so deep that no one could find the bottom. That picture matters. A cave with water that seemed to go down forever could easily seem like an opening to the world below.

Readers weigh the place differently. Some see a shrine to a Greek god, beside a cave that seemed bottomless, at the foot of 1 Enoch’s mountain, as a strong picture of the old powers. Others note that shrines at springs were common in the ancient world and see Panias as one of many. Both agree it was a well-known place of worship that was not Jewish.

23.5

A City Named for Caesar

Main ideaHerod the Great built a temple for Augustus at Panias, and his son Philip turned the site into a city called Caesarea Philippi.

In about 20 BCE, the Roman emperor Augustus gave the region around Panias to Herod the Great, king of Judea. Josephus reports that Herod thanked him by building a temple of white stone near the spring, dedicated to Augustus. So the site now honored two kinds of power: a Greek god of nature, and a Roman ruler who received worship through the .

When Herod died in 4 BCE, his lands were divided among his sons. The northern part went to Philip, who ruled as a , the ruler of part of a divided kingdom. Philip built up Panias into a city and made it his capital. He named it Caesarea, again to honor Caesar Augustus.

There was already a famous Caesarea on the Mediterranean coast, a port built by Herod. To tell them apart, people called the new city , meaning Philip’s Caesarea. The Gospels of Matthew and Mark use this name. Mark 8:27 says Jesus went to the villages around Caesarea Philippi, while Matthew 16:13 speaks of its district.

Some readers see layers in the setting. The old shrine of Pan, the new temple to a ruler honored as divine, and the mountain of 1 Enoch’s Watchers all met in one place. Many scholars simply note that Jesus often withdrew to quieter areas outside Jewish towns. Either way, Caesarea Philippi was far from Jerusalem, both in distance and in its religious world.

Section 3

Peter's Confession

23.6

The Question at Caesarea

Main ideaIn Matthew 16:13–17, Jesus asks his followers who he is, and Peter answers that he is the Messiah, the Son of the living God.

Matthew 16:13 sets the scene: Jesus comes into the region of Caesarea Philippi. He asks his disciples who people say the is. They report what the crowds are saying. Some say John the Baptist, some Elijah, others Jeremiah or one of the prophets.

Then Jesus asks them directly who they say he is. Simon Peter answers. In Matthew he calls Jesus the Christ, the Son of the living God. is the Greek form of the Hebrew , meaning anointed one. Mark 8:29 gives a shorter answer, and Luke 9:20 has the Christ of God. In Matthew, Jesus tells Peter that this was not shown to him by flesh and blood but by his Father in heaven.

The place gives the words a backdrop. Around them stood a cave with niches for statues of a nature god and a temple to a ruler. In that place, Peter names the living God. Many commentators draw this contrast between the living God and carved images. George Nickelsburg, in his 1981 article, went further. He set Peter’s revelation in Matthew 16:17 beside older Jewish texts in which Enoch and Levi receive visions in this same region.

Other readers focus on the question itself more than the place. For them, the passage is the turning point of the Gospel. Up to here, people wonder who Jesus is. From here on, as Matthew 16:21 says, Jesus begins to tell the disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer. Readers of every view agree that this is a turning point.

23.7

The Rock and the Gates

Main ideaJesus' promise that the gates of Hades will not overcome his church uses a Greek name for the realm of the dead, which Heiser read as a challenge.

Matthew 16:18 contains a wordplay in Greek. Peter’s name is Petros, and the word for rock is petra. Jesus says that Peter is Petros and that on this petra he will build his . The Greek word for church, ekklesia, means an assembly, a gathered people. Then Jesus adds that the gates of will not prevail against it.

Hades was the Greek name for the realm of the dead. The , the old Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, often uses it for the Hebrew word . The Hebrew Bible speaks of the gates of death and the gates of Sheol. In Isaiah 38:10, King Hezekiah, fearing he will die young, says he is being sent to the gates of Sheol. The phrase names the power of death.

Heiser read the line as a challenge. Gates do not attack; they defend. So he argued that Jesus pictured his people going on the offensive against the realm of death and the powers behind it. He also suggested that this rock could point to the place itself, the rock of the cliff and the mountain where they stood. Near a cave some linked with the underworld, the words had a sharp edge in his reading.

Many other readers take the gates of Hades to mean death itself. In that reading, Jesus promises that death will not destroy his community, a promise tied to his own resurrection. Heiser’s reading and this one share one point. Both say the church will outlast what stands against it.

23.8

Other Readings of the Rock

Main ideaReaders disagree about what the rock in Matthew 16:18 is: Peter himself, Peter's confession, Christ, or, some suggest, the place itself.

Few verses have been read in as many ways as Matthew 16:18. The question sounds simple: what is the rock? Christian traditions have given different answers for many centuries. The answer has shaped how they understand leadership in the church. The next verse matters too, because there Jesus gives Peter the .

Catholic teaching holds that the rock is Peter himself. It reads the gift of the keys as a special role for Peter. Catholic tradition sees that role continued in the bishops of Rome, the popes. Many Eastern Orthodox writers stress Peter’s faith and see his role as shared among all the bishops who keep that faith.

Many Protestant readers, following reformers such as Martin Luther, say the rock is Peter’s of faith, or Christ himself. They point to 1 Corinthians 3:11, where Paul says no one can lay any foundation other than Jesus Christ. Many modern scholars from several traditions say Peter is the rock as the first leader among the , without deciding questions about later church offices.

Some recent readers add a layer. Jesus spoke at the foot of Hermon, near the cave of Pan. So they suggest the rock could also call up the place itself: Jesus would build his assembly on ground linked with rival powers. Most commentators do not read it this way. Readers can weigh each view by asking what the text says and what setting it gives.

Up the High Mountain

23.9

The Transfiguration

Main ideaSix days after Peter's confession, Matthew 17:1–8 says Jesus was transfigured on a high mountain, with Moses and Elijah beside him.

Matthew 17:1 begins six days after the scene at Caesarea Philippi. Jesus takes Peter, James and John up a high mountain by themselves. There he is , changed in form before them. His face shines like the sun, and his clothes become white as light. Mark 9:2–8 tells the same event. Luke 9:28–36 says about eight days and adds that Jesus went up to pray.

Moses and Elijah appear and talk with Jesus. Together they are often read as standing for : Moses for the Law, Elijah for the prophets. In the Hebrew Bible both men met God on a mountain, Moses on Sinai in Exodus 24 and 34, and Elijah on in 1 Kings 19. Peter offers to build three shelters. A bright cloud covers them, and a voice speaks.

The disciples fall on their faces, afraid. Jesus touches them and tells them to get up and not be afraid. When they look up, they see only Jesus. On the way down, in Matthew 17:9, he tells them to tell no one until the Son of Man is raised from the dead. Later, 2 Peter 1:16–18 recalls the voice heard on the holy mountain.

Readers who place the event on Hermon see extra meaning in it: glory shown on the mountain where 1 Enoch sets the Watchers’ oath. Other readers focus on the scene’s links to Sinai: a mountain, a cloud, a voice, and a shining face, as with Moses in Exodus 34:29–35. Both readings begin from the same details in the text.

23.10

Which Mountain?

Main ideaThe Gospels do not name the mountain; old tradition names Mount Tabor, while many modern scholars argue for Hermon.

Matthew, Mark and Luke all say only a high mountain. None gives its name. So readers have had to reason from clues. By the 300s CE, Christian writers such as Cyril of Jerusalem named , a round hill in . Churches were built on Tabor, and a large church stands on its top today, finished in 1924.

Tabor has strong support in , and it stands out above the flat plain around it. Some scholars raise questions, though. Tabor is not very high, about 575 meters. Josephus says there was a settlement on its top, which he fortified during the war against Rome in the 60s CE. That could make it hard for Jesus and three disciples to be alone there.

Many modern scholars, and Heiser with them, point instead to Hermon. The event comes six days after the scene at Caesarea Philippi, which lies at Hermon’s foot. Hermon is by far the highest mountain nearby. Heiser added a further reason from his reading: the glory of Jesus appears on the very mountain of the Watchers. Some others suggest Mount Meron in upper Galilee.

No archaeological find settles the question. Both sides work from the same text: a high mountain, six days after Caesarea Philippi, then a return to Galilee in Matthew 17:22. Weighing clues like these, and saying how sure the evidence lets us be, is a basic skill in reading ancient texts.

23.11

The Road to Jerusalem

Main ideaMatthew moves from the confession at Hermon's foot, to glory on the mountain, to the road to Jerusalem; readers ask what that order means.

Order matters in a story. In Matthew, the events come in a clear line. At Caesarea Philippi, Peter names Jesus as the Messiah (16:13–20). Right after, Jesus begins to speak of his coming death (16:21). Six days later comes the Transfiguration (17:1–8). Then the story turns south toward Jerusalem (19:1; 20:17), and Jesus enters the city (21:1–11).

Heiser read this as purposeful. First, Jesus goes to the place tied to the Watchers and speaks of the gates of Hades. Next, he shows his glory on the mountain. Then he goes to Jerusalem to die. In Heiser’s reading, the cross was not a defeat but the way the powers were beaten. He pointed to Colossians 2:15, which says Christ disarmed the .

Heiser also pointed to 1 Corinthians 2:8. There Paul says that if the rulers of this age had understood God’s wisdom, they would not have the Lord of glory. Heiser read those rulers as including unseen powers. Clinton Arnold, in Powers of Darkness (1992), also read Paul’s powers as real spiritual beings. Many other scholars read these rulers as the human leaders who condemned Jesus, such as Pilate and the temple leaders. Walter Wink, in Naming the Powers (1984), argued that Paul’s power words often hold the human and the spiritual together.

Readers who do not share this reading still see a turning point here. The Gospel shifts from teaching in Galilee to the road to the cross. Some scholars call this the hinge of Matthew and Mark. Heiser added the unseen realm to the map. In his reading, the road leads from the Watchers’ mountain to Jerusalem’s hill.

The story

Every Voice in Its Own Language

On a festival day in Jerusalem, visitors from across the known world hear one message in many languages.

It is the Jewish festival of Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks, in Jerusalem, around the year 30 CE. The streets are packed. Pilgrims have come from Egypt and Libya, from Rome, from Crete and Arabia, and from the lands of the Parthians far to the east. They speak Aramaic, Greek, Latin, Egyptian and more. Many of them are Jews whose families have lived abroad for generations.

Acts 2 tells what happens next. A sound like a rushing wind fills a house where Jesus' followers are gathered. Something like tongues of fire appears and rests on each of them. They begin to speak in other languages. A crowd gathers, confused and amazed, because each person hears them speaking in his or her own language.

In Luke's telling, people in the crowd ask how this can be. The speakers are all from Galilee, yet each listener hears his own native language. The crowd names their homelands one after another, a long list stretching from Persia to Rome. Some are amazed. A few laugh and say the speakers must have had too much wine.

Christian readers since early times have set this scene beside an older story, the tower of Babel in Genesis 11. There, one language became many, and the nations were scattered. Here, many languages carry one message, and people from the nations are gathered. Michael Heiser called it Babel in reverse. Other readers hear a different background, the giving of the Law at Sinai. This chapter reads both and follows the mission out to the edge of the ancient map.

Babel and the Scattered Nations

24.1

The Tower of Babel

Main ideaGenesis 11:1–9 tells how God confused human language at Babel and scattered people across the earth.

Genesis 11 opens with the whole earth speaking one language. People move to a plain in , in Mesopotamia, and decide to build a city with a tower whose top reaches the heavens. They say they want to make a name for themselves and not be scattered. They bake bricks and use tar for mortar, the building methods of Mesopotamia.

The LORD comes down to see the city. He says that if they have begun this, nothing they plan will be impossible for them. So he confuses their language, and they can no longer understand each other. They stop building, and God scatters them over the face of the earth. The city is named , which sounds like the Hebrew verb balal, to confuse.

Many scholars think the tower recalls a , a stepped temple tower of Mesopotamia. Ziggurats were seen as places where gods came down to meet people. The best known stood in Babylon, and Babel is the Hebrew name of Babylon. So the story may be about more than building. It is about trying to reach heaven on human terms.

Other ancient writers retold the story. The Book of Jubilees, from the 100s BCE, retells it in chapter 10 and adds that the Lord sent a great wind that threw the tower down. Many readers see Babel mainly as a story about pride and the origin of languages. Others read it together with Deuteronomy 32:8, which the next lesson explores. On that reading, the scattering at Babel is also the moment the nations were set under other heavenly beings.

24.2

Nations and the Sons of God

Main ideaIn the Dead Sea Scrolls reading, Deuteronomy 32:8–9 says the Most High divided the nations by the number of the sons of God, keeping Israel as his own.

Deuteronomy 32 is a long poem called the Song of Moses. Verses 8–9 look back to ancient times. When the Most High gave the nations their inheritance and divided humankind, he set the borders of the peoples. But the LORD’s own portion was his people, Jacob his allotted inheritance. Many readers take these verses as the Bible’s own comment on what happened after Babel.

The key phrase is how the borders were set. The standard Hebrew text, the , says by the number of the sons of Israel. A Hebrew copy among the , called 4QDeut-j, reads the sons of God. The has the angels of God in most copies. Many modern translations now follow the scroll: the ESV has sons of God, and the NRSV has gods.

Michael Heiser wrote his doctoral dissertation (2004) on the divine council and argued that the sons of God reading is older. Many text scholars agree. Heiser read it to mean that God placed the nations under lesser heavenly beings, , after Babel, and kept Israel for himself. He saw the same idea in Deuteronomy 4:19–20, where the sun, moon and stars are allotted to the peoples, but the LORD takes Israel.

Other researchers read the verse differently. Mark S. Smith, in The Origins of Biblical Monotheism (2001), argued that it keeps a very old picture: the Most High gives each nation to one of his sons, and the LORD receives Israel as his share. Heiser read the Most High and the LORD as the same God. Some scholars keep the Masoretic reading and link the number to Jacob’s seventy descendants in Exodus 1:5. Jubilees 15:31–32 shows an ancient Jewish reading: God set spirits over the many nations, but set no angel over Israel, for he alone is its ruler.

24.3

Seventy Nations

Main ideaGenesis 10, the Table of Nations, lists seventy nations in the Hebrew text and seventy-two in the Greek, numbers that echo in later texts.

Genesis 10 comes just before the Babel story. It lists the descendants of Noah’s three sons, Shem, Ham and Japheth, and the peoples that came from them. Scholars call it the . It is a map in the form of a family tree. Egypt, Canaan, Assyria, the Greek islands and Arabia all appear on it.

If you count the names in the Hebrew Masoretic Text, you get seventy nations. The Greek Septuagint adds a couple of names, so its count is seventy-two. Jewish tradition kept seventy as a for all the nations of the world. Later rabbis spoke of the seventy languages of humanity. The tells that God’s voice at Sinai divided into seventy languages (Shabbat 88b).

These numbers link texts together. The part of 1 Enoch called the Animal Apocalypse speaks of seventy shepherds set over Israel for a time (1 Enoch 89:59). Many scholars read those shepherds as angels. Later Jewish writings speak of seventy angels set over seventy nations. Some readers link all these seventies to the nations scattered at Babel and to Deuteronomy 32:8. Seventy became shorthand for the whole non-Israelite world.

Not every scholar thinks the writers counted so carefully. The names in Genesis 10 include cities, peoples and regions, and counting them depends on choices. Still, seventy as a symbol for the nations is well attested in Jewish writing. That symbol returns in the Gospel of Luke, as a later lesson shows.

Main ideaRead beside Genesis 11, Pentecost looks like Babel reversed, one message in many tongues gathering the nations; other readers see a new Sinai.

24.10

Paul's Road to Spain

Main ideaRomans 15 says Paul planned to go to Spain; some scholars, and Heiser after them, tie Spain to Tarshish, the far-west edge of the Table of Nations.

Around 57 CE Paul wrote his letter to the Romans. Near the end, in Romans 15:24 and 28, he says he plans to visit Rome on his way to Spain. He wants to preach where Christ has not yet been named (Romans 15:20). Spain was the western edge of the Roman world, where the land met the Atlantic.

Why Spain? Some scholars, including James M. Scott in Paul and the Nations (1995), read Paul’s plans against the Table of Nations. Heiser built on this work. These readers point to . In Genesis 10:4, Tarshish is a descendant of Javan on the Table of Nations. Many scholars place Tarshish in southern Spain, often linking it with , a region Greek writers described. It is also where Jonah tried to flee in Jonah 1:3, a place at the far end of the known world.

Isaiah 66:18–20 describes God gathering all nations and tongues. Survivors are sent to the nations: to Tarshish, Pul, Lud, Tubal, Javan and the distant . They bring back people from all nations as an offering to the LORD in Jerusalem. In Romans 15:16 Paul describes his work as making the offering of the Gentiles acceptable. On this reading, Paul was following Isaiah’s map and aiming at its farthest point.

Did Paul reach Spain? Acts ends with Paul under guard in Rome, around 60–62 CE. A letter called 1 Clement, written from Rome around 96 CE, says Paul reached the limit of the west. Some scholars take that as Spain. Others think Paul never got there. Still others accept Spain as Paul’s goal simply because it was the far west, without the Tarshish link.

24.11

Wisdom Shown to the Powers

Main ideaEphesians 3:10 says God's wisdom is made known through the church to the powers in the heavenly places, and readers debate who those powers are.

Ephesians 3 describes a : that Gentiles are now fellow heirs with Israel, members of one body. Ephesians 3:10 adds who is watching. Through the church, God’s many-sided wisdom is made known to the in the . Christian tradition names Paul as the author. Many modern scholars think a follower of Paul wrote it.

The letter names these powers more than once. Ephesians 1:21 says Christ is seated far above every rule, authority, power and dominion. Ephesians 6:12 says the struggle is not against flesh and blood but against rulers and powers of this dark world, in the heavenly places. These verses are central to any study of the powers in Paul’s letters.

Clinton Arnold, in Powers of Darkness (1992), read these powers as real spiritual beings opposed to God. Some readers go further and link them to the gods of the nations in Deuteronomy 32. On that reading, the church itself is the sign. Made of Jews and people from the nations together, its very existence tells the powers that the nations they held are coming back.

Other scholars read the powers differently. Walter Wink, in Naming the Powers (1984), argued that the language also describes the inner spirit of human institutions, such as empires and systems. Many commentators hold that the writer meant spiritual beings and that this shaped how he saw earthly rulers too. Arnold’s reading stresses the beings themselves; Wink’s stresses how power works through human systems.
