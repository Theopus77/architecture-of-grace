Babel and the Nations

The Third Rebellion

The story of Babel is short, only nine verses in Genesis 11. People build a tower. Their language gets mixed up, and they are scattered. Right before it, Genesis 10 lists about seventy nations. Right after it, God calls one man, Abram. This unit reads these chapters together, beside other old texts.

You will read old copies of Deuteronomy 32:8 from the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Greek Septuagint. You will meet old Jewish books, like Jubilees, Sirach and the history of Josephus. You will also meet modern scholars, like Mark S. Smith and Michael Heiser. They do not all agree, and you will see why.

By the end, you will be able to retell the Babel story and compare old copies of Deuteronomy 32:8. You will describe how different readers explain the gods of the nations. You will also trace the promise to Abram, that all families of the earth would be blessed, and explain how Jewish and Christian readers understand it.

The big question

What happened to the nations after Babel, and how did the promise to Abram speak to them, as ancient and modern readers see it?

The story

Seventy Names on a Map

Maya counts the nations in Genesis 10 and finds a number that shows up again and again.

Ms. Ortiz unrolled a big paper map across two desks. It showed the lands around the Mediterranean Sea. It stretched east to the rivers of Iraq. "This is the world the writers of Genesis knew," she said. "Today we will read a list of nations." Maya held a pencil and a stack of sticky notes.

The class opened to Genesis 10. The chapter lists the families that came from Noah's three sons, Shem, Ham and Japheth. Each time Maya read a new name, she wrote it on a note. She stuck it on the map. Some names were easy, like Mizraim, the Hebrew name for Egypt. Others, like Tarshish, made the class guess and look things up.

When the list ended, Maya counted her notes. "Seventy!" she said. Ms. Ortiz smiled. "Many readers count seventy in the Hebrew text," she said. "The old Greek translation has a couple more. That number, seventy, shows up in some surprising places."

"Where else?" asked Maya. Ms. Ortiz pointed to the next chapter, Genesis 11, and the tower of Babel. Then she held up the book of Deuteronomy. "Many scholars read these chapters together," she said. "Old copies of Deuteronomy may tell us who was put in charge of the nations. Let's read them and see."

Section 1

The Tower of Babel

11.1

One Language, One Plan

Main ideaGenesis 11 says everyone spoke one language and decided to build a city and a tower that reached toward the sky.

Genesis 11 begins with a picture. The whole earth had one language. People moved east and settled on a plain in the land of . There they made bricks and baked them hard. They used a sticky black tar to hold the bricks together.

Shinar is an old name for southern . That is the land between two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates. Today most of it is in Iraq. In the story, the people made a plan there. They would build a city and a tower whose top reached into the sky (Genesis 11:4).

The builders gave two reasons. They wanted to make a name for themselves. They also did not want to be scattered over the earth. Earlier, God had told Noah’s family to fill the earth (Genesis 9:1). Many Jewish and Christian readers see Babel as a story about pride. The builders put their own name first.

11.2

God Comes Down

Main ideaIn the story, God comes down, mixes up the people's language and scatters them, and the city is called Babel.

Genesis 11:5 says the LORD came down to see the city and the tower. The builders wanted to reach up to heaven. Instead, God comes down to them. Many readers notice the gentle joke here. The tower seemed very tall to its builders. Yet God still had to come down to see it.

Then God speaks. In Genesis 11:7, God says, let us go down and mix up their language. Who is us? Philo, a Jewish writer in Egypt, wrote a whole book on this story. It is called On the Confusion of Tongues. He said God was speaking with the heavenly powers that serve him. Many scholars today say God is speaking to his .

Other readers hear a royal way of speaking, like a king who says we. Some Christians hear a hint of the Trinity. After God speaks, the people cannot understand each other. They stop building and spread out. The city is named . It sounds like balal, a Hebrew word that means to mix up. Babel is also the Hebrew name for Babylon.

11.3

A Tower Like a Ziggurat

Main ideaThe tower was likely pictured as a ziggurat, and readers from Josephus to Heiser have asked what the builders wanted.

Archaeologists have found the remains of ziggurats in Iraq. A was a tall tower built in steps, like a giant staircase. It stood beside a temple. People in Mesopotamia believed a god could come down to the temple there. The great ziggurat of Babylon was called Etemenanki. Its name means temple of the foundation of heaven and earth.

Many scholars think the tower of Babel was pictured as a ziggurat. The Jewish historian Josephus retold the story around 94 CE, in his Antiquities of the Jews. He said a proud king named Nimrod led the builders. He said they used baked brick and tar, as Genesis does. Genesis itself never names a leader for the tower.

Michael Heiser, a scholar of the Bible’s old languages, thought the builders wanted to bring the gods down on their own terms. In The Unseen Realm (2015), he called Babel the third big in Genesis, after Eden and Genesis 6. Other readers agree Babel is about pride. They may not count it as one of three.

Think it through

Picture a hill made of dried mud bricks, flat on top, rising in wide steps. Each step is smaller than the one below. A long staircase climbs the front. At the very top stands a small shrine. The people below believed their god could come down to this place.

Why might a tower like this remind readers of the tower of Babel, whose top reached toward the sky?

Section 2

The Table of Nations

11.4

Noah's Three Sons

Main ideaGenesis 10 lists the peoples who came from Noah's sons Shem, Ham and Japheth, and scholars call it the Table of Nations.

Genesis 10 is a long list of names. It is often called the . It tells which peoples came from each of Noah’s three sons: Japheth, Ham and Shem. Many names are people. Many are also names of lands or cities. The list moves from one son to the next.

Each part of the list ends the same way. It says these peoples had their own families, languages, lands and (Genesis 10:5, 20 and 31). Some names are easy to know. Mizraim is the Hebrew name for Egypt. Canaan is the land where Israel later lived. Other names, like Tarshish, still puzzle scholars.

The list also names Nimrod, a mighty hunter and king. Genesis 10:10 says his kingdom began in Babel and other cities in Shinar. So Genesis 10 mentions Babel before chapter 11 tells its story. The two chapters are linked. Chapter 10 shows the nations spread out. Chapter 11 then tells a story about why.

11.5

Counting Seventy

Main ideaReaders count about seventy nations in the Hebrew text of Genesis 10, and seventy came to stand for all the nations.

Readers who count the names in the Hebrew text of Genesis 10 get about seventy. The has a couple more, about seventy-two. The Septuagint is an old Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible. Its first books were made in Egypt around 250 BCE. In later Jewish writing, the seventy nations came to mean all the peoples of the world.

The number seventy shows up in Israel’s own story too. Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5 say seventy people from Jacob’s family went down to Egypt. So the world has seventy nations, and Israel begins with seventy people. Many readers think this match is on purpose.

Clay tablets from , an old city in Syria, add another seventy. They tell of the high god El and his wife Athirat, who had seventy sons. E. Theodore Mullen studied El’s council in these tablets in a 1980 book. Mark S. Smith (2001) and other scholars compare this picture with Deuteronomy 32, later in this chapter.

11.6

First the List, Then the Story

Main ideaGenesis puts the list of nations before the Babel story, so readers see the result first and then the story of why.

Genesis 10 comes before Genesis 11, but the events are not in that order. Genesis 10 already shows many languages. Genesis 11 begins with one language. So the writer shows the result first. Then he goes back to tell the story behind it. Storytellers still do this today.

Genesis 10:25 gives a clue. It names a man called . It says that in his days the earth was divided. The name Peleg sounds like the Hebrew word for divide. Many readers link this verse to the scattering at Babel. Others think it means something else, such as a dividing of land.

Deuteronomy 32:8 also talks about God dividing the nations. Many scholars read Genesis 10, Genesis 11 and this verse together. In that reading, the three passages tell one story. People were scattered at Babel. Then God did something about the nations. The next section reads what Deuteronomy says he did.

Moses' Song and the Old Copies

11.7

The Most High Divides the Nations

Main ideaDeuteronomy 32:8–9 says the Most High divided the nations and set their borders, and the LORD kept Israel as his portion.

Deuteronomy 32 is a long poem. It is often called the Song of Moses. In the story, Moses teaches it to Israel near the end of his life. The song looks back over Israel’s history. Verse 8 looks all the way back to the time the nations were split up.

Verse 8 says the gave the nations their . He divided the peoples and set their borders. Verse 9 says the LORD’s is his people. Jacob, another name for Israel, is his share. So the nations get their lands. The LORD keeps one people for himself.

One phrase in verse 8 is hard. It says the borders were set by the number of someone. But whose number? Old copies of the Bible say different things here. Scholars have written a great deal about this small phrase. The next lesson shows the choices.

11.8

Sons of Israel or Sons of God?

Main ideaThe standard Hebrew text says sons of Israel in Deuteronomy 32:8, but a Dead Sea Scroll says sons of God, and the Septuagint points the same way.

Most printed Hebrew Bibles use the . Careful Jewish scribes called the Masoretes copied it. Its oldest complete copy of the Hebrew Bible, the Leningrad Codex, was made in 1008 CE. In Deuteronomy 32:8, this text says the borders were set by the number of the sons of Israel. The King James Version follows it.

In 1947, people began finding the in caves near the Dead Sea. Many of the scrolls are more than two thousand years old. One copy of Deuteronomy from these caves says sons of God in verse 8. Many copies of the Greek Septuagint point the same way. They say angels of God or sons of God.

So which reading came first? Many scholars, such as Mark S. Smith, think sons of God is older. They think a later scribe changed it, perhaps to avoid talk of other heavenly beings. Scholars who disagree on much else agree on this. Some newer English Bibles now follow the scroll. Others keep sons of Israel and add a note.

Section 4

The Gods of the Nations

11.9

Rulers Over the Nations

Main ideaOld Jewish books like Sirach and Jubilees say God set rulers over the nations, and Heiser read Deuteronomy 32 the same way.

Other old Jewish books also say the nations were given heavenly rulers. Sirach was written around 180 BCE. It says God set a ruler over every nation, but Israel is the Lord’s portion (Sirach 17:17). The book of says God put spirits in charge of the nations. But over Israel, it says, God alone rules (Jubilees 15:31–32).

Many scholars follow sons of God in Deuteronomy 32:8 and read it this way too. Michael Heiser built a large idea on it. In his reading, after Babel God set the nations under the sons of God, lesser in his council. God kept one people for himself, Israel. That is why verse 9 calls Israel his portion.

Elohim is a Hebrew word. It can mean God. It can also mean other spirit beings. Psalm 82 shows God judging the elohim for being unfair to the weak. Heiser read the gods of the nations as spirit beings that became , not only statues. He argued that the LORD made them and is far above them.

11.10

Other Ways to Read It

Main ideaDeuteronomy 4:19–20 shows a similar pattern, and readers explain the sons in Deuteronomy 32:8 in different ways.

Deuteronomy 4:19–20 has a similar pattern. There Moses warns Israel not to worship the sun, moon and stars, the . He says the LORD gave these to all the nations. But the LORD took Israel out of Egypt to be his own people. Many readers see the same idea as in Deuteronomy 32.

Not everyone reads it this way. Rashi was a famous Jewish teacher in France who died in 1105. He followed sons of Israel. He linked the seventy nations to the seventy people of Jacob’s family who went down to Egypt. Many Christian readers have also kept sons of Israel.

Other Jewish and Christian readers follow sons of God, or the Septuagint’s angels of God, but read them as . In this view, each nation has an angel who watches over it. Daniel 10:20 speaks of a prince of Persia and a prince of Greece. All these readers agree the verse is about God and the nations.

The story

Packing Up in Haran

A servant boy asks Abram where the family is going, and the answer surprises him.

The tents in Haran were coming down. Camels knelt while servants tied on water skins and rolled up rugs. Sheep and goats called out in the dust. Abram, who was seventy-five years old, walked among them. His wife Sarai counted jars of grain. His nephew Lot checked the ropes on the donkeys.

A young servant boy lifted a basket, then set it down again. "Master Abram," he asked, "where are we going?" Abram looked out past the fields. "To a land God will show me," he said. The boy frowned. "But you do not know where it is?" Abram smiled. "Not yet."

The Bible tells this moment in just a few verses, in Genesis 12. In the story, God tells Abram to leave his country, his relatives and his father's house. God promises to make him a great nation and to bless him. Then comes the biggest promise. Through Abram, all the families of the earth will be blessed.

This story comes right after Babel, where the nations were scattered. Many readers notice that timing, and they think it matters. The nations had just been sent away. Now God was starting a new family. The promise said this one family would bring blessing to all the others.

Section 1

Abram Is Called

12.1

From Ur to Haran

Main ideaAbram's family came from Ur and settled in Haran, and Joshua 24:2 says his father's family served other gods.

At the end of Genesis 11, the story narrows to one family. Terah had three sons: Abram, Nahor and Haran. Abram married Sarai. Genesis 11:30 says Sarai had no children. Terah took Abram, Sarai and his grandson Lot and left . They set out for Canaan. But they stopped in the city of and settled there.

Ur was a great city in southern Mesopotamia. Archaeologists have dug up its ziggurat, and part of it still stands. Haran was a trading town far to the north, in what is now Turkey. Both cities honored the moon god. In Ur he was called Nanna. In Haran he was called Sin.

Joshua 24:2 adds a detail. It says Abram’s ancestors, including Terah, lived beyond the river and served other gods. So Abram did not come from a family that already followed Israel’s God. Later Jewish writers told more. The book of (chapter 12) says young Abram turned from idols and prayed to the one God.

12.2

Go to the Land

Main ideaIn Genesis 12:1–7, God tells Abram to leave his land and family, and Abram goes at age seventy-five into Canaan.

Genesis 12:1 begins with a command. God tells Abram to go. He must leave three things: his country, his relatives and his father’s house. Each one is closer to home than the last. God does not name the new land. He says only that he will show it to Abram.

Genesis 12:4 says Abram went, as the LORD had told him. He was seventy-five years old. Sarai and Lot went too, with the people and goods they had gained in Haran. They traveled into Canaan. At Shechem, God promised this land to Abram’s (Genesis 12:7). Abram built an there.

The New Testament book of Hebrews looks back on this moment. Hebrews 11:8 says Abraham went out without knowing where he was going. Jewish and Christian readers both honor Abraham’s trust. A Jewish teaching in Pirkei Avot 5:3 says Abraham was tested ten times. Jewish tradition counts this journey among the tests.

12.3

A Great Name

Main ideaAt Babel people tried to make a name for themselves, but in Genesis 12:2 God promises to make Abram's name great.

Put Genesis 11:4 and Genesis 12:2 side by side. At Babel, the builders wanted to make a name for themselves. Just one chapter later, God tells Abram, I will make your name great. The same Hebrew word for name, , is in both verses. Shem is also the name of Noah’s son, Abram’s ancestor.

Many Jewish and Christian readers notice this match. The builders reached for a great name by their own work. Abram receives a great name as a . The builders tried to stay together in one place. Abram leaves home and trusts what God said. The two stories seem to answer each other.

In the story, God does not destroy the scattered nations. Instead he starts over with one man and his family. Many readers see Abram’s call as the turning point after Babel. The builders tried to hold on to one place. Abram lets go of his home. Through his family, the promise says, blessing will reach the nations again.

Section 2

A Blessing for All Families

12.4

The Promises to Abram

Main ideaGenesis 12:2–3 gives a set of promises: a great nation, blessing, a great name, and blessing for all the families of the earth.

Genesis 12:2–3 is short, but it is packed with promises. God says he will make Abram a great nation. He will bless Abram. He will make his name great. Abram will be a to others. These first promises are about Abram’s own family growing and doing well.

Then the promises reach outward. God says he will bless those who bless Abram. He will whoever treats Abram with scorn. Then comes the last promise. In Abram, all the families of the earth will be blessed. Many readers see this last line as the biggest of all.

At that moment Abram and Sarai had no children at all. So the promise of a great nation sounds almost impossible. That is part of the story’s pull. Readers wait through many chapters to see what happens. Genesis 21 finally tells of the birth of their son, Isaac.

Genesis 12:2–3, paraphrased from the King James Version (1611) (paraphrased)

12.5

All the Families of the Earth

Main ideaThe promise to bless all families points back to the scattered nations of Genesis 10 and forward through the rest of Genesis.

Genesis 10 listed the families of the earth, spread out by their languages and lands. Genesis 12:3 uses the same Hebrew word for families, . So the promise points back to that list. In the story, the scattered nations are not forgotten. The blessing is meant to reach them.

Genesis repeats the promise later. In Genesis 22:18, God tells Abraham that all the nations of the earth will be blessed through his offspring. In Genesis 26:4, the promise is repeated to Isaac. In Genesis 28:14, it is repeated to Jacob. Three hear the same promise.

Genesis is shaped around this turn. Chapters 1 to 11 tell about the whole world. Chapters 12 to 50 follow one family. Some readers compare it to a camera zooming in. The story narrows to Abraham’s family, but the promise keeps the whole world in view.

12.6

A New Family After Babel

Main ideaGenesis puts Abram's call right after Babel, and Heiser argued God began a new family to win back the nations.

Look at the order of Genesis. Chapter 10 lists the nations. Chapter 11 scatters them at Babel. Chapter 12 begins with God calling Abram. Michael Heiser read this order with Deuteronomy 32:8–9 in mind. In his reading, God set the nations under . Then, right away, he called Abram. God gave up the nations for a time, but not for good.

Heiser often spoke of God’s family. In his reading, God wanted a family in heaven, the sons of God, and a family on earth, people. Eden had held both together. After Babel, Abram’s line would be the start of the earthly family again. Through it, the blessing would one day reach every nation.

So, for Heiser, Abram’s call began a long plan to the nations. Israel was to be the family through whom they would be won back. Other readers put more weight on Abram’s faith and God’s covenant with his family. Many readers see both themes in the text.

Section 3

God's Own Portion

12.7

Israel, the LORD's Portion

Main ideaDeuteronomy 32:9 calls Israel the LORD's portion, and Exodus 19:5–6 calls Israel a treasured people and a kingdom of priests.

Deuteronomy 32:9 says the LORD’s portion is his people. Jacob is his share. A portion is a share, like a piece of land given to a family. In this verse, the nations get their lands, but the LORD keeps a people. The people of Israel come from Abram’s grandson Jacob, who was also called Israel.

Exodus 19:5–6 says something similar. At Mount Sinai, God tells Israel that if they keep his , they will be his treasure among all peoples. They will be a and a holy nation. A priest serves God for others. Many readers think this means Israel was to show God to the nations.

Many readers connect these verses. Israel was God’s portion, but not to keep God to itself. Christian readers often say Israel was meant to show the nations what God is like. Jewish readers also see Israel’s calling as holy and lasting. Many stress living by God’s teaching, the , as the heart of that calling.

Think it through

Picture a farmer who divides his fields among his children. Each child gets a portion. But the farmer keeps one small field for himself. He plants it with care. Deuteronomy 32:8–9 uses this kind of picture. The nations get lands, and the LORD keeps Israel as his own portion.

In the picture, why might the farmer keep a field for himself? How does the picture help you read Deuteronomy 32:9?

12.8

Rise Up, O God

Main ideaPsalm 82:8 asks God to rise up and judge the earth because he will inherit all the nations.

Psalm 82 begins with God standing in his council. He judges the elohim, the gods, because they do not defend the weak, the poor and the orphan. In verse 6 he calls them sons of the Most High. In verse 7 he says they will die like humans. Then comes verse 8.

In verse 8, the writer turns to God. He asks God to rise up and judge the earth. He gives a reason: God will all the nations. In Hebrew, inherit comes from the same root as the inheritance in Deuteronomy 32:8. The two verses use the same word family.

Many scholars read Psalm 82 as a scene in God’s heavenly council. E. Theodore Mullen (1980) and Mark S. Smith (2001) studied it beside the Ugarit tablets. Heiser read it as the answer to Babel. God judges the gods who ruled the nations badly, then takes the nations back. Other readers think the gods are human judges. For them, verse 8 asks God to set right.

12.9

A Light to the Nations

Main ideaIsaiah 49:6 pictures God's servant as a light to the nations, so that God's rescue reaches the end of the earth.

The book of Isaiah picks up the promise to Abram. In Isaiah 49:6, God speaks to his . It is too small a thing, God says, only to bring back the tribes of Jacob. God will also make the servant a light to the nations. Then his will reach the end of the earth.

Readers differ on who the servant is. Many Jewish readers see the servant as Israel itself, as Isaiah 49:3 says. Some see a prophet. Christian readers have often seen the servant as pointing to Jesus. The New Testament quotes this verse in Acts 13:47. There Paul and Barnabas use it to describe their own work.

Isaiah 42:6 uses the same picture. There God gives his servant as a covenant for the people and a light for the nations. Many readers see a thread here that starts in Genesis 12. Abram’s family was chosen, but not for itself alone. In this reading, the light was meant to spread to every nation.

Section 4

How Readers See the Promise

12.10

Jewish Readings of the Covenant

Main ideaJewish tradition honors Abraham as the father of the covenant people and tells how he turned from idols to the one God.

In Genesis 15, God makes a covenant with Abram. He tells him to look at the stars and count them, if he can. So many, God says, will your offspring be. Genesis 15:6 says Abram believed the LORD. In Genesis 17, God changes his name to Abraham and gives the sign of the covenant.

Jewish tradition honors Abraham as the first father of the Jewish people. The covenant with him is seen as lasting. Many Jewish readers stress that it is lived out by keeping God’s commandments, the . The historian Josephus wrote that Abraham was the first to teach boldly that there is one God, the maker of all (Antiquities 1.7).

A famous old story, called a , is told in Genesis Rabbah, a Jewish book on Genesis. In it, Abram’s father Terah sold idols. Young Abram broke them and said the biggest idol did it. His father said idols cannot move. Abram asked, then why worship them? The story shows Abraham turning to the one God.

12.11

Christian Readings: The Promise and Jesus

Main ideaPaul wrote that the promise to Abraham was good news for the nations, and many Christians see Pentecost as Babel turned around.

Christian readers have long read Genesis 12:3 as pointing to Jesus. In Galatians 3:8, Paul says the Scripture told the good news to Abraham ahead of time. He repeats the promise that all nations would be blessed in him. Paul taught that people from every nation could join Abraham’s family through faith in Jesus (Galatians 3:29).

At the end of Matthew’s Gospel, Jesus sends his followers to make of all nations (Matthew 28:19). In Acts 2, at , people from many lands hear the message in their own languages. Many Christian readers, Heiser among them, see this as Babel turned around. At Babel, languages split people apart. At Pentecost, one message came through many languages.

Jewish tradition links this festival, called Shavuot, to the giving of the Torah at Sinai. A teaching in the Talmud (Shabbat 88b) says each word God spoke there split into seventy languages. Jewish readers see the covenant with Abraham going on with the Jewish people, not pointing to Jesus. This course describes both readings without choosing between them.
