Time, Water, a Line

Origins

Long ago, a race was simple. Runners dashed down a straight dirt path at Olympia, and judges named who came first. Swimmers raced in the sea and in rivers. Rich players hit balls in walled palace courts. None of these could be measured the same way twice.

In this unit, you will see how three sports got their measured rooms. Track got a 400-meter lap, staggered lanes and clocks. Swimming got built pools, lane lines and separate records for each pool length. Tennis moved from an indoor palace game to a painted rectangle on grass.

You will also read the Illinois record books. They show when each state title began, and who was left out for many years. By the end, you will be able to explain how a shared length and a shared clock let two towns compare their athletes fairly.

The big question

How did clocks, ropes and painted lines let people in different places compare races and games fairly?

A Clock on a Track

Track
Big questionHow did a footrace on a dirt path become eight lanes, a measured lap and a time on paper?
The story

A Hand Drops

At a spring meet, eight runners start at eight different marks and finish as eight written times.

It is a spring afternoon at an Illinois high school track. Eight runners wait in eight lanes. Their starting marks do not line up. The runner in lane eight stands far ahead of the runner in lane one.

The starter raises a hand, and the signal sounds. Near the finish line, a camera points straight across the track. A screen beside it waits to show each runner's time to the hundredth of a second.

One hundred years ago, this meet would have looked different. The track was likely made of crushed cinders. Timers stood at the line holding stopwatches. They wrote down times to a fifth of a second.

Nearly three thousand years ago, runners at Olympia in Greece raced on a straight dirt path. No one kept a clock at all. Judges simply named who crossed first. The oval, the lanes and the clock all came much later.

Races Before the Oval

13.1

A Straight Race at Olympia

Main ideaThe oldest known Olympic race was one length of a straight track called a stade.

Olympia was a holy place in southern Greece. Greeks held games there to honor the god Zeus. By tradition, the first games took place in 776 BCE. Later Greek writers counted from that year.

The first race was one length of the . That word comes from the Greek , a measure of length. At Olympia, the track was a straight strip of packed earth about 192 meters long. Runners raced from one end to the other.

The Greek writer Pausanias visited Olympia in the 100s CE. He wrote that the footrace was the only event at the first games. He named the winner as Coroebus of Elis. Historians treat these early lists as tradition, not as exact records.

13.2

Who Could Run at Olympia

Main ideaOnly free Greek men and boys could compete at the ancient Olympic Games.

The ancient games had strict rules about who could enter. Athletes had to be free Greek men or boys. Enslaved people could not compete. People who were not Greek were also shut out.

Married women were not allowed to watch the men’s games. Pausanias wrote that girls held their own footraces at Olympia. These were the Heraia, games honoring the goddess Hera. The girls ran a track shorter than the men’s stade.

The modern Olympics began in Athens in 1896. Women did not run track events at the Olympics until 1928, in Amsterdam. That year, women raced the 100 meters and the 800 meters for the first time.

13.3

Yards, Miles and Meters

Main ideaOlder American and British tracks used a 440-yard lap; the world later settled on 400 meters.

In the 1800s, British and American runners measured races in yards and miles. Many tracks were one quarter of a mile around. That lap is 440 yards. Four laps made one .

France created the in the 1790s as a new measure. Most of Europe later used it for races. The modern Olympics measured track races in meters from 1896 on. A 440-yard lap is about 402 meters, a little longer than 400.

Two measures made trouble. A time for 440 yards could not sit on the same list as a time for 400 meters. Over the 1900s, most American high school and college tracks switched to meters. Today the outdoor lap is 400 meters in almost every country.

Lanes and Surfaces

13.4

The World's Track Rules

Main ideaA world body, now called World Athletics, writes the rules that make every standard track the same.

In 1912, leaders from many countries met in Stockholm, Sweden. They formed the International Amateur Athletic Federation, or IAAF. Its job was to write one set of track rules. It also kept the list of official world records.

The IAAF renamed itself in 2019. Its rule book says a standard outdoor track is 400 meters in the inside lane. Each may be up to 1.22 meters wide. Most standard tracks have eight lanes.

School tracks in the United States follow a rule book from the National Federation of State High School Associations, or NFHS. The IHSA uses NFHS rules for Illinois meets. Those rules also measure races in meters.

13.5

Why Starts Are Staggered

Main ideaStaggered starting marks make every lane the same distance in a race that goes around a curve.

A track has two straight sides and two curves. On a curve, an outer lane makes a bigger circle than an inner lane. If everyone started on one line, the outside runner would run farther.

So officials paint a . That is a set of starting marks that step forward, lane by lane. The runner in lane eight starts farthest ahead. Each runner still covers exactly the same distance.

In races on one straight, like the 100 meters, everyone starts on the same line. Staggers matter only when the race runs around a curve. The finish line is one straight line across all lanes.

From Cinders to Rubber

Main ideaTracks changed from dirt and cinders to all-weather rubber surfaces, first used at an Olympics in 1968.

Early tracks were packed dirt or grass. Later, many were covered with . Cinders are crushed leftovers from burned coal. Rain turned them soft, and runners’ spikes dug deep holes.

In the 1960s, companies made new track surfaces from rubber and plastic. These are called tracks. They drain rain and stay the same in all weather. The 1968 Olympic Games in Mexico City were the first to use one.

Before blocks, sprinters dug small holes in cinders to push from at the start. are metal frames that replaced those holes. They came into wide use in the 1930s and 1940s. Today, rule books require blocks for short races at big meets.

Time on Paper

13.7

The Hand-Held Stopwatch

Main ideaStopwatches let officials write down a race as a time, so meets on different days could be compared.

At first, a race only showed who finished first. A changed that. It is a watch that starts and stops when a button is pressed. Now each race could leave behind a number.

At early meets, a stood at the finish with a stopwatch. Several timers often clocked the same runner. Early watches measured to a fifth of a second. Later ones measured to a tenth.

Hand timing has a problem. People press the button a little late or early. So a time from one timer may not match another. Record keepers needed a clock that did not depend on a human thumb.

13.8

The Photo Finish

Main ideaCameras and electronic clocks now decide close races and record times to the hundredth of a second.

Sometimes two runners reach the line together. The eye cannot tell who was first. A camera solves this. It points straight along the finish line and takes a picture of the moment runners cross.

Photo-finish cameras spread at big meets in the 1900s. They were joined to electronic clocks. The start signal starts the clock by itself. The camera shows the exact time each runner reaches the line.

This is called . It measures to the hundredth of a second. From 1977, the IAAF accepted world records in short races only if they were timed this way.

14.1

Races in Open Water

Main ideaThe first modern Olympic swimming races were held in open water; a built pool came in 1908.

At the first modern Olympics in Athens in 1896, swimmers raced in the sea. The races were held in a bay near the port of Piraeus. The water was cold and rough. There were no walls and no lanes.

At the 1900 Paris Games, swimmers raced in the river Seine. The river’s current pushed them along. That made times faster than in still water. Those times could not be compared with times from other places.

For the 1908 London Games, a was built inside the main stadium. It was 100 meters long. For the first time, Olympic swimmers raced in a still, measured tank. Later Games used built pools every time.

14.3

Lane Lines and Walls

Main ideaFloating lane lines and painted lines on the pool floor give each swimmer a fair, marked path.

A is a rope strung with floats. It runs from one end wall to the other. It marks where one swimmer’s lane ends and the next begins. Rule books set how many lanes a race pool has.

Early lane lines were simple ropes with cork floats. Later designs used plastic discs that spin in the water. These discs break up waves. So a swimmer in one lane is less bothered by splashing next door.

Rule books also call for a dark line on the floor down each lane. At each end, a can sit on the wall. When a swimmer touches it, the clock stops. This is how pools use automatic timing.

Real Tennis Indoors

Main ideaLong before lawn tennis, people played an older game, now called real tennis, in walled indoor courts.

In France in the Middle Ages, people played a ball game called . The name means game of the palm. At first, players hit the ball with their hands. Later they used rackets.

The game moved into large walled rooms. In English it became known as tennis. Today it is called , to tell it apart from lawn tennis. King Henry VIII had a court built at Hampton Court Palace near London.

Real tennis courts were costly. Mostly kings, nobles and rich men played. Each court was a bit different in size and shape. That made it hard to set one plan everyone could share.

Wingfield's Boxed Game

Main ideaWalter Clopton Wingfield introduced a lawn tennis game in 1873 and patented it in 1874.

Walter Clopton Wingfield was a British army officer. In 1873 he introduced an outdoor game for lawns. In 1874 he took out a on it. He gave it a Greek name, , meaning ball game.

Wingfield sold the game in a box. It held rackets, balls, a net, poles and a printed rule book. Buyers marked out the court on their own grass. His court was wider at the ends and narrow at the net, like an hourglass.

The boxed set sold well in Britain and beyond. Lawn tennis spread to gardens, clubs and parks. Players soon changed his rules and his court shape. But his box helped many people start playing the same game.

Main ideaThe first Wimbledon tournament in 1877 used a rectangle court that became the standard.

The All England Croquet Club was in Wimbledon, near London. In 1875 it added lawn tennis. In 1877 it held a lawn tennis championship. This was the first .

For that event, the club wrote its own rules. It dropped the hourglass shape. The singles court became a rectangle 78 feet long and 27 feet wide. That shape is still used today.

Twenty-two men entered the first championship. Spencer Gore won. Women were not included at first. Wimbledon added a women’s singles event in 1884, and Maud Watson won it.
