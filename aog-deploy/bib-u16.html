Reading the Gospels Closely

Gospels Close Up

This unit slows down. Earlier units walked through the whole Bible; this one stays inside the four gospels and reads a few chapters closely. First comes the teaching of Jesus: the long sermon in Matthew 5–7, its shorter cousin in Luke 6, and the parables, short stories that end with a turn. Then come the last chapters of each gospel, the Passion narratives, which describe one Friday four times, and the accounts of an empty tomb.

Close reading means noticing. Which gospel says "the poor" and which says "the poor in spirit"? Which manuscripts end Mark at 16:8? What does Luke's Jesus say from the cross that John's does not? Every difference is evidence of what a writer chose, and every reader, Jewish, Christian, Muslim, secular, brings a way of reading. This unit teaches you to name the text, the writer, the manuscript and the reader before you say what a passage means.

By the end you will be able to compare parallel gospel passages, explain how a parable works, describe what historians can and cannot say about Jesus of Nazareth, and state how Jewish, Christian and Muslim traditions each understand him, without deciding for anyone which is right. That is the skill a public school can teach, and it is a skill you can use on any text.

The story

A Question Answered With a Story

A legal expert asks for a definition and gets a road, a wounded man and three travelers instead.

Luke 10 sets the scene. A man the text calls a lawyer, an expert in the Torah, the law of Moses, stands up to test a traveling teacher named Jesus. He asks what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus turns the question back: what does the law say? The lawyer answers with two commands from his own scriptures: love God with all your heart (Deuteronomy 6:5) and love your neighbor as yourself (Leviticus 19:18). Jesus tells him he has answered well.

Then the lawyer asks a second question, and Luke says he asked it to justify himself: "And who is my neighbor?" It is a fair question. A word like neighbor needs edges. Does it mean the person next door, a fellow Israelite, anyone at all? A teacher could have given a definition. This teacher does not. He tells a story.

A man walks the steep road down from Jerusalem to Jericho. Robbers beat him, strip him and leave him half dead. A priest comes by, sees him, and passes on the other side. A Levite, a temple worker, does the same. Then a Samaritan comes. Samaritans and Jews in the first century were neighbors who did not get along; each side saw the other as worshiping wrongly. The Samaritan stops, bandages the wounds, lifts the man onto his own animal, pays an innkeeper and promises to come back.

Jesus does not answer the lawyer's question. He asks a different one: which of these three was a neighbor to the man who was robbed? The lawyer cannot bring himself to say the word Samaritan. He says, "the one who showed mercy." Jesus says: go and do the same. The question "who counts as my neighbor?" has been replaced by "how do I act as one?"

This chapter reads the teaching of Jesus the way the lawyer heard it: as sayings and stories that refuse to sit still. We start with the long sermon in Matthew 5–7, compare it with Luke's shorter version, and then study the parable as a form. Along the way we set Jesus beside a Jewish teacher of the same era, Hillel, whose sayings sound strikingly close.

The Sermon on the Mount

31.1

A Mountain and Nine Blessings

Main ideaMatthew 5–7 gathers the teaching of Jesus into one long sermon, and it opens with the Beatitudes, nine blessings that reverse everyday ideas of who is fortunate.

The of Matthew is one of four books in the New Testament that tell the life of Jesus. In Matthew 5:1–2 Jesus sees the crowds, goes up a mountain, sits down, and begins to teach. Chapters 5, 6 and 7 form one continuous speech. Readers have called it the since at least the fourth century; the name is not in the text. Many scholars think Matthew collected sayings that Jesus gave on many occasions and arranged them here, because Luke scatters the same sayings across his book.

The setting matters. In the Hebrew Bible, Moses receives the law on a mountain, Mount Sinai (Exodus 19–20). Matthew, whose gospel quotes the Hebrew scriptures more than any other, puts Jesus on a mountain to teach about the law. Many Christian and academic readers see a deliberate echo: Jesus is presented as a teacher who interprets the law of Moses, not as one who throws it away. Matthew 5:17 has Jesus say he came to fulfill the law, not to destroy it.

The sermon opens with the , from the Latin word for blessed. Each sentence has the same shape: "Blessed are the [kind of person], for [what they will receive]." Matthew lists nine: the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek, those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers, those persecuted for righteousness, and those insulted for Jesus’s sake. The rewards are the kingdom of heaven, comfort, the earth, being filled, mercy, seeing God, being called God’s children.

Notice the reversal. A first-century audience, like a modern one, would expect the fortunate to be the rich, the strong and the comfortable. The Beatitudes name the mourning and the meek instead. Christians have read them in many ways: as a description of the ideal disciple, as promises for the world to come, or as a portrait of Jesus himself. The Beatitudes also echo Psalm 37:11, where the meek inherit the land, which shows the sermon drawing on the Hebrew Bible from its first lines.

31.2

You Have Heard, But I Say

Main ideaIn six sayings called the antitheses, Matthew's Jesus quotes a command from the Torah and then pushes it inward, from the act to the intention behind it.

Matthew 5:21–48 contains six sayings with the same pattern. Each begins "You have heard that it was said," quotes a command or a common reading of one, and continues "but I say to you." Scholars call them the , from a Greek word for setting one thing against another. The six topics are murder, adultery, divorce, oaths, retaliation and love of enemies. The first quotation is the commandment against murder (Exodus 20:13). Jesus adds that anger at a brother and insulting words are also answerable to judgment.

The word antithesis can mislead. In most of the six, Jesus does not cancel the command; he extends it. Not killing becomes not hating. Not committing adultery becomes not looking with lust. "An eye for an eye" (Exodus 21:24), a law that limited revenge to equal measure, becomes turning the other cheek. Many scholars note that Jewish teachers of the period also read commands this way, asking what a law is for and building a of stricter practice around it. Read like that, the sermon sits inside Jewish debate, not outside it.

The sixth antithesis is the one readers quote most. Matthew 5:43–44 has Jesus say, "You have heard that it was said, love your neighbor and hate your enemy; but I say to you, love your enemies." The command to love the neighbor is Leviticus 19:18. The words "hate your enemy" appear nowhere in the Hebrew Bible; they may reflect a popular saying or the attitude of some groups of the time. Jewish readers have pointed this out, and careful Christian readers agree that Jesus is not quoting the Torah at that point.

The section ends at 5:48: be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect. Christians have argued for centuries about how to live these sayings. Some have taken them as rules for every believer; historic peace churches such as the Mennonites refuse military service because of them. Others have read them as ideals that show how far everyone falls short, or as rules for a future kingdom. The text itself gives no instructions on how literally to take it.

31.3

The Prayer in Two Versions

Main ideaThe Lord's Prayer appears in Matthew 6 and, in a shorter form, in Luke 11; comparing them shows how the gospels shaped the same words and how manuscripts changed over time.

Matthew 6 turns from the law to religious practice: giving, praying and fasting. The text warns against doing each one to be seen. In the middle sits a short model prayer, Matthew 6:9–13, which Christians call the Lord&#x27;s Prayer or, from its first words in Latin, the Pater Noster. It addresses God as father, asks that God’s name be honored and God’s kingdom come, and then asks for bread, forgiveness and rescue from testing. Matthew’s version has seven petitions, requests, in about sixty Greek words.

Luke 11:2–4 gives the same prayer in a different setting and a shorter form. In Luke a disciple asks Jesus to teach them to pray, as John the Baptist taught his followers. Luke’s prayer has five petitions and begins simply, "Father." Most scholars think the two versions descend from one prayer that Jesus taught in Aramaic, the everyday language of Galilee, and that each gospel recorded it as its own community prayed it. Neither version is a quotation of the other.

Manuscripts add another layer. The closing line that most Protestants say, "for thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever," called the , is missing from the oldest surviving manuscripts of Matthew. Scribes seem to have added it from church practice. The King James Version (1611) includes it because the manuscripts its translators used included it; most modern translations put it in a footnote. Catholics traditionally end the prayer before the doxology, which is why the versions said in different churches differ.

The prayer’s ideas are Jewish ideas. Calling God father, asking that God’s name be sanctified and that God’s kingdom come, all have parallels in Jewish prayers such as the Kaddish, which asks that God’s great name be made holy and that God’s kingdom be established. Scholars do not agree on which prayer came first, or whether both draw on common language. What is clear is that the Lord’s Prayer, said daily by Christians around the world, is built from the prayer language of first-century Judaism.

31.4

The Golden Rule and Two Houses

Main ideaThe sermon closes with a one-line summary of the law, the golden rule, and a parable of two builders that asks the hearer to act on what was heard.

Matthew 7:12 gives a single sentence that readers have called the since about the seventeenth century: do to others whatever you would want them to do to you. The verse adds, "for this is the law and the prophets," which means that this one rule sums up the Hebrew scriptures. Luke 6:31 has the same saying in the Sermon on the Plain. The saying is phrased positively: do. Other versions of the rule, older and later, are phrased negatively: do not do to others what you would hate.

The negative form is well known in Jewish sources. The book of Tobit, in the Apocrypha, says not to do to anyone what you hate (Tobit 4:15). The Talmud tells of a non-Jew who asked the teacher Hillel, a generation before Jesus, to teach him the whole Torah while standing on one foot. Hillel answered: what is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; that is the whole Torah, and the rest is commentary, go and learn (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a). Confucius in China gave a negative form centuries earlier. Some Christian writers once claimed the positive form was superior; most scholars today see the two forms as expressing the same principle.

The sermon ends with a short , a story that teaches by comparison. Matthew 7:24–27: whoever hears these words and does them is like a wise man who built his house on rock; rain fell, floods came, winds blew, and the house stood. Whoever hears and does not do them is like a foolish man who built on sand, and that house fell with a great crash. Luke 6:47–49 tells the same story with a builder who dug deep for a foundation. The point is the same: hearing is not enough.

Matthew 7:28–29 says the crowds were astonished, because Jesus taught with authority and not like their scribes. A scribe, a trained copyist and teacher of the law, would normally cite earlier teachers for support. The sermon has cited scripture and then said "but I say to you." That is the claim the crowds heard. Christians believe that authority was real and divine; Jewish tradition has generally read Jesus as one Jewish teacher among many; historians describe the claim and study how it was heard.

Luke's Sermon on the Plain

31.5

Mountain and Plain Compared

Main ideaLuke 6:17–49 contains a shorter sermon on level ground with much of the same material as Matthew 5–7, and the differences show each gospel's choices.

Luke 6:17 says Jesus came down with his disciples and stood on a level place, and there taught a great crowd. The speech that follows, Luke 6:20–49, is called the . It is about thirty verses long against Matthew’s hundred and eleven. It contains beatitudes, the command to love enemies, the golden rule, the saying about judging, the tree known by its fruit, and the two builders. It leaves out the antitheses, the Lord’s Prayer and the teaching on giving, praying and fasting, which Luke places elsewhere or omits.

Why two sermons? Readers have answered in different ways. Some Christians, from the early church onward, have held that Jesus gave two similar sermons on two occasions. Most modern scholars think Matthew and Luke drew on a shared collection of sayings, which they call from the German word Quelle, source, and that each evangelist arranged it in his own way. On this view, Matthew built a long sermon that opens his account of Jesus’s teaching; Luke kept a shorter one and spread the rest through his travel narrative.

The differences are instructive rather than random. Matthew’s sermon is addressed to disciples in front of a crowd and is concerned with the law: fulfilling it, extending it, summing it up. Luke’s sermon is addressed to disciples and "a great multitude of people" from Judea, Jerusalem and the coast of Tyre and Sidon, which includes non-Jewish territory. Luke’s gospel as a whole is interested in the poor, in outsiders and in the reversal of fortune, and his sermon shows it.

Comparing the two versions is the basic tool of close reading in the gospels. Scholars lay the texts side by side in a , a book that prints parallel passages in columns. Doing so lets a reader see what each writer kept, added, reordered or dropped. That does not settle which version is older or closer to what Jesus said; it shows the shape of each gospel’s choices. Chapter 32 will use the same method on the Passion narratives.

31.6

Blessed Are the Poor, Woe to the Rich

Main ideaLuke's beatitudes bless the poor and hungry without qualification and add four woes, which gives his sermon a sharper edge than Matthew's.

Matthew 5:3 blesses "the poor in spirit." Luke 6:20 blesses "the poor." Matthew 5:6 blesses those who hunger and thirst "for righteousness." Luke 6:21 blesses those who "hunger now." Two words in Matthew and one in Luke change the meaning. Matthew’s blessings can be read as describing an inner attitude, humility and longing for justice. Luke’s describe a social condition: having no money and no food. Readers have argued for centuries about which is closer to the original saying.

Luke then does something Matthew’s sermon does not. After four blessings he gives four : woe to you who are rich, who are full, who laugh, and of whom everyone speaks well. A woe is a form from the Hebrew prophets, a cry of warning or grief, as in Amos 6:1 and Isaiah 5:8. Luke’s Jesus speaks like a prophet announcing a reversal. This fits Luke as a whole. In Luke 1 Mary sings that God has brought down the mighty and lifted the lowly, filled the hungry and sent the rich away empty (Luke 1:52–53).

Both readings have shaped Christian life. Liberation theologians in Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s, such as Gustavo Gutiérrez, read Luke’s version as God’s , a claim that the text puts God on the side of the poor. Other Christians read Matthew’s "poor in spirit" as the primary meaning and understand the blessing as spiritual humility open to anyone. Neither reading erases the other verse; the two gospels keep both in the canon.

For a student of the text, the lesson is about method. A single English word, poor, can carry a whole social theology or a spiritual one depending on two words that follow it. Comparing the versions does not tell you which is right. It shows you what each writer chose to say, and it warns you against quoting one gospel as if it were all four.

The Parable as a Form

31.7

Seed on Four Kinds of Ground

Main ideaThe parable of the sower appears in Mark, Matthew and Luke with an explanation attached, and it introduces both the form of the parable and a puzzle about why Jesus used it.

Mark 4 opens with Jesus teaching from a boat to a crowd on the shore. A sower goes out to sow. Seed falls on the path and birds eat it; on rocky ground, where it sprouts and withers; among thorns, which choke it; and on good soil, where it yields thirty, sixty and a hundred times over. The story is the of the sower. Matthew 13 and Luke 8 tell it too. In all three the disciples ask what it means, and Jesus explains: the seed is the word, and the four soils are four kinds of hearer.

The Hebrew word behind the Greek word parable is , which covers proverbs, riddles and short comparison stories. The prophet Nathan tells King David a mashal about a rich man who steals a poor man’s one lamb, and David condemns the rich man before realizing the story is about himself (2 Samuel 12:1–7). That is the pattern: a story from ordinary life that pulls the listener in and then turns. Rabbis of the following centuries told thousands of such stories, often beginning "a parable: to what is the matter like?"

Between the sower and its explanation stands a hard saying. In Mark 4:11–12 Jesus tells the disciples that they have been given the secret of the kingdom, but for those outside everything comes in parables, so that seeing they may not perceive and hearing they may not understand. The words echo Isaiah 6:9–10. Readers have long puzzled over this. Does it mean parables were meant to hide? Matthew 13:13 softens it to "because" they do not see. Many scholars think Mark is explaining, after the fact, why so many did not accept the message.

Most parables in the gospels come without an explanation. The sower is unusual in having one, and some scholars think the explanation, which reads like a sermon on the story, was added by the early church. Others hold that Jesus explained his own stories to his followers. Either way, the sower shows how the gospels treat parables: as stories about the kingdom of God that reward the hearer who keeps listening.

31.8

Who Is My Neighbor?

Main ideaThe Good Samaritan turns the question "who is my neighbor?" into "who acted as a neighbor?" and makes a member of a despised group the hero.

The story that opened this chapter is found only in Luke 10:25–37. Three men pass a wounded traveler. The first two are a priest and a Levite, both from the tribe that served in the Jerusalem temple. The third is a . Samaritans lived in the hill country between Judea and Galilee, worshiped the God of Israel on Mount Gerizim rather than in Jerusalem, and kept their own version of the Torah. Jews and Samaritans in the first century treated each other as heretics; John 4:9 says plainly that Jews did not share things with Samaritans.

A first-century Jewish listener would expect the third traveler to be an ordinary Israelite, a layperson, after two clergy. Making him a Samaritan is the parable’s shock. Readers have often guessed that the priest and Levite feared touching a corpse, which would make them ritually unclean; the text does not say so, and Jewish law required saving life over purity. The story does not explain their motives. It simply shows who stopped.

The ending is a of the question. The lawyer asked who counts as a neighbor, a question about the limits of duty. Jesus asks which of the three was a neighbor, a question about behavior. The lawyer answers, "the one who showed mercy," without saying the word Samaritan. The story defines neighbor by what a person does, not by who they are. Christians have read it as the heart of Jesus’s ethics; Jewish readers note that Leviticus 19:34 already commands love of the stranger, and that the parable stands within that tradition.

The Good Samaritan has become a phrase in ordinary English for anyone who helps a stranger. Many U.S. states, including Illinois, have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who give emergency help from being sued. Martin Luther King Jr., in his last speech in Memphis on April 3, 1968, retold the parable and said the priest and Levite asked what would happen to them if they stopped, while the Samaritan asked what would happen to the wounded man if he did not.

A Father and Two Sons

Main ideaThe parable of the prodigal son ends without telling whether the elder brother comes in, leaving the listener to finish the story.

Luke 15 holds three parables about lost things: a lost sheep, a lost coin, and a lost son. Luke says Jesus told them because religious leaders complained that he ate with tax collectors and sinners. The third story, Luke 15:11–32, is the longest parable in the gospels. A younger son asks for his share of the inheritance, goes to a far country, wastes it, and ends up feeding pigs, animals that the Torah calls unclean (Leviticus 11:7). Starving, he decides to go home and ask to be a hired servant.

The father sees him from far off, runs to him, and orders a robe, a ring, sandals and a feast. The son’s prepared speech is cut short. Christians have traditionally called this the parable of the son, from a word meaning wasteful; many modern readers call it the parable of the forgiving father, because the father is the figure who acts. Christians read the father as an image of God’s welcome to the repentant, and the story is read in many churches during Lent.

The story does not end at the feast. The elder son, coming from the field, hears music, learns the reason, and refuses to go in. He has served for years and never been given even a young goat. The father comes out to him too and says: you are always with me, and all that I have is yours; but it was right to celebrate, because your brother was dead and is alive. The parable stops there. Whether the elder brother goes in is never told.

The open ending is the parable’s method. Luke’s audience of complaining leaders is placed in the elder brother’s shoes and left to decide. Jewish readers have noted that the father’s welcome fits the Hebrew Bible’s picture of a God who receives the one who returns, as in Hosea 14 and Jonah 3. Some scholars also read the story politically, as a picture of Israel and outsiders; others warn against reading the elder brother as a stand-in for Judaism, a reading that has fed hostility in the past. The text itself names no nations, only a family.

The story ends with a saying that also appears just before it (Matthew 19:30): so the last shall be first, and the first last. The parable illustrates the saying, but the illustration is uncomfortable. Every reader who has held a job feels the dawn workers’ complaint. Nothing in the story says the owner was unjust; everyone received what was promised. The sting is that equal pay for unequal work feels wrong even when no one is cheated. That discomfort is the point the parable presses.

Christians have read the vineyard in several ways. Early readers took the hours as stages of history or of a life, with the late workers as those who come to faith late. Others read it as Matthew’s word to Jewish followers of Jesus who had kept the law all their lives and now saw Gentiles, non-Jews, welcomed on equal terms. Many modern readers see a teaching about , favor that is given rather than earned. Jewish sources have a similar story: a rabbinic parable in the Jerusalem Talmud tells of a king who paid a short-time worker a full wage because he had done more in two hours than others did all day.

31.11

What the Kingdom Means

Main idea"Kingdom of God" is the central phrase of Jesus's teaching in the first three gospels, and readers have long debated whether it means a present reality, a coming one, or both.

Mark 1:15 gives the first words of Jesus in that gospel: the time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe the good news. Matthew usually writes instead, using heaven as a respectful way to avoid saying God, a common Jewish practice. The two phrases mean the same thing. The Greek word basileia means a reign or rule more than a territory. Most of the parables begin by saying what the kingdom is like: a seed, yeast, a treasure, a pearl, a net, a feast.

The idea has roots in the Hebrew Bible, where God is called king (Psalm 47, Isaiah 52:7) and the prophets look for a day when God’s rule will be plain to all. Jewish prayers of the period, like the Kaddish, ask for that kingdom to come. Jesus does not define the term; he assumes his hearers know it and tells stories about it. That is one reason readers have disagreed about what he meant.

Three readings have dominated modern scholarship. Some, following Albert Schweitzer in 1906, read the kingdom as , a coming end of the present age that Jesus expected soon. Others, following C. H. Dodd in the 1930s, read it as already present in Jesus’s work: Luke 17:21 says the kingdom is within, or among, you. Most scholars today hold some form of both: the kingdom in the gospels is already at work and not yet complete. Christians across traditions pray for it to come and also speak of living in it now.

Jewish and Christian expectations differ here. Judaism has generally looked for God’s kingdom as a future age of peace, with the messiah as its human king, and has not seen it as having arrived. Christians believe the kingdom began with Jesus and will be completed at his return. Both draw on the same prophets. A student can describe this difference precisely without deciding it, and precision is what the sources require: which text, which phrase, which reader.

Main ideaHillel, a Jewish teacher a generation before Jesus, left sayings that sound close to the gospels, and setting the two side by side shows Jesus's teaching within first-century Judaism.

was a Jewish teacher active in Jerusalem around the end of the first century BCE. Tradition says he came from Babylonia, was poor as a student, and became head of a school whose rulings usually favored the more lenient reading of the law. His rival Shammai led a stricter school. The tractate , Sayings of the Fathers, records Hillel saying: if I am not for myself, who will be for me? And when I am only for myself, what am I? And if not now, when? (Avot 1:14). Jewish scholars such as Joseph Klausner in the 1920s and, later, Geza Vermes and Amy-Jill Levine have read Jesus as a Jewish teacher of the same world, whose sayings belong in that conversation. Many Christian scholars agree, and treat the parallels as evidence of a shared tradition rather than of borrowing in either direction.

The differences matter as much as the likeness. Hillel’s sayings are preserved as rulings and maxims in a legal tradition; the gospels present Jesus’s sayings inside a narrative that ends in his death and, Christians believe, his resurrection. Jewish tradition remembers Hillel as one of its founding teachers; Christian tradition remembers Jesus as far more than a teacher. Reading the two together is not a way to blend them. It is a way to see the first-century Jewish world both belonged to.

A class lays the last chapters of four gospels side by side and starts counting what matches and what does not.

The columns fill with color fast. A supper. A garden. An arrest at night. A hearing before the high priest, then a hearing before Pilate, the Roman governor. A crowd. A man named Barabbas released. A cross outside the city, two others crucified alongside, a sign over the head, a burial before sundown. All four have these. The order is almost the same. The students expected more disagreement than this.

Then the single-column marks begin. Only Luke sends Jesus to King Herod for a hearing. Only Matthew tells of Pilate's wife's dream and Pilate washing his hands. Only John records a long conversation between Jesus and Pilate that ends with the governor asking, "What is truth?" The last words from the cross differ: Mark and Matthew give one saying, Luke three different ones, John three others. The sign over the cross is worded four ways.

A student asks the obvious question. Which one is right? The teacher answers with another question. Right about what? If the question is what a Roman execution looked like, historians can say a good deal. If the question is what the death of Jesus means, that is what each gospel is answering, and each answers in its own way. A reader can hold both questions at once, as long as she knows which one she is asking.

Reading in Four Columns

Main ideaThe Passion narratives are the longest continuous stories in the gospels, and reading them side by side is the standard method for seeing what each writer chose.

The is the Christian name for the suffering and death of Jesus, from the Latin word for suffering. Each gospel devotes about two chapters to the last day, far more space than to any other single day. Many scholars think the Passion narrative was the first part of the Jesus story to be told as a connected account, before the gospels were written, because the four agree so closely on its order. The rest of each gospel is arranged more freely.

The four gospels are not four independent reports. Most scholars date Mark first, around 70 CE, and think Matthew and Luke used Mark as a source, adding material of their own. These three are called the , from a Greek word meaning seen together, because they can be laid in parallel columns. John, usually dated around 90 to 100 CE, tells the story in a different order with long speeches and few parables, and many scholars think it was written without direct use of the other three.

None of the gospels names its author in the text. The names Matthew, Mark, Luke and John come from titles attached in the second century, and church tradition identifies them with two apostles and two companions of apostles. Many scholars treat the authors as unknown early Christians writing for particular communities. This course uses the traditional names as labels for the books, as scholars of all views do.

Reading in columns has a name: , the study of how each editor, or redactor, shaped shared material. When Matthew keeps a scene from Mark but adds a line, or Luke drops one, the change is evidence of that writer’s concerns. The method does not tell a reader what happened. It shows what each gospel wants its reader to see, which is a different and answerable question.

Last Words and an Empty Tomb

32.5

Seven Sayings in Three Sets

Main ideaNo gospel records all seven last words from the cross; Mark and Matthew give one, Luke three and John three, and each set fits its gospel's portrait of Jesus.

Christians have traditionally counted seven , sayings of Jesus from the cross. Many churches read all seven together on Good Friday. But no single gospel has all seven. Mark and Matthew record one: a cry in Aramaic, translated as "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" It is the first line of Psalm 22. That psalm begins in abandonment and ends in praise. Readers disagree whether the cry is despair or a quotation that points to the psalm’s hopeful ending.

Luke has three different sayings, and none is the cry of Psalm 22. Jesus prays, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do." That verse is missing from some early manuscripts. He tells one of the men crucified beside him, who has asked to be remembered, that today he will be with him in paradise. He dies saying, "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit," from Psalm 31:5. Luke’s Jesus dies as he lived in that gospel: forgiving, welcoming an outcast, and trusting.

John has three others. Jesus gives his mother into the care of the beloved disciple; he says, "I thirst," which John links to scripture; and his final word is "It is finished," a single Greek word meaning completed or accomplished. John’s Jesus is in command to the end, as he was in the garden. The seven sayings, then, are three portraits laid over one another. Reading them separately, each in its own gospel, shows the portraits more sharply than reading them merged.

The same holds for the moment of death. In Mark the temple curtain tears and a centurion says, "Truly this man was the Son of God." Matthew adds an earthquake and tombs opening. Luke’s centurion says, "Certainly this was a righteous man." John has a spear thrust and blood and water. A student who notices which detail belongs to which gospel is reading the way scholars read, and also the way careful believers have read since the early church.

The Women at the Tomb

Main ideaAll four gospels say women found the tomb empty on the first day of the week, and Mark's oldest ending stops there, with the women afraid and silent.

Each gospel says that early on the first day of the week, Sunday, women went to the tomb and found the stone rolled away and the body gone. Mark names Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome. Matthew names two Marys. Luke names three women and others. John names Mary Magdalene alone, though she says "we" do not know where they have laid him. The number of women, the number of messengers at the tomb, one young man, one angel, two men, two angels, and what the women were told all vary.

Mark’s account has a famous puzzle. The oldest and best manuscripts, including and Codex Vaticanus from the fourth century, end Mark at 16:8: the women flee from the tomb, trembling, and say nothing to anyone, because they are afraid. A longer ending, Mark 16:9–20, with appearances of the risen Jesus, is found in later manuscripts and in the King James Version. Nearly all scholars think it was added in the second century, and most modern Bibles mark it off. Whether Mark meant to end at 16:8, or his original ending was lost, is still debated.

Christians believe that the tomb was empty because Jesus rose from the dead, and Easter, the Sunday festival of the , is the oldest and central Christian holiday. Historians can say that belief in the resurrection arose very early; the apostle Paul, writing around 54 CE in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, quotes a list of appearances that he says he received, which most scholars date within a few years of the crucifixion. What a historian cannot do with historical tools is decide whether a resurrection occurred. That is a question of faith, and this course describes rather than decides it.

One detail draws comment from every kind of reader. In a culture where women’s testimony carried little legal weight, all four gospels make women the first witnesses of the empty tomb. Christian apologists have argued the church would not have invented such witnesses. Some historians agree that the tradition is early; others note that the gospels also record women following Jesus throughout, so their presence at the tomb fits the story. Both note the same fact; they weigh it differently.

The Appearances and Their Settings

Main ideaThe gospels and Paul describe appearances of the risen Jesus in different places and to different people, and Christians hold them as the foundation of their faith.

John has appearances in Jerusalem, including one to Thomas, who had doubted. John’s final chapter is set by the Sea of Galilee.

The oldest written account is not in the gospels. Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians is usually dated around 54 CE. It lists appearances to Peter, to the twelve, to more than five hundred at once, to James, to all the apostles, and last to Paul himself. Paul says he passed on what he had received. Most scholars think the list is a formula he learned when he joined the movement, within a few years of the crucifixion. Paul does not mention the empty tomb or the women. The gospels do not mention the five hundred.

Christians across traditions hold that these appearances were real encounters with a risen Jesus. The Nicene Creed, recited in Catholic, Orthodox and many Protestant churches, says he rose on the third day. Within Christianity there are different views on whether the body was physically raised or transformed in some other way. The texts have been read to support both: Luke’s Jesus eats fish, and John’s passes through locked doors. Scholars who are not Christians describe the accounts as the earliest expressions of a belief that transformed a small Jewish movement.

The differences among the accounts have been studied for centuries. Harmonizers, from the second century on, have combined them into one sequence. Others, since the eighteenth century, have argued that the differences show the stories developing. A student needs neither verdict to do the basic work. Name each account, its date, its setting, its witnesses, and what it emphasizes. That is the work this whole unit has been practicing.

32.9

How Historians Weigh the Gospels

Main ideaHistorians use stated criteria to sort gospel traditions, and although they disagree on much, a short list of facts about Jesus commands wide agreement.

The is the name for two centuries of scholarly effort to describe Jesus using the tools of history. It began in Germany in the late 1700s, was surveyed by Albert Schweitzer in 1906, and continues today in a phase often called the third quest, which pays special attention to Jesus’s Jewish setting. Its practitioners include Christians, Jews and people of no religion, and they argue with each other constantly.

The tools are called . Multiple attestation: a saying or event reported in independent sources, such as Mark, Q, John and Paul, is more likely early. Embarrassment: a tradition awkward for the early church, like Jesus being baptized by John, whose baptism was for repentance, is unlikely to be invented. Dissimilarity: a saying unlike both Judaism of the time and the later church may go back to Jesus, though critics note it makes Jesus a stranger to his own world. Coherence with a first-century Galilean setting. None of the criteria is a proof; each is a reason to lean one way.

Applied with care, the criteria yield a short list that most historians accept. Jesus was a Jew from Galilee, from Nazareth. He was baptized by John the Baptist. He gathered followers, including a group of twelve, taught in parables and sayings, spoke of the kingdom of God, was known as a healer, and caused a disturbance in the temple. He was crucified by order of Pontius Pilate around 30 CE, and his followers soon afterward proclaimed that he had been raised. E. P. Sanders, whose 1985 book Jesus and Judaism helped shape the third quest, listed facts much like these.

Beyond the list, portraits diverge: an end-time prophet, a wisdom teacher, a healer, a social reformer, a messiah in his own eyes or not. The divergence is not a failure of the method; it reflects the limits of the evidence and the questions each historian brings. A student of this unit can recognize the moves. When a writer says "Jesus must have," ask which criterion is at work and which text is being weighed.

32.10

Jesus in Jewish Tradition

Main ideaJudaism does not regard Jesus as messiah or divine, and modern Jewish scholars have studied him as a Jewish teacher of the Second Temple period.

Jesus was born, lived and died a Jew, and every one of his first followers was Jewish. Judaism as a religion, however, has never accepted the claims Christians make about him. In Jewish understanding, the is a human descendant of David. He will gather the exiles, restore peace and see the world acknowledge God. None of that happened in the first century. Jewish tradition also holds that God is one and not a human being. So the Christian belief that Jesus is divine is, for Judaism, not possible. Jewish readers say this plainly, and Christian readers who study with them expect to hear it.

For most of the last two thousand years Jews said little about Jesus in writing. One reason is that Christian rulers punished what they took as insult. The Babylonian Talmud contains a few passages that some manuscripts and readers connect to Jesus. They are late, disputed and were censored in many editions. Medieval Jewish thinkers such as Maimonides, in the twelfth century, rejected Christian claims about Jesus. Maimonides also wrote that Christianity had spread knowledge of the Torah among the nations.

The modern change came with scholarship. Joseph Klausner, a professor in Jerusalem, published a Hebrew life of Jesus in 1922 that treated him as a Jewish moral teacher. Geza Vermes, a scholar at Oxford, titled his 1973 book Jesus the Jew. He set Jesus among the Galilean holy men of his day. Amy-Jill Levine, a Jewish New Testament professor in the United States, co-edited The Jewish Annotated New Testament (2011). In it Jewish scholars write notes on every book. The phrase names the period from the rebuilding of the temple in 516 BCE to its destruction in 70 CE. It has become the standard frame for placing Jesus.

Dialogue has followed scholarship. Since Nostra Aetate in 1965 and parallel Protestant statements, Jewish and Christian scholars have often taught the gospels together. Chicago has seen some of this work. The 1893 World’s Parliament of Religions in the city brought Jewish and Christian speakers to one platform. The city’s seminaries and Jewish institutions have hosted joint study since. Jews studying Jesus as a Jewish teacher, and Christians studying him within Judaism, are two different projects. They share a great deal of ground.

Main ideaIslam honors Jesus, called Isa, as a prophet born of the virgin Maryam who performed miracles by God's permission, but the Qur'an denies that he was God's son and, in most Muslim readings, that he was crucified.

They hold that his followers later altered it.

It was made to appear so to them. The next verse says God raised him to himself (4:158). The majority Muslim reading holds that Jesus was not crucified. Many Muslims also expect Jesus to return before the Day of Judgment.

Christians see the messiah, the Son of God, crucified and risen. Muslims see a great prophet, born of a virgin, not divine, not crucified, raised to God. Each tradition reads its own scripture, and each claims to describe him rightly. A public-school class describes all three accurately. It leaves the judgment where it belongs, with the reader.

Believing a Text and Studying It

Main ideaA person can read the gospels as scripture, as history, or as literature, and a public school teaches the second and third while respecting the first.

Three students read Mark 16:8 together. One is a Christian who believes the tomb was empty because Jesus rose; for her the verse is a doorway to Easter. One is Jewish and reads it as a text from another tradition, interesting and important, but not her scripture. One has no religion and reads it as a first-century Greek narrative that stops strangely. All three can answer the same questions: which manuscripts end here, what the women were told, how Matthew changed the scene. Their answers to those questions will match. Their answers to "is it true?" will not, and the class is not where that question gets settled.

This is the distinction between and . Devotional reading asks what the text means for me and my community, and it takes place in churches, synagogues, mosques and homes. Academic study asks what the text says, how it was made, how it has been read, and what can be known about its world. The two are not enemies. Many scholars are believers; many believers read critically. But they are different activities with different rules, and a public school does only the second.

The law in the United States draws exactly this line. In Abington School District v. Schempp (1963), the Supreme Court ruled that required Bible reading as a devotional exercise in public schools violated the First Amendment. The same opinion said that study of the Bible for its literary and historic qualities, presented objectively as part of a secular program of education, is consistent with the Constitution. This course exists because of that second sentence. It teaches about the Bible; it does not teach the Bible as true or as false.

The skill this unit has practiced is the skill the Court described. Lay the accounts side by side. Name the writer, the date, the manuscript, the reader. Attribute each belief to those who hold it: Christians believe, Jewish tradition holds, Muslims read, most historians think. Say what the text does and what it has meant. A student who can do that with the Passion narratives, the most contested chapters in the New Testament, can do it with any text she meets.

6. Which two non-Christian writers mention Jesus within about ninety years of his death?

Why: Josephus (c. 93 CE) and Tacitus (c. 116 CE) both mention him. The others lived before Jesus or did not write about him.

7. How does the Qur'an describe Jesus?

Why: The Qur'an honors Isa as a prophet and messenger born of Maryam, denies he is God's son (4:171), and says he was not crucified (4:157).

8. Which of these is a criterion historians use to weigh gospel traditions?

Why: Multiple attestation, along with embarrassment, dissimilarity and coherence, is a standard criterion. Popularity, length and translation are not.

1. The Sermon on the Mount is found in which chapters?

Why: Matthew 5–7 is the Sermon on the Mount. Luke 6 holds the shorter Sermon on the Plain.

2. Which blessing appears in Luke without Matthew's qualifier "in spirit"?

Why: Luke 6:20 says "blessed be ye poor"; Matthew 5:3 says "poor in spirit." The other three are only in Matthew.

3. "An eye for an eye" in the antitheses is a quotation from which book?

Why: Exodus 21:24 states the law of equal retaliation, which Matthew 5:38 quotes.

4. Why is the doxology of the Lord's Prayer printed in the King James Version but footnoted in most modern translations?

Why: The doxology is missing from the oldest manuscripts, so scholars judge it a later addition from church practice.

5. In the parable of the Good Samaritan, who are the two who pass by?

Why: Luke 10:31–32 names a priest and a Levite. The Samaritan is the one who stops.

6. What makes the parable of the laborers in the vineyard uncomfortable?

Why: Everyone gets the agreed denarius, so no one is cheated, yet equal pay for unequal work feels unfair. That discomfort is the parable's point.

7. Hillel's saying "what is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor" is found where?

Why: The Talmud records Hillel's answer to the man who asked to learn the Torah on one foot. Tobit has a similar rule but not Hillel's words.

8. What does the term Synoptic Gospels mean?

Why: Synoptic means seen together; Matthew, Mark and Luke share enough material to be laid in columns. John stands apart.

9. Which gospel shows Jesus stepping forward to meet his arresters with no anguished prayer?

Why: John 18 has no Gethsemane prayer; Jesus asks whom they seek and the soldiers fall back.

10. What did Nostra Aetate (1965) reject?

Why: The Vatican declaration stated the Passion cannot be charged against all Jews then or now.

11. Which gospels record "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"

Why: Mark 15:34 and Matthew 27:46 record the cry from Psalm 22. Luke and John give other sayings.

12. Where do Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus end the Gospel of Mark?

Why: Both fourth-century manuscripts end at 16:8, with the women afraid and silent.

13. What does Tacitus, Annals 15.44, say about Christus?

Why: Tacitus records only the execution under Pilate in Tiberius's reign, in his account of Nero and the fire of Rome.

14. How does Jewish tradition understand Jesus?

Why: Judaism awaits a human messiah who brings visible peace and holds that God is not a human being. The last choice describes the Muslim view.

15. What did Abington v. Schempp (1963) permit in public schools?

Why: The Court ended devotional exercises but said objective study as part of a secular education is consistent with the Constitution.
