Southeast Asia and Travel

Place

Southeast Asia is home to many fighting arts with their own names: Muay Thai in Thailand, lethwei in Myanmar, pencak silat in Indonesia, silat in Malaysia, arnis in the Philippines and Vovinam in Vietnam. Each grew in its own place, under its own kings, colonial rulers and new governments. In this unit you will study the objects, stadiums, laws and federations that shaped them.

These arts also traveled. Filipino workers carried eskrima to California. Indo families carried silat to the Netherlands. Thai trainers opened gyms around the world, and refugees carried Vovinam abroad. Some arts came to Chicago. By the end of the unit, you can name each art's place, explain its key objects and laws, and compare a village festival with a city gym.

The big question

What happens to a martial art's objects and rules when it moves from its home to a nation's stadiums and then to gyms around the world?

Muay Thai Objects

Muay Thai
Big questionHow did the objects of a Thai boxing gym, like the mongkon and the pra jiad, carry history from old Siam into a world sport?
The story

A Head Hoop on a Ring Plan

On a desk in a Bangkok gym, two small objects sit on top of a drawing of a boxing ring.

A drawing of a boxing ring lies on a desk in a Bangkok gym. It shows four corners, four ropes and a square canvas floor. On top of the drawing sits a stiff loop of rope wrapped in cloth. Beside it lies a thin band of knotted cloth.

The loop is a mongkon, a head hoop. A trainer places it on a fighter's head before a bout. The band is a pra jiad, an armband. Many fighters tie it around the upper arm and wear it during the fight. Neither object is part of the written scoring rules.

The ring plan is modern. Square rings with ropes came to Bangkok in the twentieth century, along with gloves, rounds and weight classes. The hoop and the armband come from older customs. Many Thai gyms treat both as gifts that link a fighter to a teacher, a family and a camp.

In the gym, these objects are not costumes. Trainers keep them on shelves or altars and hand them over with care. Their story shows how one sport kept older marks while it adopted new rules from Western boxing.

Siam Before the Stadium

17.1

Fighting Arts in Old Siam

Main ideaLong before modern rings, the kingdom of Siam had unarmed and armed fighting arts tied to war and festivals.

Thailand was called Siam until 1939. From 1351 to 1767, its main kingdom had its capital at Ayutthaya, a river city north of today’s Bangkok. Soldiers of Ayutthaya trained with swords, spears and staffs, and they also trained to fight without weapons.

Thai people today often call the older unarmed styles , which means ancient boxing. The armed art is called , named for the sword and the staff. Both were taught in villages, temples and royal circles, and they were often shown at temple fairs.

Records from these centuries are few. Much of Ayutthaya’s written history burned when a Burmese army destroyed the city in 1767. Historians piece together the older arts from later chronicles, paintings, poems and the memories of teaching families.

17.2

The Story of Nai Khanom Tom

Main ideaNai Khanom Tom is a famous Thai story about a captured boxer; it is honored as legend and set beside what records show.

After Ayutthaya fell in 1767, many Siamese people were taken to Burma as captives. A well-known story says one of them, , fought Burmese boxers before the Burmese king. In the story he wins match after match and earns his freedom.

The story comes from later retellings, not from a record written at the time of the matches. Historians treat it as a , a story handed down that may hold some truth but cannot be checked. The details change from one telling to another.

The legend still matters as history of another kind. It shows how Thai people in the twentieth century linked boxing to national pride and survival. Thailand honors Nai Khanom Tom with a yearly boxers’ day, and statues of him stand in Ayutthaya province.

17.3

From Bound Cords to Gloves

Main ideaIn the early twentieth century, Bangkok boxing moved from cord-wrapped hands to gloves, rounds and a roped ring.

In older bouts, fighters wrapped their hands and forearms in long strips of cord or hemp. The Thai name for this wrapping is . Matches were held in open spaces, such as a temple yard or a cleared field, and a match often ran without set rounds.

In the 1920s and 1930s, Bangkok promoters and officials brought in rules modeled on international boxing. Fighters began to wear padded . Bouts were split into timed rounds, and a square ring with ropes replaced the open ground.

Reports of serious injuries in cord bouts pushed these changes forward. Weight classes, taken from Western boxing, later let fighters of similar size meet. The name Muay Thai, meaning Thai boxing, came into wide use as the sport took this new shape.

17.5

A Written Bout

Main ideaModern Thai professional bouts follow written rules for rounds, rest and scoring, set by stadiums and later by law.

A professional Muay Thai bout in Thailand usually lasts five rounds. Each round runs three minutes, with a two-minute rest between rounds. Three judges sit beside the ring and score each round, and a referee works inside the ropes.

Stadium rules also name what is allowed and what is a , a broken rule. Fighters must weigh in before the bout to prove they fit their weight class. Doctors check fighters, and officials record each result.

Fans at the stadiums have long bet on bouts, and the betting shaped how fights were watched. Many Thai fans say early rounds are often slower, and later rounds decide the score. Scoring customs like this are part of the stadium’s own culture, and they differ from Olympic-style amateur rules.

17.6

The Boxing Act of 1999

Main ideaThailand's Boxing Act of 1999 put professional boxing, including Muay Thai, under national law and a national board.

For most of the twentieth century, each stadium and promoter set its own rules. In 1999 Thailand passed the , known in Thailand as the Boxing Act B.E. 2542. B.E. stands for the Buddhist Era, the calendar used for official dates in Thailand.

The law set up a national boxing board under the Sports Authority of Thailand. It required boxers, trainers, gyms, promoters and officials to , or sign up with the government. It also set rules for fighter safety and for young boxers.

The act turned the ring into a licensed public institution. A gym now needed papers, not only a trainer and a floor. Thai lawmakers have since debated changes to the rules for child boxers, a topic that remains part of public argument in Thailand.

Objects of the Gym

17.7

The Mongkon

Main ideaThe mongkon is a head hoop given by a teacher; many gyms treat it as a sign of the camp, not a costume.

The is a stiff loop worn on the head. It is usually made of rope or cord wound tightly and wrapped in cloth, with a tail at the back. In many Thai gyms, the trainer places it on the fighter’s head before the bout.

The fighter wears the mongkon while walking to the ring and during the opening ceremony. The trainer then removes it before the first round. Many trainers teach that a fighter should not touch it with the feet or leave it on the floor.

The mongkon links a fighter to a , the Thai gym where fighters live and train. A camp may keep its mongkon on a shelf or altar between fights. Some gyms outside Thailand now award colored hoops as ranks, a newer custom that Thai stadium camps did not use.

17.8

The Pra Jiad

Main ideaThe pra jiad is an armband many Thai fighters wear during a bout, often described as a gift from family or teacher.

The is a band of cloth tied around the upper arm. Unlike the mongkon, many fighters keep it on for the whole bout. Some fighters wear one band, and some wear one on each arm.

Thai families and trainers often tell how, in earlier times, a mother tore a strip from her own clothing and tied it on her son’s arm before he left to fight. Some bands hold small written prayers or objects inside. Like the mongkon, the pra jiad is described by many fighters as a gift and a reminder of home.

In some gyms outside Thailand, colored armbands mark grades, similar to belts in judo or karate. This is a newer use that grew as Muay Thai spread to other countries. The same object can mean a family gift in one room and a rank in another.

17.9

Wai Kru and the Ring Music

Main ideaBefore a bout, many Thai boxers perform the wai kru ram muay, a ceremony honoring teachers, to live music.

Before a traditional bout, many fighters perform the . Wai kru means to pay respect to the teacher, and ram muay means boxing dance. Fighters describe it as a way to thank their teachers, their camp and their family.

Each camp may have its own version, so fans can sometimes tell a fighter’s gym from the ceremony. The fighter circles the ring and bows in each direction. The mongkon is still on the fighter’s head during the ceremony.

Live musicians play during the ceremony and the bout. The group usually includes a , a reed pipe, along with two-headed drums and small hand cymbals called ching. The music speeds up as the rounds go on.

Main ideaMyanmar, Cambodia and Laos have their own ring arts; each country names and governs its art separately.

Thailand’s neighbors have ring arts of their own. In Myanmar, the art is called . It is known for bouts fought with wrapped hands instead of gloves, and for matches at village festivals and pagoda fairs.

17.11

From Camp to World Federation

Main ideaAn international federation for amateur Muaythai grew up and won full recognition from the IOC in 2021.

From the 1970s on, Thai fighters and trainers taught in Japan, Europe, Australia and the United States. Gyms abroad used Thai words and Thai objects, and many sent students to train in Thai camps. Amateur competitions grew beside the professional stadiums.

The , or IFMA, organizes amateur Muaythai around the world. Amateur rules use shorter bouts and require protective gear such as head guards. IFMA writes the sport’s name as one word, Muaythai.

The International Olympic Committee gave IFMA provisional recognition in 2016 and full in 2021. Recognition means the IOC accepts the federation as the world body for the sport. It does not by itself place the sport in the Olympic Games.

Competition has two kinds of events. In , two athletes compete for points. In , the artistic events, athletes perform set sequences alone, in pairs or in teams, and judges score them.

Main ideaPigafetta's record of the 1521 battle at Mactan shows armed island warriors, though it does not describe arnis by name.

In April 1521 the explorer Ferdinand Magellan, sailing for Spain, fought the forces of , a leader on the island of Mactan near Cebu. Magellan was killed in the battle. Antonio , an Italian who sailed with Magellan, wrote an account of the voyage.

Pigafetta described a large force of island warriors with spears, shields and cutlasses. His account is one of the oldest written records of fighting in the Philippines. It was written by an outsider who survived the loss.

Many Filipino teachers today honor Lapulapu as an early hero of their arts. The account shows that islanders were skilled with weapons in 1521. It does not name arnis or describe a school, so historians keep the link as a tradition rather than a proven line.

Antonio Pigafetta, account of Magellan's voyage, c. 1525, summarized (paraphrased)

18.6

A National Sport by Law

Main ideaIn 2009 the Philippines passed Republic Act 9850, naming arnis the national martial art and sport.

In December 2009 the Philippines passed . The law declared arnis the national martial art and sport of the Philippines. It named the Department of Education as one agency to carry this out.

The law asked schools to include arnis in physical education. It also asked the Philippine Sports Commission to support arnis competitions. This is how a stick art came to sit in school PE closets across the country.

A law like this is a kind of , a sign that a country uses to describe itself. Other national symbols include flags, anthems and national flowers. Naming a sport in law shows a government choosing which heritage to put forward.

From the source

Arnis is declared the national martial art and sport of the Philippines. The Department of Education shall include arnis in the physical education courses of schools.
Republic of the Philippines, Republic Act No. 9850 (2009), summarized (paraphrased)

18.7

Sport Arnis at the SEA Games

Main ideaArnis became a Southeast Asian Games sport in 2005 using padded sticks, head guards and scored rules.

Arnis first appeared at the Southeast Asian Games in 2005, when the Philippines hosted them. It returned in 2019, again in the Philippines. A host nation may add sports it cares about to the program.

Sport arnis uses and protective gear such as head guards and body armor. Judges score points under written rules. There are also form events, in which athletes perform set sequences for judges, much like the seni events of pencak silat.

Sport rules make the art safer to compete in and easier to judge. Some teachers say they also narrow what the art includes. The same debate appears in many martial arts that join big sporting events.

18.8

The Manongs of California and Hawaii

Main ideaFilipino workers who came to Hawaii and California in the early 1900s carried eskrima, which later opened to the public.

From 1906, Filipino men came to Hawaii to work on sugar and pineapple plantations. In the 1920s many more came to California to work on farms in places like Stockton and the Salinas Valley. They were later called the , from an Ilocano word for older brother.

The manongs faced hard limits. California law barred marriages between Filipinos and white Americans until 1948. Many worked long seasons for low pay and lived in crowded labor camps.

Some manongs practiced eskrima in back yards and union halls, often only with other Filipinos. In the 1960s teachers in Stockton began to open eskrima classes to the public. The art moved from a private family and worker tradition to an American storefront school.

18.9

Silat in the Netherlands

Main ideaAfter Indonesian independence, Dutch-Indonesian families moved to the Netherlands and brought pencak silat with them.

After Indonesia became independent, about 300,000 people moved from Indonesia to the Netherlands in the 1950s. Many were families, people of mixed Dutch and Indonesian descent. They arrived in a cold country that had ruled their birthplace.

Some Indo men had trained in pencak silat in Java. In the Netherlands they opened clubs in cities and towns, often in rented halls. For a time the arts were practiced mostly inside the Indo community.

Later, Dutch students of all backgrounds joined these clubs. The Netherlands became one of the first European centers of pencak silat. This is an example of diaspora history, where migrants carry an art and a new country slowly takes part.

18.10

Vovinam, From Hanoi Outward

Main ideaVovinam was founded in Hanoi in 1938, and Vietnamese refugees carried it abroad after 1975.

In 1938 Nguyễn Lộc founded in Hanoi, in what was then French colonial Vietnam. He built it from older Vietnamese fighting arts. He wanted a school art that Vietnamese people could see as their own.

Vovinam grew in southern Vietnam after the country was divided in 1954. When the Vietnam War ended in 1975, many Vietnamese people left as , people forced to flee their home country. Teachers among them opened Vovinam clubs in France, the United States and other countries.

Vovinam students wear blue uniforms at many levels, and belt colors mark rank. Today Vovinam has international federations and is a sport at the Southeast Asian Games. It shows a newer art founded under colonial rule becoming part of a diaspora.

The Gym and the Festival

18.11

A Chicago Room

Main ideaFilipino, Thai and other Southeast Asian families in Chicago helped bring these arts into city gyms and classes.

Filipino students came to Chicago in the early 1900s under the pensionado program, which sent them to study at American colleges. The program began in 1903. Filipino workers followed, and Chicago became home to one of the Midwest’s largest Filipino communities.

After the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended quotas based on national origin, many more families came from the Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam. Chicago’s Argyle Street area on the North Side became known for Vietnamese and other Southeast Asian shops. New arrivals brought foods, festivals and arts with them.

Today Chicago-area gyms and community programs offer classes in Muay Thai, arnis and silat. A city runs on schedules, fees and waivers. The same art in a village follows the calendar of festivals and families.

18.12

Both Rooms Are Real

Main ideaA village festival and a city gym are different institutions, and each is a real home for the same art.

In West Java, performers show pencak silat to the music of drums and a reed pipe at weddings and community feasts. The drum music for this is called kendang pencak. The performance honors families and guests, and no one keeps a score.

In a city gym in Chicago, Manila or Amsterdam, the same art may be taught for fitness, sport or self-defense. There are sign-up sheets, class times and belt or grade systems. The gym answers to its members and to local laws.

Neither room is fake. Each is an , a lasting set of people, places and rules. Historians ask which rules each room follows and who decides them, instead of asking which one is the true art.
