Workers, Makers and Markets

Producers and Consumers

Who made your lunch? Who made your shoes? Many people worked to make them. In this unit, you meet the workers and makers.

You will follow bread from a farm to your table. Then your class will hold a market day. You will buy, sell and count money.

By the end, you can tell a producer from a consumer. You can explain what a market is. You can say why prices go up or down.

The big question

How do workers, makers and buyers help each other get what they need?

Step 2

Producers
Big questionHow many people help bring one loaf of bread to your table?
The story

One Loaf, Six Helpers

A loaf of bread passes through many hands before you eat it.

Maya ate a slice of toast. She asked her dad a question. "Where does bread come from?" Dad smiled. "Let us follow it back," he said.

First, a farmer planted wheat seeds. The wheat grew tall and gold. The farmer cut it down. A truck driver took the wheat to a mill.

At the mill, a miller ground the wheat. It turned into soft flour. A baker mixed the flour with water and yeast. The bread baked in a hot oven.

A driver took the bread to the store. A clerk put it on the shelf. Dad bought it. Then Maya ate it. Six people helped make her toast!

Talk about itWho was the most important helper in Maya's bread story? Can the bread get to her without any one of them?
Section 1

Makers and Users

5.1

Producers Make Things

Main ideaA producer makes a good or gives a service.

A baker makes bread. A farmer grows corn. A person who makes things is a . The bread and corn are . Goods are things you can touch.

Some producers do not make things. A doctor helps you feel better. A bus driver gives you a ride. A helpful job like that is a .

So a producer can make a good. A producer can give a service. Both are work that helps people.

Think it through
The Busy Street

Look down a busy street. A baker pulls hot rolls from an oven. A barber cuts a man's hair. A woman sells red apples from a cart.

Which helper gives a service? Which helpers sell goods?

Words to know
producer

things people make that you can touch, like bread or shoes

Check yourself

1. A baker makes bread. What is the baker?

Why: The baker makes something, so the baker is a producer.

2. Which one is a service?

Why: A haircut is a job someone does for you. Shoes and bread are goods you can touch.

3. Which one is a good?

Why: Crayons are things you can touch, so they are goods.
5.2

Consumers Use Things

Main ideaA consumer buys or uses goods and services.

Maya eats toast. She is using a good. A person who buys or uses goods and services is a .

You are a consumer every day. You eat lunch. You ride the bus. You wear shoes. Other people made these things for you.

Producers make things. Consumers use them. Producers need consumers. Consumers need producers too.

Think it through
Breakfast Time

Who is the consumer at this table? Name one producer who helped.

Why: The bus driver gives you a ride. A ride is a service.
Section 2

The Path of Bread

5.3

Farmer, Mill and Bakery

Main ideaBread is made in steps, and each step has a worker.

Bread starts on a farm. A farmer plants wheat. The wheat grows all summer. Then the farmer cuts it. This is the first step.

Next, the wheat goes to a . The mill grinds the wheat into flour. Then a baker mixes flour, water and yeast. The baker bakes the bread.

Farm, then mill, then bakery. Each step needs the step before it. No wheat means no flour. No flour means no bread.

Words to know

Check yourself

1. What does a mill make from wheat?

Why: A mill grinds wheat into flour.

2. What comes first in making bread?

Why: Without wheat from the farm, there is nothing to grind or bake.

3. The mill is closed. What happens?

Why: Each step needs the one before it. No mill means no flour for the baker.
5.4

Trucks, Trains and Stores

Main ideaTrucks and trains carry goods, and stores sell them.

Wheat does not walk to the mill! A truck or a train carries it. Bread rides in a truck to the store. Moving goods is called .

At the store, a clerk puts the bread on a shelf. The store sells the bread to families. A is a place where people buy goods.

Drivers and clerks are workers too. They do not make the bread. But they help it get to you.

Why: A clerk works in the store and puts goods out for people to buy.

3. Is a truck driver a worker who helps bread?

Why: The driver does not make bread, but moving it is a job that helps.

Illinois has many farms. Farmers there grow lots of corn and . Some grow wheat too. Big fields stretch far and wide.

Corn feeds people and farm animals. Soybeans are used to make oil and food. Trucks and trains carry these all over.

Every farm needs workers. Someone drives the tractor. Someone fixes it. Someone sells the seeds. Every step is a job.

5.6

You Can Be Both

Main ideaYou are a consumer every day and a producer sometimes.

You eat, play and learn. You use goods and services. So you are a consumer every day.

You can be a producer too. You draw a card for Grandma. You help rake leaves. You made a good. You gave a service.

Most grown-ups are both. A baker makes bread at work. At home, the baker buys shoes. Everyone uses things. Many people also make things.

A Class Market Day

Markets
Big questionWhat happens when buyers and sellers meet at a market?
The story

The Bookmark Table

Room 12 sets up nine tables, and one sells out before recess.

Room 12 had a market day. The class set up nine tables. Kids made cards, bookmarks and paper flowers. Each table had a price sign.

Ana and Sam made bookmarks. They had tassels and bright stars. The sign said "25 cents each" (a class example). Kids lined up fast.

Before recess, every bookmark was gone! Other kids still wanted one. At the card table, only a few cards sold. The card sellers felt puzzled.

After school, the class counted the money. They talked about why the bookmarks sold out. They talked about what to change next time.

Talk about itWhy do you think the bookmarks sold out? What could the card table try next time?
Section 1

What a Market Is

6.1

Buyers and Sellers Meet

Main ideaA market is any place where buyers and sellers meet.

A has something to trade for money. A wants to get it. They need a place to meet.

That place is a . A farmers’ market is one. A store is one. Room 12’s market day is one too.

At a market, buyers look and choose. Sellers show what they have. When both agree, a sale happens.

Think it through
Saturday Market

Look at a farmers' market. Tables hold red tomatoes and yellow corn. A woman hands a bag of peaches to a boy. The boy's mom gives her money.

Who is the seller? Who are the buyers?

Words to know
market
a place where buyers and sellers meet to trade
buyer
a person who pays money to get something
seller
a person who gives something in trade for money
Check yourself

1. What is a market?

Why: A market is any place where buyers and sellers meet to trade.

2. Sam gives money for a bookmark. What is Sam?

Why: Sam pays money to get the bookmark, so Sam is a buyer.

3. Which one is a market?

Why: At a grocery store, buyers and sellers meet. So it is a market.
6.2

Setting a Price

Main ideaA seller sets a price, and a buyer decides to buy or not.

A is how much money something costs. The seller picks the price. Ana writes "25 cents" on her sign. That is a class example.

The buyer decides. Is the bookmark worth 25 cents to me? If yes, the buyer pays. If no, the buyer walks on.

Two bookmarks at 25 cents each cost 50 cents. 25 plus 25 is 50. The buyer pays 50 cents for both.

Section 2

How Prices Change

6.3

When Nobody Buys

Main ideaIf nobody buys, the seller may lower the price.

The card table sells cards for 50 cents each (a class example). Ten minutes go by. Nobody buys a card.

Leo has an idea. He lowers the price to 30 cents. Now three kids buy cards! A lower price can bring more buyers.

Sellers watch buyers. When things do not sell, a seller can try a lower price. Or the seller can make the goods better.

Read the numbers
Cards Sold

Made-up example for this lesson

What happened to cards sold when the price went down?

Words to know
lower the price
to make something cost less money
Check yourself

1. No one buys the cards. What might the seller try?

Why: When nothing sells, a lower price can bring more buyers.

2. Leo lowers the price from 50 to 30 cents. How much lower?

Why: 50 take away 30 is 20 cents.

3. Look at the Cards Sold table. At 20 cents, how many sold?

Why: The table shows 6 cards sold at 20 cents.
6.4

When Everyone Wants It

Main ideaWhen many buyers want something, it can sell out.

Ana and Sam made 20 bookmarks. Many more kids wanted one. Soon all 20 were gone. They .

When lots of buyers want a thing, it sells fast. Some buyers go home without one. The seller could make more next time.

A seller could also raise the price a little. Then some buyers may choose something else. Sellers watch and learn.

Think it through
The Empty Table

Picture the bookmark table after ten minutes. The box is empty. Four kids still stand in line. They look sad. Ana and Sam look at each other.

What could Ana and Sam do next market day?

Words to know
sold out
when a seller has none left because all of it was bought
Check yourself

1. What does sold out mean?

Why: Sold out means all of it was bought, so none is left.

2. Many kids want bookmarks. What happens?

Why: When many buyers want something, it sells fast and can sell out.

3. Ana made 20 bookmarks and sold 20. How many are left?

Why: 20 take away 20 is 0. None are left.

Good sellers think about buyers. What do my classmates like? Bright colors? Stars? Then they make that.

6.6

Counting and Fair Rules

Main ideaFair markets have honest signs, one price for all, and turns.

After market day, Room 12 counted the money. Ana sold 20 bookmarks at 25 cents (class example). 20 times 25 cents is 500 cents. That is $5.

A good market has rules. Signs tell the truth. Everyone pays the same price. Buyers take turns in line.

Fair rules help everyone. Buyers can trust the sellers. Sellers get buyers who come back. Kind markets work well.

Think it through
Two Signs

One table has a sign that says "Big cards." The cards are really tiny. Another table has a sign that says "Small cards, 20 cents." The cards are small.

Which table is fair to buyers? Why?

Words to know
fair
honest and the same for everyone
money
coins and bills people use to buy things
Check yourself

1. Which rule is fair?

Why: A fair market has one price for everyone.

2. Ana sold 4 bookmarks at 25 cents each (example). How much money?

Why: 25 plus 25 plus 25 plus 25 is 100 cents. That is $1.

3. Why should signs tell the truth?

Why: Honest signs help buyers trust sellers and come back.
