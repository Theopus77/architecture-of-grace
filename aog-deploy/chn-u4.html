What the Classics Are

The Books

Two short books from ancient China have been read for more than 2,000 years. One is the Analects, the sayings of Confucius. The other is the Daodejing, the book connected with Laozi. This unit is about what these books are and how they came to us.

You will see how the Analects was gathered by students, and how the Daodejing fits into 81 short chapters. You will learn how books were written on bamboo and silk. You will meet the old copies archaeologists found in tombs.

By the end, you can explain what each book is, who tradition says made it, and what scholars think. You can read a passage number like Analects 15.23 or Daodejing chapter 8. You can compare the two books.

The big question

What are the Analects and the Daodejing, and how did they reach readers today?

The Analects: Sayings Collected by Students

The Analects
Big questionHow did a teacher's spoken words become a book read for 2,000 years?
The story

What Do You Remember?

After their teacher dies, his students try to save his words.

The teacher had died. His students sat together in a quiet room. For many years they had followed Confucius. They had asked him questions. They had watched how he lived. Now he was gone.

One student spoke first. 'I remember what the Master said about learning,' he said. 'He said it is a joy to learn and practice what you learn.' Another student nodded. He picked up a thin strip of bamboo and a brush.

A second student remembered a different day. 'The stables burned once,' he said. 'When the Master came home, he asked only one thing: Was anyone hurt?' The brush moved again. Another strip was filled.

Each student had a different memory. Some were long talks. Some were only a single line. The strips were tied together with cord. Over many years, their own students added more.

The collection grew into a book. It did not tell one long story. It was full of short scenes, like snapshots. People would call it the Analects. Its words have traveled far from that quiet room.

Talk about itIf your class wrote down what your teacher says, what kinds of things would you save?

A Book of Sayings

7.1

Collected Sayings

Main ideaThe Analects, called Lunyu in Chinese, is a collection of short sayings and conversations of Confucius.

The Chinese name of the book is . It means something like collected or discussed sayings. In English, we call it the . That word comes from Greek and means gathered pieces.

The Analects is not a story from beginning to end. It is a collection of hundreds of short passages. Some are one sentence long. Others are short talks between Confucius and his students.

The book is split into 20 parts, called books. Each book is short. Scholars number each passage by book and passage. For example, 15.23 means book 15, passage 23. Different editions sometimes number a few passages differently.

7.2

The Master Said

Main ideaMany passages begin 'The Master said,' and 'the Master' means Confucius.

Many passages in the Analects begin the same way: ’The Master said.’ The Master is Confucius. His students used this title to show respect. Calling him the Master was a sign of honor.

Other passages are conversations. A student asks a question. Confucius answers. Often he gives different answers to different students, to fit what each one needed.

Some passages do not quote Confucius at all. Book 10 describes how he dressed, ate and greeted people. Some passages quote his students instead. These scenes help readers picture him as a real person.

7.3

Who Put It Together?

Main ideaConfucius's students, and their students, gathered the Analects after his death; scholars still study how.

Confucius did not write the Analects himself. Tradition says his students gathered his words after he died. That was in 479 BCE, by the traditional date.

Scholars think the book grew over a long time. Students of his students added passages too. Some parts may be older than others. Scholars which parts came first.

Confucius called himself a ’transmitter.’ He said he passed on the wisdom of the past. He did not claim to invent it. His students then passed on his words.

Section 2

How the Words Were Kept

7.4

Bamboo Strips and Cord

Main ideaEarly Chinese books were written on thin strips of bamboo tied together with cord.

In Confucius’s time, there was no paper. Writers used a brush and ink. They wrote on thin, flat strips of . Each strip held one column of characters.

The strips were tied side by side with cord. Then the bundle could be rolled up. A long book needed many bundles. Books were heavy to carry. Carrying books took real effort.

Sometimes the cords broke. Then strips could get mixed up or lost. Scholars think this is one reason some old books have passages in a strange order. This is still studied today.

7.5

Copies and Versions

Main ideaEarly scholars knew more than one version of the Analects, and later these were joined into the text read today.

Every book had to be copied by hand. Copies made in different places were not always the same. Scholars call these different forms . A copier could skip or change a word by mistake.

In the Han dynasty, over 2,000 years ago, scholars knew more than one version of the Analects. Later scholars compared them and joined them. The text most people read today comes from that work.

Archaeologists have also found old copies in tombs. One was written on bamboo and buried in a tomb over 2,000 years ago. These finds help scholars see how the book was copied. Scholars study finds like this with great care.

7.6

The First Line

Main ideaThe Analects opens with Confucius asking whether learning and friendship are not a joy.

The very first passage of the Analects is famous. It is Analects 1.1. Many Chinese students learned it by heart. It sets the tone for everything that follows.

In it, Confucius asks three questions. Isn’t it a to learn and keep practicing? Isn’t it a delight when friends come from far away? Isn’t a person good who stays calm when others do not notice them?

Readers see the whole book’s themes here: learning, friendship and good character. They note that Confucius links learning to practice, not only to reading.

Read for Centuries

7.7

One of the Four Books

Main ideaLater scholars made the Analects one of the Four Books that students studied for government exams for about 600 years.

In the 1100s, a scholar named Zhu Xi chose four short books. They were the Analects, the Mencius, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean. They became known as the .

From 1313, China’s government exams were based on the Four Books with Zhu Xi’s notes. Students who wanted government jobs memorized them. The exams went on until 1905.

The exams were hard. Few people passed. Many families could not pay for years of study, and women could not take them. Still, the books shaped how many people thought and spoke.

James Legge's Translation

Main ideaJames Legge, a Scottish scholar, published an English translation of the Analects in 1861.

James Legge came from Scotland. He lived in Hong Kong for many years as a missionary and teacher. He studied Chinese classics with Chinese scholars, including Wang Tao.

In 1861, Legge published the Analects in English. He added the Chinese text and long notes. He later became the first professor of Chinese at Oxford University in England.

A moves words from one language to another. Legge’s English is old-fashioned now. Many newer translations exist. His is still read, and it is free for anyone to use.

7.9

Readers Across East Asia

Main ideaThe Analects has been read in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam, and around the world today.

The Analects spread beyond China. Scholars in Korea, Japan and Vietnam read it for many centuries. Korea and Vietnam also held exams based on Confucian books.

Readers did not all agree about what it means. Some read it as a guide to government. Some read it as a guide to family life. Some debate whether is a religion or a way of thinking.

Today people read the Analects in many languages. Some admire it. Some criticize parts of it. Many simply study it as one of the world’s great books. Readers keep talking with the book today.

The Daodejing: 81 Short Chapters

The Daodejing
Big questionHow can a book so short be read in so many ways?
The story

Counting Characters

A girl counts the characters in one short poem and makes a surprising guess.

Lin sat at the kitchen table in Chicago with a small book. It was her grandfather's copy of the Daodejing. On each page was a short poem in Chinese, with English beside it.

'How long is this book?' she asked. Her grandfather smiled. 'Count the characters in one chapter,' he said. Lin put her finger on each character and counted slowly.

The first chapter had only about 60 characters. She turned the pages. Some chapters were even shorter. 'There are 81 chapters,' she said. 'So the whole book cannot be very long.'

Her grandfather nodded. 'About 5,000 characters,' he said. 'You could copy it all in a few days. But people have spent their whole lives reading it.' Lin looked at the book again.

Lin looked at the first line again. It said the Way that can be put into words is not the lasting Way. 'How can I read a book,' she asked, 'that says it cannot be said?' Her grandfather laughed. 'That,' he said, 'is where everyone starts.'

The Name and the Book

8.1

What the Title Means

Main ideaDaodejing means 'the classic of the Way and its power': Dao is the Way, de is its power, jing is a classic.

The title has three parts. means the Way, like a road or path. means power or virtue, the good strength that comes from following the Way. means a classic, an important book.

So Daodejing means the classic of the Way and its power. It is also spelled Tao Te Ching. That older spelling uses a system called Wade-Giles. James Legge wrote it as Tao Teh King.

The book is also called the Laozi. Tradition says Laozi, the Old Master, wrote it. So the book can go by the name of its traditional author, too. Readers use both names today.

Think it through
Three Parts of a Title

Dao = the Way. De = its power or virtue. Jing = a classic book. Put them together: Dao-de-jing. Older books spell it Tao Te Ching, and Legge wrote Tao Teh King.

8.2

81 Chapters in Two Parts

Main ideaThe Daodejing has about 5,000 characters in 81 short chapters, split into two parts.

The Daodejing is short. It has about 5,000 Chinese characters. An old history says Laozi wrote a book of about 5,000 words, so the number is famous. That is shorter than many picture books.

The book has 81 chapters. Many are only a few lines. The chapters are like short poems. Many lines have rhythm, and some rhyme in Chinese. Some chapters have fewer than 30 characters. Readers can read a chapter in a minute.

The book has two parts. Chapters 1 to 37 are often called the Dao part. Chapters 38 to 81 are the De part. Together they make the Dao and De of the title.

8.3

The Famous First Line

Main ideaThe Daodejing begins by saying the Way that can be put into words is not the lasting Way.

The first chapter is one of the most famous in the book. It says the Way that can be spoken of is not the lasting Way. The name that can be named is not the lasting name.

This is a . A paradox seems to say two things that do not fit. A book full of words says the Way cannot be fully put in words. Readers have thought about this puzzle for centuries.

Readers explain this in different ways. Some say words can point to the Way but cannot hold all of it. Some say the line asks readers to be humble about what they know.

8.5

Who Wrote It?

Main ideaTradition names Laozi as the author, while many scholars think the book was gathered over time.

Tradition says Laozi wrote the Daodejing. The historian Sima Qian told the story of Laozi writing it at a mountain pass. Many Daoists honor Laozi as the book’s author.

Many modern scholars think the book grew over time. They think it may hold sayings from several teachers. They often date it to about the 300s BCE, though dates vary.

Both views are held by thoughtful people. Readers can study the tradition and the scholarship side by side. You do not have to choose one to study the book.

Bamboo Found at Guodian

Main ideaIn 1993, the oldest known copies of parts of the Daodejing were found on bamboo in a tomb at Guodian.

In 1993, archaeologists opened an old tomb at Guodian, in Hubei province in China. Inside were hundreds of bamboo strips with writing. The strips had been buried for more than 2,000 years.

Some strips held parts of the Daodejing. Scholars date the tomb to about 300 BCE. That makes these the oldest known copies of the text. Before this find, the oldest copies known were the silk ones from Mawangdui.

The Guodian strips do not hold the whole book. They have only some of its chapters, in a different order. Scholars what this means about how the book was made.

8.8

Silk Copies at Mawangdui

Main ideaTwo copies written on silk, found at Mawangdui in 1973, put the De part before the Dao part.

In 1973, archaeologists opened tombs at Mawangdui, in Hunan province. One tomb was closed in 168 BCE. Inside were books written on cloth. Silk was costly, so these books were treasured.

Two of these were copies of the Daodejing. They are nearly complete. But they put the De part first and the Dao part second. Scholars call these two copies Text A and Text B.

Finds like these help scholars compare versions. They show that the book was read and copied long ago, and that its order could differ. Most editions today still follow the Dao-first order.

Read Around the World

Main ideaThe Daodejing is one of the most translated books in the world; Legge's English version appeared in 1891.

James Legge published an English Daodejing in 1891. He called it The Tao Teh King. It appeared in a series called the Sacred Books of the East. That series printed English translations of sacred books from Asia.

Since then, many more translations have been made. The Daodejing is one of the most translated books in the world. Translations often differ a lot, because the Chinese is short and open.

People read it in many ways. Daoist priests and believers treat it as a holy text. Philosophers study its ideas. Others read it for advice on leading or on living calmly.

Check yourself

1. When did Legge publish his English Daodejing?

Why: Legge's Tao Teh King appeared in 1891.

2. Why do translations of the Daodejing often differ?

Why: Its short, open lines can be carried into English in many ways.

3. How do Daoist priests and believers treat the Daodejing?

Why: Many Daoists honor it as a sacred text.
Chapter review

The Daodejing: 81 Short Chapters

0 / 8

1. What does Daodejing mean?

Why: Dao is the Way, de its power, jing a classic.

2. How many chapters does the Daodejing have?

Why: It has 81 short chapters.

3. What does the first chapter say?

Why: That is the famous opening of chapter 1. 'Learning is a joy' is Analects 1.1.

4. Where were bamboo copies of Daodejing chapters found in 1993?

Why: The Guodian tomb held them; Mawangdui's copies are on silk.

5. What is special about the Mawangdui copies?

Why: The silk copies put De before Dao.

6. Which line is from chapter 64?

Why: Chapter 64 gives the journey of a thousand li.

7. What do many modern scholars think about the author?

Why: Many think it took shape over time from several teachers.

8. How is Laozi honored in Daoist temples?

Why: Daoist temples honor Laozi as a deity.

Unit test

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Which book is a collection of Confucius's sayings?

Why: The Analects gathers the sayings of Confucius.

2. Which book has 81 short chapters?

Why: The Daodejing has 81 chapters; the Analects has 20 books.

3. Who gathered the Analects, as tradition says?

Why: Tradition says his students gathered his words.

4. What does 'the Master said' refer to in the Analects?

Why: The Analects calls Confucius 'the Master.'

5. What is the Daodejing's famous first idea?

Why: That is chapter 1. 'Learning is a joy' opens the Analects.

6. What does de mean?

Why: De means power or virtue.

7. What were many early Chinese books written on?

Why: Early books were often written on bamboo strips tied with cord.

8. Where were the oldest known copies of Daodejing chapters found?

Why: The Guodian bamboo strips, from about 300 BCE, are the oldest known.

9. What is unusual about the Mawangdui silk copies?

Why: They put the De part before the Dao part.

10. Who grouped the Four Books?

Why: Zhu Xi grouped them in the 1100s.

11. When did China's government exams end?

Why: The exams ended in 1905.

12. Who translated both the Analects and the Daodejing into English in the 1800s?

Why: Legge published the Analects in 1861 and the Daodejing in 1891.

13. What do many scholars think about the Daodejing's author?

Why: Many scholars think it took shape over time; tradition names Laozi.

14. What does Analects 7.1 show about Confucius?

Why: He called himself a transmitter, not a maker.

15. Which describes a paradox?

Why: Daodejing chapter 1 is a famous paradox.

Write it

Compare the two books. How is the Analects different from the Daodejing in how it was made and how it teaches? Use two or three passages, such as Analects 1.1, Analects 7.1 and Daodejing chapter 1 or 64 (Legge's translations), and say what readers have made of them.

  • Name the book and the passage number each time.
  • Say 'tradition says' or 'scholars think' when you tell who made a book.
  • Compare the two books; do not judge which is better.
  • Use unit words like passage, paradox and version.
