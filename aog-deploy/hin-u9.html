The Upanishads

Shruti II

A father dissolves salt in water. A boy waits three nights at Death's door. A woman scholar stands up in a king's court and will not stop asking. These scenes come from the Upanishads, the last layer of the Vedas, which scholars date from about 800 BCE onward.

In this unit you will learn the two great words of the Upanishads: atman, the self, and Brahman, ultimate reality. You will read famous lines such as 'That art thou' (Chandogya 6.8.7) and 'Lead me from the unreal to the real' (Brihadaranyaka 1.3.28). You will see how Shankara, Ramanuja and Madhva read the same words in different ways.

Then you will meet the people in the stories: Nachiketa, Svetaketu, Satyakama, Gargi and Maitreyi. By the end, you will be able to explain key ideas, cite passages and describe how different Hindu schools read them.

The big question

What do the Upanishads teach about the self and reality, and why do they teach it through questions?

Big Ideas
Big questionWhat do the Upanishads teach about the self and ultimate reality, and how have Hindu schools read those teachings differently?
The story

Salt in the Water

A father asks his son to find salt he cannot see.

Svetaketu has come home after twelve years of study. He is twenty-four and proud of all he knows. His father, Uddalaka, watches him for a while. Then he asks a question: have you learned the teaching by which the unheard becomes heard, and the unknown becomes known?

Svetaketu has not. So his father begins to teach. One evening he hands his son a lump of salt. "Put this in a cup of water," he says, "and come to me in the morning." Svetaketu does as he is told, though he does not yet see the point.

In the morning his father says, "Bring me the salt." Svetaketu looks in the cup. He cannot see any salt. It has dissolved. "Taste the water from the top," his father says. It is salty. "From the middle." Salty. "From the bottom." Salty still.

"In the same way," his father says, "you do not see the true being here, yet it is here." He ends, as he has ended other lessons, with words that have been repeated for many centuries. Many readers render them in English as: "That art thou."

This story comes from the Chandogya Upanishad, chapter 6. Hindu teachers, students and philosophers have discussed it for well over two thousand years. They do not all agree on what it means. You will meet several of their readings in this chapter.

Talk about itWhy do you think Uddalaka used salt and water instead of just telling his son the answer?
Section 1

What the Upanishads Are

17.1

Sitting Near a Teacher

Main ideaThe Upanishads are texts of teachings and dialogues, the oldest composed c. 800–200 BCE by scholarly estimate.

Picture a student sitting on the ground at a teacher’s feet, asking questions. Many scholars link the word to this picture: sitting down near. Others explain it as a hidden teaching. Both ideas fit the texts.

The Upanishads are the last layer of the Vedas. Tradition lists more than a hundred, but about a dozen are called the principal Upanishads. They include the Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya, Katha, Isha and Mundaka.

Scholars date the oldest ones, the Brihadaranyaka and the Chandogya, to about 800–500 BCE. Others came later, some after 200 BCE. Many Hindus regard them, like all the Vedas, as shruti, eternal truth that was heard by seers rather than invented.

The Upanishads are not a single story. They mix dialogues, riddles, lists and short poems. Many follow a teacher and a student, a husband and a wife, or a king and a sage as they ask what is truly real.

17.2

Vedanta: The End of the Veda

Main ideaThe Upanishads are called Vedanta, the end of the Veda, and they became the basis of the Vedanta schools.

Remember the four layers of each Veda. The Upanishads come last. So they are called , which means the end of the Veda. Many Hindus also read end as goal: the Upanishads show what the whole Veda aims at.

Later, Hindu thinkers built whole schools on the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita and the Brahma Sutras. These three are called the three starting points. The schools are also called Vedanta.

The most famous teachers include Shankara (c. 8th century CE), Ramanuja (c. 11th–12th century) and Madhva (c. 13th century). Each read the Upanishads differently. Their followers still debate these readings today.

17.3

Max Müller's Translations

Main ideaF. Max Müller translated twelve principal Upanishads into English in the Sacred Books of the East (1879 and 1884).

In the 1870s, a German-born scholar at Oxford named F. Max Müller began a huge project. He edited a series called the . It put texts from many Asian religions into English, fifty volumes in all.

Müller himself translated the Upanishads in volumes 1 (1879) and 15 (1884). These books are now in the public domain, so they can be quoted freely. They helped many Western readers meet the Upanishads for the first time.

Müller’s English sounds old today, with thee and thou. Some of his views about India reflected the attitudes of his time. Modern translators such as Patrick Olivelle offer newer versions. Readers often compare them.

Section 2

Atman and Brahman

17.4

Atman: The Self

Main ideaThe Upanishads use the word atman for the true self, which they describe as deeper than body and mind.

Ask yourself: who am I? My body? My thoughts? My name? The Upanishads ask this again and again. Their answer points to the , the true self. It is a word you will meet on almost every page.

Many passages say the atman is not the body, which changes and dies, and not even the mind, which changes all day. The Katha Upanishad says the self is not born and does not die. Hindus who follow these texts believe the atman is what passes from life to life in samsara.

The Upanishads say the atman is hard to see, like the salt in water. Knowing it, they teach, brings freedom. This knowing is not just learning facts. The texts describe it as a deep, direct understanding.

17.5

Brahman: Ultimate Reality

Main ideaThe Upanishads use Brahman for the ultimate reality that is the ground of everything.

In the salt story, Uddalaka speaks of a fine essence in everything. The Upanishads name the deepest reality . It is not one god among many. The texts describe it as the source and ground of all that is.

The Upanishads often say Brahman cannot be fully described. One famous phrase in the Brihadaranyaka is neti neti, not this, not this. Whatever you point to, the teacher says, Brahman is more than that.

Other passages describe Brahman positively, as being, awareness and bliss. Later Hindu teachers joined these as sat-chit-ananda. Many Vaishnavas and Shaivas identify Brahman with Vishnu or Shiva as a loving, personal God.

17.6

That Art Thou

Main ideaThe Chandogya Upanishad's teaching, often rendered 'That art thou' (6.8.7), links the self to ultimate reality.

Nine times in Chandogya Upanishad chapter 6, Uddalaka ends a lesson with the same Sanskrit words: . Many readers render them ’That art thou’ or ’You are that.’ The first time is in 6.8.7.

What is that? Uddalaka has been speaking of the fine essence in all things, the true being. So many readers take the phrase to say that the self, atman, is linked at its root to Brahman.

Not everyone reads the grammar the same way. Some later readers split the words differently to mean ’you are not that.’ This small difference shows how one line can carry very different ideas. You will see the main readings in the next section.

17.7

Shankara: Not Two

Main ideaShankara's Advaita Vedanta reads the Upanishads to say the self and Brahman are not two but one.

Shankara, a teacher from Kerala generally dated to the 8th century CE, wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Gita and the Brahma Sutras. His school is . Advaita means not two.

Shankara taught that Brahman alone is fully real. He read ’That art thou’ to mean the atman is identical to Brahman. The world of many separate things, he said, is appearance, called maya. Knowing the truth ends ignorance and brings moksha.

Advaita has been very influential. Swami Vivekananda, who spoke in Chicago in 1893, taught a form of it. Many Hindus follow other schools, though, and argue that Shankara’s reading leaves too little room for a personal God.

17.8

Ramanuja and Madhva

Main ideaRamanuja and Madhva read the same Upanishads as teaching a loving relationship between the soul and a personal God.

Ramanuja, a Tamil teacher of the 11th–12th century, read the Upanishads differently. His school is , qualified non-dualism. He taught that souls and the world are real, like the body of God, who is Vishnu.

For Ramanuja, ’That art thou’ means the self belongs to Brahman as a part belongs to a whole, not that they are the same. Moksha is loving service of God, reached through bhakti and God’s grace.

Madhva, of the 13th century, went further. His school is , dualism. He taught that God, souls and the world are forever distinct. Both schools remain alive in South India and beyond. Followers of each read the same verses with care and conviction.

17.9

Lead Me from the Unreal

Main ideaA short prayer from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (1.3.28) is still recited widely by Hindus today.

Many Hindu schools and gatherings open or close with a short Sanskrit prayer. It begins asato ma sad gamaya. It comes from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.3.28, where it is chanted as part of a ritual.

The prayer asks three things. Lead me from the unreal to the real. From darkness to light. From death to immortality. The text itself explains that the unreal and darkness mean death, and the real and light mean immortality.

The prayer shows how Upanishad words live on in daily use. Many families teach it to children. It is printed on school walls in India and recited at interfaith events. People of different schools each find their own meaning in it.

17.10

The Upanishads and the World

Main ideaThe Upanishads have shaped Hindu thought and have drawn readers from around the world.

In 1657 a Mughal prince, Dara Shikoh, had fifty Upanishads translated into Persian. He believed they shared truths with his own Sufi Islamic tradition. A French scholar later put that Persian version into Latin.

The German philosopher Arthur Schopenhauer read the Latin version in the 1800s and praised it highly. American writers like Ralph Waldo Emerson were also drawn to Upanishad ideas, which they read in translation.

In 1893, Swami Vivekananda brought Vedanta to Chicago. He founded Vedanta Societies in the United States soon after. For many Hindus, though, the Upanishads matter most as living teaching, studied with a teacher and lived with care.

Stories of the Upanishads

Dialogues
Big questionWhy do the Upanishads teach through questions and conversations, and what do their stories show about learning?
The story

Three Nights at Death's Door

A boy waits three days at the house of Death, and Death offers him three wishes.

A father named Vajashravas is giving away his wealth in a great ritual. But the cows he gives are old and worn out. His young son, Nachiketa, notices. He worries that such a gift is not a true gift. "Father," he asks, "to whom will you give me?"

His father does not answer. Nachiketa asks again, and again. At last his father snaps in anger: "I give you to Death!" The words cannot be taken back. So Nachiketa goes to the house of Yama, the lord of death.

Yama is away. Nachiketa waits at the door for three nights with no food and no welcome. When Yama returns, he is troubled. A guest has been left hungry. To make it right, he offers the boy three wishes, one for each night.

Nachiketa's first wish is that his father will be calm and glad to see him again. His second is to learn a sacred fire ritual. His third is the hardest. "When a person dies," he asks, "some say he still is, and some say he is not. Teach me which is true."

Yama tries to talk him out of it. He offers long life, gold, horses and kingdoms instead. Nachiketa refuses every one. "Keep your horses," he says. "This is the wish I choose." This story opens the Katha Upanishad.

A Boy Who Asks

Main ideaIn the Katha Upanishad, young Nachiketa questions Yama, the lord of death, about what happens after death.

The Katha Upanishad opens with a problem in a ritual. Nachiketa sees his father giving away useless cows. He asks, three times, whom he himself will be given to. His father’s angry answer sends him to Yama.

is the lord of death in Hindu texts. He is not a monster. In the Katha he is a wise teacher who respects his young guest. Because he left Nachiketa waiting three nights, he offers three , or wishes.

Scholars date the Katha Upanishad to roughly the 5th century BCE or later. Its story form makes deep ideas easy to follow. Many Hindu children hear the Nachiketa story, and teachers use it to praise curiosity and courage.

18.2

The Good and the Pleasant

Main ideaYama teaches that people must choose between what is pleasant and what is truly good.

Yama tests Nachiketa by offering riches. When the boy refuses, Yama praises him. He says there are two paths: the , the good, and the , the pleasant. The wise choose the good. The foolish choose the pleasant.

Yama then teaches about the self. He says the self is not born and does not die. It is hidden in the heart of every being. It cannot be reached just by study or cleverness, but by a mind that is calm and clear.

Many readers see Nachiketa as a model student. He cares more about the truth than about reward. Some Hindu teachers compare Yama’s test to the choices students face every day, like study or play.

18.3

The Chariot

Main ideaThe Katha Upanishad compares a person to a chariot, with the self as the rider and the mind as the reins.

Yama gives Nachiketa a famous picture. Think of the self as the rider in a . The body is the chariot. Understanding is the driver. The mind is the reins. The senses are the horses.

If the driver is careless, the horses run wild. If the driver is wise and holds the reins well, the horses obey and the rider reaches the goal. Yama uses this image to teach self-control.

Readers have loved this picture for centuries. It is similar to images in Greek philosophy, such as Plato’s chariot, though the two are not the same. Yoga teachers still use it to explain calming the senses.

18.4

Svetaketu Learns Again

Main ideaIn Chandogya Upanishad 6, Uddalaka teaches his proud son Svetaketu through everyday examples.

Svetaketu comes home after twelve years of study, proud and sure of himself. He thinks he has learned everything. His father, , asks a simple question he cannot answer. So the real lesson begins.

Uddalaka uses things from the kitchen and the yard. He shows how many clay pots are all clay. He asks Svetaketu to split a tiny banyan seed and look inside. There seems to be nothing, yet a great tree grows from it.

Then comes the salt in the water. Each lesson ends with the refrain ’That art thou.’ Many teachers point out the lesson inside the story: being proud of knowing a lot is not the same as understanding.

18.5

Satyakama Tells the Truth

Main ideaIn Chandogya 4.4, Satyakama honestly admits he does not know his father's name, and the teacher accepts him for his truthfulness.

A boy named Satyakama wants to study with a teacher. He asks his mother about his family line. She tells him she was a servant, moved from place to place, and does not know who his father was.

Satyakama goes to the teacher, Gautama, and tells him exactly that. The teacher is moved. He says only a true Brahmin would speak so honestly. He accepts Satyakama as his student and begins to teach him.

Many Hindu teachers use this story to say that truthfulness matters more than birth. Reformers have pointed to it in arguments against caste. Others note it still uses the word Brahmin. Readers discuss both sides.

From the source
An Honest Answer
The teacher said to him: no one who was not a Brahmin could speak so plainly. Go, fetch fuel, friend; I shall take you as my student. You have not turned from the truth.
Chandogya Upanishad 4.4.5, paraphrased (see F. Max Müller, SBE 1, 1879) (paraphrased)

Why does the teacher accept Satyakama? What does he value most?

18.6

Questioning as Learning

Main ideaThe Upanishads present asking questions as a respected way to learn.

Notice how many Upanishads begin: with a question. Nachiketa asks about death. Uddalaka asks Svetaketu about the teaching that makes the unknown known. The Kena Upanishad opens: by whom is the mind sent forth?

In these texts, good students ask, and good teachers welcome it. Teachers sometimes ask back, or give a riddle, or send the student away to think. Learning is a long , not a single answer.

Hindu traditions call a student who seeks this knowledge a jijnasu, one who wishes to know. The Brahma Sutras begin with the words ’Now, therefore, the desire to know Brahman.’ Many teachers today encourage students to bring hard questions.

Think it through
Openings

Katha: a boy asks what happens after death. Kena: 'By whom is the mind sent forth?' Brahma Sutras: 'Now, therefore, the desire to know Brahman.' Chandogya 6: 'Have you asked for that teaching?'

What do these openings have in common? What does that suggest about how these texts see learning?

Women Who Asked

18.7

Gargi at the King's Court

Main ideaIn Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3, Gargi Vachaknavi questions the sage Yajnavalkya in a public debate.

King Janaka of Videha holds a great contest. He offers a thousand cows, their horns tied with gold, to the wisest scholar. The sage tells his student to lead the cows away. The other scholars challenge him.

Among them is Vachaknavi. She asks what the world is woven on, like cloth on a loom. Each time he answers, she asks what that is woven on, and so on. Yajnavalkya warns her not to ask too far.

Later Gargi returns with two questions, which she compares to two sharp arrows. Yajnavalkya answers with the teaching of the imperishable. Gargi then tells the scholars no one will defeat him. Many readers see her as a model of fearless learning.

Think it through
Woven On What?

Gargi: What is water woven on? Yajnavalkya: On air. Gargi: And air? On the space between. And that? On the worlds of the gods... (Brihadaranyaka 3.6)

What is Gargi doing with her questions? Why might a teacher say, 'Do not ask too far'?

18.8

Maitreyi's Question

Main ideaIn the Brihadaranyaka, Maitreyi asks Yajnavalkya what will make her immortal, and he teaches her about the self.

Yajnavalkya is about to leave home to become a wandering seeker. He plans to divide his property between his two wives, Katyayani and . Maitreyi asks a question: if the whole earth full of wealth were mine, would it make me immortal?

Yajnavalkya says no. Wealth brings only a wealthy life. Maitreyi answers: then what use is it to me? Tell me what you know. Pleased with her question, he agrees to teach her about the self.

He says that a husband, a wife, children and wealth are dear not for their own sake but for the sake of the self. The self must be seen, heard and reflected on. This dialogue appears twice, in chapters 2 and 4.

From the source
What Use Is Wealth?
Maitreyi asks: if this whole earth full of wealth were mine, would I become immortal by it? Yajnavalkya answers: no; your life would be like the life of rich people, but there is no hope of immortality through wealth.
Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.2, paraphrased (see F. Max Müller, SBE 15, 1884) (paraphrased)

What does Maitreyi choose instead of wealth? How is her choice like Nachiketa's?

18.9

Reading the Stories Today

Main ideaHindus and scholars today read the Upanishad stories as teachings about curiosity, honesty and who may learn.

Many Hindu families tell Nachiketa’s story to praise courage. Satyakama’s story is told to praise honesty. Gargi and Maitreyi are often named as women philosophers from ancient India, and Indian schools and prizes carry Gargi’s name.

Scholars add context. The stories come from a world where most teachers were men of priestly families. Gargi and Maitreyi stand out partly because they are rare in the texts. Scholars debate how much the stories reflect real history.

Still, the pattern is clear. In the Upanishads, a good question can come from a boy, a servant’s son or a woman. Many readers find in that pattern a lasting lesson about who has the right to ask.
