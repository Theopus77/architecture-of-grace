The World

Look around your home and your street. You might eat food from Mexico, wear shoes made in Vietnam and hear a song from Nigeria. People all over the world are connected. This unit helps you see how.

First, you will learn what culture is. Culture is the shared way of life of a group: its language, food, beliefs, art and manners. You will see how people learn culture, and how cultures meet and mix. Then you will take a tour of Europe, Asia, Africa and the Americas.

By the end, you will be able to name the continents and read a map. You will be able to describe life in four regions. You will also be able to talk about other cultures with respect, and without stereotypes.

The story

Marco's Kitchen List

A boy in Chicago goes looking for the world and finds it in his own home.

Marco lives in an apartment in Chicago. One rainy Saturday, his teacher's homework seemed strange. Make a list of things in your home that came from another country. Marco grabbed a pencil and a notebook. He started in the kitchen.

The rice bag said it came from Thailand. The bananas had a sticker from Guatemala. His grandmother's tortilla press came from Mexico, where she grew up. The teapot was a gift from a neighbor who moved from China. Marco's list was getting long fast.

In the living room he found more. His sister's soccer ball was made in Pakistan. The TV was built in South Korea. A drum on the shelf came from Ghana. His dad had bought it at a street fair on the South Side. Even the word pajamas came from a language of South Asia.

Marco counted. He had found things from seven countries on three continents. He thought about it. His family ate tamales and fried rice. They watched soccer and baseball. They spoke English and Spanish. Maybe his home was not just one culture. Maybe it was many, mixed together.

The Shared Ways of a Group

7.1

What Culture Means

Main ideaCulture is the shared way of life of a group of people.

Think about how your family eats dinner. Do you use forks, chopsticks or your hands? Do you eat at a table or on the floor? Families in different places answer these questions in different ways.

These shared ways are called . Culture is the way of life a group of people shares. It includes language, food, clothing and beliefs. It also includes art, music, stories, holidays and manners.

Every group has a culture. A country has one. A town can have one. Even a school has its own ways, like its songs and rules. A is one habit that a group shares, like shaking hands to say hello.

7.2

Language, Food and Clothing

Main ideaLanguage, food and clothing are three parts of culture you can hear, taste and see.

Walk down a busy street in Chicago. You might hear English, Spanish, Polish and Arabic. Each is a way people share ideas with words. Around the world, people speak about 7,000 languages.

Food is part of culture too. In Mexico, corn tortillas are a daily food. In Japan, rice is served with many meals. In Ethiopia, people share a flat bread called injera. Families pass their recipes down from grandparents to grandchildren.

Clothing shows culture as well. In Scotland, some people wear a kilt on special days. In India, many women wear a long cloth called a sari. A piece of clothing that is part of a group’s history is called clothing.

7.3

Beliefs, Art and Holidays

Main ideaBeliefs, art, stories and holidays show what a group thinks is important.

Every year, many families in Mexico celebrate the Day of the Dead. They build a small table called an ofrenda. They put photos, flowers and favorite foods on it. The holiday is a loving way to remember family members who have died.

Holidays often come from . A belief is an idea a person holds to be true, like a religious teaching. Christians celebrate Christmas. Jews celebrate Passover. Muslims celebrate Eid al-Fitr. Hindus celebrate Diwali, a festival of lights.

Art and stories carry culture too. Groups tell old stories called folktales. They teach lessons, like being brave or kind. Painting, dance, weaving and music help people remember who they are.

Culture Is Learned and Changes

7.4

We Learn Our Culture

Main ideaPeople are not born knowing their culture; they learn it from family, friends and school.

A baby is not born knowing how to say thank you. She learns it. Her parents say it again and again. Soon she says it too. Culture works this way. People learn it by watching and listening.

Families teach songs, recipes and manners. Schools teach a country’s history and holidays. Friends teach games and jokes. Places of worship teach beliefs. Learning the ways of your group is called growing up in a culture.

Because culture is learned, anyone can learn about another culture. A child who moves to a new country can learn a new language. A family can learn to cook a new food. People can hold on to their own culture and learn a new one too.

Cultures Meet and Mix

Main ideaWhen people move and trade, their cultures meet, mix and change.

Chicago is famous for deep-dish pizza. But pizza first came from Italy. Italian families brought it to America. Then cooks in Chicago made a new kind in a deep pan. A food from one place changed in a new place.

The same thing happens with other foods. Tacos came from Mexico. Ramen noodle soup is popular in Japan, and its roots go back to China. Today you can eat all three in Chicago. When cultures meet and share, this is called .

People often move to a new country for work, for family or for safety. A person who moves to live in a new country is an . Immigrants bring their foods, words and music. Over time, these become part of the new place too.

Words That Traveled

Main ideaLanguages borrow words from each other, so English is full of words from around the world.

Say these words out loud: ketchup, pajamas, tomato, safari. They all sound like English. But each one came from another language. Words travel when people travel.

Pajamas comes from languages of South Asia. Tomato comes from Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs in Mexico. Safari comes from Swahili, a language of East Africa. A word that one language takes from another is called a .

Chicago’s own name has a story too. It comes from a word in a Native American language of the area. Many people say it named a wild onion or garlic plant that grew near the river. Place names often hold a piece of history.

Maps, Continents and Respect

7.7

The Seven Continents

Main ideaMost maps in U.S. schools show seven continents, but some countries count five or six.

Look at a world map. You will see big pieces of land with ocean around them. A very large piece of land is called a . Most U.S. schools teach seven: Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America and South America.

Not everyone counts the same way. In many Latin American countries, students learn that North and South America are one continent, called America. Some count Europe and Asia as one, called Eurasia. So some people count six continents, and some count five.

Asia is the largest continent. Australia is the smallest. Antarctica is covered in ice, and no one lives there all year. Scientists visit and stay there for months at a time to do research.

Maps and Globes

Main ideaA globe shows Earth's true shape, and a flat map is easier to carry but stretches some places.

Spin a globe in your classroom. It is round like Earth. A is a model of Earth shaped like a ball. It shows the sizes and shapes of land and water most truly.

A is a flat drawing of a place. You can fold it and carry it. But Earth is round, so a flat map must stretch some parts. On many world maps, Greenland looks as big as Africa. Really, Africa is about 14 times larger.

Maps have helpful tools. A compass rose shows north, south, east and west. A key explains the symbols. The equator is an imaginary line around the middle of Earth. It splits the world into a north half and a south half.

Many Countries Close Together

Main ideaEurope is a small continent with many countries and many languages close together.

In Europe, you can ride a train for a few hours and cross into another country. Europe is one of the smallest continents. But it has more than 40 countries packed close together.

8.7

The Empire of Mali

Main ideaLong ago, Mali in West Africa was a rich empire known for gold, trade and learning.

About 700 years ago, a great ruled much of West Africa. An empire is a group of lands ruled by one leader. This was the Mali Empire. It grew rich from gold and from salt, which traders carried across the Sahara on camels.

Its most famous ruler was Mansa Musa. In 1324 he traveled to Mecca, the holy city of Islam. He brought so much gold that people talked about him for years. Cairo, a city on his way, remembered his visit long after.

The city of Timbuktu in Mali became a center of learning. Scholars there wrote and copied books about religion, law, math and the stars. Some of these old books are still kept in Timbuktu today.

Section 4

The Americas

8.9

Indigenous Nations

Main ideaIndigenous nations lived in the Americas for thousands of years and still live there today.

Long before Europeans arrived, the Americas were home to many nations. The Maya built cities with tall stone temples. The Inca built roads through the Andes. In Illinois, people built great earth mounds at Cahokia, near today’s St. Louis.

The first peoples of a place are called peoples. In the Americas they spoke hundreds of languages. Each nation had its own leaders, farming, stories and beliefs. Many grew corn, beans and squash.

After Europeans came, Indigenous peoples lost much land and many lives to war and disease. But they did not disappear. Today there are 574 federally recognized tribal nations in the United States. Millions of people in Mexico, Guatemala, Peru and Bolivia speak Indigenous languages.

8.10

Many Languages in the Americas

Main ideaBecause of history, people in the Americas speak Spanish, Portuguese, English, French and Indigenous languages.

About 500 years ago, ships from Europe began arriving in the Americas. Spain, Portugal, England and France each set up colonies. A colony is land ruled by a faraway country. Enslaved Africans were also forced to come, and they shaped these lands too.

The colonies left their languages behind. Most of Central and South America speaks Spanish. Brazil speaks Portuguese. The United States and most of Canada speak English. In Quebec, in Canada, most people speak French.

Culture in the Americas is a mix. It blends Indigenous, European and African ways. Music like jazz from New Orleans and samba from Brazil grew partly from African roots. Foods like corn and chocolate came from Indigenous farmers.
