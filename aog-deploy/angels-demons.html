Angels and Demons

How messengers and opposing spirits appear in the texts of the ancient Near East, Second Temple Judaism, early Christianity, and related traditions. This page studies the sources. It does not assert that such beings exist or do not exist.

Sources

The figures called angels and demons in later tradition grow out of older Near Eastern ideas of divine messengers, guardian spirits, and chaotic or hostile powers. The texts below are the main witnesses.

  • Hebrew Bible — mal’akh (“messenger”) for beings sent by God; the satan appears as an accuser in the divine council (Job, Zechariah). No developed demonology.
  • Second Temple literature — 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and the Dead Sea Scrolls expand the picture: named angels, a fall of watchers, and opposing spirits.
  • New Testament — angels announce and assist; Jesus and the apostles speak of unclean spirits and of Satan as an adversary.
  • Early Christian and rabbinic writings — further classification of ranks and of hostile powers.
  • Related Near Eastern texts — Mesopotamian apkallu and protective spirits; Egyptian and Canaanite divine messengers. These supply the cultural background, not a direct identity.

The page uses published critical editions and catalogue descriptions from the institutions listed below. It does not claim access to restricted archives.

Angels

In the Hebrew Bible an angel is first of all a messenger. The word does not yet mean a fixed class of winged beings. Later texts add names, ranks, and functions.

  • Messengers — appear to Hagar, Abraham, Moses, and the prophets. Sometimes the messenger is hard to distinguish from God’s own presence.
  • Heavenly court — 1 Kings 22 and Job 1–2 picture a council. The satan is a member who brings charges, not yet a fallen opponent.
  • Named angels — Daniel names Michael and Gabriel. 1 Enoch and other Second Temple works add many more and organize them into groups.
  • Ranks — later Jewish and Christian lists (for example Pseudo-Dionysius) arrange angels in orders. These lists are medieval syntheses, not biblical inventories.

The consistent early function is service: carrying a word, guarding, or executing a judgment. Wings and halos are later artistic conventions.

Demons and opposing spirits

Hostile or chaotic powers appear under several names. They are not a single category in the oldest texts.

  • Chaos figures — Tiamat, Leviathan, and Rahab represent unordered forces. They are defeated or bounded, not worshipped as demons in the later sense.
  • The satan — in Job and Zechariah an accuser within the court. In later texts (and in the New Testament) Satan becomes the chief adversary.
  • Unclean spirits — in the Gospels, beings that afflict people and are cast out. The language is practical and exorcistic rather than systematic.
  • Watchers and giants — 1 Enoch tells of angels who descend, teach forbidden arts, and produce offspring. This story influences later ideas of fallen angels.
  • Protective and harmful spirits in Mesopotamia — apkallu, udug, and other figures appear on amulets and in incantations. They form part of the wider cultural setting.

A full dualism of good angels versus evil demons develops gradually. It is clearer in some Second Temple and early Christian texts than in the Hebrew Bible itself.

Key texts

Hebrew Bible

Genesis 18–19 (messengers at Mamre and Sodom), Job 1–2 (the satan in the council), Daniel 8–12 (Michael and Gabriel), Zechariah 3 (the satan as accuser).

1 Enoch

The Book of the Watchers describes angels who leave their station, the names of archangels who petition God, and the binding of the watchers. Fragments of 1 Enoch in Aramaic were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Dead Sea Scrolls

The Community Rule and the War Scroll speak of the Prince of Light and the Angel of Darkness, and of opposing spirits allotted to human beings. These texts are held by the Israel Antiquities Authority and published in the Discoveries in the Judaean Desert series.

New Testament

Angels appear at the annunciation, the resurrection, and in visions (Revelation). Unclean spirits are confronted in the Gospels and Acts. The picture is narrative rather than a systematic demonology.

Institutions that hold the sources

  • Israel Antiquities Authority and the Shrine of the Book (Israel Museum) — Dead Sea Scrolls.
  • British Library and the Bodleian Library — Hebrew, Greek, and Ethiopic manuscripts of 1 Enoch and related works.
  • Vatican Library — Greek and Latin biblical and patristic manuscripts.
  • Bibliothèque nationale de France — Eastern Christian and Jewish manuscripts.
  • Cambridge University Library — Cairo Genizah fragments that illuminate Jewish magical and angelic texts of a later period.
  • Critical editions published by the Society of Biblical Literature, the Oxford and Cambridge presses, and the Discoveries in the Judaean Desert series.
