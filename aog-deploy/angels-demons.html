Angels and Demons

How the figures later called angels and demons appear in the texts of the ancient Near East, Second Temple Judaism, and early Christianity.

Sources

The figures called angels and demons in later tradition grow out of older Near Eastern ideas of divine messengers, guardian spirits, and harmful spirits. A full dualism of good angels versus evil demons develops gradually. It is clearer in some Second Temple and early Christian texts than in the Hebrew Bible itself.

Angels

In the Hebrew Bible a malakh is a messenger, human or divine. Genesis 18–19 has messengers at Mamre and Sodom. Daniel names Michael and Gabriel. Zechariah 3 has an accuser in the council.

1 Enoch, the Book of the Watchers, names archangels and describes angels who leave their station. Aramaic fragments were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Demons

Harmful spirits in Mesopotamia include the udug and others named on amulets and in incantations. In the Dead Sea Scrolls the Community Rule and the War Scroll speak of the Prince of Light and the Angel of Darkness.

In the Gospels and Acts, unclean spirits are confronted. The picture is narrative, not a systematic demonology.

Key texts

Genesis 18–19, Job 1–2, Daniel 8–12, Zechariah 3. 1 Enoch. The Community Rule and the War Scroll. The annunciation and resurrection narratives, and Revelation.

Institutions

The Israel Antiquities Authority and the Shrine of the Book hold the Dead Sea Scrolls. The British Library and the Bodleian hold Hebrew, Greek, and Ethiopic manuscripts of 1 Enoch. The Vatican Library and the Bibliothèque nationale de France hold biblical and Eastern Christian manuscripts.
