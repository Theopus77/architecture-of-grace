The First Peoples of North America

History and Geography

Long before there was a place called Illinois, there was a city. Around the year 1100, tens of thousands of people lived, farmed and traded at Cahokia, on the Mississippi River. They raised a mound of earth taller than a ten-story building. They built it one basket of dirt at a time. Cahokia was only one of hundreds of nations that lived across North America, each one shaped by its own land: forests, plains, deserts, coasts and ice.

Then, in 1492, ships crossed the Atlantic. What followed changed both sides of the ocean. Corn and potatoes fed Europe. Horses changed the Plains. Diseases killed more people than any war. Spanish soldiers, French traders and English farmers each treated the peoples they met differently. Native nations fought, made deals, adapted and survived.

In this unit you will learn how the land shaped Native ways of life, what the meeting of two worlds cost and carried, and how to read a source by asking who wrote it and why. You will end able to explain the same event from more than one point of view, using real evidence.

The big question

How did the land shape the first peoples of North America, and how did meeting Europeans change both worlds?

The story

Market Day Under the Great Mound

Around the year 1100, the biggest city north of Mexico stood beside the Mississippi River, a short walk from where St. Louis is today.

The sun comes up over the river, and the city is already awake. A girl named Little Fawn walks with her mother toward the great plaza. Smoke rises from hundreds of houses with thatched roofs. Ahead of them stands a mound of earth taller than any tree. On top of it sits the house of the city's leader. Little Fawn has climbed the steps of that mound only once, on a feast day.

The plaza is bigger than a whole village. It is packed with people. Some have walked in from farms a day away. Others came by canoe from far up the river. Little Fawn hears words she does not know. Traders from the south have brought seashells. Traders from the north have brought copper that shines like the sun. Her mother trades a basket of dried corn for a small copper bead.

In one corner of the plaza, men are playing chunkey. One player rolls a smooth stone disk across the ground. Two others throw long poles, trying to land closest to where the disk will stop. A crowd shouts and bets on the game. Little Fawn knows this game is played in villages hundreds of miles away. Cahokia's ideas travel as far as its traders do.

Near the edge of the city, Little Fawn passes a circle of tall wooden posts. A priest watches the sun through the posts to track the seasons. He will tell the farmers when to plant. Corn, beans and squash feed this city of thousands. Without them, there would be no plaza, no mound and no market.

Nobody in Little Fawn's world writes anything down. So how do we know about her city? Archaeologists dig into the soil and find house posts, broken pots, corn cobs, shell beads and copper. They measure the mounds. They count the houses. From those clues, they rebuild a day like this one. Cahokia's people left no books, but they left a city.

Talk about itCahokia had no writing. What kinds of things would you look for in the ground if you wanted to learn how its people lived?
Section 1

Thousands of Years Here

11.1

The First People Arrive

Main ideaPeople have lived in North America for at least fifteen thousand years, and we know it from the tools and bones they left behind.

Long before any city, people walked and paddled into North America. Most scientists think they came from Asia. During the last , so much water was frozen in ice that the sea was lower. Land connected Asia and Alaska. People may have crossed that land, or paddled along the coast. This happened at least fifteen thousand years ago. Some sites may be older.

How do we know? Nobody wrote it down. Instead, people left things behind. Near Clovis, New Mexico, hunters made long stone spear points. They dropped them next to the bones of mammoths. Scientists call these , objects that people made. They can tell how old an artifact is by testing the bone or charcoal found with it.

Illinois has these clues too. Farmers plowing fields have turned up stone spear points thousands of years old. Along the rivers, people fished and gathered nuts. Slowly they learned to grow plants. By about a thousand years ago, corn had become the main food in many places. That change made villages and cities possible.

11.2

Many Lands, Many Ways

Main ideaNative peoples lived in hundreds of nations, each with its own language and its own way of living that fit its land.

When Europeans arrived in the 1490s, millions of people already lived in North America. They did not belong to one group. They belonged to hundreds of . A nation is a people with its own land, its own leaders and its own language. The Lakota, the Hopi and the Haudenosaunee were very different from each other. They were as different as Spain is from China.

The land shaped how each nation lived. In the forests of the east, people built homes from bark and grew corn. On the grassy plains, people hunted bison. In the dry Southwest, people built with mud brick and watered their crops by hand. On the rainy northwest coast, people fished for salmon and carved giant cedar trees. In the frozen north, people hunted seals on the ice.

Scientists call each of these areas a . Nations in the same region often shared foods, homes and tools. They faced the same weather and the same plants and animals. But they still had their own languages and stories. In this chapter you will visit five regions and one great city. Keep asking one question: how did the land shape the way people lived?

Section 2

Forests, Plains and Deserts

11.3

Longhouses of the Eastern Woodlands

Main ideaIn the eastern forests, people built longhouses from bark and grew corn, beans and squash together.

Picture a forest of oak and maple that goes on for weeks of walking. This was the Eastern Woodlands, from the Atlantic coast to the Mississippi River. Wood was everywhere, so people built with it. The Haudenosaunee of what is now New York built . A longhouse had a frame of bent poles covered with sheets of elm bark. It could be as long as a school hallway.

Several related families lived in one longhouse. Each family had its own space along the wall, with a shared fire in the middle. The oldest woman in the family was in charge of the house. Other Woodlands peoples, like the Algonquian nations, built smaller round homes called wigwams. Both kinds of home used bark, because bark was free and easy to find.

Woodlands farmers planted three crops in the same mound of soil. They called them the : corn, beans and squash. The corn grew tall. The beans climbed the corn stalks. The squash spread its wide leaves on the ground and kept the soil damp. Beans also put back into the soil what corn used up. Together, the three sisters fed whole villages. Corn from these fields still shapes what Illinois grows today.

11.4

Bison and Tipis on the Plains

Main ideaOn the treeless Great Plains, people depended on the bison for food, shelter, clothing and tools.

West of the Mississippi the trees thin out. Then they stop. The Great Plains are a sea of grass that stretches from Texas to Canada. Millions of , the big shaggy animals many people call buffalo, grazed on that grass. Before Europeans came, there were tens of millions of them. For the peoples of the Plains, the bison was life.

Plains peoples used every part of the animal. Meat was eaten fresh or dried for winter. Hides became blankets, robes and the walls of homes. Bones became tools and needles. Horns became spoons. Even the stomach could be a cooking pot. Wasting a bison was unthinkable, because the whole village depended on it.

Bison herds moved with the seasons, so many Plains peoples moved too. Their home was the . It was a cone of long poles covered with bison hides. A tipi could be taken down in minutes and packed onto a dog sled. Before Europeans brought horses, dogs did the hauling. Nations like the Lakota and Cheyenne followed the herds across the grass, and their homes came with them.

11.5

Pueblos and Ditches of the Southwest

Main ideaIn the dry Southwest, people built towns of stone and mud brick and dug ditches to bring water to their corn.

Now picture red cliffs, dry ground and a hot sun. This is the Southwest, in what is now Arizona and New Mexico. Rain is rare. Yet people have farmed here for more than two thousand years. The Ancestral Puebloans, the Hopi, the Zuni and others built towns of stone and mud brick. The Spanish later called these towns , their word for villages.

A pueblo looked like an apartment building made of earth. Rooms were stacked several stories high. People climbed ladders to reach the upper floors. Thick walls kept rooms cool in the day and warm at night. Some pueblos were built into the sides of cliffs, high above the valley floor. Others, like Acoma in New Mexico, sit on top of a mesa and have been lived in for hundreds of years.

Farming with so little rain took skill. People dug ditches to carry river water to their fields. This is called . They planted corn deep in the ground where the soil stayed damp. They saved seeds from the plants that survived dry years. Corn, beans and squash grew here too, along with cotton for clothes. The Hopi still plant corn in the desert today, using ways their families have kept for centuries.

Section 3

Coast, Ice and a Great City

11.6

Cedar and Salmon on the Northwest Coast

Main ideaOn the rainy Pacific Northwest coast, giant cedar trees and rivers full of salmon made rich villages possible.

On the Pacific coast, from Oregon up to Alaska, it rains most of the year. Giant cedar trees grow taller than a ten-story building. Every spring and fall, rivers fill with swimming upstream to lay their eggs. Peoples like the Haida, Tlingit and Chinook had plenty of food. They did not need to farm. They caught salmon by the thousands. They dried and smoked the fish for winter.

Cedar was the wood for everything. People split the soft, straight-grained trunks into planks and built large houses. They hollowed whole logs into canoes that could go out on the ocean. They wove cedar bark into clothes, mats and baskets. And they carved tall cedar posts with animals and family symbols. These are the that stood in front of homes.

A totem pole is not a god or an idol. It is a family record. The carved raven, bear or whale shows the family’s history and its rights. Rich families held a feast called a potlatch. The host gave away blankets, food and other gifts to hundreds of guests. Giving more away showed more honor. In this land of plenty, being generous was the way to become great.

11.7

Ice and Sea in the Arctic

Main ideaIn the Arctic, where nothing can be farmed, the Inuit lived by hunting seals and whales and inventing tools for ice and sea.

At the top of the continent, the ground stays frozen all year. No trees grow. In winter the sun barely rises. This is the Arctic, home of the Inuit and their relatives across Alaska, Canada and Greenland. You cannot farm here. But the sea is full of life. Seals, walrus, whales and fish became food, fuel and clothing.

Inuit inventions fit this world exactly. The was a light one-person boat with a skin cover stretched over a frame. A hunter sat inside, sealed against the icy water. Sleds pulled by dogs crossed the snow. Boots and parkas were sewn from seal and caribou skins with sinew thread. Seal oil burned in stone lamps for light and heat.

In winter some Inuit families built domed houses from blocks of packed snow. The snow held in body heat. In summer they lived in tents of skin. They knew the ice the way a farmer knows a field. They knew which ice would hold and which would break. They knew where seals came up to breathe. Today Inuit still hunt in the Arctic, often by snowmobile, and their languages are still spoken.

11.8

Cahokia, a City of Mounds

Main ideaAround the year 1100, Cahokia in Illinois was the largest city north of Mexico, built by farmers who raised huge mounds of earth.

Cahokia stood on the east side of the Mississippi River, across from what is now St. Louis. It was in Illinois. Around the year 1100 it may have held ten thousand to twenty thousand people. No city in the United States would be that big again until Philadelphia in the 1700s. Its people belonged to a way of life scientists call Mississippian.

The city was famous for its . People carried soil in baskets, one load at a time, and piled it up. The biggest, now called Monks Mound, is about one hundred feet tall. Its base covers about fourteen acres, more than ten football fields. About one hundred twenty mounds once stood in the city. Leaders lived on top of some. Others held the dead.

Corn made Cahokia possible. Farmers in the rich river bottom grew enough to feed thousands of people who did not farm: builders, priests, potters, traders. By about 1350, the city was empty. Nobody knows exactly why. Floods, crop failures, war and worn-out soil are all possible. The mounds still stand at Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site, and you can climb Monks Mound today.

Section 4

Councils, Stories and Today

11.9

The Haudenosaunee Council

Main ideaFive nations in the Northeast joined under the Great Law of Peace, with a council that decided together and clan mothers who chose the leaders.

Long before the United States, five nations in what is now New York State were at war with each other. According to their own history, a man called the Peacemaker traveled among them. He urged them to stop fighting and join together. The Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca agreed. They called themselves the Haudenosaunee, the people of the longhouse. Later the Tuscarora joined as a sixth nation.

Their agreement is called the Great Law of Peace. It set up a , a group of nations that stay separate but act together on big matters. Each nation sent chiefs to a Grand Council. The council met at Onondaga, in the middle. Chiefs talked until they agreed. No one nation could force the others. A war against one was a war against all.

Women held real power. Each family belonged to a clan, and the oldest women, the , chose the chiefs. If a chief did wrong, a clan mother could remove him. Some early Americans, including Benjamin Franklin, knew about the council and admired how the nations worked together. The Haudenosaunee Grand Council still meets today.

11.10

Stories, Art and Trade Routes

Main ideaNative peoples passed down knowledge through spoken stories and art, and traded goods across thousands of miles.

Most Native nations did not use writing. Instead, they kept their history by , passing stories from one generation to the next by speech. Elders told how the world began, how the clan got its name and where the boundaries were. Children heard the stories many times until they knew them. A good storyteller was as important as a good hunter.

Art carried memory too. The Haudenosaunee wove beads of white and purple shell into belts called . A wampum belt recorded a treaty or an important event. Its pattern was read aloud at council. Plains peoples painted the year’s big event on a bison hide, adding one picture each winter. Pueblo potters painted designs that told who made the pot and where.

Trade tied the continent together. Archaeologists at Cahokia have found copper from Lake Superior, seashells from the Gulf of Mexico and a shiny stone from the Rocky Mountains. Each of these traveled hundreds of miles by canoe and on foot. Goods, ideas and games moved along the same paths. When Europeans arrived, they walked onto trails that were already old.

He said that a city of thousands must have stood there, and that raising such a work must have taken years of labor by many people.

11.11

Native Nations Today

Main ideaNative nations are still here: more than five hundred are recognized by the United States, and tens of thousands of Native people live in Illinois, many in Chicago.

Native peoples are not only part of the past. Today the United States recognizes more than five hundred seventy Native nations. Each is a nation, which means it has its own government, laws and leaders. Many have their own lands, called reservations. Others do not. Their citizens are also citizens of the United States.

Illinois is named for the Illinois people. They called themselves the Illiniwek. Most of their descendants were pushed west in the 1800s. Today they belong to the Peoria Tribe of Indians of Oklahoma. The Potawatomi, Kickapoo, Miami, Sauk, Meskwaki and Ho-Chunk also lived here. In recent years the Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation regained some land in northern Illinois.

Chicago has one of the largest urban Native populations in the country. People from many nations moved there for work in the 1900s. The American Indian Center of Chicago opened in 1953. It holds a every year. Native students go to Chicago schools. Native artists, doctors and lawyers work across the state. The first peoples are still here. They are still speaking and still building.

Encounter and Change

Contact
Big questionWhen Europeans and Native peoples met, what changed for each of them, and who got to tell the story?
The story

Furs and Kettles on the River

In the winter of 1680, a French trader and an Illinois family met on the Illinois River, and each of them wanted something the other had.

The canoe scrapes onto the frozen mud near where Peoria stands today. A Frenchman named Pierre climbs out, stamping his cold feet. He has paddled from the north with three companions. In his canoe are iron kettles, steel knives, glass beads, wool blankets and a musket. He has come to trade. Up the bank is a village of the Illinois people, hundreds of long houses covered with woven mats.

A man named Chicagou walks down to meet him with his wife, Keewatin, and their son. They have met French traders before. Chicagou carries beaver pelts, soft and thick from the winter. Beaver fur is worth a lot in Paris, where hat makers pay silver for it. Pierre knows this. Chicagou knows Pierre knows it. They begin to talk, half in French, half in the Illinois language, with a lot of hand signs.

Keewatin wants the kettle. Her clay pots crack in the fire and take days to make. An iron kettle lasts for years. Chicagou wants the knife and the musket. A steel blade cuts faster than stone. A musket can kill a deer from far away, and it can frighten an enemy. Pierre wants as many beaver pelts as he can carry. They argue politely for an hour. In the end, both sides walk away pleased.

That evening Pierre eats corn and venison in the family's house. He has learned to smoke the long pipe that the Illinois offer to guests. It means peace. He tells them that a French leader named La Salle is building a fort nearby. Chicagou nods. More French means more kettles. It might also mean more of the sickness that has emptied some villages. He does not say this out loud.

Pierre and Chicagou are made-up names, but this trade really happened, thousands of times, all along the rivers of Illinois. The French wrote about it in their letters and journals. The Illinois told about it in their own stories. Both sides gained, and both sides lost. That is the story of this chapter.

Section 1

Why Europeans Sailed West

12.1

Spices, Gold and God

Main ideaEuropeans in the 1400s wanted a sea route to Asia for trade, and they also wanted gold, land and converts to their religion.

In the 1400s a pinch of pepper in Europe was worth more than its weight in silver. Pepper, cinnamon and cloves came from Asia. So did silk. The goods traveled thousands of miles by land, and every merchant along the way raised the price. European kings and merchants wanted a cheaper way. They wanted to sail to Asia and buy the directly.

Portugal tried first. Its sailors crept down the coast of Africa, year after year. In 1488 a Portuguese captain rounded the southern tip of Africa. In 1498 another reached India by sea. Spain, Portugal’s rival, wanted its own route. A sailor from Italy named Christopher Columbus had an idea. He would sail west, across the ocean, and reach Asia from the other side.

Trade was not the only reason. Europeans also wanted gold, because gold was money. They wanted land, because land made a family rich. And many wanted to spread their religion, . Priests sailed with the soldiers. One historian summed up the reasons as gold, God and glory. All three were on the ships that sailed west.

Think it through
The Price of Pepper

A sack of pepper leaves India on a ship. It is unloaded and carried by camel across a desert. It is sold to a new merchant in a city on the Mediterranean Sea. Then it is shipped to Italy, and sold again. Then it goes over the mountains to Germany. At every step, someone adds a fee. By the time a cook in Germany buys a pinch, it costs twenty times what it cost in India.

If you could sail straight from Europe to India and skip all those middle steps, what would happen to the price? Who would gain and who would lose?

12.2

What 1492 Set in Motion

Main ideaColumbus landed in the Caribbean in 1492 believing he had reached Asia, and his voyage started centuries of contact that changed both worlds.

On October 12, 1492, after about five weeks at sea, three Spanish ships reached a small island in the Bahamas. Columbus went ashore and claimed it for the king and queen of Spain. The people who lived there, the , came out to meet the strangers. Columbus thought he had reached islands near India. So he called the people Indians. The name stuck, though it was a mistake.

Columbus wrote in his journal that the Taíno were gentle and generous. They brought parrots, cotton thread and food. In the same pages, he wrote that they would make good servants and could be easily ruled. He took some Taíno back to Spain by force. On later trips, the Spanish made Taíno people dig for gold. Within a few decades, war, hunger and disease had killed most of the Taíno on the islands.

Columbus never reached Asia and never understood that he had found continents unknown to Europe. But his voyage changed history. Ships began to cross the Atlantic every year. Plants, animals, people, ideas and diseases moved in both directions. Historians call this the . The next lessons show what it carried.

Section 2

The Great Exchange

12.3

Corn Goes East, Wheat Goes West

Main ideaFoods from the Americas fed the world, and animals and crops from Europe changed the Americas.

Look at a pizza. The wheat crust and the cheese came from Europe. The tomato sauce came from the Americas. Before 1492, no one in Italy had ever seen a . No one in Ireland had seen a potato. No one in Africa had grown corn. No one in Mexico had eaten a cow or tasted wheat bread. The Columbian Exchange put them all on one plate.

Native farmers had bred corn, potatoes, tomatoes, peppers, beans, squash, peanuts, cacao for chocolate and many more plants over thousands of years. Once these crossed the ocean, they fed millions. Potatoes grow well in cold, poor soil, so they became the main food of Ireland and northern Europe. Corn spread across Africa and China. The population of the world grew partly because of American crops.

Going the other way came wheat, rice, sugar cane, coffee, bananas and fruit trees. Also animals: cattle, pigs, sheep, goats, chickens and horses. The Americas had no large farm animals before this except the llama. Pigs and cattle ran wild and multiplied. They also trampled Native cornfields. Sugar cane grew so well in the Caribbean that Europeans built huge farms, and soon they were bringing enslaved Africans by force to work them.

12.4

The Horse Changes the Plains

Main ideaHorses brought by the Spanish spread north and turned Plains peoples into fast-moving hunters and riders within about two hundred years.

There were no horses in the Americas when Columbus landed. The Spanish brought them to Mexico in 1519. Native people had never seen anything like them. Some Spanish soldiers on horseback seemed at first to be one giant animal. But soon Native peoples learned to ride, and they learned fast. Horses escaped or were traded, and they spread north across the land.

For Plains peoples, the horse was a revolution. Before, a family could carry only what their dogs could pull. Hunting bison on foot was slow and dangerous. On horseback, a hunter could ride into a herd and pick the animal he wanted. Tipis grew bigger, because horses could haul longer poles. Families could move farther and faster. Nations like the Comanche and Lakota became some of the best riders in the world.

The horse also brought conflict. Nations moved onto the Plains that had never lived there before. They competed for hunting grounds and for horses. Raids to take horses became common. By the 1700s, before most Plains people had ever met a white settler, their lives had already been changed by a European animal. That is how the Columbian Exchange worked: its effects ran ahead of the Europeans themselves.

12.5

Sickness Crosses the Sea

Main ideaDiseases from Europe killed more Native people than any war, because no one in the Americas had ever been exposed to them.

The deadliest thing on the European ships was invisible. It was . Smallpox, measles and the flu had lived in Europe, Asia and Africa for thousands of years. People there often caught them as children. Many died. Many others survived. A survivor could not catch the disease again. That is called . No one in the Americas had ever met these germs. No one had immunity.

When smallpox reached a Native village, nearly everyone got sick at the same time. There was no one healthy to bring water or food. In 1520, smallpox reached the great Aztec city of Tenochtitlan in Mexico. It killed the emperor and a large share of the people. The Spanish captured the weakened city the next year. Disease did more to defeat the Aztecs than Spanish steel.

Sickness spread along trade routes faster than Europeans traveled. Some villages lost most of their people before they ever saw a European. Historians do not agree on exact numbers. But they agree that a very large share of Native people died in the two centuries after 1492. It was one of the greatest losses of life in human history. The survivors rebuilt. But their world was smaller, and their nations were weaker when the settlers came.

Section 3

Three Ways of Settling

12.6

Spanish Soldiers and Missions

Main ideaThe Spanish conquered Native empires, forced people to work and built missions to change their religion, and in 1680 the Pueblo people drove them out.

The Spanish came first and came to rule. In 1519 a soldier named Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico with a few hundred men. He had horses, steel, guns and smallpox on his side. He also had thousands of Native allies who hated the Aztecs. He captured Tenochtitlan in 1521. Spain took the Aztec gold and silver and ruled Mexico for three hundred years. Spanish soldiers who conquered are called .

In the lands they took, the Spanish gave Native villages to Spanish settlers. The villagers had to work for the settler and pay him. Priests built , churches with farms around them. Native people were required to live there, work and become Christians. Some priests protected the people from the worst abuse. Many did not. Native religions were banned, and ceremonies were punished.

The Pueblo peoples of New Mexico put up with this for about eighty years. Then in 1680, a religious leader named Popé sent runners to every pueblo with knotted cords. The knots counted the days until the uprising. On the chosen day the pueblos rose together. They killed hundreds of Spanish and drove the rest out of New Mexico. The Spanish did not return for twelve years. No other Native uprising against Europeans in what is now the United States was so successful.

12.7

French Traders on the Rivers

Main ideaThe French came in small numbers to trade furs, so they relied on Native alliances, learned Native languages and often married into Native families, including in Illinois.

The French did things differently. Few French people wanted to move across the ocean. Those who came wanted beaver fur, which sold for high prices in Europe for hats. To get fur, they needed Native hunters. So the French built trading posts, not big farms. They learned Native languages. Many French traders married Native women. Their children grew up in both worlds.

In 1673 a priest named Jacques Marquette and a trader named Louis Jolliet came down the Mississippi. They were paddling canoes. They were the first French to see Illinois. The Illinois people welcomed them with the , a long peace pipe. On the way home they went up the Illinois River. They passed a village of thousands near where Utica is today. Marquette wrote that the land was rich and the people kind.

Soon French forts and missions dotted the rivers. La Salle built a fort near Peoria in 1680. The town of Kaskaskia in southern Illinois started as a French mission in 1703. Illinois hunters brought furs, and French traders brought kettles, cloth, guns and brandy. The French also brought disease. They drew the Illinois into wars against the Iroquois and others. But French and Native people in Illinois lived side by side for nearly a century. Many families were both.

12.8

English Farms and Fences

Main ideaThe English came as families to farm, so they took more land than the Spanish or French, and land became the main cause of war.

The English came last and came to stay. Jamestown in Virginia began in 1607. Plymouth in Massachusetts began in 1620. Unlike the French, English settlers came by the thousands, as whole families. They wanted farms of their own. They cleared forests, built fences and let cattle and pigs roam. Every new farm was land that a Native nation had used for hunting, fishing or planting.

At first, Native nations helped. The Powhatan people traded corn to starving Jamestown. The Wampanoag leader Massasoit made a treaty with Plymouth and shared a harvest feast with them in 1621. But the English kept coming. The two sides also thought about land differently. To Native peoples, land was shared by a nation for use. To the English, land was that one person owned and could fence and sell.

Within fifty years the friendship broke. In 1675 Massasoit’s son Metacom, called King Philip by the English, led many nations against the settlers. The war burned dozens of towns on both sides. Thousands died. The English won, and Metacom was killed. After that, the pattern in English lands was set: settlers moved in, the moved west, and Native nations were pushed off the land.

Section 4

Loss, Resistance and Points of View

12.9

Land Lost, Lives Lost

Main ideaOver three centuries, Native nations lost most of their land and many of their people through disease, war and treaties, including here in Illinois.

Add it up. Disease killed a very large share of Native people. Wars killed more. Then came treaties, written agreements between a Native nation and a government. On paper, a treaty was a deal between equals. In practice, nations weakened by sickness and war often had no better choice. So they signed. Many treaties were broken as soon as settlers wanted more land.

Illinois shows the whole story. When Marquette came in 1673, the Illinois people numbered many thousands in big villages. Disease and wars with other nations shrank them. In 1803 the Kaskaskia, one of the Illinois nations, signed a treaty giving up most of their land to the United States. The Sauk leader Black Hawk tried to return to his village on the Rock River in 1832. Soldiers and militia drove his people out in a short, brutal war.

In 1833 a treaty was signed in Chicago. In it the Potawatomi gave up their remaining land in northern Illinois. Within a few years they were marched west. By the 1840s almost no Native nation held land in Illinois. The state was named for a people who were no longer allowed to live in it. That was the cost of the encounter. The people who were here first paid it.

From the source

He said he loved his town, his cornfields and the home of his people, and that he had fought for it. He asked what people would think of a man who did not fight for the place where his family was buried.

12.10

Resisting and Adapting

Main ideaNative peoples fought back, made alliances, took up new tools and kept their languages and ceremonies alive, and they are still doing so.

Native nations were never just victims. They chose, planned and fought. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 drove out an empire. Metacom nearly did the same in New England. In 1763 an Ottawa leader named Pontiac united nations around the Great Lakes and captured most of the British forts west of the mountains. Illinois nations joined him. The British had to promise that settlers would stay east of the mountains, though the promise was soon broken.

Nations also adapted, which means changing to fit new conditions. They took the horse and became the finest riders on Earth. They took iron, guns and cloth and made them their own. Some nations played the French, Spanish and English against each other to protect their land. Native leaders learned to read treaties and argue in court. Adapting was not giving up. It was survival.

The hardest thing to protect was : language, ceremony, story. Governments and missions worked for centuries to stamp these out. Yet the Haudenosaunee still hold council. The Hopi still plant corn the old way. Native languages are being taught again to children in Illinois and across the country. The Illinois language itself, called Miami-Illinois, is being relearned from old French dictionaries. Resistance did not end. It changed shape.

12.11

Same Event, Different Stories

Main ideaThe same event looks different depending on who tells it, so good historians read more than one side and ask who wrote a source and why.

Here is one event. In 1621 Wampanoag men and English settlers at Plymouth ate together after the harvest. For many Americans, this is the first Thanksgiving, a happy story of sharing. For many Wampanoag today, the same day is the start of a story that ends with their land taken and their nation nearly destroyed. Both are talking about the same meal. Both are telling the truth about part of it.

That is why a matters. A point of view is the position a person sees from. A Spanish soldier, a Spanish priest, an Aztec warrior and an Aztec farmer would each describe the fall of Tenochtitlan differently. Most written records from this time were made by Europeans. Native accounts were spoken, painted or remembered. To hear them, historians have to look harder.

Good readers ask three questions about any . Who made it? When? Why? Columbus wrote his journal to impress the king and queen who paid for his trip. Black Hawk told his story to explain himself after losing a war. Neither one is lying, but each has a reason to tell it his way. Today some places mark the second Monday of October as Columbus Day, and others call it Indigenous Peoples’ Day. That argument is about which story we choose to remember.

A very good bargain.'

Both think they won. Is one of them wrong? What could a historian learn by reading both accounts instead of one?
