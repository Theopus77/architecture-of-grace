Social Studies · Activity 14

How a Bill Becomes a Law

First the simulation: you are a member of Congress, and a bill of your own walks the real road — two houses, one desk, and the forks after a veto. Then three sets of questions turn the road into evidence: the road itself, one bill walked step by step, and impeachment — the other road on the same page of the outline.

The simulation — walk one bill yourself

The bill walk

You are a member of Congress. Pick a bill, then walk it down the whole road — two houses, one desk, and the forks after a veto. Wrong turns cost nothing here: every one explains where that road actually leads. Nothing is recorded, and walking it twice — once signed, once vetoed — is the best move on this page.

Ready to be quizzed?

This page is the lesson: walk the road yourself, use the hints, and take the wrong turns on purpose. The scored quiz on the road and impeachment now lives in one place with every other social studies quiz.

Show me one first

This one is nobody's answer — it is only here to show the moves before you touch yours. Watching it is instruction, never a trial, and it counts toward nothing.

Your class wants a new national holiday. A representative agrees to carry the idea. Where is the bill after the FIRST house passes it — done, or somewhere on the road?
Walk the road in order: first house by majority, second house on the bill as written, then the president's desk.
One house down means the bill is at the HALFWAY point — a second majority and a signature still stand between it and the calendar.
Somewhere on the road — exactly halfway. Every question on this page is answered by finding the bill's spot on the road first.

Need help?

Answer in your own words. At the bottom, draw the road from memory: first house, second house, the desk, and the three answers to a veto.

  1. 1What is a bill?
  2. 2A bill passes a house of Congress by earning a majority. What is a majority?
  3. 3Where does a bill's journey begin?
  4. 4The first house votes on the bill. What do the two outcomes mean?
  5. 5The bill arrives in the second house. How does that house vote on it?
  6. 6Both houses have passed the bill. It reaches the president's desk. What are the president's two doors?
  7. 7The president vetoes the bill. What are Congress's three options?
  8. 8Congress decides to override the veto. What does the override take?

The road, walked and proven.

Two numbers carried every answer: a majority — one more than half — moves a bill along the ordinary road, and two-thirds answers the rare moments, the override and the impeachment verdict. Hold the two numbers and the road draws itself.

The road here is the road the class outline teaches — one house proposes, the second votes on the bill AS WRITTEN (failure sends it back, not to a conference), then sign/veto and the three congressional answers — deliberately WITHOUT the committee stage, so the page never contradicts the test. The simulation up top is the class's own Bill-to-Law simulation made digital: the student sponsors a bill, chooses at every fork, and both desk doors are playable — sign, veto-and-accept, veto-and-change (the bill walks both houses again), veto-and-override (the 300-of-435 / 70-of-100 walls). It records nothing and changes no probe number. Set 1 is the road step by step with the outline's vocabulary (bill, majority, ratify, veto, override); Set 2 walks one bill through scenarios including the override arithmetic; Set 3 is impeachment — charge vs. removal, House majority, Senate two-thirds, Chief Justice presiding — plus the why of checks. Items 1–5 are steps; 6–8 are forks.

Three doors on this page: Show me one first is a worked example and counts toward nothing; the eight items are the walk; and a finished set opens One more, if you want it — a stretch with two tries and the thinking shown warmly either way. Whether each door was used rides along when a probe row is copied or sent — as information, never as a score.
