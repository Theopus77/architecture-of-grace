The Novel

Literature

A novel is the longest thing most people ever read on purpose. Two hundred pages, sometimes five hundred, about people who never existed, doing things that never happened. And yet readers cry over them, argue about them, and remember them for the rest of their lives. Something is going on in those pages that a summary cannot give you, and this unit is about learning to see it.

You will take a novel apart the way a mechanic takes apart an engine, not to ruin it but to understand why it runs. You will learn how writers build a character out of nothing but words, how they make a person change across a whole book, and who gets to tell the story and why that matters. You will learn the shape of a plot, what a setting is for, and the difference between what a story is about and what it says. You will learn to spot a symbol and to prove it is one.

Then you will learn to read a whole book on your own: with stamina, a pencil, a journal, and the right questions. You will read three short masters closely, learn the rules of five kinds of story, give a book talk, and pick your next book wisely. The novel this site keeps its own room for, The Outsiders, will be the example again and again. But every tool here works on any book with a person in it.

The story

The Boy Outside the Theater

A novel written by a teenager opens with one boy, one sidewalk, and a feeling every reader knows.

The book opens with a boy stepping out of a movie theater into bright sunlight. He is alone, and he is thinking about two things: the actor Paul Newman, and how to get home. He has a long walk ahead of him, and he knows that a boy like him, walking alone in his part of town, might get jumped. He walks anyway. Within a few pages he is on the ground with a knife at his throat, and his older brothers are running to pull the attackers off.

The boy is Ponyboy Curtis, fourteen years old, the narrator of The Outsiders. The author, S. E. Hinton, started the novel when she was a high school student in Oklahoma and published it in 1967. She was tired of books about teenagers that did not sound like anyone she knew. So she wrote about the two groups at her own school: the greasers, poor kids with long, oiled hair, and the Socs, rich kids with nice cars. In her book, the greasers tell the story.

What makes that first scene work is not the fight. It is everything Ponyboy tells you before the fight. He says he likes movies and books, which most of his friends do not. He says his oldest brother, Darry, is hard on him. He says he wishes he looked tougher than he does. By the time the Socs pull up, you already know him. You are scared for him because you know him.

That is the trick of every good novel. A writer cannot hand you a real person. A writer can only hand you words: what a character says, does, wants, fears, and notices. From those words you build a person in your head. And once you have built him, you cannot help caring what happens to him. You will argue with friends about whether Darry loves Ponyboy or just bosses him. You will decide who is to blame for what happens in the park.

This chapter is about how that building happens. You will learn how writers show character, what pushes a character to act, how a person can change across three hundred pages, and who gets to tell the story. The Outsiders is the novel this site has its own room for, so it will come up often. But the tools work on any book with a person in it, which is nearly all of them.

Section 1

Knowing a Character

22.1

Telling and Showing

Main ideaWriters reveal character two ways: by telling you directly, or by showing actions, words, looks and thoughts and letting you decide.

Read these two sentences about the same girl. One: Maya was generous. Two: Maya slid half her sandwich across the table without looking up, as if she had never wanted the whole thing. The first sentence tells you what to think. The second makes you figure it out. Both are ways of building a character, and good writers use both.

When the narrator states a trait outright, that is . It is fast and clear: "Darry was tough, cool, and smart." You do not have to guess. Writers use it when they need you to know something quickly, or when a narrator is the kind of person who sums people up.

When you have to infer a trait from evidence, that is . The evidence comes in five kinds: what the character says, what the character does, what the character thinks, how the character looks, and how other people react to the character. Maya sliding the sandwich is an action. Her not looking up is a small action that shows she does not want thanks.

Indirect characterization asks more of you, but it pays more back. When you work out that Maya is generous, you believe it, because you saw the proof. When a book tells you a character is brave and then never shows it, you stop trusting the book. Watch for the gap between what a narrator says about a person and what that person actually does.

22.2

What a Character Wants

Main ideaMotivation is the reason behind a character's actions, and finding it explains choices that look strange from outside.

A boy in a novel steals a loaf of bread. Is he a thief? Then you read that his sister has not eaten in two days. Now the same act means something different. Nothing about the action changed. What changed is that you learned his , the reason behind what he did.

Every character who matters wants something. It can be big, like freedom or revenge, or small, like being left alone for one afternoon. Writers rarely announce it. They let you see the character reach for it again and again. Ponyboy in The Outsiders wants to be seen as more than a greaser. That want explains why he keeps reading books and watching sunsets, even when his friends tease him.

To find motivation, ask two questions about any action: what does this character get from doing it, and what would they lose by not doing it? A character who lies to a friend might be protecting himself, protecting the friend, or protecting a secret. Each answer makes a different person.

Motivation is also where real conflict starts. Two characters who want different things will collide. Two characters who want the same thing, like one job or one parent’s approval, will collide harder. When you can name what each person wants, the whole plot starts to make sense.

22.3

Hearing Character in Dialogue

Main ideaHow a character talks, including word choice, what they avoid saying, and how they treat others, reveals who they are.

Two brothers answer the same question: "Where were you?" The first says, "Out." The second says, "I went by the library, then I stopped at Marco’s, and then I lost track of time, I’m sorry." You have never met these brothers. You already know that one is closed and one is open, one is maybe angry and one is anxious to be forgiven. Dialogue did that in twenty words.

is the words characters speak. Writers use it to reveal character in several ways. Word choice shows education, region, and mood. Length shows whether a person is guarded or eager. What a character refuses to talk about can matter more than what they say. And how a character speaks to different people, gently to a child and sharply to a waiter, shows their values.

In The Outsiders, the greasers’ slang marks them as a group, and it separates them from the Socs. But within the group, each boy sounds different. Two-Bit jokes constantly. Johnny says little, and when he speaks, the others listen. Ponyboy notices that Cherry Valance, a Soc, talks to him like a person, and that small thing changes how he sees her whole side of town.

When you read dialogue, do not just ask what was said. Ask what the speaker wanted from saying it. Ask what they left out. Ask whether their words match their actions, because a character who says "I don’t care" and then checks the window every five minutes has told you something true without meaning to.

Section 2

Characters Who Change

22.4

Static and Dynamic Characters

Main ideaA dynamic character changes inside during the story; a static character stays the same, and both kinds have jobs to do.

At the start of a story a girl believes her older cousin is perfect. By the end she has watched him lie, cover it up, and lie again, and she cannot see him the same way. She has not grown taller or changed schools. She has changed inside. A character who changes in some important way is called .

A character who stays the same all through the story is . Static is not an insult. A story needs people who hold steady. A best friend who is loyal on page one and loyal on the last page gives the main character something to lean on. A villain who never softens gives the hero something to push against. The question is not whether a character is good or bad but whether they change.

The change in a dynamic character is usually in beliefs, values, or understanding, not in situation. A character who gets rich but thinks exactly as before is static. A character who loses everything and learns what mattered is dynamic. In The Outsiders, Ponyboy begins by seeing Socs as one thing and greasers as another. By the end he understands that people on both sides watch the same sunset. That is his change.

To track a change, find three moments: how the character sees things at the start, the event that shakes that view, and how they see things at the end. If you can name all three with evidence from the text, you can prove a character is dynamic. If the third moment matches the first, you have a static character, and you can prove that too.

22.5

Conflict Inside

Main ideaInternal conflict is a struggle within one character between two things they want, fear, or believe.

A boy finds a wallet with two hundred dollars in it. Nobody saw him pick it up. His family needs the money. He also knows exactly what his grandmother would say. For the next three pages he does nothing but walk and think, and those are the tensest pages in the book. Nobody is chasing him. The fight is inside.

That is : a struggle within a single character. It is often written as a person versus themselves. The two sides can be a want against a value, a fear against a duty, or two loyalties that cannot both be kept. The character has to choose, and either choice costs something.

Internal conflict is what makes a character feel real rather than like a game piece. A hero who never doubts is boring. A character who wants to run and also wants to stay, who must decide and cannot, is someone you recognize. In The Outsiders, Johnny wrestles with whether to turn himself in after the park, and Ponyboy wrestles with his anger at Darry against his need for him.

You can spot internal conflict by looking for a character’s thoughts that argue with each other, a decision that is put off, or a moment when a character does one thing and then undoes it. When you find one, name the two sides. "He wants the money but he wants to be honest" is a complete description of a conflict.

22.6

Conflict Outside

Main ideaExternal conflict is a struggle between a character and something outside them: another person, society, nature, or fate.

A swimmer is caught in a current. A student is called to the principal’s office over a rule she thinks is unfair. Two boys in a parking lot square up over a girl. In each case the character is pushing against something outside themselves. That is , and most stories have more than one.

Writers and teachers usually sort external conflicts into a few kinds. Person versus person: two characters want different things. Person versus society: a character pushes against laws, customs, or what everyone expects. Person versus nature: a character fights weather, wilderness, or illness. Some add person versus fate or the supernatural, when the force is beyond human control.

The Outsiders runs on person versus society. The greasers and Socs are not just individuals who dislike each other. They are two groups that the whole town has sorted by money, and the boys inherit the fight whether they want it or not. Ponyboy’s fight with the Socs at the movie theater is person versus person on the surface, but the reason those boys are enemies is the society behind them.

Internal and external conflicts usually work together. The outside fight forces the inside choice. A boy attacked in a park has an external conflict, and afterward he has an internal one: run or confess. When you describe a story’s conflict, try to name one of each and show how the outside one caused the inside one.

Who Tells the Story

22.7

First Person

Main ideaIn first-person point of view, a character tells the story as "I," so you get their thoughts but only what they know and choose to say.

"I never meant to break the window." Five words in and you already have a voice, a person, and a hint that something is being explained away. The story is told by someone inside it, using I and me and my. That is . The teller is called the .

First person gets you close. You hear the narrator’s private thoughts, feel their fear, and hear the way they talk. Twain’s Huckleberry Finn opens with Huck speaking straight to you in his own uneducated, honest voice, and that voice is most of why the book works. The Outsiders is first person too: everything comes through Ponyboy, including his mistakes.

But first person has walls. The narrator can only tell what they saw, heard, or were told. If Ponyboy is not in the room, you are not in the room. And a narrator can be wrong, or biased, or protecting someone. When Ponyboy says Darry does not love him, you have his feeling, not the fact. Later he learns otherwise, and so do you, but only because the book lets him find out.

When you read first person, keep two questions running. What does this narrator know? And what does this narrator want me to think? A narrator’s blind spots are part of the story. Sometimes they are the whole point.

22.8

Third Person: Limited and Omniscient

Main ideaThird-person narrators use he, she and they; a limited narrator stays inside one character's mind, while an omniscient narrator can enter any mind.

"Marisol did not hear the footsteps behind her." No character could say that about herself in the moment. The narrator is standing outside the story, using she and her, and knows something Marisol does not. That is third-person point of view. It comes in two main kinds, and the difference is how much the narrator can see.

A third-person narrator follows one character closely, reporting that person’s thoughts and feelings but nobody else’s. You see the world over that character’s shoulder. It feels almost like first person, but the narrator can add things the character would not say, like "she did not hear the footsteps." Many modern novels use limited third person because it is close and clear at the same time.

An narrator, from a Latin word meaning all-knowing, can enter any character’s mind and move from one to another, even in the same scene. Dickens often does this: he can tell you what the rich man thinks and then what the beggar at his gate thinks. Omniscient narration gives a wide view of a whole world, but it keeps you a little farther from any one person.

To tell them apart, count minds. If the narrator only ever reports one character’s inner life, it is limited. If it reports two or more, it is omniscient. Then ask why the writer chose that. A mystery often stays limited so you cannot see the culprit’s thoughts. A novel about a whole town goes omniscient so you can.

22.9

Narrators You Cannot Trust

Main ideaAn unreliable narrator tells the story in a way the reader should doubt, and the clues to that doubt are part of the story's meaning.

A narrator opens by insisting he is not mad. He says his senses are sharp, that he heard things in heaven and hell, and that he will tell the story calmly to prove it. Then he explains, very calmly, how he planned a murder for a week. This is Poe’s "The Tell-Tale Heart," and the more the narrator insists on his sanity, the less you believe him. He is an .

An unreliable narrator is one whose telling you cannot fully trust. Sometimes the narrator lies. More often the narrator is honest but limited: a child who does not understand what the adults are doing, a person so angry they cannot see straight, someone who wants badly to look good. The story is still true in the world of the book. The telling of it is bent.

Writers plant clues so that you can see around the narrator. Look for a gap between what the narrator claims and what other characters do or say. Look for a narrator who protests too much, like Poe’s. Look for details the narrator drops without noticing what they mean. Ponyboy is mostly reliable, but he is fourteen and hurt, and when he calls Darry cold, the text shows Darry working two jobs to keep the family together.

Reading an unreliable narrator is like listening to a friend tell a story about a fight they were in. You believe the fight happened. You also know they are the hero of their own version. The skill is to hold both things at once and to keep looking for evidence.

Proving What You Think

22.10

Inference With Evidence

Main ideaAn inference about a character is a claim; to make it count, cite the exact words from the text that support it and explain the link.

A student says, "Darry is really scared of losing his brothers." Her teacher asks, "Where?" That one word is the whole skill. In literature, what you think about a character is an , a conclusion drawn from clues. Inferences are welcome, but they have to be backed by : words from the book that you can point to.

The strongest evidence is specific. "Darry acts worried" is weak. "Darry yells at Ponyboy for coming home late, then the book shows him working two jobs and turning down college" is strong, because anyone can check it. When you quote, quote only the part that matters, and put it in quotation marks. When you paraphrase, say where it comes from.

Then explain the link. Evidence does not speak for itself. Write the bridge: "Darry yells about a late night because he is responsible for keeping the family together, and one mistake could get his brothers taken away." Now a reader sees the claim, the proof, and the reasoning that connects them.

Good evidence can also prove you wrong, and that is useful. If you think a character is cruel and then find three scenes where he is kind to a stranger, your inference needs to change. Readers who go back to the text with an open mind end up knowing characters better than readers who decide early and stop looking.

22.11

Following One Character Through a Book

Main ideaTo understand a character in a whole novel, track them at the start, at turning points, and at the end, and compare the evidence.

A single scene shows you a character in one light. A whole novel shows you the same person under many lights, and the changes between them are where the meaning lives. Reading a whole book well means keeping a record, because by chapter ten you will have forgotten the exact words of chapter one.

One simple method: keep a page for each major character with three columns, labeled start, turning points, and end. Under start, write what the character wants and believes in the first chapters, with page numbers. Under turning points, note each event that pushes on those wants and beliefs. Under end, write what the character wants and believes at the close. Then compare the first and last columns.

Try it with Ponyboy in The Outsiders. At the start he believes the Socs have it easy and Darry is against him. Turning points pile up: a conversation with Cherry about sunsets, the night in the park, the days hiding in the church, the fire, the hospital, the rumble, and a note left in a book. At the end he understands that Socs have troubles too, that Darry was protecting him, and that he can tell this story instead of fighting it.

The last pages of the novel reveal that the whole book is a school assignment Ponyboy has written, and its first line is the same as the line he chooses to begin with. That structure is the author’s way of saying the change is real: the boy who walked out of the theater alone is now a writer telling you why. A character sheet lets you prove that with evidence, chapter by chapter.

It's a nice sky. Move on."

Cherry says she watches them too, from her side of town. Miguel pointed at the page. "Same sunset. Different sides of town. The whole book is right there." Jada read it twice and did not say anything for a while.

Then she went looking. She found the sunset again near the end, in the note Johnny leaves for Ponyboy, where he talks about staying gold and about how Ponyboy can still see sunsets like a kid does. She found a poem in the middle of the book, by Robert Frost, about how the first green of spring is gold and does not last. She found that every time the sunset came back, somebody had lost something.

By the end of the period, Jada had changed her mind, but not all the way. "Fine," she said. "The sunset means something. But the book is still about the fight. The fight is what happens. The sunset is what it means." Miguel thought about that and decided she was right. They had discovered, without the words for it yet, the difference between plot and theme.

This chapter gives you those words. Plot is what happens, in what order, and why one thing leads to the next. Setting is where and when it happens, and why the where matters. Theme is what the story says about people, the part that stays true when you close the book. Symbols, like a sunset, are how a writer gets from one to the other without preaching. You will learn to find all of them and to prove what you find.

23.1

Five Parts of a Plot

Main ideaMost plots move through exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution, and naming those parts helps you see how a story is built.

A girl moves to a new town and knows nobody. She joins the track team and gets faster. A rival on the team starts sabotaging her. On the day of the championship, she has to decide whether to run or to report the rival and miss the race. She runs, and wins, and reports the rival afterward. In the last scene she is eating pizza with three new friends. You just read a whole plot, and it has five parts.

The sets things up: who, where, and what is normal. The girl is new and alone. The is the chain of events that builds tension: the team, the speed, the sabotage. Each event makes the next one more likely, and the stakes go up. The is the moment of highest tension, where the main character faces the central choice or conflict head on: run or report.

The shows the results of the climax playing out: she wins and reports. The shows the new normal: pizza with friends. Some stories skip or shrink a part. A short story might have one line of exposition. A mystery might put the climax on the last page and give you almost no falling action. But the shape is there under most stories, and once you see it, you can predict where a book is headed.

In The Outsiders, the exposition is Ponyboy’s walk home and the fight, plus what he tells you about his brothers and friends. The rising action runs from the drive-in through the park. The night in the park, and the flight to the church, push the story toward its climax. Readers argue about exactly which scene is the climax, the fire or the rumble or the hospital, and that argument is a good one to have with evidence.

23.2

Why the Climax Comes Late

Main ideaWriters place the climax near the end and build to it so that the resolution feels earned, and the placement shapes how a reader feels.

Imagine a story where the big fight happens in chapter two and the next twelve chapters are cleanup. You would put it down. Now imagine the fight comes in chapter thirteen of fourteen, after every chapter has made it harder to avoid. You cannot put it down. Same fight. Different place. Where a writer puts the climax controls how a reader feels for the whole book.

The rising action is supposed to make the climax feel necessary. Each event should close off an easier choice. In The Outsiders, the greasers and Socs could have kept their distance. Then a fight in a park goes wrong, and hiding becomes impossible, and a church fire turns the boys into something the town has to look at. By the time of the rumble, nobody can back out. That is the feeling of rising action done well: the walls closing in.

After the climax, the falling action and resolution are usually short, but they are not filler. They tell you what the climax cost and what it changed. A story that ends the moment the fight ends leaves you hanging. A story that shows the day after, quietly, tells you what the fight was for. The last chapters of The Outsiders are mostly Ponyboy trying to get up in the morning, and that is the point.

When you read, mark the page where you think the climax is. Then check it two ways. Was everything before it building pressure? Is everything after it release? If yes, you found it. If the pressure keeps building after your mark, you were early. Being early is not a mistake. It means the writer fooled you on purpose, and that is worth noticing too.

Subplots

Main ideaA subplot is a smaller story running beside the main one, and it usually reflects or pushes the main plot.

The main story is about a boy trying to get his family through a hard year. Beside it, in a few scenes a chapter, is a smaller story about his sister learning to drive. The driving lessons never become the main event. But they show the same family under less pressure, and the day she finally passes her test is the day the boy first believes things might turn out. The small story is a .

A subplot is a second, smaller plot woven through the main one. It has its own beginning, rise, and end. Writers use subplots for several reasons. A subplot can give the reader a break from the main tension. It can develop a minor character. Most importantly, it can echo the main plot’s theme from a different angle, or cross into the main plot and change it.

In The Outsiders, the friendship between Ponyboy and Cherry Valance is a subplot. It does not drive the fights. But every time it appears, it shows that a greaser and a Soc can talk like people, which is what the main plot keeps saying is impossible. Sodapop’s troubles with his girlfriend are another subplot, and they come crashing into the main story when Ponyboy and Darry’s arguing finally becomes too much for him.

To find a subplot, look for a thread of scenes about a different problem than the main one, with its own little arc. Then ask what it is doing there. If it echoes the main plot, name the echo. If it crosses into the main plot, mark the crossing. A subplot that does neither may just be padding, and readers are allowed to say so.

Setting That Does Work

Main ideaSetting is when and where a story happens, and in good writing it shapes characters, creates conflict, and builds mood.

Take a story about two friends who fall out and set it in a Chicago apartment in January, with the radiators clanking and nobody able to go outside. Now set the same story in a wide, empty field in June. The words the friends say could be identical, but the stories are not. In the first, they are trapped together. In the second, one can walk away. The changed what the fight can be.

Setting is the time and place of a story: the year, the season, the hour, the city, the room. Weak writing treats it as a backdrop, a painted wall behind the actors. Strong writing makes it do work. Setting can shape characters, because a boy raised in a town that sorts people by money grows up thinking that is normal. Setting can create conflict, because a blizzard or a border or a curfew is a force to fight against.

Setting also builds , the feeling a reader gets from a scene. A writer chooses details on purpose: a dripping faucet, a streetlight buzzing, a hallway that smells like floor cleaner. Each one tunes the reader’s nerves. When you notice a writer spending a paragraph on a place, ask what feeling the details are building and what that feeling is for.

The Outsiders never names its city, but its setting is precise: a mid-1960s American town with an east side and a west side, drive-in theaters, vacant lots for fights, and an abandoned church in the countryside. The east-west line is the whole conflict. The church, far from town, is the one place a greaser and a Soc could not be told apart, which is exactly what the boys learn there.

What a Story Means

23.6

Theme, Topic and Moral

Main ideaA topic is what a story is about in a word; a theme is what it says about that topic in a sentence; a moral is a lesson it orders you to follow.

Ask three students what The Outsiders is about. One says "friendship." One says "people on both sides of a divide have the same fears and the same sky." One says "don’t judge people by their money." All three are pointing at something real, but they are pointing at three different things: a topic, a theme, and a moral.

A is a subject, usually one or two words: friendship, class, loss, growing up. A topic is not yet an idea. A is what the story says about the topic, and it takes a full sentence: "Loyalty to friends can cost more than it gives." A theme is an insight about people or life that the story shows to be true, and a strong novel has more than one.

A is a direct lesson or rule, the kind that ends a fable: be honest, work hard, do not judge. Fables have morals. Most novels do not. A novel with a moral tends to feel like a lecture. A novel with a theme trusts you to think. The difference is that a theme describes how the world is, and a moral tells you what to do about it.

When you write a theme, avoid two mistakes. Do not stop at the topic: "The book is about class" is a start, not a theme. Do not name the characters: "Ponyboy learns that Socs have problems too" is a plot summary. A theme leaves the characters behind and says something that could be true outside the book: "People who seem to have everything can still be hurting."

23.7

How a Theme Grows

Main ideaTheme is not stated once; it develops across a whole text through repeated situations, changing characters, and the ending.

A theme is not hiding in one sentence you can underline. It grows. Early in a book a situation comes up. Later it comes up again, a little different. A character reacts one way the first time and another way the last time. The ending tips the balance. By the last page, the book has made an argument without ever stating it, and you have to put it into words yourself.

The best way to watch a theme grow is to follow one : a repeated situation, image, or question. In The Outsiders, the pattern is people looking at each other across a line. Greasers look at Socs and see money. Socs look at greasers and see trouble. Then Cherry talks to Ponyboy. Then a Soc named Randy tells Ponyboy he is tired of the fighting. Each time the pattern returns, the line gets a little thinner.

Character change is the second way theme develops. When a dynamic character ends up believing something new, the book is usually asking you to consider believing it too. Ponyboy’s shift from "they have it easy" to "things are rough all over" is the theme walking on two legs. If you can describe the change, you can state the theme.

The ending is the last word. A story that ends with a reunion says something different from one that ends with a funeral, even if everything before was the same. When you write about theme, use all three sources: the pattern, the change, and the ending. A theme with evidence from all three is hard to argue with.

23.8

Symbols and Motifs

Main ideaA symbol is a thing in a story that stands for a larger idea; a motif is a repeated element that builds meaning; both need evidence, not guesswork.

A sunset is light hitting the atmosphere at a low angle. In The Outsiders it is also the one thing a greaser and a Soc can share, and a sign of the innocence Johnny begs Ponyboy to keep. When a concrete thing in a story carries a larger meaning, it is a . The sunset does not stop being a sunset. It becomes a sunset and something more.

Writers signal symbols by repetition and by placement. A thing that appears once is probably just a thing. A thing that appears at the beginning, at a turning point, and at the end, and that characters talk about, is asking to be read. The gold in Frost’s poem, which Ponyboy recites in the church, links up with the sunset and with Johnny’s last words, and together they make one symbol out of three pieces.

A is a repeated element, like an image, a phrase, or a sound, that builds meaning over a text. Motifs are broader than symbols. In The Outsiders, hair is a motif: the greasers’ long hair is their pride and their label, and cutting it in the church is a real loss. A motif may not stand for one idea the way a symbol does, but it keeps a theme in front of you.

Be careful. Not everything means something, and readers who insist that every blue curtain stands for sadness get things wrong. Test a symbol the way you test any inference: find the repetitions, find the placement, find a character or narrator who treats it as important, and state what it stands for in the book’s own terms. If you cannot find that evidence, it is a curtain.

23.9

The Book and the Movie

Main ideaComparing a novel to its film shows what each form can and cannot do, and every change a filmmaker makes is a choice worth explaining.

In 1983 the director Francis Ford Coppola made The Outsiders into a movie with a cast of young actors who later became famous. Students who read the book and then watch the film usually notice the same things. The movie looks right: the hair, the cars, the lot. But something is missing. Ponyboy’s thoughts, which fill every page, are mostly gone, because a camera cannot film a thought.

That is the first thing a comparison teaches: the two forms have different tools. A novel can go inside a mind. It can stretch a moment across three pages or skip a year in a sentence. A film has faces, music, light, and sound, and it can make you feel a sunset in one shot that took the book a paragraph. Neither is better. Each has to do the other’s work in its own way.

The second thing to learn is that every change is a choice. Filmmakers cut scenes for time, add scenes for clarity, and move things around for pace. When you notice a difference, do not just say the movie left it out. Ask what the change does. Does it make a character simpler? Does it lose a subplot that carried a theme? Does it make the ending happier or sadder than the book’s? The original release of The Outsiders film trimmed the story’s beginning and end, and a later, longer edition restored some of those scenes.

To write a comparison, pick one scene that both versions include. Describe exactly what each does with it. Then argue which version served the theme better and why. That is a fair fight, and it teaches you more about the book than praising or bashing the movie ever could.

Main ideaA strong theme paragraph states the theme as a sentence, supports it with specific evidence from the text, and explains how the evidence shows the theme.

Here is a weak paragraph about theme: "The theme of The Outsiders is that people are the same. Ponyboy learns this. It is a good lesson." It names a topic, not a theme. It has no evidence. It ends with an opinion. A teacher reading it cannot tell whether the writer read the book. Here is how to fix it, step by step.

First, state the theme as a full sentence about life, not about the characters: "People divided by money and class often share the same fears and the same losses." Second, give specific evidence from the text. Not "Cherry and Ponyboy talk," but the sunset exchange at the drive-in, where Cherry says she watches the same sunset from her side of town. Add a second piece: Randy, a Soc, telling Ponyboy he is sick of the fighting.

Third, explain the link. "Both moments show a Soc admitting to a greaser something the town says they cannot share. The sunset is the same sky over both neighborhoods, and Randy’s exhaustion is the same fear both sides feel. The book puts these scenes at its center to show that the line between the groups is drawn by money, not by what people actually feel."

Finally, deal with the other side. A strong paragraph admits what might argue against the theme, such as the fact that the fighting does not stop, and answers it: the book does not claim the line disappears, only that it is not real in the way the boys believed. Claim, evidence, reasoning, and an honest look at the other side. That is a paragraph a teacher can trust.

The Story That Told You Its Ending First

A three-page story from 1894, read once for the shock and once more for the clues, changed how one reader reads everything.

Andre read Kate Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" in one sitting on the bus, because it is only about a thousand words long. A woman named Mrs. Mallard is told, gently, that her husband has died in a train accident. She weeps, goes to her room alone, and sits by an open window. Then something strange happens. Instead of grief, she feels a rising joy. She whispers a word to herself: free. She had loved him, sometimes. But now the years ahead belong to her.

Then the front door opens and her husband walks in. He was nowhere near the accident. The story ends with one sentence: the doctors said she died of heart disease, of the joy that kills. Andre sat back. He had not seen it coming. He thought the ending was a trick, a twist thrown in to shock the reader. He said so in class the next day, and his teacher asked him to read it again, out loud, slowly, starting with the first sentence.

The first sentence says that Mrs. Mallard has a heart trouble, and that is why the news was broken to her gently. Andre stopped. He had read that sentence yesterday and skipped right over it. He kept going. The story is built on that heart. Her sister worries she will make herself ill. The joy she feels is described as something that runs through her body, that she cannot beat back. The ending was in the first line. The writer had told him, and he had not been listening.

That second reading did something to Andre that the first one could not. He stopped reading for what happens next and started reading for what the writer was doing. He noticed that the whole story happens in an hour, in one house, and that the open window matters. He noticed that the last sentence is ironic: the doctors think she died of joy at seeing her husband alive, and the reader knows the truth. Only a second reading could show him that, and only a slow one.

This chapter is about becoming that kind of reader on purpose, not just when a teacher makes you. You will learn how to build the stamina for a long book, how to mark a page so it talks back, how to keep a journal that makes the second reading unnecessary because you did it the first time. You will read three short masters closely, sort books by genre, learn how to talk about a book, and learn how to pick the next one.

24.1

Building Reading Stamina

Main ideaReading a long book is a skill you build with steady time, a plan, and honest strategies for the pages that are hard.

A runner does not start with a marathon. She starts with a mile, then two, then adds a little each week. Reading a whole book works the same way. If you have never finished a two-hundred-page novel, the problem is usually not your ability. It is that nobody helped you train. , the power to keep going, is built, not born.

Start with time, not pages. Set a timer for ten minutes and read without stopping. Do that every day for a week. Then move to fifteen, then twenty. Most readers who feel slow are actually fine once they stop checking the page number. A book of two hundred pages at ten pages a session is twenty sessions, which is a month. That is a real plan with an ending.

Then have strategies for the hard parts. When a paragraph does not make sense, read it again, out loud if you can, and ask what the last clear thing was. When a chapter is slow, ask what it is setting up. When you are lost about a character, go back to where they first appeared. Strong readers get lost too. The difference is that they have a move for it.

Finally, protect the habit. Read in the same place at the same time. Keep the book where you will see it. Choose a book you actually want to open, which is a skill of its own covered later in this chapter. And know that the first fifty pages of any novel are the hardest, because you are still learning its world. After that, the book starts pulling you instead of you pushing it.

24.2

Marking the Page

Main ideaAnnotating means writing brief notes, marks, and questions on or beside a text so that it becomes a conversation instead of a wall of words.

Open a book that belongs to a strong reader and you will find it written on. Underlines. A star beside a line. A question mark in the margin. A single word: "why?" This is , and it is the single fastest way to turn reading from something that happens to you into something you do. Your brain pays attention to what your hand is about to write.

You do not need a system with six colors. You need three or four marks that you always use. Underline a line that matters and write one word about why. Put a question mark where you are confused, and come back to it. Put a star where a character changes or a pattern repeats. Draw an arrow when a page connects to something earlier, and write the page number it connects to.

Keep the notes short. "Setting = trap" beside the snowed-in apartment is enough. "Foreshadow? Bridge unsafe" is enough. Long notes slow you down and you stop making them. The goal is not to write an essay in the margin. It is to leave a trail so that when you come back, or when you need evidence, you can find it in seconds.

If the book is not yours, use sticky notes or a folded sheet of paper with page numbers down the side. Same marks, same short notes. A library book with a paper trail beside it is just as annotated as one with ink in it. What matters is that you argued with the page while you were on it.

24.3

Keeping a Reading Journal

Main ideaA reading journal records what you noticed, asked, and predicted as you read, so your first reading already contains the second.

Andre’s second reading of "The Story of an Hour" found what his first one missed. A reading journal is a way to get some of that second reading the first time. It is a notebook, paper or digital, where you stop every chapter or so and write a few lines. Not a summary for a teacher. A record for yourself of what the book is doing and what you think about it.

A useful entry has three parts and takes five minutes. Noticed: one detail that seemed to matter, with a page number. Asked: one question you do not have the answer to yet. Predicted: one guess about what will happen or what something will turn out to mean. The prediction is the important part, because being wrong later teaches you exactly what the writer did to fool you.

Over a whole book, the journal becomes a map. You can see where you first suspected the sunset meant something. You can see the question you asked in chapter three and the page where it was answered. When it is time to write an essay, you already have your evidence with page numbers. When it is time for a book talk, you already have the moment that surprised you.

Two rules keep the journal alive. Write in your own voice, including complaints. "This chapter was slow and I think it is on purpose" is a real observation. And date each entry, so you can watch yourself change as a reader. A journal from September read in May shows you a person who did not yet know how to read the book you now understand.

24.4

Asking the Text Questions

Main ideaStrong readers interrogate a text with questions about what the writer chose and why, not only what happened.

Here are two readers at the same paragraph. One asks, "What happened?" The other asks, "Why did the writer put the window in this scene?" The first question has one answer and it is in the book. The second has no single answer, and answering it is what reading well means. Strong readers a text, which means they question it the way a detective questions a witness.

Some questions are about the writer’s choices. Why start here and not earlier? Why tell it in first person? Why this word and not a plainer one? Why does this scene get three pages and that one gets three lines? Every choice a writer makes could have been made differently, and asking why turns every page into evidence.

Some questions are about you. Why did I stop trusting this narrator? What did I expect here, and why did the book not give it to me? Where did I get bored, and is that my fault or the writer’s? These are honest questions, and they lead to the same place: an understanding of how the book works on a reader.

Write your questions down, in the margin or the journal, and do not worry about answering them right away. Many will answer themselves in later chapters. The ones that do not are the ones to bring to a class discussion, because a good unanswered question makes better conversation than any answer.

24.5

Realistic and Historical Fiction

Main ideaRealistic fiction invents people and events that could happen now; historical fiction invents them inside a real past, and each has rules readers expect.

A is a kind of story with its own habits, the way a sport has rules. Knowing the genre tells you what to expect and lets you notice when a writer breaks the pattern. Two genres look almost alike on the shelf. tells a made-up story that could happen in the world as it is: no magic, no future, no impossible luck. The Outsiders is realistic fiction. So is Louisa May Alcott’s Little Women, published in 1868, about four sisters in a house much like the author’s own.

Realistic fiction earns its power by being believable. Readers expect the characters to act like people, the problems to be problems they recognize, and the ending to be one the world could actually produce. When a realistic novel ends with everyone suddenly happy, readers feel cheated. When it ends with something hard but honest, they feel it was worth it.

sets invented characters and events inside a real time and place from the past. Dickens’s A Tale of Two Cities, published in 1859, follows made-up people through the real French Revolution of the 1790s. The rules are stricter: the writer must get the real events, tools, clothes, and speech right, or the reader stops believing. Inside those facts, the writer invents freely.

When you read either genre, ask what the writer wants you to understand about the world, now or then. Realistic fiction often says: this is how life is for people like these. Historical fiction often says: this is what it felt like to be there. Both ask you to believe in people who never existed, and both are lying to tell the truth.

24.6

Fantasy and Mystery

Main ideaFantasy builds a world with its own consistent rules; mystery sets a puzzle and plays fair with the clues, and readers judge each by its own conventions.

A girl follows a rabbit down a hole and lands in a world where she can shrink and grow and where a cat can vanish, leaving its grin behind. Lewis Carroll’s Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, published in 1865, is : a story with events that cannot happen in our world. But fantasy is not "anything goes." A good fantasy sets its own rules early and then keeps them. If the magic can do anything, nothing is at stake.

When you read fantasy, ask what the rules of this world are and whether the writer sticks to them. Ask what the impossible thing lets the writer show about people that a realistic story could not. A world where you can shrink by drinking from a bottle is a strange, exact way of writing about how it feels to be a child among adults who make no sense.

A is built around a puzzle, usually a crime, and a person who solves it. Edgar Allan Poe’s "The Murders in the Rue Morgue," published in 1841, is often called the first detective story. Arthur Conan Doyle’s Sherlock Holmes followed. The genre has one central rule: fair play. The clues the detective uses must be shown to the reader too, so that a sharp reader could solve it first.

When you read a mystery, read like a suspect is in the room. Note every detail that seems too small to mention, because the writer mentioned it for a reason. Keep a list of who could have done it and why. And when the answer comes, go back and check whether the clues were really there. If they were, the writer played fair. If not, you are allowed to be annoyed.

24.7

Memoir: A True Story Shaped

Main ideaA memoir is a true account of part of the author's own life, shaped like a story, and readers judge it by both its honesty and its craft.

A man born into slavery in Maryland teaches himself to read in secret, escapes to the North, and writes a book about it. Frederick Douglass published his Narrative in 1845, and it is a : a true account of part of the author’s life, told by the author. It is not fiction. The people are real and the events happened. But it is shaped like a story, with scenes, dialogue, and a turning point, and that shaping is a choice.

That is the tension inside every memoir. The writer promises the truth. The writer also chooses what to include, what to leave out, where to start, and where to end. Douglass begins with what he does not know, his own birthday, and that choice tells you what slavery took from him before the first chapter is over. A memoir is true the way a map is true: accurate, but drawn by someone who decided what to show.

Memoir differs from autobiography, which usually covers a whole life from birth onward. A memoir picks a slice: one year, one relationship, one journey. It differs from realistic fiction because the author is telling you this happened to me, and that promise changes how you read. A hard scene in a memoir hits differently than the same scene in a novel, because the person on the page is the person who wrote it.

When you read a memoir, ask two sets of questions. About honesty: what does the writer admit that makes them look bad, and does the writer say how they know what they claim? About craft: where does the story start and end, which scenes get the most space, and what does the writer want you to understand about their life? A good memoir survives both.

24.8

O. Henry and the Turn

Main ideaO. Henry's stories build to a surprise ending, and reading one closely shows how a writer plants the turn while keeping you looking elsewhere.

A young wife counts her money three times and has one dollar and eighty-seven cents. Tomorrow is Christmas. She wants to buy her husband a chain for his prized pocket watch. She has one thing of value, her long hair, so she sells it. He, meanwhile, has sold the watch to buy combs for her hair. This is "The Gift of the Magi," published in 1905 by O. Henry, the pen name of William Sydney Porter, and it is the most famous surprise ending in American short fiction.

Read it closely and you see the machine. The story tells you early that the couple has exactly two treasures: her hair and his watch. It says so plainly, almost like a warning. Then it sends you with the wife to the hairdresser and the shop and keeps you busy with her worry about how she will look. You are so close to her that you forget the husband is somewhere doing the same math. The works because the clue was in plain sight and your attention was elsewhere.

O. Henry’s endings are not tricks. They are reversals that reveal something. Both gifts are useless, and the story’s last paragraph says the two of them were the wisest givers of all, because each gave up the thing they loved most. The surprise delivers the theme: the gift is the sacrifice, not the object. A twist that does not mean something is just a prank. A twist that does is a story.

When you read any story with a surprise ending, do what Andre did with Chopin. Read it again and find where the writer told you. Mark the sentence that names the two treasures. Notice how the story keeps you inside one character’s worry. That is how you learn the craft, and it is also how you start to see endings coming, which is a strange, satisfying power.

24.9

Jack London and the Cold

Main ideaIn "To Build a Fire," London uses a limited narrator and precise physical detail to turn a walk in the cold into a study of judgment and pride.

A man walks alone through the Yukon in winter with a dog beside him. It is far colder than he understands. An old-timer had warned him never to travel alone below fifty degrees below zero, and he had laughed. He breaks through ice, gets his feet wet, and must build a fire or die. He builds it under a spruce tree. The snow on the branches falls and puts it out. Jack London published "To Build a Fire" in 1908, and it is one of the coldest stories ever written.

London tells it in third person, staying close to the man but never naming him. That choice matters. The man is not a person you know; he is any person who trusts his own cleverness too much. The narrator keeps noting what the man does not notice. He sees his spit crackle in the air and does not take the hint. He is quick with his hands and slow in his mind, and the story says so almost directly.

The dog is the story’s second mind. It has no words, but it has instinct, and instinct knows it is too cold to travel. The narrator moves between the man’s confidence and the dog’s unease, and that contrast is the whole argument. Nature is not cruel here. It is simply indifferent, and the man’s error is to think that his plans mean anything to the cold.

Read one paragraph of this story slowly and count the physical details: the numb fingers, the matches he cannot grip, the ice on his beard. London makes you feel the cold in your own hands, and that is what makes the man’s pride so frightening. When you read London, watch how physical detail does the work that explanation would do in a weaker story.

Kate Chopin and the Hour

Main ideaChopin's "The Story of an Hour" packs a whole life into one hour and one room, and its irony depends on the reader knowing what the characters do not.

You already know the plot from this chapter’s opening story. Now look at the design. Kate Chopin, who lived in St. Louis and Louisiana, published the story in 1894. It takes place in one hour, in one house, mostly in one room with an open window. Almost nothing happens on the outside. Everything happens inside a woman who sits in a chair. That is a bold way to build a story, and Chopin makes it work through what the window shows.

Through the window, Mrs. Mallard sees the tops of trees full of new spring life, hears a peddler and a distant song, and smells rain. Chopin does not say these things stand for anything. She does not need to. The world outside is waking up while the woman inside is supposed to be grieving, and the reader feels the mismatch before the woman does. When her feeling finally arrives, it comes from the window in, and the setting has done the work.

The ending is : the doctors believe she died of joy at seeing her husband alive. The reader knows she died of the shock of losing the freedom she had just tasted. Irony is when the meaning the reader understands differs from what a character believes or says. Chopin trusts you to hold both meanings at once and to feel the gap between them. That gap is the story.

Notice what the story does not do. It does not call the husband cruel. It says his face had never looked at her except with love. Chopin is not writing about a bad marriage. She is writing about a woman who discovers that even a kind marriage, in her time and place, was a kind of cage. That is a harder and truer thing to say, and the story says it in a thousand words.

24.11

Giving a Book Talk

Main ideaA book talk is a short, planned spoken pitch that makes listeners want to read a book, built from a hook, a taste, and a reason, without spoiling the ending.

Two minutes. Twenty classmates. One book you finished. A is not a report and not a summary. It is a pitch. Your job is to make three people in that room want to read the book by Friday. Everything you say should serve that, and you plan it the way a writer plans a story: with an opening that grabs, a middle that shows, and an ending that leaves a question.

Start with a hook. Not "This book is called The Outsiders and it is by S. E. Hinton." Start with a boy walking out of a movie theater alone, knowing he might get jumped. Give the listener a person and a problem in two sentences. Then give a taste: read a short passage aloud, chosen for voice, no more than a few lines. Practice it so you do not stumble. A well-read passage sells a book better than any praise.

Then give a reason. Say what the book did to you. "I argued with my brother about whether Darry was right." "I re-read the last page three times." Be specific and honest, including a fair warning: it starts slow, it is sad, it uses old slang. Listeners trust a talk that admits something. Finally, end with a question the book raised and do not answer it. Never give away the ending.

Speak up, look at people, and hold the book so they can see the cover. Time yourself; two minutes is shorter than you think and longer than it feels. And listen to the other talks. A room full of book talks is the best reading list you will ever get, because every recommendation comes from someone who actually finished the book.

24.12

Books You Can Read and Books That Stretch You

Main ideaStrong readers read a mix of books they can read easily and books that stretch them, and they use simple tests to tell the difference before committing.

There are two kinds of books worth your time, and you need both. One kind you can read with ease: you know most of the words, the sentences do not fight you, and you can read for half an hour and forget you are reading. That is where stamina and pleasure grow. The other kind stretches you: a few words per page you do not know, sentences you sometimes re-read, a world you have to work to enter. That is where you grow.

The mistake is to read only one kind. A reader who only reads easy books gets fast but does not get stronger. A reader who only picks hard books, often to impress someone, quits in chapter three and decides reading is not for them. The mix is the point: mostly books you can read, and one stretch book at a time, with a plan for it.

Here is a quick test. Open the book to any full page in the middle and read it. Count the words you do not know and cannot figure out from the sentence. Zero to two means it is an easy book. Three to five means it is a stretch book, and a good one to try with a journal and a dictionary nearby. More than that, on page after page, means it is not the right book yet. Not never. Yet.

Also ask whether you want to turn the page. A book at the right level that you do not care about is still the wrong book. Ask a librarian, listen to book talks, read the first page of five books before choosing one, and give yourself permission to stop a book that is not working and try another. Readers who choose well read more, and readers who read more become the readers who can read anything.

  • State your claim in one sentence: dynamic or static, and in what way.
