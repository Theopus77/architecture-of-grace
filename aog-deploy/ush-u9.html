Postwar America

1945–1973

World War II ended in 1945 with the United States as the richest and strongest nation on Earth. Soldiers came home, families moved to new suburbs, and babies were born at record rates. Televisions, cars and new highways changed daily life. For millions of Americans, the “American Dream” of a home, a steady job and a better future for their children seemed within reach.

But the peace was uneasy. The United States and the Soviet Union became rivals in a Cold War that lasted more than 40 years. Fear of nuclear war and of communist spies shaped everything from school drills to foreign wars in Korea and Vietnam. At home, many Americans were shut out of the dream by segregation, poverty and discrimination.

Ordinary people refused to accept that. A six-year-old walked into a new school. Students sat at lunch counters. Farmworkers, women, Native Americans and people with disabilities organized for their rights. By 1973, new laws had changed the country, and a long, painful war had divided it. In this unit, you will see how Americans argued, marched and voted to decide what freedom should mean for everyone.

The big question

After World War II, how did Americans struggle to live up to the nation's promise of freedom and equality at home and abroad?

The story

The Box in the Living Room

In about ten years, television went from a rare gadget to the center of the American home.

Picture an evening in 1948. A family sits around a big wooden radio, listening to a comedy show. Down the street, one neighbor has something new: a heavy cabinet with a small, round glass screen. Kids from the whole block crowd onto that neighbor's floor. They stare at a fuzzy black-and-white picture that flickers and hums. In 1950, fewer than one in ten American homes owned a television set.

Then everything changed fast. Factories that had built radios and radar during World War II switched to making TV sets. Prices dropped, and families bought sets on credit, paying a little each month. Metal antennas sprouted on rooftops like new trees. By 1960, almost nine out of ten homes had a TV. Furniture was rearranged so every chair faced the screen. Companies even sold frozen “TV dinners” so families could eat while they watched.

What did people watch? Comedies like I Love Lucy made millions laugh at the same jokes on the same night. Children watched puppet shows and cowboy adventures. Families watched quiz shows, variety shows and sports. Many shows showed a certain picture of American life: a white, middle-class family in a neat house in the suburbs. Millions of Americans who were Black, Latino, Asian American, Native American or poor rarely saw families like their own on the screen.

Television also carried the news into the living room. In 1954, millions watched Senator Joseph McCarthy question witnesses in hearings about communists in government. Many viewers did not like what they saw, and his power faded. Political campaigns began to buy TV ads. Soon, cameras would bring the struggle for civil rights, a war in Vietnam and a trip to the Moon into American homes.

Television helped tie a huge country together. A family in Illinois and a family in California laughed at the same shows and saw the same ads for cars, cereal and washing machines. That shared screen helped build the dream of a good life that many Americans chased after the war. It also raised a question that still matters: whose stories get told, and whose get left out?

Home From the War

25.1

Veterans Come Home

Main ideaThe GI Bill helped millions of veterans go to school and buy homes, but not every veteran got the same help.

When World War II ended in 1945, more than 12 million Americans were in uniform. Almost all of them came home within about two years. They needed jobs, homes and a chance to rebuild their lives. Many leaders worried the country would slide back into a depression, like the hard times of the 1930s.

Congress had already passed a law to help. It was called the . GI was a nickname for American soldiers. The law paid for veterans to go to college or learn a trade. It also helped them get home loans with low payments. Millions of veterans used it. Colleges filled up, and many families became the first in their line to own a home or earn a degree.

Not everyone shared equally. Banks and local offices ran much of the program. In many places, especially in the South, Black veterans were turned away from loans and from colleges. This was , treating people unfairly because of their race or another group they belong to. Women who had worked in war factories were often pushed out of those jobs so returning men could have them.

Still, the GI Bill changed the country. It helped create a large , a group of families who earned enough to own homes, buy cars and save a little money. Many historians see it as one of the most important laws of the twentieth century.

25.2

The Baby Boom

Main ideaAfter the war, Americans had babies at record rates, and this baby boom changed schools, homes and businesses for decades.

During the Great Depression and the war, many couples waited to start families. Once peace came and jobs were plentiful, they stopped waiting. Births in the United States jumped in 1946 and stayed high until the mid-1960s. This huge rise in births is called the .

The children born in these years are called . There were so many of them that they changed almost everything they touched. Towns rushed to build new schools and hire teachers. Classrooms were packed. Companies sold more diapers, toys, cereal and bicycles than ever before.

Doctors and scientists helped these children grow up healthier. In 1955, a vaccine made by Dr. Jonas Salk was approved to protect against polio. Polio was a disease that had paralyzed thousands of children each year. Parents lined up to get their kids the shots, and cases dropped quickly.

The baby boom kept shaping the nation as the boomers grew. In the 1960s they became teenagers and college students, and many joined protests and new movements. Much later, as they grew old, they changed health care and retirement plans. One big generation can move a whole country.

25.3

Truman's Fair Deal

Main ideaPresident Truman tried to build on the New Deal with his Fair Deal, and he won a surprise election in 1948.

Harry S. Truman became president in April 1945, when Franklin Roosevelt died. Truman was a plain-spoken man from Missouri. He faced hard problems right away. Prices rose quickly after the war, a problem called . Millions of workers went on strike for higher pay. Many voters were unhappy.

In 1947, Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act over Truman’s veto. The law limited some of the things labor unions could do during strikes. Truman and the unions were angry about it. Congress, controlled by Republicans at the time, blocked many of Truman’s ideas.

Almost everyone expected Truman to lose the 1948 election to Thomas Dewey, the governor of New York. Truman’s own Democratic Party split three ways. Truman traveled the country by train and gave hundreds of short speeches. On election night, the Chicago Daily Tribune printed the headline “Dewey Defeats Truman” before all the votes were counted. Truman won. A famous photo shows him grinning and holding up that wrong headline.

Truman called his plan the . Congress raised the minimum wage, expanded Social Security and paid to build housing for low-income families. But Congress turned down his plan for national health insurance. The Fair Deal kept much of the New Deal alive, even as the nation’s attention turned to a new rival overseas.

25.4

Two Superpowers Split

Main ideaAfter World War II, the United States and the Soviet Union became rivals in a long struggle called the Cold War.

The United States and the Soviet Union fought on the same side in World War II. Once the war ended, their friendship fell apart fast. Both countries came out of the war as , nations with huge armies and great influence around the world. But they believed in very different ways of life.

The United States had and democracy. People and companies owned businesses, and voters chose their leaders in free elections. The Soviet Union had under a dictator, Joseph Stalin. The government owned farms and factories, allowed only one political party and jailed people who spoke against it.

Soviet armies had pushed the Germans out of Eastern Europe. After the war, they stayed. Stalin helped communists take power in Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia and other countries. In 1946, Britain’s former leader Winston Churchill spoke at a college in Fulton, Missouri. He said an “iron curtain” had fallen across Europe, cutting the East off from the West.

This struggle became known as the . It was “cold” because the United States and the Soviet Union never fought each other directly in a full war. Instead they competed for allies, built weapons, spied on each other and backed opposite sides in wars in other countries. The Cold War lasted more than 40 years.

Containment in Action

Main ideaThe U.S. tried to stop communism from spreading with a policy called containment, using aid like the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan.

American leaders asked a big question: how do we stop communism from spreading without starting another world war? An American diplomat named George Kennan offered an answer. He said the United States should firmly block Soviet power wherever it tried to grow. This idea was called , which means keeping something inside its limits.

In 1947, Greece and Turkey were in danger. Communist fighters were at war with the government in Greece, and the Soviets were pressing Turkey. Truman asked Congress for money to help both nations. His promise to help free peoples resist communist takeovers became known as the . Congress agreed.

Western Europe had another problem: it was in ruins. Cities were bombed, factories were broken and people were hungry. Hungry, desperate people might turn to communism. Secretary of State George Marshall proposed a giant aid program. Under the , the United States sent about 13 billion dollars to help rebuild Europe.

The Marshall Plan worked. Western European countries grew strong again and stayed democracies. They also became good trading partners for American businesses. The Soviet Union refused the aid and did not let the countries it controlled accept it either. Europe was now split into two camps.

25.6

The Berlin Airlift and NATO

Main ideaWhen the Soviets blocked the roads into West Berlin, the U.S. and its allies flew in supplies, and soon they formed the NATO alliance.

After the war, Germany was split into four zones. The United States, Britain and France controlled the western parts. The Soviet Union controlled the east. Berlin, the capital, sat deep inside the Soviet zone. It was also split, with West Berlin run by the Western allies.

In June 1948, Stalin cut off every road, railroad and canal into West Berlin. He hoped to force the Western allies out. More than two million people there could soon run out of food and coal. Truman did not want to start a war by forcing his way in. He also did not want to give up the city.

The answer came by air. American and British pilots flew cargo planes into West Berlin day and night. At the busiest times, a plane landed every few minutes. They carried flour, milk, medicine and coal. One pilot dropped candy on tiny parachutes to children. This lasted almost a year. In May 1949, Stalin gave up and lifted the .

The crisis pushed Western nations closer together. In 1949, the United States, Canada and ten European countries formed , the North Atlantic Treaty Organization. Members promised that an attack on one would be treated as an attack on all. In 1955 the Soviet Union formed its own alliance, the Warsaw Pact. Germany stayed divided into West Germany and East Germany.

25.7

China and the Korean War

Main ideaAfter communists won China in 1949, the U.S. fought in Korea to stop communism from spreading, and the war ended in a stalemate.

In 1949, two shocks hit Americans. First, the Soviet Union tested its own atomic bomb. Second, communists led by Mao Zedong won a long civil war in China, the world’s most populous country. China’s old government fled to the island of Taiwan. Many Americans asked, “Who lost China?” Fear of communism grew.

Korea, a peninsula next to China, had been split after World War II at a line called the 38th parallel. The north had a communist government backed by the Soviets. The south was backed by the United States. In June 1950, North Korea’s army invaded South Korea. The United Nations voted to defend South Korea. Most of the troops sent were American, led by General Douglas MacArthur.

The war swung back and forth. MacArthur’s forces pushed the North Koreans almost to the Chinese border. Then hundreds of thousands of Chinese soldiers poured in and drove them back. MacArthur wanted to attack China itself. Truman feared that could start a third world war. When MacArthur criticized the president in public, Truman fired him in 1951. In the U.S., the Constitution puts elected leaders, not generals, in charge of the military.

After two more years of hard fighting, both sides signed an in July 1953. Korea stayed divided near where it started. More than 36,000 Americans died, along with many hundreds of thousands of Koreans and Chinese. The war ended in a . Korea is still divided today.

The Red Scare at Home

Main ideaFear of communist spies led to a Red Scare in which many Americans were accused without proof and lost their jobs.

Communists were often called “Reds,” after the color of the Soviet flag. In the late 1940s and 1950s, a wave of fear called the swept the country. Some real spy cases made the fear grow. Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were convicted of passing atomic secrets to the Soviets and were executed in 1953.

The government began checking the loyalty of millions of workers. A committee in Congress called HUAC questioned writers, actors and teachers about their political beliefs. Hollywood studios put hundreds of people on a , a list of people who would not be hired. Many lost their careers even though they had broken no law.

In 1950, Senator Joseph McCarthy of Wisconsin claimed he had a list of communists working in the State Department. He never proved it. 25.9

Living With the Bomb

Main ideaThe U.S. and Soviet Union raced to build more powerful weapons, and fear of nuclear war became part of everyday life.

Once the Soviets had the atomic bomb in 1949, an began. Each side rushed to build more and stronger weapons than the other. In 1952, the United States tested a hydrogen bomb, far more powerful than the bombs dropped on Japan. The Soviets soon tested one too. Both sides built long-range bombers and, later, missiles that could cross oceans.

Ordinary Americans lived with the fear of nuclear war. Schools held drills where children practiced ducking under their desks and covering their heads. A cartoon turtle named Bert taught kids to “duck and cover.” Some families built rooms in their backyards or basements and stocked them with canned food and water.

On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched , the first human-made satellite, into orbit around Earth. Americans were stunned. If the Soviets could launch a satellite, they could launch a missile. Many people worried the United States had fallen behind in science.

The government acted fast. In 1958, Congress created NASA to run the American space program. It also passed a law that paid for more science, math and foreign language classes in schools and colleges. The space race had begun, and classrooms were part of it.

25.10

Highways and Suburbs

Main ideaUnder President Eisenhower, new highways and cheap homes helped millions of families move to the suburbs.

In 1952, voters elected Dwight D. Eisenhower, the general who had led the D-Day invasion. People called him “Ike.” He was a Republican, but he kept most New Deal programs like Social Security. He wanted to balance the budget and keep the country calm and steady.

As a young army officer, Eisenhower had crossed the country in a slow truck convoy on muddy roads. In Germany during the war, he saw its fast highways. In 1956 he signed the Federal-Aid Highway Act. It paid to build about 41,000 miles of , wide roads with no stoplights linking cities across the country.

Highways helped create the , neighborhoods of houses outside a city. In 1947, builder William Levitt began Levittown on Long Island, New York. His workers built houses like an assembly line, one step at a time, so they were cheap. With GI Bill loans, a veteran could buy one for a small payment each month. Similar suburbs grew around Chicago and cities across the country.

But many suburbs were closed to Black families. Levittown’s rules at first said homes could not be sold to anyone who was not white. Banks and the government often refused loans in Black neighborhoods, a practice called . As white families left for the suburbs, many cities lost money and jobs, while Black families were kept out.

Left Out of the Boom

Main ideaMillions of Americans, including many Black, Latino and Native American families, did not share in the 1950s boom.

TV shows made it look as if every family had a new house and a new car. That was not true. In 1959, about one in five Americans lived in , meaning they did not have enough money for basic needs. Poor families lived in rural areas like Appalachia and the Deep South, and in crowded neighborhoods in big cities.

Black Americans faced segregation in the South and discrimination in the North. In Chicago, many Black families who had come north in the Great Migration were crowded into a few neighborhoods. Landlords charged high rents for poor housing. Good jobs and new suburbs were often closed to them.

Mexican Americans and Mexican workers also struggled. Under the Bracero Program, millions of Mexican workers came to work on American farms for low pay and in hard conditions. Another program encouraged Native people to leave reservations for cities such as Chicago, Denver and Los Angeles. Many found low-paying jobs and felt cut off from home. Many tribes fought to keep their land and their nations alive.

The story

Ruby Bridges Walks to School

In 1960, a six-year-old girl walked past an angry crowd to go to first grade.

On the morning of November 14, 1960, a six-year-old girl named Ruby Bridges put on her best dress in New Orleans, Louisiana. Four U.S. marshals, officers of the federal government, came to her door. They drove Ruby and her mother to William Frantz Elementary School. Every student at that school was white. Ruby was about to become the first Black child to attend it.

A federal court had ordered New Orleans to integrate its schools, which means to open them to students of all races. Ruby had been chosen after passing a test given to Black children. Outside the school, crowds of white adults shouted and waved signs. The marshals walked in front of and behind Ruby, so she was surrounded by protection. She kept walking.

Inside, many white parents pulled their children out of the school. For most of that year, Ruby was the only student in her classroom. Her teacher, Barbara Henry, had come from Boston to teach. Every day the two of them worked together on reading, writing and math. Mrs. Henry treated Ruby with warmth and care.

Ruby's family paid a price for her bravery. Her father lost his job. Her grandparents, who farmed land in Mississippi, were forced to leave it. But neighbors stepped in. Some watched Ruby's younger brothers and sisters. Some walked behind the marshals' car to make sure she got to school safely. Slowly, a few white families sent their children back. The next year, more Black students joined the school.

In 1964, the artist Norman Rockwell painted Ruby walking between the marshals, with a small, straight back and a crowd's hateful words on the wall. He called it “The Problem We All Live With.” Ruby Bridges grew up, raised a family and spent years speaking to students about fairness. Her walk became a symbol of the courage it took, even for a child, to change the country.

Main ideaLawyers for Mexican American and Black families won court cases that ruled segregated schools unfair, ending with Brown v. Board of Education in 1954.

1968: The Year That Shook the Nation

In a single year, war, murder, protest and a trip around the Moon filled America's TV screens.

On January 30, 1968, during the Vietnamese New Year holiday called Tet, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces launched surprise attacks on more than 100 cities and towns across South Vietnam. American leaders had told the public the war was being won. Now TV news showed fighting even at the U.S. Embassy in Saigon. Walter Cronkite, the most trusted newsman in America, told viewers he believed the war was stuck in a stalemate.

On March 31, President Lyndon Johnson went on TV and surprised the nation: he would not run for another term. Four days later, on April 4, Martin Luther King Jr. was shot and killed in Memphis, Tennessee. He had gone there to support Black sanitation workers on strike for fair pay. Grief and anger exploded. Riots broke out in more than 100 cities, including on Chicago's West Side. A week later, Congress passed the Fair Housing Act, which banned discrimination in selling and renting homes.

In June, Senator Robert F. Kennedy, the brother of the late president, won California's primary election in his run for president. Minutes after his victory speech in Los Angeles, he was shot. He died the next day. Many young Americans who had hoped he would end the war felt lost.

In August, the Democratic Party held its national convention in Chicago. Thousands of antiwar protesters came to the city. Mayor Richard J. Daley's police and National Guard troops clashed with them in Grant Park and on Michigan Avenue. TV cameras showed police beating protesters, and protesters chanted, “The whole world is watching!” Inside, the party nominated Vice President Hubert Humphrey. American leaders believed in the . They feared that if Vietnam fell to communism, nearby countries would fall one after another, like a row of dominoes. Presidents Eisenhower and Kennedy sent money, weapons and military advisers to South Vietnam. By 1963, about 16,000 American advisers were there.

Many Vietnamese saw the war differently. To them, it was a long fight for independence from foreign control, first from France and then from the United States. This difference in how each side saw the war helps explain why it was so hard for the U.S. to win.
