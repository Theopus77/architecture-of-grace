Sugyot in Paraphrase II: Argument and Mercy

Aggadah and Ethics

Two schools argue about hundreds of rules. A heavenly voice takes a side, and the rabbis politely say no. A great teacher leaves the path, and his student keeps learning from him anyway. These are some of the most famous passages in the Talmud. In this unit you will read them in paraphrase and follow how they move.

The unit has two threads. The first is argument: how the rabbis disagreed, who decided, and why they kept the losing side on the page. The second is mercy: how the Talmud's stories and sayings protect people from shame, gossip and harsh judgment.

By the end you will be able to explain Eruvin 13b and Bava Metzia 59b in your own words. You will be able to quote Pirkei Avot and say who said what. And you will be able to show how Jewish readers, from traditional to academic, have read these texts differently.

The big question

How can people argue hard and still treat each other with mercy?

The story

The Voice That Was Overruled

A heavenly voice takes one side in a debate, and the rabbis politely say that it does not get a vote.

Picture a study hall in the Land of Israel around the year 100 CE. The floor is stone. Men sit in rows on low benches. At the front, a teacher named Rabbi Eliezer is arguing about an oven. It is a strange oven, built from pieces with sand between them. Can it become ritually impure, or not? Rabbi Eliezer says it cannot. Every other rabbi in the room says it can.

The Talmud, in the tractate Bava Metzia on page 59b, tells what happened next. Rabbi Eliezer gave every argument he had. The others did not accept them. So he said: if the law is as I say, let this carob tree prove it. The story says the tree tore itself out of the ground and moved. The rabbis answered that a tree proves nothing. He called on a stream of water, and it ran backward. He called on the walls of the study hall, and they began to lean. Still the rabbis said no.

Then Rabbi Eliezer called on heaven itself. The story says a voice came from the sky and asked why they argued with him, since the law always followed him. At that moment Rabbi Joshua stood up. He quoted a line from the Torah, from Deuteronomy chapter 30: the Torah "is not in heaven." A later teacher, Rabbi Jeremiah, explained what he meant. The Torah was already given at Mount Sinai. Now people decide its meaning, and they decide by majority.

That is only half the story. The Talmud goes on to tell what the majority did after they won, and how their treatment of Rabbi Eliezer caused great pain. The whole passage sits inside a discussion about hurting people with words. So the story is about two things at once. It is about who has the authority to decide. It is also about how to treat the person who loses.

In this chapter you will meet the two great Houses, Hillel and Shammai, who argued for years until a voice said that both spoke truth. You will read the Oven of Akhnai in paraphrase, first half and second half. You will see how the Mishnah keeps the opinions that lost. And you will ask what it means to argue for the sake of heaven.

Talk about itRabbi Joshua would not let a miracle settle an argument. What do you think a good argument should be settled by?
Section 1

Two Houses, One Law

21.1

The Houses of Hillel and Shammai

Main ideaHillel and Shammai were two teachers around 30 BCE, and the schools named for them disagreed on hundreds of rules.

Hillel and Shammai were two teachers who lived in Jerusalem around 30 BCE, in the time of King Herod. The Mishnah names them as the last of the , the pairs of leaders who came before the rabbis. Hillel is remembered in the Talmud as patient and gentle. Shammai is remembered as strict and quick. Each gathered students, and each group kept teaching after its founder died.

The students formed two schools. The Talmud calls them and , the House of Hillel and the House of Shammai. The word beit means house. The two Houses argued about hundreds of questions. How should the Hanukkah lamps be lit? What may a person carry on Shabbat? Which blessing comes first at a meal? Most of the time, Beit Shammai took the stricter reading and Beit Hillel the more lenient one. Not always.

The Mishnah records both sides of these debates. In most cases it does not say who won. That is important. The Mishnah, edited around the year 200 CE, was written long after both Houses were gone. By then Jewish practice followed Beit Hillel almost everywhere. Yet the editors kept Beit Shammai’s words on the page. The next lesson tells the story the Talmud gives for why.

21.2

"These and Those"

Main ideaThe Talmud in Eruvin 13b says a heavenly voice called both Houses' words "the words of the living God" but ruled with Beit Hillel.

The tractate is about the boundaries of Shabbat, where a person may walk and carry. On page 13b it pauses for a story. The Gemara says that Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel argued for three years. Each side said: the law follows us. Then a , a voice from heaven, spoke. The Gemara reports its words this way: these and those are the words of the living God, and the law follows Beit Hillel.

Readers have found this puzzling for centuries. If both are the words of the living God, how can one side be wrong? The Gemara does not treat the two as one right and one wrong. It treats them as two true ways to read the Torah. Yet a community needs one practice. People must know which lamp to light and which blessing to say first. So the voice picked one for daily life without calling the other false.

Different Jewish readers explain this in different ways. Some traditional commentators say each House saw a real side of the truth. Some modern academic readers say the story reflects a time when Beit Hillel had already won, and the story explains why. Rabbinic tradition holds both ideas together: the argument is kept, and one ruling is followed. This is the pattern for the whole Talmud.

21.3

Why the Law Follows Hillel

Main ideaThe Gemara says Beit Hillel earned the ruling by being humble and teaching the other side's view first.

The Gemara in Eruvin 13b asks a fair question. If both Houses spoke the words of the living God, why did the voice pick Beit Hillel? The answer it gives is not about being smarter. It says the students of Hillel were kind and humble. They taught their own opinions and the opinions of Beit Shammai. And when they taught, they put Beit Shammai’s words before their own.

Think about what that means in a classroom. Before you say why you are right, you say what the other side thinks, in their words, fairly. You even say it first. The Gemara treats this habit as a reason to trust a school’s rulings. A teacher who has really listened to the other side is more likely to get the law right. And a community can follow such a teacher without feeling that one side was crushed.

The Gemara adds a lesson it draws from the story: whoever makes themselves low, the Holy One raises up. Rabbinic tradition reads the Hillel story as a lesson in , humility. Historians add another layer. The Mishnah was edited by Judah the Prince, who traced his family line to Hillel. Both readings can be true of the same text. One is about character; the other is about who was holding the pen.

Section 2

The Oven of Akhnai

21.4

The Oven and the Miracles

Main ideaIn Bava Metzia 59b, Rabbi Eliezer calls on a tree, a stream and walls to prove his ruling, and the sages refuse each one.

The story begins in the Mishnah, in the tractate Kelim, which deals with vessels. An oven was built in pieces, with sand packed between them. Rabbi Eliezer said that such an oven could not become ritually impure, because it was not really one object. The other sages said it could. The Mishnah calls it the oven of . The Gemara says the name means snake, because the arguments wound around it like a snake.

The Gemara in 59b tells what happened. Rabbi Eliezer brought every proof he could think of. The sages did not accept them. So he said: if the law is with me, let this carob tree prove it. The tree pulled itself up and moved a long way. The sages answered that no proof comes from a carob tree. He said: let the stream prove it. The water ran backward. They said no proof comes from a stream.

Then he said: let the walls of the study hall prove it. The walls began to fall in. Rabbi Joshua scolded the walls. He said that when scholars argue about the law, walls have no business joining in. The Gemara adds a gentle detail. Out of respect for Rabbi Joshua the walls did not fall, and out of respect for Rabbi Eliezer they did not stand straight again. They stayed leaning.

Notice what the sages are doing. They never say the miracles did not happen. They say the miracles are not the kind of proof that counts. A rule of the Torah is decided by reasons and by the vote of the scholars. Rabbi Eliezer, a tanna of the generation after the Temple’s fall in 70 CE, was one of the greatest scholars alive. That is what makes the story hard, and the next lesson shows the turn.

Think it through
Three Proofs Refused

A carob tree moves. A stream runs uphill. The walls of the room lean in. After each one, the sages say the same thing: this is not proof. Rabbi Joshua even tells the walls to stay out of the argument.

Why might the rabbis have decided that a wonder, even a real one, should not settle a question about the law?

21.5

"It Is Not in Heaven"

Main ideaRabbi Joshua answers the heavenly voice with Deuteronomy 30:12, and Rabbi Jeremiah explains that the law is decided by majority.

Rabbi Eliezer had one proof left. He said: if the law is with me, let heaven prove it. The Gemara says a bat kol went out and said: why do you argue with Rabbi Eliezer, when the law follows him in every place? This was the moment. A voice from heaven had taken a side. The whole room heard it.

Rabbi Joshua stood on his feet and quoted the Torah. The verse is Deuteronomy 30:12, and it says the commandment "is not in heaven." In Deuteronomy the verse means that the Torah is not far away or too hard to reach. Rabbi Joshua reads it in a new way. The Torah has already been given. It is on earth now, in the hands of people who study it.

The Gemara asks what Rabbi Joshua meant, and it lets a later teacher explain. Rabbi Jeremiah, an amora who taught in the Land of Israel in the fourth century, says: the Torah was already given at Mount Sinai, so we pay no attention to a heavenly voice. He points to another verse, Exodus 23:2, which the rabbis read as: follow the . Once the Torah was given, the rule is decided by the vote of the scholars.

This is one of the most quoted moments in the Talmud. Readers have used it to say that the law lives in human hands and human argument. Others add that this freedom has limits: the majority must reason from the text, not invent. Notice too that Rabbi Joshua does not call the voice a lie. He says it is not the way the law is decided. The Gemara has one more scene, and it changes everything.

21.6

Elijah's Report

Main ideaRabbi Nathan meets the prophet Elijah, who says that God laughed and said, "My children have defeated me."

The Gemara adds a short scene. Rabbi Nathan, a tanna of the second century, met the prophet . In rabbinic stories Elijah is a figure who did not die and who appears to certain rabbis to tell them things. Rabbi Nathan asked him: what did the Holy One do at that hour, when Rabbi Joshua overruled the voice?

Elijah answered that God laughed and said: my children have defeated me, my children have defeated me. In the story the response is not anger. It is something like a parent’s delight when a child wins an argument fairly. Rabbinic tradition reads this as approval. The rabbis were supposed to take the Torah into their own hands and reason with it.

Different readers stress different things here. Many traditional readers see the laugh as proof that human courts have real authority. Some academic readers point out that the story was told by rabbis and gives authority to rabbis, so it should be read as their own view of themselves. Both agree on what the text does: it makes the majority’s decision stand, and it makes heaven agree.

Stop and notice the shape of the so far. A Mishnah states a dispute. The Gemara brings proofs, and each is rejected. A voice gives an answer, and a verse answers the voice. A later amora explains the verse. A story confirms the result. This is a full argument, with every move in place. And then the Gemara asks what happened next.

21.7

The Ban and the Wave

Main ideaThe second half of the story shows the majority hurting Rabbi Eliezer, and it sits inside a discussion of wronging people with words.

The Gemara does not end with the laugh. It says that on that day the sages gathered everything Rabbi Eliezer had declared pure and burned it. Then they voted to place him under a , a kind of separation from the community. They asked: who will go and tell him? Rabbi Akiva, his own student, said he would, so that no one less careful would break the news and destroy the world.

Rabbi Akiva dressed in black and sat at a distance. Rabbi Eliezer asked why this day was different. Rabbi Akiva said gently that it seemed his friends were keeping away from him. Rabbi Eliezer understood. He tore his clothes and wept. The Gemara says that at that hour a third of the olives, wheat and barley in the world were struck. It is telling the reader that his pain was not small.

Rabban Gamliel, the head of the court, was on a ship. A great wave rose against him. He said: I know this is because of Rabbi Eliezer. He prayed that he had not acted for his own honor but to stop disputes from spreading, and the sea grew calm. Later, his sister Imma Shalom, who was Rabbi Eliezer’s wife, tried to keep her husband from a certain prayer. One day she failed. The Gemara says Rabban Gamliel died. Told gently, the point is this: the majority won, and it still caused harm.

Why is this story in Bava Metzia, a tractate about money? Because the Mishnah before it, Bava Metzia 4:10, is about ona&#x27;at devarim, wronging with words. The Gemara says that all the gates of heaven may be locked except the gate through which a wounded person’s cry enters. The Akhnai story is placed there as an example. Rabbi Eliezer was wronged with words. Authority and mercy are argued on the same page.

Think it through
One Story, Two Halves

First half: the majority overrules a heavenly voice, and heaven laughs. Second half: the majority burns Rabbi Eliezer's rulings, bans him, and his weeping brings harm to the world and to the head of the court. The Gemara tells both halves in one breath, inside a discussion about hurting people with words.

Why do you think the editors of the Talmud kept the second half instead of ending the story at the laugh?

Section 3

Arguing Well

21.8

Rabbi Joshua and the Emperor's Daughter

Main ideaIn Ta'anit 7a, Rabbi Joshua answers an insult about his looks with a lesson about plain jars and good wine.

Rabbi Joshua ben Hananiah, the same teacher who answered the heavenly voice, appears in many stories with Romans. The tractate Ta&#x27;anit, which is about fast days, tells one on page 7a. The emperor’s daughter said to him: such beautiful wisdom in such an ugly vessel. She was mocking his face. He did not get angry. He asked a question instead.

He asked her what her father kept his wine in. She said: in plain clay jars. He said: everyone keeps wine in clay jars. A great emperor should keep his in gold and silver. So she had the wine moved into gold and silver vessels, and it turned sour. When she asked him why, he said: wisdom is the same. It keeps best in a plain vessel.

The Gemara adds a small twist. She asked whether there were not handsome people who were also wise. He said yes, but they would be wiser if they were plain. The story is , a story rather than a law. It shows the rabbis’ picture of Rabbi Joshua: quick, calm and able to turn an insult into a lesson without returning it. That is the same skill he used against the voice from heaven.

Think it through
Wine in a Gold Jar

Someone tells you that your idea is good but you do not look like a person who could have thought of it. You could answer with an insult of your own. Rabbi Joshua answered with a question about wine jars, and let the other person find the answer.

What does the story suggest about the best way to answer someone who is being rude?

21.9

Keeping the Minority Voice

Main ideaMishnah Eduyot 1:4–6 explains why the opinions that lost are still written down.

A small tractate of the Mishnah called , meaning testimonies, asks a question most law books never ask. Why record the words of a single sage against the majority, when the law follows the majority? The Mishnah gives two answers in its first chapter, in mishnayot 1:4 through 1:6.

The first answer, in Eduyot 1:4, is about the Houses. Why keep Shammai’s and Hillel’s words when they were not accepted? To teach future generations that a person should not stand stubbornly on their own words, since even these great teachers did not have every word accepted. The second answer, in 1:5, is about courts. A later court may see the view and rely on it, because it was recorded. If it were erased, no one could ever use it.

This is the opposite of how many systems work. In many places the losing side is forgotten. The Mishnah keeps it. Rabbi Judah, in Eduyot 1:6, adds a third reason: so that if someone later says, I have a tradition like this, you can answer, you heard it from that individual, not from the majority. Keeping the minority view lets you know exactly what is and is not the rule.

Historians note that this habit is one reason the Talmud looks the way it does: pages of disagreement rather than a list of answers. For readers of the Akhnai story, it adds something. Rabbi Eliezer lost the vote. His opinion is still on the page, with his name. Being overruled is not the same as being erased.

21.10

For the Sake of Heaven

Main ideaPirkei Avot 5:17 says an argument for the sake of heaven lasts, and it names Hillel and Shammai as the example.

, the Chapters of the Fathers, is a tractate of the Mishnah made entirely of sayings. In chapter 5, mishnah 17, it sorts arguments into two kinds. An argument that is for the sake of heaven will endure. An argument that is not for the sake of heaven will not endure. Then it gives one example of each.

The example of an argument for the sake of heaven is Hillel and Shammai. The example of the other kind is Korah and his group, who in the book of Numbers rebelled against Moses. Readers have asked what "endure" means here. Many say it means the argument keeps giving fruit: both sides are studied, both are remembered, and people keep learning from it. The Houses’ debates are still studied today. Korah’s rebellion ended and taught nothing.

What makes an argument for the sake of heaven? Commentators such as Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura, who wrote on the Mishnah in the fifteenth century, say it is one in which both sides want the truth, not victory. Each side is willing to be shown wrong. Both sides respect the other. That fits Beit Hillel teaching Beit Shammai’s view first. It also gives a way to think about the Akhnai story: the argument was for the sake of heaven, and yet the way it ended still hurt.

This mishnah is often used in Jewish schools and adult study today, across Orthodox, Conservative and Reform communities, as a rule for how to disagree. In a public school it can be read the same way any wisdom text is read: as one tradition’s answer to a question everyone faces. When you argue, are you trying to win, or trying to find out what is true?

How Readers Read Akhnai Today

Main ideaOrthodox, Conservative, Reform and academic readers all use the Akhnai story, and each draws a different lesson from it.

The Oven of Akhnai is one of the most discussed passages in the Talmud. Different Jewish readers use it to say different things. Many readers stress that the majority won because it followed the rules of the Torah itself, including the verse about the majority. For them the story shows that the law is decided by trained scholars using the text, not by miracles and not by whatever a person feels.

Many readers stress the same story as proof that the law grows through human decision. If the rabbis could overrule a heavenly voice, then later courts can also make changes, as long as they reason from the sources. Many readers go further and read the story as a charter for human freedom and conscience in interpreting tradition. These are ways of reading one text; the text does not settle which is right.

Academic readers, in universities including those in Chicago, often ask a different set of questions. Who told this story and when? The heroes are rabbis, and the story gives authority to rabbis. Its second half, they note, was likely edited into place by the Talmud’s redactors to fit the theme of wronging with words. Some also compare the version in the Jerusalem Talmud, which is shorter.

None of these readings has to be the only one. The Talmud itself kept the argument, the voice, the laugh, the ban and the wave together. A reader in a public school can do the same: describe what the text says, describe how each group reads it, and then think for yourself. That is what this unit means by argument. The next chapter turns to the other thread, mercy.

22.1

Stories Beside the Law

Main ideaAggadah is the Talmud's stories, sayings and ethics, woven between its legal debates.

Open almost any tractate and you find two kinds of writing. One kind argues about rules. How much must a finder announce? When may a worker leave a job? That legal part is called . The other kind tells stories, gives advice and explains verses. That part is called , from an Aramaic word for telling.

The two are mixed together on the same page. A debate about the Sabbath may stop for a story about Hillel. A list of rules about damages may end with a saying about kindness. Scholars estimate that aggadah fills about a quarter to a third of the Babylonian Talmud. Estimates vary by how it is counted.

Readers have used aggadah in different ways. Many Jewish teachers read it as the heart of the law, the reason behind the rules. Maimonides warned readers not to take every story only at face value. Academic scholars study it as a window into the rabbis’ world. In this chapter you will read the aggadah that teaches ethics: how to speak, how to change and how to treat people.

Main ideaPirkei Avot opens with a chain of teachers that links the rabbis back to Moses, as rabbinic tradition holds.

means Chapters of the Fathers. It is a tractate of the Mishnah, but it has almost no law in it. It is a collection of sayings by teachers across about five hundred years. Many Jews read one chapter each Sabbath afternoon in the summer.

The first mishnah names a chain. Moses received the Torah at Sinai and passed it to Joshua. Joshua passed it to the elders, the elders to the prophets, and the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly. Then the chain runs through named teachers, down to Hillel and Shammai and the rabbis after them. This line is called the , the handing down.

Rabbinic tradition holds that this chain carried the Oral Torah beside the written one. Historians study the chain as a statement of the rabbis’ authority. It tells readers why the rabbis felt they could interpret the Torah. Both groups agree on one thing. The chain puts teachers, not kings or priests, at the center of the story.

Hillel's Three Questions

Main ideaHillel's saying balances caring for yourself, caring for others, and acting now.

One of the most quoted lines in Avot comes from Hillel. It is Avot 1:14. It is three short questions. If I am not for myself, who will be for me? If I am only for myself, what am I? And if not now, when?

Each question pulls against the one before. The first says a person has a duty to stand up for their own life and needs. The second warns that a life only for yourself is too small. The third says good plans mean nothing until you act. Teachers call this a balance, not a list.

Readers have heard the saying in many ways. Jewish commentators linked the first question to a person’s own learning and good deeds. Some modern readers hear it as a call for self-advocacy, speaking up for what helps you. Others hear it as a call to join the community. The saying leaves room for all of these readings.

Main ideaRabbi Tarfon taught that no one must finish the work alone, but no one is free to quit it.

Speech That Harms

Main ideaThe rabbis treated lashon hara, true but harmful talk about others, as a serious wrong.

means evil tongue. In rabbinic teaching it is saying something true but harmful about another person. Spreading lies is a separate wrong. The Talmud treats both as serious.

The Talmud in Arakhin 15b says harmful gossip hurts three people. It hurts the one who speaks, the one who listens and the one spoken about. The Gemara reads the Bible’s stories of skin disease, such as Miriam’s in Numbers 12, as warnings about speech.

Rabbinic tradition builds these teachings on a verse. Leviticus 19:16 says do not go up and down as a talebearer. Later teachers wrote whole books on the ethics of speech. The best known is by the Chafetz Chaim, a rabbi in Poland who died in 1933. Many schools today teach his rules as a guide to kind talk.

Main ideaThe Talmud pairs its sharp arguments with teachings about mercy.

The Talmud is famous for argument. It is also full of teachings about mercy. In Berakhot 7a the Gemara says, in the words of a rabbinic story, that God prays too. The prayer is that mercy may overcome anger.

This idea shows up in the law as well. In Bava Metzia 30b Rabbi Yochanan says Jerusalem was destroyed because its judges ruled only by the strict letter of the law. The Talmud calls going past strict law lifnim mishurat hadin, inside the line of the law. It means giving more than the rule requires.

Readers see the two threads as partners. Argument keeps the rules sharp and honest. Mercy keeps them from hurting people. Aggadah, the sayings of Avot, the teachings on speech and on teshuvah all belong to this second thread. The rabbis did not treat it as softer than the law. They treated it as part of the law’s purpose.
