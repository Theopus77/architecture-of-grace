The twelve anchor cards

One big idea for each session. It goes up on the wall that week and stays there, so all twelve are up by the last session.

← The Adult Edition

The Compact is the agreement we make about how this room will work. It is not a list of rules. It is the relational contract that makes the rest of the work possible. Five clauses. Co-authored. Posted in the room for all twelve sessions.

1. What is said here, stays here. The exceptions are the legal exceptions — imminent harm, abuse, court order — named at the start.

2. You may pass at any time, at any prompt, without explanation.

3. We do not give advice unless asked. We listen.

4. We do not narrate other people's stories. We speak only to our own.

5. The work is between you and the page. The room witnesses; it does not extract.

The mistake is something I did. It is not who I am. What I did matters. The harm was real. The accountability is real. And — none of it exhausts who I am or determines what I am capable of. When identity collapses into behavior, two things go wrong at once: I cannot be honest about what I did (because honesty would mean I am a permanent monster), and I cannot change (because what is essential cannot be revised). The distinction is not a trick. It is the bedrock. Every other piece of work in this program rests on it.

The voice that says "You'll never get this right." is not the only voice. The voice that says "That was the wrong choice and I can do better starting now." is also a voice. Both voices speak. Always. We do not get to silence either one. We do get to choose which one we listen to. Naming the voices makes them visible. Visible self-talk is workable self-talk.

Every feeling carries information. Naming a feeling makes it workable. No feeling is bad — although what we do with a feeling can be helpful or unhelpful. Anger is a messenger. Shame is a messenger. Grief is a messenger. Joy is a messenger. Even numbness is a messenger — it tells you something is being kept at a distance. The work is not to eliminate the feeling. It is to read what it is telling you, and then to choose what to do with the information.

Everyone is carrying a backpack. The backpack contains everything you are carrying that is yours to carry — and some things that are not yours to carry but that you have been carrying anyway. Some of what is in the backpack is grief. Some is resentment. Some is shame. Some is unfinished apology. Some is unfinished mourning. Some is responsibility for things you did. Some is responsibility you have taken for things you did not do. We cannot decide what to do with what we are carrying until we have seen what is in there. Today, we open the backpack.

Honest. Fair. Kind. Without punishment. Not a cheerleader. The Witness does not say "You are wonderful, you did nothing wrong." The Witness says, accurately, what was true — and says it without contempt for the person it is naming. The Witness is the voice that knows the difference between what you did and who you are. The Witness is built. Most adults do not arrive with one already developed. Building it is much of the work of this program.

Grief says: "I wish things had been different." It is a response to loss. Resentment says: "I blame." It is a response to perceived culpability — someone (sometimes me) is responsible, and the responsibility has not been resolved. Both can be appropriate. Both can be painful. They require different tools. Grief is moved through with mourning, time, witness, and ritual. Resentment is moved through with the work of forgiveness — which is different from reconciliation, and different from forgetting. When we apply the wrong tool — when we try to mourn what is actually resentment, or try to forgive what is actually grief — the work does not move. The weight does not lift. The distinction matters operationally, every day.

The younger self is the version of you who did the thing — or to whom the thing was done — that you have not been able to forgive. The younger self knew what they knew at the time. They did not know what you know now. They did not have what you have now. Self-forgiveness is not the same as self-excuse. It does not mean what was done was acceptable. It means: the verdict you have been holding against the version of you who did it, or to whom it was done, is one you are choosing to release — without minimizing what happened, without denying what was real. The Compassionate Witness — honest, fair, kind, without punishment — is the voice you bring to this work. Self-forgiveness is the Witness applied inward, across time, to the self who needs what only you can give.

Some of what is in your backpack is not yours. It was placed in you — by a family, by a community, by a culture — before you were old enough to refuse it. Inherited shame is shame about who you are, what your people are, what your body is, what your history is — that you did not author and could not have authored. It feels indistinguishable, from the inside, from shame you authored. The Compassionate Witness can tell the difference. The Witness asks: did I put this in me, or did someone else? Returning the weight to its origin does not mean blaming the people who carried it before you. It means accurately naming where it began — so that you can stop carrying it as if it were yours.

An apology is a statement about the past. A living amend is a sustained behavioral commitment that addresses the pattern. The apology says: I am sorry for what I did. The living amend says: I am changing the pattern that produced what I did, and I am sustaining the change. Some harms are addressed by an apology. Patterns of harm are not. Patterns require living amends. The most important living amend, for many people, is the one owed to the self. The sustained behavioral change you owe to the version of you who has been kept in the same condition by your own pattern.

Forgiveness is internal. It is the release of the verdict you have been holding against another person — or against yourself. Reconciliation is interpersonal. It is the restoration of a relationship. It requires conditions forgiveness does not — including the consent and capacity of the other person, and the safety of both parties. Forgiveness can happen without reconciliation. Reconciliation, to be real, requires more than forgiveness. Conflating the two has cost many people their safety, their integrity, or their forgiveness itself. The distinction is the difference between an inward gift you can give yourself and an outward arrangement that is not yours alone to make.

The Audit is the explicit, structured decision about what stays in the backpack going forward. Three categories: WHAT YOU PUT DOWN — items you are choosing not to carry the way you have been carrying them. WHAT YOU CARRY DIFFERENTLY — items you will continue to carry but in a changed form, with the Witness's voice, in the company of the work you have done. WHAT YOU CHOOSE TO CARRY — items that are appropriately yours and that you carry consciously, going forward, with the tools you have built. The Audit does not solve anything. It names what you have decided. The decision is the work. The Audit is followed by the reopening of the Arrival Letter — the version of you on Day One, met by the version of you twelve weeks later.

Crisis & safety resources

Free, private, any time.

  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline — call or text 988. 24/7, free, confidential.
  • Crisis Text Line — text HOME to 741741.
  • SAMHSA National Helpline — 1-800-662-4357. Free, confidential treatment referral and information, 24/7.
  • Veterans Crisis Line — dial 988, then press 1 (or text 838255).
  • Immediate danger — call 911 or your local emergency number.
