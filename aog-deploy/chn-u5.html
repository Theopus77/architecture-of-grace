Key Teachings of Confucius

Confucian Ethics

Confucius and Mencius taught about how people should treat one another. They used key words like ren, li and yi. They described a kind of person they called the junzi. This unit explains those words and where they appear in the texts.

You will read short passages from the Analects and the Mencius. You will meet the five relationships and the famous story of Mencius's mother. You will see how Mencius argued that people are born with sprouts of goodness, and how another Confucian, Xunzi, disagreed.

By the end, you can explain each key word with a passage. You can tell what Mencius meant by the four sprouts. You can describe how readers, past and present, have agreed and disagreed about these teachings.

The big question

What did Confucius and Mencius teach about being a good person and living well with others?

The story

The Bow She Forgot

A new student forgets to bow and wonders whether manners really matter.

It was Ana's first week at a Chinese weekend school in Chicago. She sat in the back row. When the teacher walked in, every student stood up and bowed a little. Ana stayed in her seat. Her cheeks went hot.

At break, a boy named Wei sat next to her. 'Don't worry,' he said. 'I forgot too, my first week.' Ana asked, 'Why do we even bow? Isn't it just a rule?'

Wei thought about it. 'My grandpa says it is like saying thank you with your body,' he said. 'He says Confucius taught about manners a lot. But he also said manners without a kind heart are empty.'

After break, the teacher came back in. Ana stood with the others and bowed. It felt strange at first. Then she noticed the teacher smile and bow back to the class.

That night Ana asked her mom, 'Can a bow be kind, or is it only polite?' Her mom said, 'That is a question people have asked for 2,500 years.' Ana decided to keep asking it.

Talk about itCan good manners show real care? Can they ever be empty? Give an example.
Section 1

Ren: Caring for Others

9.1

What Ren Means

Main ideaRen, the central virtue in the Analects, means humaneness: caring for other people.

The word appears over 100 times in the Analects. It is the most important virtue in the book. English translators use words like humaneness, goodness, kindness or benevolence. Legge often wrote ’perfect virtue.’

A student named Fan Chi once asked Confucius what ren means. Confucius gave a short answer. He said it means to love people. That answer is in Analects 12.22.

The Chinese character for ren is made of two parts. One part means person. The other part means two. Many readers say this shows that ren is about how people treat each other, not about being alone.

From the source
Fan Chi's Question
Fan Chi asked about ren. The Master said: it is to love people.
Analects, book 12, passage 22, retold simply from James Legge's translation (1861) (paraphrased)

Why do you think Confucius gave such a short answer to a big question?

Words to know

virtue

9.2

The One-Word Rule

Main ideaConfucius taught: what you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others.

In Analects 15.23, a student named Zigong asked for one word to guide a whole life. Confucius answered with the word . Legge translated it as reciprocity. Others say consideration or empathy.

Then Confucius explained. What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others. This is sometimes called the Silver Rule, because it is said in a ’do not’ form.

Many traditions have a teaching like this. Readers often compare it to the Golden Rule in the Christian Gospels. Scholars discuss how the ’do not’ and ’do’ forms are alike and different.

From the source

Analects, book 15, passage 23, retold simply from James Legge's translation (1861) (paraphrased)

Think of a moment at school when this rule could help. What would you do?

Words to know
shu

reciprocity

Check yourself

9.3

Ren Is Close at Hand

Main ideaConfucius taught that ren is not far away: a person who truly wants it can start now.

Some people thought goodness was only for great heroes. Confucius said something different. In Analects 7.29, he asked, is ren really far away? He said it more simply than they expected.

He answered his own question. If I truly want ren, he said, it is right here. Many readers take this as hope. Anyone can begin to act with care, today.

But Confucius also said ren is hard to keep up every day. He praised his student Yan Hui for keeping to it for months. So readers see two sides: ren is close, and it takes practice.

From the source
Analects 7.29
The Master said: Is ren far away? If I want ren, here it is.
Analects, book 7, passage 29, retold simply from James Legge's translation (1861) (paraphrased)

Section 2

Li: Ritual and Respect

9.4

What Li Means

Main ideaLi means ritual and good manners: the set ways people show respect in daily life and ceremonies.

The word first meant rituals, like ceremonies to honor ancestors. Confucius used it more widely. Li also means everyday manners: how you greet, bow, sit, eat and speak.

In Analects 12.1, Confucius’s favorite student Yan Hui asked about ren. Confucius told him to guide himself by li. Do not look, listen, speak or move in ways that go against li.

So in the Analects, li and ren go together. Li gives care a shape people can see. A bow, a greeting or a thank-you are small forms of li. Readers still discuss how the two fit.

From the source
Analects 12.1
Yan Hui asked how to practice ren. The Master said: Do not look at what is against li. Do not listen to what is against li. Do not speak or act in ways that are against li.
Analects, book 12, passage 1, retold simply from James Legge's translation (1861) (paraphrased)

What four things does Confucius say to guide by li?

Words to know
li

ritual
a ceremony or action done in a set way, often to show honor

9.5

Manners With a Heart

Main ideaConfucius taught that ritual without a caring heart is empty.

Confucius did not want empty manners. In Analects 3.3 he asked: if a person has no ren, what good is li to them? What good is music to them? Manners, for him, were meant to carry real feeling.

In Analects 3.4, someone asked about the root of li. Confucius said that in a funeral, deep sorrow matters more than perfect details. In ceremonies, it is better to be simple than showy.

Readers say this means li should come from the heart. A bow done with care means something. A bow done only to look good means much less, Confucius taught.

From the source
Analects 3.3
The Master said: If a person has no ren, what has he to do with li? If a person has no ren, what has he to do with music?
Analects, book 3, passage 3, retold simply from James Legge's translation (1861) (paraphrased)

In your own words, what is Confucius saying about manners without care?

Words to know
sincere

ceremony
a formal event done in a set way, like a wedding or funeral
Check yourself

Why: Confucius asks what good li is to someone without ren.

2. What did Confucius say matters most at a funeral?

9.6

Li at School

Main ideaEveryday school habits, like greeting and taking turns, are modern examples of what Confucius called li.

Li is not only for ancient China. Every school has its own customs. Students line up, raise hands and greet visitors. Each custom helps people live and learn together.

Confucius taught that small customs matter. They help people know what to expect. They also show respect in a way others can see and understand. They make a shared life run more smoothly.

Customs differ from place to place. A bow in one school may be a handshake or a wave in another. Readers of the Analects often say the respect matters more than the exact form.

Yi and the Junzi

9.7

Yi: Doing What Is Right

Main ideaYi means doing what is right, even when another choice would bring more gain.

Another key word is . It means rightness or doing what is right. Legge translated it as righteousness. It asks: what is the fair and proper thing to do here?

In Analects 4.16, Confucius compared two kinds of people. The junzi, he said, understands what is right. The small person understands only what brings profit.

Confucius did not say gain is always bad. In Analects 4.5 he says people want wealth and honor. But he taught that they should not be gained in the wrong way.

From the source
Analects 4.16
The Master said: The junzi understands what is right. The small person understands what brings profit.

9.8

The Junzi

Main ideaThe junzi is the 'exemplary person' Confucius described: someone good because of how they act, not their birth.

The word once meant the son of a lord, a person of noble birth. Confucius changed how it was used. For him, a junzi is noble because of character, not family.

Translators write ’exemplary person,’ ’gentleman’ or, in Legge’s words, ’the superior man.’ The junzi learns, practices ren and li, and does what is right.

The Analects often contrasts the junzi with the ’small person.’ In 14.29, Confucius says the junzi is modest in speech but goes beyond it in action. He does more than he says.

9.9

Learning and Thinking

Main ideaConfucius taught that learning and thinking must go together, and both lead to practice.

The Analects opens with learning. In 1.1 Confucius asks: isn’t it a joy to learn and to practice what you learn? For him, learning meant changing how you live.

In Analects 2.15, he said learning without thinking is wasted. Thinking without learning is dangerous. A good student reads, asks questions and thinks it through.

Many Confucian readers say this is how a person becomes a junzi. You study good examples. You think about them. Then you practice, again and again, in real life.

Five Relationships and Mencius's Sprouts

Relationships
Big questionAre people born with the seeds of goodness, and what helps those seeds grow?
The story

Three Houses

A mother moves house three times until her son copies the right neighbors.

An old Chinese story tells of a boy named Meng Ke and his mother. The boy's father had died, and his mother raised him alone. At first they lived near a graveyard.

Little Meng Ke watched the funerals. Soon he was playing funeral in the yard, pretending to dig graves and weep. His mother frowned. 'This is not the place for my son to grow up,' she said. So they moved.

Next they lived beside a busy market. The boy watched the sellers. Soon he was playing market, shouting prices and bargaining with pretend coins. His mother shook her head. They moved again.

This time they lived next to a school. The boy watched the students bow to their teacher. He watched them read and practice ceremonies. Soon he was bowing and reciting too. His mother smiled. 'This is the place,' she said. They stayed.

The boy grew up to be Mencius, one of the most famous teachers in Chinese history. People still tell this story about his mother. Many use it to talk about how much our surroundings shape us.

Talk about itWhy did the mother keep moving? How do the places around you shape what you do?

The Five Relationships

10.1

Five Pairs

Main ideaConfucian teaching names five key relationships, each with its own duties.

Confucian thinkers wrote about five key . They are ruler and subject, parent and child, husband and wife, older and younger sibling, and friend and friend.

The book Mencius lists them in 3A.4. It names a quality for each pair. Parent and child should share affection. Ruler and minister should share rightness. Friends should share trust.

The other two pairs have their own qualities in that list. Husband and wife each have their own roles. Older and younger should keep a proper order. Together, the five describe a whole society.

10.2

Duties Both Ways

Main ideaIn Confucian teaching, each side of a relationship has duties, not only the lower one.

People sometimes think the five relationships are only about obeying. But Confucian texts give duties to both sides. A parent must care for a child. A ruler must care for the people.

In Analects 12.11, a duke asked Confucius about good government. Confucius said: let the ruler be a ruler, the minister a minister, the father a father, the son a son.

Readers say this means each person should live up to their role. A ruler who is cruel is not being a true ruler. Mencius went further and said the people matter most in a state.

From the source

10.3

Hard Questions

Main ideaPeople have debated the five relationships, especially the place of women, and they respond in many ways.

Four of the five relationships put one person above the other. Only friends are equals. In old China, this often meant wives had less power than husbands. Daughters had fewer chances to study than sons.

Many people have questioned this. In the 1900s, Chinese writers and reformers criticized these rankings. They called for equal rights and schooling for girls and women.

Today readers respond in different ways. Some say the rankings belong to the past. Some keep the idea of care and duty but drop the ranking. Some scholars point to women who studied the classics long ago.

Think it through

10.4

The Second Sage

Main ideaMencius was a later Confucian teacher, often called the Second Sage, whose teachings fill a book of seven parts.

is the Latin form of Mengzi, meaning Master Meng. He lived about a century after Confucius. Traditional dates are about 372 to 289 BCE, but these are not certain.

10.5

Mencius's Mother

Main ideaA famous story says Mencius's mother moved three times to find a good place for her son to learn.

The story of the three moves comes from an old book of stories about women, the Lienü zhuan. It was put together by Liu Xiang around the first century BCE, long after Mencius lived.

The same book tells a second story. Once young Mencius quit his studies early. His mother cut the cloth she was weaving. She said quitting learning was like ruining the cloth. He went back to study hard.

Historians cannot check these stories. But people in China have told them for over 2,000 years. They honor Mencius’s mother as a model of a caring, wise parent.

10.6

Talking With Kings

Main ideaMencius told kings to rule with humaneness and rightness rather than chasing profit.

The book Mencius opens with a meeting. King Hui of Liang asks Mencius how he can profit the kingdom. It is found in Mencius 1A.1. It is one of the best-known scenes in the book.

Mencius answers: why must Your Majesty speak of profit? He says there is only humaneness and rightness. If everyone chases profit, from the king down, the kingdom will be in danger.

Mencius taught that a good ruler cares for the people like a parent. He said that if a ruler is cruel, the people may rightly turn away. This idea was bold in his time.

Section 3

Sprouts of Goodness

10.7

The Child at the Well

Main ideaMencius argued that anyone who sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and care at once.

Mencius told a famous example in Mencius 2A.6. Imagine you suddenly see a small child about to fall into a well. What would you feel? He asked his listeners to picture it clearly.

Mencius said anyone would feel alarm and . You would not feel it to win praise, or to please the child’s parents. You would simply feel it, right away.

Mencius used this example to argue that people are born with a feeling of care for others. He said a person without it would hardly be human. This is his way of showing that human nature is good.

10.8

Four Sprouts

Main ideaMencius taught that four feelings in every person are sprouts that can grow into four virtues.

In the same passage, Mencius named four . A sprout is a tiny new plant. He said every person is born with four feelings that are like sprouts.

The feeling of compassion is the sprout of ren. The feeling of shame at doing wrong is the sprout of yi. Modesty is the sprout of li. Knowing right from wrong is the sprout of wisdom.

Sprouts are small. Mencius taught they must be cared for to grow into full virtues. Like plants, they need the right conditions, just as his mother looked for the right home.

Ox Mountain and a Different View

Main ideaMencius said goodness can be damaged like a stripped mountain, while another Confucian, Xunzi, said goodness must be learned.

In Mencius 6A.8, Mencius described Ox Mountain. Its trees were once beautiful. But people cut them down, and cattle ate every new shoot. Now it looks bare.

Mencius said people might think the mountain never had trees. But it did. In the same way, he said, a person who acts badly still had sprouts of goodness. They were just harmed.

Not all Confucians agreed. A later thinker, , taught that human nature tends toward selfishness. He said goodness comes from learning and ritual. Readers still compare the two views.

Think it through
Two Confucian Views

Mencius: People are born with sprouts of goodness. With care, they grow. When harmed, they wither, like Ox Mountain. Xunzi: People are born tending toward selfishness. Teachers, study and ritual shape them into good people, as a craftsman straightens wood.

How are these two views alike? How are they different?

Words to know
Xunzi
a later Confucian thinker who taught that goodness must be learned through study and ritual
Ox Mountain
