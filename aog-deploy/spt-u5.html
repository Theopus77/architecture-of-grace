An Indoor Winter Game

Origins

In December 1891, a class of eighteen men in Springfield, Massachusetts, was stuck indoors for the winter. Their teacher, James Naismith, nailed two peach baskets to a balcony railing. He handed them a soccer ball and thirteen typed rules. Basketball began in that one small gym.

In this unit you will follow the game out of that room. YMCA graduates carried it to other cities and countries. Colleges formed teams, and Senda Berenson wrote rules for women at Smith College. Illinois high schools held state tournaments, first for boys in 1908 and for girls in 1977.

You will be able to explain why the goal is ten feet high and why backboards appeared. You will name the people, places and dates that shaped the game. You will also tell who was left out of some tournaments, and which law helped open the door.

The big question

How did the room where basketball began shape the game, and how did new rooms and new rule books change who could play?

Big questionHow did one crowded winter gym in Springfield give basketball its goals, its height and its first rules?
The story

Two Weeks Before Christmas

In December 1891 a young teacher has a restless class, a small gym and a deadline.

It is December 1891 in Springfield, Massachusetts. Snow covers the playing fields. Inside the International YMCA Training School, a class of eighteen young men is bored. They have marched, lifted and climbed ropes for weeks.

James Naismith is their new teacher. He is 30 years old and comes from Canada. His boss, Luther Gulick, has given him a job. Invent an indoor game for this class, and do it in about two weeks.

Naismith looks up at the gym. A running track circles the room on a balcony. Its railing is about ten feet above the floor. He asks the building superintendent for two boxes to use as goals.

The man has no boxes. He comes back with two old peach baskets. Naismith nails one to the railing at each end. He types a list of thirteen rules and pins it to the bulletin board. A new game is about to begin.

Talk about itNaismith used a room that was built for something else. How might the room have shaped his new game?

Before the Basket

9.1

Older Games With a Target

Main ideaPeople played ball games at targets for thousands of years, but basketball was invented new in 1891.

Ball games are very old. In Mexico and Central America, people built stone more than 3,000 years ago. The Great Ball Court at Chichén Itzá has a stone ring set high on each wall.

That Mesoamerican game used a heavy rubber ball. Players struck it with their hips, not their hands. It had religious meaning for the Maya and other peoples. It was played for many centuries before Europeans arrived.

Basketball did not grow from that game. Historians call it an because one person made it on purpose. Naismith later said he borrowed an idea from Duck on a Rock, a stone-tossing game from his Canadian childhood.

9.2

A School for YMCA Leaders

Main ideaThe YMCA ran a training school in Springfield where young men learned to lead gyms.

The means the Young Men’s Christian Association. George Williams started it in London in 1844. It came to Boston in 1851. Soon many American cities had a YMCA building.

Many YMCA buildings had a gym. The YMCA needed trained leaders to run them. A school in Springfield, Massachusetts, prepared those leaders. It later became Springfield College.

Students there studied the Bible, the body and physical training. When they graduated, they took jobs at YMCAs across the country. That job path later carried basketball far from Springfield.

9.5

Peach Baskets on a Railing

Main ideaNaismith nailed two peach baskets to the gym balcony and used a soccer ball.

Naismith wanted a goal the ball had to drop into from above. A high goal meant players could not simply slam the ball in. He asked the building superintendent, often named as Pop Stebbins, for two boxes.

There were no boxes. The superintendent brought two instead. Naismith nailed them to the railing, one at each end. The railing held a running track above the gym floor.

The ball was a soccer ball. No special basketball existed yet. The baskets still had bottoms. After each goal, someone climbed up to take the ball out.

9.6

Thirteen Typed Rules

Main ideaNaismith wrote thirteen rules, and the school paper printed them in January 1892.

Naismith wrote his and had them typed. There were thirteen. He pinned the pages to the gym bulletin board. The class read them before the first game.

The rules said the ball could be thrown or batted, but not carried. They banned shouldering, holding, pushing and tripping. A player who broke those rules twice sat out until the next goal.

On 15 January 1892, the school’s newspaper, , printed the rules. Readers at other YMCAs could now copy them. The typed pages still exist and were sold at auction in 2010.

9.7

The First Game

Main ideaThe first game had nine players on each side and ended with one goal.

Naismith’s class of eighteen split into two teams of nine. That first game was played in December 1891. Nobody was sure yet how the new rules would work.

Naismith later recalled that only one goal was scored. Many histories name the scorer as William R. Chase. The score was 1–0. The players fouled often, because they were used to football.

The class liked the game. Some students asked to call it Naismith Ball. Naismith said no. A student named Frank Mahan suggested , and the name stuck. It was written as two words for years.

The Room Shapes the Game

9.8

Ten Feet Up

Main ideaThe goal is ten feet high because the Springfield balcony railing was about that high.

Today’s basketball rim sits ten feet above the floor. That number did not come from a study or a test. It came from the Springfield gym. The balcony railing happened to be about ten feet up.

Naismith nailed the baskets where he could. The stayed as the game spread. Later rule books wrote ten feet down as a fixed rule. High school, college and pro games all use it.

The rim height has stayed at ten feet for more than 130 years. Players have grown taller. The court has grown longer in many places. The height from one old railing never changed.

9.9

Backboards Against the Balcony

Main ideaBackboards were first added to stop fans on the balcony from touching the ball.

In early gyms, fans often watched from the balcony. The baskets hung right on the balcony railing. Fans could reach out and knock the ball away. Some helped their own team’s shots go in.

Gyms fixed the problem with a . It was a board or screen placed behind the basket. It blocked hands from the balcony. Backboards spread in the mid-1890s.

The board was meant as a shield. But the ball could bounce off it. The backboard soon became part of the goal itself. Later backboards were made of glass, so fans behind them could see.

9.10

From Closed Basket to Open Net

Main ideaClosed peach baskets gave way to metal hoops with open nets.

A closed peach basket held the ball after every goal. Someone had to climb a ladder or use a pole to get it out. Play stopped each time, and the crowd waited.

Makers soon sold iron with cord nets. At first, the nets were closed too. A person pulled a chain to drop the ball out. By the early 1900s, nets were open at the bottom.

With an open , the ball fell through by itself. Play could restart faster. The net still slowed the ball, so people could see a goal was scored. The idea of a high target at each end never changed.

Graduates Carry the Game

Main ideaYMCA graduates and the printed rules carried basketball to many cities within a few years.

Students at the Springfield school came from many places. After , they went to lead YMCAs in other cities. Many took Naismith’s game with them.

The printed rules helped. A YMCA director in another city could read the thirteen rules and start a team. The YMCA’s many gyms gave the game ready-made rooms.

The game also traveled abroad. YMCA workers took it to Canada, China, Japan, France and other countries in the 1890s and early 1900s. Within a few years, basketball was a world game.

10.2

Colleges Start Teams

Main ideaOn 9 February 1895 two Minnesota schools played an early college-against-college game.

Colleges picked up the game fast. Amos Alonzo Stagg had been a student-teacher at the Springfield school. In 1892 he moved to the new University of Chicago. Chicago soon had basketball teams.

On 9 February 1895, the Minnesota State School of Agriculture played Hamline College. The Minnesota school won 9–3. Many histories call it the first basketball game.

The game was only three years old. The final score was low because goals were hard to make. College games soon drew crowds and reports in local newspapers.

10.3

Teams of Five

Main ideaRule makers set teams at five players a side by the late 1890s.

Naismith’s first rules did not fix the number of players. A class of eighteen played nine against nine. Some early games had even more players crowded on the floor.

Crowded games were rough and slow. By the late 1890s, rule makers settled on five a side. The rule books of YMCA and college groups printed that number.

Rule books also changed the goals. Open hoops and backboards became part of the printed rules. Each new edition of a kept Naismith’s core ideas and added new ones.

10.5

A Court in Three Zones

Main ideaBerenson's rules split the court into three zones and kept players inside their own zone.

Berenson changed Naismith’s rules for her students. She divided the court into three . Each player had to stay in her own zone. Players could not grab the ball from another player.

Berenson said she wanted a game with less rough play. Many doctors and teachers then warned that hard sport could harm women. Her rules answered those worries while still letting women play.

Her rules spread to other women’s colleges and schools. They set the pattern for women’s basketball in the United States for decades. The women’s game and the men’s game went on with different rules.

10.6

Two Rule Books for Decades

Main ideaWomen's basketball followed separate rules from the 1890s until the early 1970s.

In 1899 a group of women teachers met to set shared rules for women’s basketball. Berenson edited the first official book of women’s rules. It was printed in 1901.

For about seventy years, women’s and men’s basketball used different rule books. Women’s rules limited how far players could move. Many schools had six players a side, split into halves of the court.

In the early 1970s, college women’s rules changed to five players on the full court. The women’s game now used nearly the same court and team size as the men’s.

10.8

A Gym Inside the School

Main ideaPublic schools built gyms, and those rooms made basketball a winter school sport.

Basketball needs a heated room with a hard floor. In the early 1900s, many new American high schools added a . Taxpayers in each town paid to build and heat it.

The school gym had many jobs. Students took physical education there during the day. At night, the same room held games, dances, concerts and town meetings.

Some gyms added pull-out bleachers along the walls. A few places also played outdoors. But the heated school gym became basketball’s main home in towns across Illinois.

10.9

Title IX and a Girls Title

Main ideaAfter Title IX in 1972, the IHSA held its first girls basketball state tournament in 1977.

For most of the 1900s, Illinois high school girls had no IHSA basketball state tournament. Many girls played in school clubs and games. But only boys could win an IHSA state title.

In 1972 Congress passed of the Education Amendments. It says no person may face discrimination because of sex in school programs that get federal money. School sports are covered.

Schools had to open more teams to girls. In 1977 the IHSA crowned its first girls basketball state champion. That came sixty-nine years after the first boys champion in 1908.

The Words of Title IX
No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.
Education Amendments of 1972, Title IX, 20 U.S.C. 1681

Naismith at Kansas

Main ideaNaismith worked at the University of Kansas from 1898 until he retired.

In 1898 Naismith became a teacher and chaplain at the in Lawrence. He also earned a medical degree that year. He led physical education there for about forty years.

Naismith was the first basketball coach at Kansas. He coached until 1907. He is the only Kansas coach with a losing record. He cared more about exercise for all students than about winning.

His player Forrest took over later. Allen won many games and is often called the father of basketball coaching. Naismith died in 1939 in Lawrence.

Berlin, 1936

Main ideaMen's basketball became an Olympic medal sport at Berlin in 1936, and Naismith was there.

Men’s basketball joined the as a medal sport in Berlin, Germany, in 1936. Teams came from Europe, Asia and the Americas. The games were played outdoors on clay and sand courts.

Naismith, then 74, traveled to Berlin to watch. Supporters in the United States had raised money for his trip. He saw teams from many countries playing the game he had invented.

The final was played in heavy rain. The turned to mud, and the ball was hard to handle. The United States beat Canada 19–8. Women’s basketball did not join the Olympics until 1976.
