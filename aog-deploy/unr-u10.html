The Gods of the Nations

The Deuteronomy 32 Worldview

In 1947 a shepherd's stone cracked a jar in a cave near the Dead Sea. That jar began a find of about 900 old manuscripts. One small scrap held a line from Deuteronomy 32:8. Its wording did not match the Hebrew Bible most people used. Where the usual text says the nations were divided by the number of the sons of Israel, the scroll says the sons of God.

That one verse opens a big question: who rules the nations? Deuteronomy 32, Daniel 10, Psalm 82, Sirach, Jubilees and 1 Enoch all give answers. In this unit you will read them, along with holy ground at Sinai and Zion and a general who carried Israel's soil home. Then you will meet the researchers who read these texts. Michael Heiser, Mark S. Smith, George Nickelsburg and others see them in different ways.

By the end of the unit you will be able to explain what a manuscript and a variant are, and compare the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls. You will be able to say what Deuteronomy 32, Daniel 10 and 2 Kings 5 say in your own words. And you will be able to describe two readings of each and compare them fairly.

The story

A Stone, a Cave and a Broken Jar

In 1947 a young shepherd tossed a stone into a cave and started one of the biggest finds in the study of the Bible.

Early in 1947, a young Bedouin shepherd was looking after goats in the dry hills near the Dead Sea. The land there is rocky and full of caves. According to the best-known telling, one goat wandered off. The shepherd threw a stone into a cave opening. Instead of a thud, he heard something crack. It sounded like pottery breaking.

Later he and his cousins climbed inside. They found tall clay jars. Some held old rolls of leather wrapped in cloth. The rolls were covered in neat lines of Hebrew letters. The young men did not know what they had. They sold the scrolls to dealers in Bethlehem, and the scrolls slowly reached scholars.

The scholars were amazed. One scroll held the whole book of Isaiah. It had been copied more than two thousand years ago. Before this find, the oldest full Hebrew copies of the Bible most people could study were about a thousand years younger. Search teams went back to the hills. Between 1947 and 1956 they found scrolls and scraps in eleven caves near a ruin called Qumran.

Among the tens of thousands of fragments were pieces of the book of Deuteronomy. One small piece held a line from a poem called the Song of Moses. Its wording did not match the Hebrew Bible most people used. That one line, Deuteronomy 32:8, became one of the most discussed lines in the study of the Bible's unseen world. This chapter follows it from the cave to the scholars who still weigh it.

Section 1

Copies Made by Hand

19.1

What a Manuscript Is

Main ideaA manuscript is a copy written by hand, and small differences between copies are called variants.

Before printing presses, every book was copied by hand. A copy written by hand is called a . The word comes from Latin words for "hand" and "written." The people who made copies were called scribes. They wrote with ink on animal skin or on papyrus, a paper made from reeds.

No one has the first copy of any book of the Bible. Those first copies wore out long ago. What survives are later copies, made from earlier copies. Scribes worked very carefully. Still, a tired eye could skip a line or swap one word for a similar one. Sometimes a scribe changed a word on purpose to make the meaning clearer.

A place where manuscripts do not agree is called a . Most variants are tiny, like a spelling change. A few change the meaning. Scholars who compare manuscripts to learn what a text first said practice . Here "criticism" means careful judging, not complaining. Every modern Bible translation depends on this kind of study.

19.2

The Masoretic Text

Main ideaThe Masoretic Text is the traditional Hebrew text of the Hebrew Bible, and its oldest complete copies come from about 900–1000 CE.

Open almost any printed Hebrew Bible today and you are reading the . It is named for the Masoretes. They were Jewish scribes and scholars who worked from about 500 to 1000 CE, mostly in Tiberias, a town in Galilee, and in Babylonia.

The Masoretes did not write a new text. They guarded an older one. Hebrew was written with consonants only. The Masoretes added small dots and marks for , so readers would say each word the traditional way. They also added notes in the margins that counted letters and words, to help later scribes copy exactly.

Two famous copies come from this work. The Aleppo Codex was written in about 930 CE. The Leningrad Codex was finished in 1008 CE and is the oldest complete Hebrew Bible that survives. A codex is a book with pages, not a scroll. Most modern translations, Jewish and Christian, use the Masoretic Text as their main base.

19.3

The Greek Septuagint

Main ideaThe Septuagint is an old Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, begun in Egypt in the 200s BCE, and it sometimes follows a different Hebrew text.

In the 200s BCE, many Jews lived in Alexandria, a large city in Egypt. Their daily language was Greek. Jewish scholars there began translating the Hebrew scriptures into Greek. They started with the Torah, the first five books. Other books followed over the next two centuries or so.

This Greek translation is called the . The name comes from the Latin word for seventy. An old story, told in a text called the Letter of Aristeas, says about seventy scholars made the translation. Scholars often write it with the Roman numerals LXX, which means seventy.

The Septuagint matters for two reasons. First, it was the Bible of many Greek-speaking Jews, and later of the early church. Writers of the New Testament often quote it. Second, the translators worked from Hebrew copies much older than the Masoretic codices. Where the Greek differs from the Masoretic Text, it can be a clue that the translators’ Hebrew copy read differently. In 1844 Lancelot Brenton published an English translation of the Septuagint, so English readers can compare the Greek too.

Section 2

The Scrolls from the Caves

19.4

Finding the Dead Sea Scrolls

Main ideaBetween 1947 and 1956, about 900 manuscripts, copied from about 250 BCE to 70 CE, were found in eleven caves near Qumran.

After the first scrolls reached scholars in 1947, the search spread. Bedouin searchers and archaeologists combed the cliffs near , an ancient site by the northwest shore of the Dead Sea. By 1956 they had found writings in eleven caves. Cave 4, found in 1952, held the most, but mostly in small, broken pieces.

Together these finds are called the . Scholars count roughly 900 different manuscripts, though exact counts vary. They were copied between about 250 BCE and 70 CE. The dry desert air kept the leather and papyrus from rotting away.

About a quarter of the manuscripts are books of the Hebrew Bible. Every book appears except Esther, at least in pieces. Other scrolls are rules for a Jewish community, prayers, commentaries, and books like 1 Enoch and Jubilees. Many scholars link the scrolls to a Jewish group called the Essenes, but others think the scrolls came from several places.

19.5

What the Scrolls Showed

Main ideaThe scrolls showed that the Hebrew text was copied very carefully, and also that more than one form of some books was in use.

Scholars first compared the Great Isaiah Scroll from Cave 1 with the Masoretic Text of Isaiah. The two were about a thousand years apart. Yet they matched closely, apart from spelling and small differences. This showed how carefully scribes had copied the text.

Other scrolls surprised scholars. Some biblical scrolls agree with the Masoretic Text. Some agree with the Hebrew text the Septuagint translators seem to have used. Some agree with neither. So before about 100 CE, more than one form of some books was in use. The Hebrew text had not yet settled into a single form.

This gave textual critics new . When the Septuagint differs from the Masoretic Text, a Hebrew scroll sometimes sides with the Greek. That shows the Greek was not just a loose translation. It was following a real Hebrew reading. Deuteronomy 32:8 is one well-known example.

Section 3

One Verse, Three Readings

19.6

The Song of Moses

Main ideaIn the Masoretic Text, Deuteronomy 32:8 says God set the borders of the peoples by the number of the sons of Israel.

Deuteronomy 32 is a long poem called the . In the story, Moses teaches it to Israel near the end of his life. The poem looks back over Israel’s history. It praises God as faithful and warns the people not to turn to other gods.

Verses 8 and 9 go back to the distant past. They say that the Most High divided the nations and gave them their inheritance. He set their borders. Then verse 9 says that the LORD’s own portion is his people, Jacob. Jacob is another name for Israel. The is a title for God.

The key words come at the end of verse 8. In the Masoretic Text, the borders are set "according to the number of the sons of Israel." The King James Version (1611) followed this reading, as did many translations after it. Jewish readers long noticed a link here. Genesis 46:27 says seventy members of Jacob’s family went down to Egypt.

19.7

Angels of God in the Greek

Main ideaMost Septuagint manuscripts of Deuteronomy 32:8 read "angels of God," and some read "sons of God."

Now open the Septuagint to the same verse. The first part matches: the Most High divided the nations and set their borders. But the ending is different. Most Greek manuscripts say the borders were set according to the number of the " of God." A few Greek manuscripts say "sons of God."

Why would a translator write "angels of God"? Many scholars think the translators’ Hebrew copy said "sons of God." In the Hebrew Bible, "sons of God" can name heavenly beings, as in Job 1:6 and Job 38:7. So a translator might explain the phrase for Greek readers by writing "angels."

Now there are two readings. "Sons of Israel" points to a people on earth. "Angels" or "" points to beings in heaven. Those two ideas are very far apart. For a long time, some scholars thought the Greek was just a free translation. A small piece of leather from Cave 4 would change that discussion.

19.8

The Scroll from Cave 4

Main ideaA Deuteronomy scroll from Qumran Cave 4, called 4QDeut-j, reads "sons of God" in 32:8, and many modern translations now follow it.

Scholars give each scroll a short label. The label 4QDeut-j means: Cave 4, Qumran, Deuteronomy, copy j. This copy is about two thousand years old. Only pieces survive. One piece holds part of Deuteronomy 32:8, and there the Hebrew reads "sons of God."

This matters. It is in Hebrew, not Greek. So it shows that a Hebrew copy of Deuteronomy really did say "sons of God." It supports the idea that the Septuagint translators had a Hebrew copy like this in front of them. Scholars of many different views agree that the scroll reads this way. They differ on what the reading means.

Many modern English translations now follow the scroll here, not the Masoretic Text. The English Standard Version (2001) reads "sons of God." The New Revised Standard Version (1989) reads "gods." Other translations, such as the King James Version and the Jewish Publication Society’s translations, keep "sons of Israel," often with a note.

19.9

Why the Readings Differ

Main ideaScholars weigh two main explanations: a scribe changed "sons of God" to "sons of Israel," or "sons of Israel" was the older reading.

How did one verse end up with different words? Many textual critics think "sons of God" came first. Later, they suggest, a scribe changed it to "sons of Israel." The phrase "sons of God" might have seemed to give other heavenly beings too much honor. Changing it would protect belief in one God. This idea is a made on purpose.

Other readers defend the Masoretic reading. They point to a of seventy. Genesis 10 lists about seventy nations after the flood. Genesis 46:27 says seventy of Jacob’s family went down to Egypt. So the nations’ number matches Israel’s family. Early Jewish teachers and the medieval commentator Rashi read the verse this way.

Researchers who favor "sons of God" often point to Ugarit, an ancient city in Syria. Its clay tablets speak of the seventy sons of the god El and his wife Athirat. Mark S. Smith, in The Origins of Biblical Monotheism (2001), read verses 8–9 as an old picture: the Most High gives each of his sons a nation, and the LORD receives Israel. Michael Heiser also accepted the scroll reading. But he argued that the Most High and the LORD are the same God in these verses. The debate is still active.

The Nations and Their Gods

19.10

Babel and the Nations

Main ideaDeuteronomy 32:8–9 and 4:19–20 picture the nations given to other powers while Israel belongs to the LORD, and many readers link this to Babel.

Genesis 11 tells of the tower of . People build a tower to reach the heavens. God scatters them and confuses their language. Genesis 10, placed just before it, lists the nations that spread across the earth. Deuteronomy 32:8 also speaks of the Most High dividing the nations. Many readers, ancient and modern, link the two passages.

Deuteronomy 4:19–20 adds a second clue. There Moses says the sun, moon and stars were allotted to all the other peoples. But the LORD took Israel out of Egypt to be his own people. Some scholars read this as the same picture as 32:8–9. Others read 4:19 simply as a warning against star worship. Later Jewish writings speak of seventy angels for seventy nations.

Michael Heiser gave this picture a name: the . A is a set of big ideas people use to make sense of everything. In The Unseen Realm (2015), he argued that at Babel God handed the nations to lesser heavenly beings, the sons of God. Then God began again with Abraham in Genesis 12. Other scholars accept parts of this picture and question other parts.

19.11

Gods Who Were Not Just Statues

Main ideaDeuteronomy 32:17 calls the gods Israel turned to shedim, and many scholars read them as spirit beings, not only statues.

Later in the Song of Moses, Israel turns away from God. Deuteronomy 32:17 says they sacrificed to , not to God. They were gods Israel had not known before, new ones that had come along recently. The King James Version translates shedim as "devils." Many modern translations say "demons."

The Hebrew word shedim is rare. It appears only here and in Psalm 106:37. It seems related to a word for protecting spirits in Akkadian, a language of ancient Mesopotamia. The Septuagint translates it daimonia, spirit beings. So many scholars think the writer meant real powers behind the nations’ worship, not just wood and stone.

Other readers take the passage differently. Isaiah 44 mocks idols as blocks of wood that a craftsman carved. Some readers think Deuteronomy 32 means the same thing: the gods were nothing. Other scholars answer that a text can mock the statues and still treat the powers behind them as real. Paul seems to hold both ideas in 1 Corinthians 8:4 and 10:19–20.

19.12

Psalm 96 and Paul

Main ideaPsalm 96:5 calls the nations' gods elilim, the Greek Septuagint calls them daimonia, and Paul echoes Deuteronomy 32:17 in 1 Corinthians 10:20.

Psalm 96 calls on all the earth to sing to the LORD. Verse 5 says that all the gods of the nations are , but the LORD made the heavens. Elilim is a Hebrew word that means worthless things or idols. It sounds a bit like elohim, a word for God or gods. The poet may be making a play on words.

The Septuagint translators chose a different word. In their Greek, the gods of the nations are , the word that later gives English "demons." So the translators read this verse as being about spirit beings. Scholars use choices like this to learn how Jewish translators read these texts before the time of Jesus.

The New Testament writer Paul used the same idea. In 1 Corinthians 10:20 he warns that sacrifices to idols are offered to daimonia, not to God. His words echo Deuteronomy 32:17. Many Christian readers see Paul as agreeing with the Song of Moses. Other readers think Paul was warning mainly about sharing in idol feasts, not teaching about spirits.

The story

Two Loads of Dirt

A foreign general is healed in Israel's river and then asks for a strange gift to take home.

The story is told in 2 Kings 5. Naaman is the commander of the army of Aram, the kingdom north of Israel, with its capital at Damascus. He is brave and respected. But he has a skin disease that will not go away. A young girl from Israel, taken from her home in a raid, works in his house. She tells his wife that a prophet in Israel could help him.

So Naaman travels south with horses, chariots and gifts. He stops at the door of the prophet Elisha. Elisha does not even come out. He sends a messenger with a simple instruction: go wash seven times in the Jordan River. Naaman is angry. Damascus has better rivers, he says. His servants gently talk him into trying. He dips seven times, and his skin becomes like a young child's.

Naaman goes back to Elisha and says that now he knows there is no God in all the earth except in Israel. He offers gifts, but Elisha will not take any. Then Naaman asks for something odd. He asks for as much soil as two mules can carry. He says he will offer sacrifices from now on only to the LORD, the God of Israel.

Why would a general want dirt? He could buy fine things in any market. Readers have puzzled over this request for a long time. Many scholars think the dirt is a clue to how people in the ancient world thought about gods, nations and land. This chapter follows that clue, from a vision in Daniel to a song sung far from home.

The Princes in Daniel

20.1

A Vision by the Tigris

Main ideaDaniel 10 tells of a heavenly messenger who comes to Daniel by the Tigris River in the time of the Persian king Cyrus.

Daniel 10 opens in the third year of Cyrus, king of Persia. Cyrus had taken Babylon in 539 BCE. In the story, Daniel is an old man by now. He has spent three weeks mourning and fasting. Then, standing by the Tigris River, he sees a man dressed in linen, shining like lightning. Daniel falls to the ground, but the messenger touches him and tells him not to be afraid.

The book of Daniel has two halves. Chapters 1 to 6 are stories about Daniel and his friends in Babylon. Chapters 7 to 12 are visions. A is a dream or sight full of symbols, here about kingdoms to come. Chapter 10 begins the last and longest vision, which runs to the end of the book.

Readers date the book in different ways. Many Jewish and Christian readers hold the traditional view that Daniel wrote it in the 500s BCE. Many modern scholars think the book was finished in about 165 BCE, when the Greek king Antiochus IV was persecuting Jews in Judea. Either way, the vision looks ahead from Persia to Greece. That matters for the princes in the next lesson.

Main ideaIn Daniel 10:13 the messenger says the prince of the kingdom of Persia blocked him for twenty-one days until Michael came to help.

Then help arrived. , "one of the chief princes," came to help the messenger. Michael is one of the few angels in the Hebrew Bible who has a name. In Daniel he is always shown on the side of Daniel’s people.

Who is the prince of Persia? He cannot be an ordinary human king. A human ruler could not block a heavenly messenger for three weeks. Also, the verse speaks of "the kings of Persia" as a separate group. So many readers, ancient and modern, think the prince is a heavenly being linked with the nation of Persia.

20.3

Michael, Your Prince

Main ideaDaniel 10:20–21 adds a prince of Greece and calls Michael "your prince," and Daniel 12:1 calls him the great prince who stands for Daniel's people.

At the end of chapter 10, the messenger says he will go back to fight the prince of Persia. When he is done, he says, the prince of Greece will come. Greece is the next empire in the vision. In history, Alexander the Great conquered Persia in 331 BCE.

Then the messenger says that no one stands with him in this except Michael, "your prince." The word "your" is plural in Hebrew. It means the prince of Daniel’s people, Israel. Daniel 12:1 says it again. It calls Michael the great prince who stands up for Daniel’s people in a time of trouble.

Put the pieces together. Persia has a prince. Greece has a prince. Israel has Michael. The book never shows a human battlefield here. It shows a struggle in the that runs alongside the rise and fall of empires on earth. Readers have explained that struggle in several ways, as the next section shows.

Rulers Over Nations

20.4

Rulers Behind the Empires

Main ideaMany readers link Daniel's princes to Deuteronomy 32:8 and Psalm 82, where God sets heavenly beings over the nations and judges them.

Start with Psalm 82. God stands in the , the gathering of heavenly beings. He judges the gods for ruling unjustly. They defend the wicked and ignore the poor. In verse 6 God calls them gods and sons of the Most High. In verse 7 he says they will die like humans. Verse 8 ends: God will inherit all the nations.

Now set Psalm 82 beside Deuteronomy 32:8–9 and Daniel 10. If God set the nations under the sons of God, each nation would have a heavenly ruler. Daniel’s prince of Persia and prince of Greece fit that picture. Many scholars of Daniel read the princes as heavenly patrons of the nations. Michael Heiser linked all three passages in The Unseen Realm (2015) and Angels (2018).

One detail matters here. Michael is a prince for Israel, but he is on God’s side. So Israel’s case differs from the other nations. In Deuteronomy 32:9, the LORD himself takes Israel as his portion. Heiser understood Michael as serving God on Israel’s behalf, not as a rival god.

20.6

How Jews and Christians Have Read the Princes

Main ideaMany Jewish and Christian readers have seen Daniel's princes as angels of the nations, while others read them as human rulers or as a picture of history.

Later Jewish tradition kept the idea of rulers over the nations. Rabbinic writings, the teachings of Jewish sages collected after about 200 CE, sometimes speak of each nation having a heavenly prince. In Jewish tradition, Michael is often called the of Israel. Some later Jewish writers also count seventy nations with seventy angels.

Many Christians have linked Daniel’s princes to words in Paul’s letters. Ephesians 6:12 speaks of a struggle not against flesh and blood, but against rulers, authorities and powers in the heavenly places. Early church writers such as Origen spoke of angels set over nations. Clinton Arnold, in Powers of Darkness (1992), read Paul’s powers as personal spirit beings, as in Jewish tradition. Walter Wink, in Naming the Powers (1984), read them as the inner spirit of nations and institutions.

Other readers take a different path. Some interpreters have argued that the prince of Persia was a human ruler or official, since sar is a common word for officials. Some modern scholars read the princes as a : a way of saying that history is not out of God’s hands. These readers agree the passage is about God’s care for his people. They differ on the princes.

20.7

The Bush and the Mountain

Main ideaIn Exodus, places where God is present become holy ground, like the burning bush and Mount Sinai.

In Exodus 3, Moses is caring for sheep near Horeb, the mountain of God. He sees a bush on fire that does not burn up. When he turns to look, God calls to him from the bush. God tells him to take off his sandals, because the place where he is standing is .

Holy means set apart for God. The ground is not special because of its rocks. It is holy because, in the story, God is present there. This idea is called . Many ancient peoples believed certain places, often mountains, were where gods lived or met people.

Later, in Exodus 19, the Israelites camp at Mount Sinai, which many readers take to be the same mountain as Horeb. The people must not touch the mountain. Moses sets limits around it. God comes down on the mountain with thunder, cloud and fire, and gives the covenant. The mountain becomes the LORD’s own ground, where the people meet him.

20.8

The Tabernacle and Zion

Main ideaThe tabernacle carried God's holy presence with Israel, and later Mount Zion in Jerusalem became the main holy place.

Israel could not stay at Sinai forever. So in Exodus 25:8, God tells Moses to have the people make a sanctuary so that he may dwell among them. This was the , a large tent that could be taken down and carried. Its inner room held the ark of the covenant, a gold-covered chest.

Later, King David brought the ark to Jerusalem, and his son Solomon built the temple there. The hill of the temple was called . Psalm 48:2 praises Mount Zion as beautiful and the joy of the whole earth. The same verse places Zion "on the sides of the north," in the King James wording.

Scholars have noticed something in that phrase. The Hebrew word for north is tsaphon. Zaphon was also the name of a mountain north of Ugarit. In Ugarit’s Baal poems, the god Baal’s palace stands there. Many scholars read Psalm 48 as giving Zion the honor those poems gave Zaphon: the true home of the true God. Some translations take the phrase simply as a direction.

20.12

Other Ways to Read the Land

Main ideaSome scholars see land and worship as linked by the unseen realm, while other readers point to verses like Psalm 24:1 and see figures of speech or a view that grew over time.

One reading ties many passages together: Deuteronomy 32, Daniel 10, Naaman’s soil, David’s complaint and Psalm 137. In Michael Heiser’s version of it, the nations belonged to lesser heavenly rulers, while the land of Israel was the LORD’s portion. Holy places and holy ground marked where the LORD had placed his name.

Other readers see it differently. Psalm 24:1 says the earth is the LORD’s, and everything in it. In Jeremiah 29:7, the prophet tells the exiles to seek the welfare of Babylon and pray for it. So some readers say the Bible never limits God to one land. To them, the land passages are about where Israel’s worship and temple were.

Other scholars suggest a over time. Mark S. Smith traced one such path in The Origins of Biblical Monotheism (2001). In their view, early Israel’s language about gods and lands came from its neighbors. Over centuries, they suggest, Israel’s writers stressed more and more that there is only one God over all the earth. Heiser disagreed with parts of this view. Readers today can compare the passages and see how each reading handles them.

Why: Heiser read Deuteronomy 32:8–9 as looking back to Babel and the nations in Genesis 10–11.

6. Which Greek word did the Septuagint use for the gods of the nations in Psalm 96:5?

Why: The Hebrew says elilim (idols); the Septuagint translated it daimonia.

7. In Daniel 10, what blocked the heavenly messenger for twenty-one days?

Why: Daniel 10:13 says the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood him twenty-one days.

8. Who does Daniel call the prince of Daniel's people?

Why: Daniel 10:21 and 12:1 name Michael as the prince who stands for Daniel's people.

9. Which ancient Jewish book says God set a ruler over every people, but Israel is the Lord's portion?

Why: Sirach 17:17 echoes Deuteronomy 32:8–9 in these words.

10. Besides heavenly rulers, how else have readers understood Daniel's princes?

Why: Some interpreters read the princes as human officials; some modern scholars read them as symbols of God's hand in history.

11. Why was the ground holy in Exodus 3:5?

Why: Holy means set apart for God; the ground is holy because God speaks from the bush there.

12. Why do many scholars think Naaman asked for Israel's soil?

Why: Many scholars see the soil as a way to worship Israel's God in Aram, since lands were tied to gods.

13. In 1 Samuel 26:19, what does David say being driven out of the LORD's inheritance is like?

Why: David says it is as if they told him, "Go, serve other gods."

14. Which verse do readers often point to when they say God is not limited to one land?

Why: Psalm 24:1 says the earth is the LORD's and everything in it.

15. In Psalm 48:2, Zion is "on the sides of the north." What name does the Hebrew word for north share?

Why: The Hebrew word for north is tsaphon; Zaphon was Baal's mountain in Ugarit's texts, and many scholars read the psalm as claiming that honor for Zion.
