Words and Structure

Vocabulary

A tenth grader freezes on a chemistry quiz because of one word, then remembers a scrap of Latin from English class and works the word out. Two farmers rebuild a stone wall in a poem, and by the last line the wall is no longer only a wall. Both moments come from the same fact about language: words are not sealed boxes. They are built from parts that travel between subjects, and they can carry a second meaning that the writer never states out loud.

This unit teaches two skills that separate readers who cope from readers who understand. The first is taking a word apart: reading the context around it, seeing the root inside it, tracing where it came from, noticing when a science word or a history word is an ordinary word in disguise, and knowing which reference tool answers which question. The second is reading a figure of speech: metaphor, symbol, allegory, irony, paradox, allusion and euphemism, each a way of saying one thing and meaning another.

By the end you will be able to meet an unfamiliar word in any class and make a defensible guess, choose the exact word when you write, and explain what a figure of speech does to the meaning and tone of a text, with evidence from the words on the page. That last skill is what every literature essay in high school and college is asking for.

The big question

How do readers and writers get more meaning out of a word than its dictionary definition holds?

The story

The Word That Flew Away From the Center

A tenth grader is stuck on a chemistry quiz until a line of Latin from English class floats back to her.

Marisol had studied the chapter. She knew how to separate salt from sand and how to filter a cloudy liquid. Then question seven asked what a centrifuge does to a mixture of blood cells and plasma. She had never heard the word. Her pencil stopped. Twenty minutes were left, and she could feel the clock more than she could read the question.

She did what she had been taught to do with a stranger of a word. She looked at the pieces. The first part, centri, looked like center. She knew that one from centimeter and from the center of a circle. The second part, fuge, did not look like anything at all. Or did it? A memory from English class nudged her.

Two weeks earlier, her English teacher had written a scrap of Latin on the board from the Roman poet Virgil: fugit irreparabile tempus. Time flees, and it cannot be brought back. The class had laughed because he said it while stealing a glance at the clock. Fugit meant flees. Fuge, then, might mean flee or fly away from. A centrifuge would be something that makes things fly away from the center.

That was enough to picture it. A machine spins a tube very fast. The heavier parts of the mixture get thrown outward, away from the center, toward the bottom of the tube. The lighter liquid stays on top. Marisol wrote that the centrifuge spins the blood so the cells settle out from the plasma. She got the point, and she got something better than the point.

The word had not been a stranger after all. It was a relative of fugitive, a person who flees, and of refuge, a place you flee to. It shared a root with a two-thousand-year-old poem. Words are built from parts, and the parts travel. Once you can see them, a new word in chemistry or history or math is often an old word wearing a lab coat.

Reading the Clues Around a Word

40.1

Five Kinds of Context Clues

Main ideaWriters often plant the meaning of a hard word nearby, and there are five common places to look for it.

Read this sentence. The council’s decision was met with derision, and people laughed openly at the plan for weeks. You may not know . But the sentence tells you. People laughed openly. Derision means scornful laughter or mockery. The writer did not stop to define the word. The writer simply put its meaning next door. That is a , a hint in the surrounding words that points to what a word means.

There are five kinds you will meet again and again. A definition clue states the meaning outright: A peninsula, which is land surrounded by water on three sides, juts into the lake. An example clue lists members of the group: Amphibians, such as frogs, toads and salamanders, live both in water and on land. A synonym clue restates the idea in an easier word: The old bridge was precarious, so shaky that no truck would cross it. A contrast clue tells you what the word is not: Unlike her talkative brother, Nia was reticent at dinner. A cause-and-effect clue shows what the word leads to: Because the medicine was potent, one small dose stopped the pain.

Notice the signal words. Which is, such as, or, unlike, because, so. These small words are flags that a clue is nearby. When you hit a hard word, do not reread the word ten times. Read the whole sentence, then the sentence before and after it, and hunt for a flag. Then make a guess and test it. Put your guess in place of the word and read the sentence again. If it still makes sense, keep going.

One warning. Context gives you a working meaning, not always the exact one. From the first sentence you can tell derision is a kind of mocking. You could not tell from context alone that it comes from a Latin word meaning to laugh at. For a quiz, a working meaning is usually enough. For a word you will need again, check a dictionary later and write it down.

40.2

When the Clue Is Far Away

Main ideaSometimes no single sentence explains a word, and you must gather hints from the whole paragraph or passage.

Here is a paragraph with no easy flags. The village had been in a drought for three summers. The wells were dry. Farmers sold their animals because there was nothing to feed them. Children were sent to cousins in the city. When the rain finally came, people stood in the road and let it soak their clothes, and some of them wept. You are not told what means in the sentence where it appears. But every sentence after it is evidence. Dry wells, nothing to feed the animals, people weeping in the rain. A drought is a long stretch without rain. The paragraph taught you by showing the effects.

This is , a conclusion you draw from clues that add up. Reading for a word this way is like being a detective. You ask, what is the situation here? What would have to be true for these details to make sense? Then you name it. The same skill works on a page of history or a science article. If a text keeps describing a thing without defining it, the description is the definition.

There is a second far-away clue: the topic itself. If you are reading about volcanoes and meet the word , the topic narrows the possibilities before you read another word. It is something to do with volcanoes, probably the hot melted rock, since the passage talks about heat and pressure underground. Headings, pictures, captions and the title all shrink the field. Use them before you get stuck, not after.

One more tool is your own experience. The paragraph about the drought works because you know what dry wells and hungry animals mean. When a passage is about something you have lived, trust what you know. When it is about something far from your life, slow down and lean harder on the text. Good readers do both, and they notice which one they are doing.

Inside the Word

40.3

Roots That Carry Meaning

Main ideaA few dozen Latin and Greek roots appear in thousands of academic words, and knowing the root gives you a foothold.

Look at these words: transport, portable, export, import, report, support, deport. Seven words, one shared piece. Port comes from the Latin portare, to carry. Transport is to carry across. Portable means it can be carried. Export is to carry out of a country. Once you see port, you can make a fair guess at any word that contains it. That shared piece is a , the part of a word that holds its core meaning.

Academic words lean heavily on a small set of roots. Dict means say: predict, contradict, verdict, dictate, dictionary, diction. Spect means look: inspect, spectator, perspective, respect, spectacle, suspect. Scrib or script means write: describe, manuscript, prescription, scribble, transcript. Bene means good or well: benefit, benevolent, benefactor, benign. Greek gives us graph for writing, bio for life, chron for time, and tele for far. A telegraph writes at a distance. A chronology is the order of events in time.

The root is a foothold, not the whole climb. Verdict is a true saying, which is what a jury gives. But respect does not mean look again in ordinary use; it has drifted to mean regard someone highly. The root gets you close. The sentence takes you the rest of the way. So use both. Ask what the root suggests, then check that meaning against the context. When the two agree, you can be confident.

Start noticing roots in your other classes. In biology, a of a cell is not on the test, but biology, biome, antibiotic and symbiosis are, and bio is in every one. In history, a chronicle and an anachronism both hold chron. A student who knows twenty roots is not memorizing twenty words. She is holding a key to several hundred.

40.4

Prefixes and Suffixes at Work

Main ideaA prefix changes a word's meaning at the front, and a suffix often changes its job in the sentence at the back.

Take the root just, meaning fair. Add un at the front and you get unjust, the opposite. Add ice at the back and you get justice, a noun. Add ify and you get justify, a verb. Add ly and you get justly, an adverb. The root never changed. The pieces stuck on either side did all the work. A piece added to the front is a . A piece added to the end is a . Together with roots, these are the building blocks of most long words.

Prefixes mostly change meaning. Un, in, non and dis make a word negative: unclear, invisible, nonsense, dishonest. The prefix in has costumes: it becomes im before p or m (impossible, immature), il before l (illegal) and ir before r (irregular). Re means again or back: rewrite, return. Pre means before: preview, predict. Sub means under: submarine, subway. Trans means across: transport, translate. Number prefixes count: mono for one, bi for two, tri for three, as in monorail, bicycle, triangle.

Suffixes mostly change a word’s part of speech. Tion, ment, ness and ity make nouns: action, movement, kindness, clarity. Ize and ify make verbs: realize, simplify. Ous, ful, less and able make adjectives: dangerous, hopeful, careless, readable. Ly makes adverbs: quickly. Once you know this, you can tell what a word is doing even when you are unsure what it means. If a word ends in ness, it is a thing, a quality. If it ends in ize, someone is doing something.

A caution about in. It does not always mean not. Sometimes it means in or into. does not mean unable to burn. It means able to burst into flame, from the Latin inflammare, to set on fire. So many people misread it that safety labels now say flammable instead. When a prefix could go two ways, let the context decide. A word on a gas can that warns you is not telling you the gas is safe.

40.5

How Words Change Meaning

Main ideaWords drift over centuries, and knowing where a word came from helps you remember it and use it well.

Six hundred years ago, calling someone nice was an insult. Nice came from the Latin nescius, which means ignorant, not knowing. Over centuries it softened from foolish to fussy to pleasant. Silly once meant blessed and happy in Old English. Awful once meant full of awe, the feeling you have before something enormous, like a storm or a mountain. Now it just means very bad. The study of where words come from and how they have changed is .

Some words keep a picture inside them. Muscle comes from the Latin musculus, little mouse, because a muscle moving under the skin looked like a small mouse running. Companion comes from com, with, and panis, bread: a companion is someone you share bread with. comes from a brutal Roman army punishment in which one soldier in every ten was killed. Today it usually means to destroy a large part of something, and most dictionaries accept that use, though a few careful writers still object.

Meaning drifts in predictable directions. Words narrow: meat once meant any food, now only animal flesh. Words widen: a holiday was once a holy day, now any day off. Words rise: knight once meant a boy or servant. Words fall: villain once meant a farm worker, then a low person, then a criminal. Knowing the direction of the drift helps you read older texts. When a character in a two-hundred-year-old novel is called nice, do not assume it is praise.

Etymology is also a memory tool. A word you know the story of is a word you keep. Centrifuge stays because of Virgil. Companion stays because of bread. When you look up a new word, glance at the origin line in the dictionary entry. It is usually in brackets, and it is often the most interesting part.

40.6

Cognates Across Languages

Main ideaIf you speak Spanish or another language built from Latin, thousands of English academic words are already half yours.

If you read Spanish, look at these English words: important, family, animal, nation, analysis, evidence, hypothesis. Now look at their Spanish twins: importante, familia, animal, nación, análisis, evidencia, hipótesis. They look alike because they are alike. They descend from the same Latin or Greek parent. Words like these are , relatives across languages. For a student who reads Spanish, Portuguese, French or Italian, the hardest words in an English textbook are often the easiest, because those are the Latin ones.

This is not a small advantage. Everyday English is mostly Germanic: house, bread, run, think. But the vocabulary of science, law, medicine and school is largely Latin and Greek: photosynthesis, constitution, circulation, geometry. A Spanish speaker who feels behind in English conversation may be ahead in English chemistry. The trick is to notice the cognate and trust it. Fotosíntesis, constitución, circulación, geometría.

But be careful of the , a word that looks like a twin and is not. In Spanish, embarazada means pregnant, not embarrassed. Actual means current, so actualmente means currently, not actually. Éxito means success, not exit. Librería means bookstore, not library. Sensible means sensitive. These traps are few compared with the thousands of true cognates, but they are worth a list of their own. When a cognate makes a sentence strange, check it.

If you speak a language that is not built from Latin, such as Polish, Arabic, Chinese, Tagalog or Urdu, cognates will be fewer, but not zero. Many languages borrowed the same Greek and Latin science words. And the roots in the last lessons work for everyone, whatever your first language. Illinois classrooms hold speakers of well over a hundred languages. Every one of those students brings a word bank into the room. Use yours.

Words at Work in Each Subject

40.7

Science Words With Everyday Twins

Main ideaMany science terms are ordinary words with a precise second meaning, and you must switch meanings when you switch rooms.

In the hallway, a solution is the answer to a problem. In chemistry, a is a mixture in which one substance is dissolved evenly in another, like salt in water. Same spelling, different word. A base in the hallway is where a runner stands. In chemistry it is a substance that accepts hydrogen ions, the opposite of an acid. Volume on your phone means loudness. Volume in a lab means how much space something takes up. These are words: words with a special meaning inside one field of study.

The trouble is that the everyday meaning is louder in your head. When a lab question says find the volume of the gas, a tired reader may think of sound and freeze. The fix is to expect the switch. In a science text, assume an ordinary word means its science meaning until proven otherwise. Then check. Does volume of the gas make sense as loudness? No. As space? Yes. Read on.

Watch for this pattern in physics and biology too. Work in physics is force times distance, not homework. Power is how fast work is done. A conductor carries electricity, not a train ticket. In biology, a cell is not a phone or a prison room, a tissue is not for your nose, and a culture is bacteria growing in a dish. Each field is its own room with its own dictionary hanging on the wall.

Here is a practical habit. When your science textbook prints a word in bold, that is a signal that the ordinary meaning is not the one you need. Write the science meaning next to the everyday one in your notes. Solution: answer, or a dissolved mixture. Seeing both side by side stops the two from blurring together at test time.

40.8

History and Math Words

Main ideaHistory and math also borrow ordinary words and sharpen them, so read the subject's meaning, not the street meaning.

A history book says the colonists demanded representation. In the cafeteria, a party is a celebration. In history, a is an organized political group, like the Democratic and Republican parties. A cabinet is not for dishes; it is the group of advisers who head a president’s departments. A bill is not what you pay; it is a proposed law. Depression can be a sad mood, a low spot in the ground, or the Great Depression, the economic collapse that began in 1929. Revolution can mean one spin of a wheel or the overthrow of a government.

Math is even stricter. The of a set of numbers is their average, nothing to do with being unkind. A product is what you get when you multiply. A radical is the root sign, not a rebel. Odd numbers are not strange. A table is a grid of values. A function pairs each input with exactly one output. In math, a word means one thing only, and the whole subject depends on that. If a problem says find the product of 6 and 4, the answer is 24, not a thing you buy.

How do you tell which meaning is on the page? The same way as in science: expect the subject’s meaning, then confirm it. But history adds a twist. Historical texts often use words in their older senses, because the writers lived then. In a document from 1776, a state may mean a nation, and awful may still mean awe-inspiring. Reading history well means reading with the etymology lesson in one hand.

One last group is the word that never appears outside its subject: integer, hypotenuse, feudalism, tariff. These are honest strangers. Nobody expects you to know them from the street. They are always defined somewhere in the chapter, usually near the first time they appear. Find that spot, and copy the definition in your own words.

40.9

Choosing the Exact Word

Main ideaNear-synonyms are not equal, and the precise word tells the reader more in less space.

He walked across the room. Now try: he strolled, he trudged, he marched, he crept, he staggered. Every one means he moved on foot from one side to the other. But strolled says relaxed. Trudged says tired and heavy. Marched says purpose and maybe anger. Crept says he did not want to be heard. Staggered says hurt or dizzy. A single verb did the work of a whole sentence of description. That is : choosing the word whose meaning fits exactly.

Words that mean almost the same thing carry different shades. Thin, slender, skinny and gaunt all describe a narrow body. Slender is admiring. Skinny is a little rude. Gaunt suggests illness or hunger. The neutral core meaning is the word’s . The feeling and the suggestion attached to it is its . Precise writers choose for both. If you write that a politician is stubborn, you have judged. If you write determined, you have praised. Same behavior, different word, different verdict.

Vague words hide meaning. Nice, good, bad, thing, stuff, very and a lot say almost nothing. The party was good tells the reader nothing about the party. The party was loud, crowded and over by ten tells the reader everything. When you revise, hunt for the vague words and ask what you actually mean. Usually there is a sharper word waiting.

Precision also matters when you read. When a poet chooses gaunt and not thin, or a scientist writes accelerate and not speed up, the choice is a clue. Ask why this word and not the easier one. In an essay about a text, quoting the exact word and explaining its shade is one of the best moves you can make.

40.10

Three Reference Tools

Main ideaA dictionary, a thesaurus and an etymology dictionary answer three different questions, and the skill is knowing which to open.

You meet the word candid in a novel. Open a . The entry gives you the spelling, how to say it, its part of speech (adjective), its meanings in order, and often a sample sentence: candid, truthful and straightforward, not hiding anything. Most entries also give a short origin in brackets: from Latin candidus, white or pure. The dictionary is the tool for a word you have met and do not know. Print or online, it answers what does this mean and how is it used.

Now you are writing, and you have used the word said six times. Open a . It lists words with related meanings: stated, remarked, muttered, declared, announced. But the thesaurus does not explain the shades. Muttered and declared are not interchangeable. So use the thesaurus to find candidates, then take the candidate back to the dictionary to check that it fits. A thesaurus without a dictionary produces essays full of words that are almost right, which is worse than plain words that are exactly right.

Big questionHow can a writer say one thing and mean another, and how do readers know when it is happening?
The story

Two Men, a Wall and a Question

Robert Frost's poem about repairing a stone wall seems to be about stones, until the reader notices what the stones stand for.

Every spring in northern New England, the ground thaws and the old stone walls between farms tumble a little. Frost heaves the stones. Hunters knock them loose. In 1914 Robert Frost published a poem called Mending Wall about two neighbors who walk the line between their farms each spring, each picking up the fallen stones on his own side and setting them back. For the first several lines, that is all that happens. Stones, hands, a walk.

The poem opens with a line that sounds like a riddle: Something there is that doesn't love a wall. The speaker does not say what the something is. Cold weather, maybe. Or something in nature that dislikes barriers. As he works, he starts to tease his neighbor. There are no cows here to keep in or out, he points out. His apple trees will never cross over and eat the neighbor's pine cones. Why do they need a wall at all?

The neighbor has one answer, and he gives it twice: Good fences make good neighbors. He does not explain. He learned it from his father, and he will not think past it. The speaker watches him carry a stone in each hand and imagines him as an old-stone savage armed, a man from a darker age moving in darkness. That is not a description of a farmer. It is a picture of a mind that has closed.

By the end, a reader realizes the poem was never only about a wall. The wall is a wall, but it is also every barrier people keep up out of habit. The neighbor's saying is a saying, but it is also the way an old idea can keep a person from thinking. And the speaker, who wants the wall gone, still shows up every spring to help rebuild it. The poem does not tell you who is right. It hands you the stones.

This is what figurative language does. It lets a plain thing, a wall, a road, a bird, carry a second weight of meaning without ever announcing it. Frost was a master of it, but the same move happens in speeches, song lyrics, ads and the way your friend says she is drowning in homework. This chapter is about noticing the second weight, naming it and saying what it does.

41.1

Metaphor and Simile, Closer Up

Main ideaA metaphor has two parts, the thing meant and the image used, and the meaning lives in what the two share.

Juliet is the sun. Romeo says so when he sees her at a window: It is the east, and Juliet is the sun. Nobody thinks Juliet is a ball of burning gas. Romeo means that she lights his world, that everything else looks dim next to her, that his day begins when she appears. He has taken one thing, Juliet, and spoken of it as though it were another thing, the sun. That is a . A simile does the same thing but announces the comparison with like or as: her smile was like sunrise.

Every metaphor has two halves. The thing actually being talked about is the , here Juliet. The image borrowed to describe it is the , here the sun. The meaning is whatever the two share: brightness, warmth, being the center of things. To read a metaphor, name both halves and then ask what they have in common. Not everything. The sun is also hot enough to burn and impossible to look at. Romeo is not saying that. Context tells you which shared traits count.

Metaphors hide in ordinary speech. Time is money. She has a heart of stone. He shot down my idea. The deadline is looming. We do not notice these because they are worn smooth. But even the worn ones shape thinking. If time is money, then time can be wasted, spent, saved and budgeted, and you will feel that a lazy afternoon is a loss. Notice the vehicle, and you notice the attitude riding inside it.

In your own writing, a fresh metaphor is worth ten adjectives. Instead of the hallway was very crowded and loud, try the hallway was a river at flood. The reader gets crowding, motion, noise and danger in six words. Just keep the vehicle consistent. If the hallway is a river, do not have students marching through it two sentences later. A river does not march.

41.2

Extended Metaphor and Conceit

Main ideaWhen a writer keeps one comparison going through a whole poem or passage, every line adds a new point of contact.

Emily Dickinson begins a poem, Hope is the thing with feathers, that perches in the soul. That alone is a metaphor: hope is a bird. But she does not stop. The bird sings a tune without words. It never stops singing. It sings sweetest in the storm. It has kept her warm in the coldest land, and it has never asked her for a crumb. Each line finds one more way a bird and hope are alike. A comparison carried through many lines is an .

Reading an extended metaphor means tracking the points of contact. Make two columns: bird on one side, hope on the other. Perches: hope settles in and stays. Sings without words: hope does not need reasons. Sings in the storm: hope is strongest in trouble. Never asks for a crumb: hope costs nothing. When you have the columns, you have the poem. Shakespeare does the same when a character says all the world’s a stage and all the men and women merely players, then runs through the seven ages of a life as if they were scenes.

A is an extended metaphor that is surprising or far-fetched, joining two things that seem to have nothing in common and making it work anyway. The poet John Donne, writing around 1600, compared two lovers who must part to the two legs of a drawing compass. One leg stays fixed at the center while the other travels in a circle, leaning toward the first, and the fixed leg’s steadiness is what makes the circle come out true. A compass is a strange way to talk about love. That strangeness is the point. It forces the reader to think.

Extended metaphors also live outside poetry. A coach calls the season a war and then talks about battles, soldiers and casualties. A politician calls the economy a ship and then speaks of steering, storms and staying the course. Once you spot the vehicle, ask what it makes you feel and what it leaves out. If the economy is a ship, there is one captain. Someone who wants you to trust one leader might like that image very much.

Beyond the Literal

41.3

Symbols

Main ideaA symbol is a real thing in a text that also stands for an idea, and you know it is a symbol because the text keeps returning to it.

In Mending Wall, the wall is a real wall. Stones fall, the men lift them. But the poem keeps circling back to it, arguing about it, asking what it is for. A thing that is fully itself and also stands for something larger is a . The wall stands for the barriers people keep up out of habit. A metaphor says A is B. A symbol just shows you A, over and over, until you feel B standing behind it.

Some symbols are shared by a whole culture. A dove for peace, a red rose for love, a skull for death, a scale for justice, a flag for a nation. You do not need a footnote for these. Others belong to one work only. In Paul Laurence Dunbar’s 1896 poem We Wear the Mask, the mask is not a real object. It is the smile Black Americans were forced to wear over pain, the poem says, and the whole poem is the mask being described. The reader learns what it means by reading, not by knowing in advance.

How do you tell a symbol from a mere object? Three tests. Repetition: does the text mention it more than once, especially at important moments? Emphasis: does it appear in the title, the first line or the last? Weight: do characters treat it as more than it is, arguing over it, protecting it, longing for it? A doorknob mentioned once is a doorknob. A locked door the whole story keeps returning to is probably more.

One warning. Students sometimes turn every object into a symbol. The blue curtains mean the author is sad. Usually the blue curtains mean the room had blue curtains. A symbol earns its meaning by the way the text uses it. If you claim something is a symbol, you should be able to point to the repetitions and the moments that gave it weight. If you cannot, let the curtains be curtains.

Allegory: The Whole Story Stands for Something

Main ideaAn allegory is a story in which the characters and events line up point by point with a second meaning outside the story.

Imagine a story about a farm where the animals drive out the farmer and run the place themselves. The pigs take charge. They promise equality, then slowly take the farmer’s privileges for themselves until nobody can tell pig from man. George Orwell published that story, Animal Farm, in 1945, and every reader knew that the farm was Russia after its revolution and the pigs were the leaders who betrayed it. A story whose characters and events stand, one for one, for something outside the story is an .

An allegory is a symbol stretched over a whole narrative. In a symbol, one object carries a second meaning. In an allegory, the whole cast does. John Bunyan’s The Pilgrim’s Progress, from 1678, follows a man named Christian who leaves the City of Destruction and walks toward the Celestial City, passing through the Slough of Despond and Vanity Fair. The names give it away. Despond means hopelessness. Vanity means empty pride. The journey is a soul’s life, and every stop is a temptation or a trial.

The ancient Greek philosopher Plato told an allegory about prisoners chained in a cave, able to see only shadows on the wall. One escapes, sees the sun and the real world, and returns to tell the others, who do not believe him. The cave is ignorance. The shadows are appearances mistaken for truth. The sun is knowledge. Plato used the story because an argument about knowledge is hard to follow, and a story about a cave is not.

To read an allegory, build the key. Write the story element on one side and its outside meaning on the other: pigs, leaders; cave, ignorance. Then ask what the story lets the writer say that a plain statement could not. Orwell could show betrayal happening step by step. Bunyan could make a life of faith into a road with a map. Allegory turns an idea into something you can watch.

Three Kinds of Irony

Main ideaIrony is a gap between what is said or expected and what is true, and it comes in verbal, situational and dramatic forms.

It is pouring rain, and your friend says, Great weather for a picnic. She means the opposite of what she says, and she expects you to know it. That is , saying one thing to mean another. When it is meant to sting, it is sarcasm. Verbal irony works only if the listener catches the gap. Tone of voice, context and a shared sense of how things are all help. In writing, where there is no tone of voice, the context has to do the whole job.

is a gap between what is expected and what happens. A fire station burns down. A lifeguard drowns. In O. Henry’s 1905 story The Gift of the Magi, a young wife sells her long hair to buy a chain for her husband’s watch. He, the same day, sells the watch to buy combs for her hair. Each gift is now useless, and each is perfect. Nobody said the opposite of what they meant; the situation itself turned inside out.

is a gap between what a character knows and what the audience knows. At the end of Romeo and Juliet, Romeo finds Juliet in the tomb and believes she is dead. The audience knows she has only taken a sleeping potion and will wake in minutes. We want to shout at him. That helplessness, watching a character act on a mistake we can see and he cannot, is the feeling dramatic irony creates. Horror films use it constantly: the audience knows what is behind the door.

To name the irony in a text, first find the gap. Between words and meaning: verbal. Between expectation and outcome: situational. Between a character’s knowledge and the audience’s: dramatic. Then ask what the gap does. Verbal irony often makes a speaker sound sharp or amused. Situational irony makes a story feel like fate or a joke. Dramatic irony makes an audience tense or sad. Irony is never just decoration. It always shifts how the reader feels.

Paradox and Oxymoron

Main ideaA paradox seems to contradict itself but holds a truth, and an oxymoron packs that contradiction into two words.

In Hamlet, the prince says, I must be cruel only to be kind. On its face that makes no sense. Cruelty is the opposite of kindness. But Hamlet means that harsh words now may save his mother from a worse fate later, the way a doctor may hurt you to heal you. A statement that seems to contradict itself and yet turns out to be true is a . The reader’s job is to find the sense hidden inside the contradiction.

Paradoxes appear wherever a writer wants to stop you cold. The witches in Macbeth chant, Fair is foul, and foul is fair, and the whole play proves them right: what looks good turns out evil, and evil wears a fair face. Everyday speech has them too. Less is more. You have to spend money to make money. The only constant is change. Each one forces you to pause and work out how both halves can hold.

An is a paradox shrunk to a phrase, usually two words that fight each other. Deafening silence. Jumbo shrimp. Bittersweet. Living dead. Juliet says parting is such sweet sorrow, and the sweet and the sorrow are both true: it hurts to leave, and it is sweet because she will see him again. The oxymoron does in two words what a paragraph of explanation might do, and it does it with a jolt.

When you meet a paradox or an oxymoron, do not dismiss it as nonsense. Ask under what conditions both halves can be true. Deafening silence: silence so complete that it feels like a pressure on the ears. Cruel to be kind: cruel now, kind in the long run. The figure is a small puzzle, and solving it is often the moment the writer’s meaning opens up. If it cannot be solved, then the writer may simply have made an error, but that is rare in good writing.

41.7

Allusion and How to Look One Up

Main ideaAn allusion borrows a famous story in a few words, and a reader who does not know the story can find it in a minute.

In 1858, Abraham Lincoln opened a speech about slavery with the words, A house divided against itself cannot stand. He did not invent the line. It comes from the Gospel of Mark, and his listeners knew it. By borrowing it, Lincoln made the nation into a house and let the Bible’s authority stand behind his warning without arguing for it. A brief reference to a well-known story, person, place or text is an . It works by trust: the writer trusts you to know the reference.

Allusions come from a few big sources. Biblical: a good Samaritan is a stranger who helps; a David against a Goliath is a small fighter against a giant; a prodigal son is a wanderer who comes home. Classical, from Greek and Roman myth: an Achilles heel is one fatal weakness; a Pandora’s box is a source of troubles better left shut; a Trojan horse is a gift hiding an attack; a task like Sisyphus’s never ends. Historical: to meet your Waterloo is to suffer your final defeat, after the battle where Napoleon lost in 1815. Literary: a Scrooge is a miser, from Dickens.

The problem is that nobody knows every story, and writers do not stop to explain. So learn to look one up fast. Take the odd phrase, the name or the place you do not recognize, and search it with the word meaning or origin. Read a source you trust, an encyclopedia or a dictionary of allusions, not the first comment thread. Then go back to the sentence and ask what the story adds. Usually it adds a whole plot in three words.

Allusions also appear in songs, films and titles. A team called the Titans alludes to giants of Greek myth. A headline about a modern Trojan horse is warning about a hidden threat. Once you start noticing, you will find that much of what you read is quietly quoting something older. Knowing the source is like getting the joke that everyone else was already laughing at.

Euphemism: The Softer Word

Main ideaA euphemism replaces a blunt word with a gentler one, and readers should ask whether it is kindness or a way to hide the truth.

Nobody at a funeral says the person died. They say she passed away, she is gone, we lost her. A company does not fire two hundred workers; it lets them go, or downsizes, or restructures. A used car is pre-owned. A lie is a misstatement. Each of these is a , a mild or indirect word put in place of one that is harsh, embarrassing or frightening. The word comes from Greek parts meaning good speech.

Some euphemisms are simple kindness. Passed away lets a grieving family avoid a blunt word in a hard hour. Restroom spares everyone a description. Nobody is fooled and nobody is harmed. But other euphemisms are built to fool. When a military report says collateral damage, it means civilians were killed. When a budget speaks of revenue enhancements, it means taxes went up. The soft word is there to keep you from feeling what the hard word would make you feel.

The test is simple. Translate the euphemism into the plainest word you can, and ask whether the writer wanted you to. If the plain word is died and the setting is a funeral, the euphemism is doing gentle work. If the plain word is killed and the setting is a report meant to reassure the public, the euphemism is doing dishonest work. Notice, too, that euphemisms tend to be longer and vaguer than the words they replace. Extra syllables are often extra distance from the truth.

As a writer, use euphemism on purpose and rarely. In a note to a grieving friend, passed away is right. In an analysis of a company’s decision, write that it fired workers, because that is what happened. And when you catch a euphemism in a speech, an ad or a news release, name it in your writing. Saying that a speaker called layoffs a restructuring is itself a sharp piece of analysis.

Why: If the writer would rather you not translate it, the euphemism is hiding something.
41.9

Reading a Figure in Its Context

Main ideaThe same figure of speech can mean different things in different passages, so always read it against the sentences around it.

Take the phrase cold as ice. In one story: her hands were cold as ice after an hour in the snow. That is a simile about temperature, nearly literal. In another: her voice was cold as ice when she told him to leave. Now it means unfriendly, controlled, without warmth. Same words, different meaning, and only the context tells you which. A figure of speech is never a fixed code. It is a suggestion that the surrounding sentences complete.

Here is a harder case. A character says, Well, that went well. If the previous paragraph showed a smooth meeting, the sentence is literal. If it showed a meeting that ended with someone storming out, it is verbal irony. Nothing in the sentence itself changes. Readers who miss the irony are not bad readers of the sentence. They are readers who did not carry the previous paragraph with them. Figures of speech make you hold more of the text in your head at once.

Sometimes a whole poem has to be read before its central figure settles. In Frost’s Mending Wall, the line Good fences make good neighbors sounds wise the first time the neighbor says it. By the second time, after the speaker has mocked it and imagined the neighbor moving in darkness, the same words sound stubborn and unexamined. The line did not change. The context around it did, and the reader’s understanding changed with it.

So when you interpret a figure, do three things. Read the sentence and identify the figure. Read the surrounding paragraph, and if needed the whole passage, to see what it is pointing at. Then state the meaning in plain words, and be ready to say which context clues told you. Cold voice: the sentence says voice, not hands, and she is telling him to leave. That is evidence. An interpretation without evidence is just a guess.

Think it through
Same Words, Two Rooms

1. After the championship, the captain lifted the trophy and said, We climbed the mountain. 2. After the third loss in a row, the captain looked at the empty stands and said, We climbed the mountain.

What does the metaphor mean in the first case? What does it mean in the second, and what kind of figure has it become? Point to the exact context words that changed it.

Words to know
figure of speech
an expression that means something other than its literal words, such as a metaphor or irony
interpret
to explain the meaning of something, especially something not stated directly
context
the words, sentences and situation surrounding a word or phrase
Check yourself

1. Why can cold as ice mean two different things?

Why: Hands after an hour in the snow is temperature; a voice telling someone to leave is unfriendliness.

2. What does a reader need to catch the irony in that went well?

Why: The sentence is identical either way; only the earlier events reveal the gap.

3. How does the meaning of Good fences make good neighbors change in Mending Wall?

Why: The words stay the same; the surrounding lines shift how the reader hears them.
Section 4

Effect and Use

41.10

What a Figure Does to Tone

Main ideaFigurative language changes not only what a sentence means but how it feels, and naming that feeling is the heart of analysis.

Read two versions. First: the city was crowded and noisy that night. Second: the city was a hive that night, humming and swarming. Both report the same facts. The second makes you feel something the first does not. A hive is busy, crowded and alive, but also organized and a little dangerous, with the possibility of stings. The metaphor did not add information. It added , the writer’s attitude toward the subject, and it added mood, the feeling produced in the reader.

Different figures push tone in different directions. Metaphor and simile can make a subject grand or small depending on the vehicle: the senator was a lion, the senator was a wet hen. Irony makes a speaker sound amused, bitter or superior. Euphemism softens and, if overused, makes a writer sound evasive. Allusion can make a passage feel learned or solemn, as Lincoln’s Bible verse did. Oxymoron and paradox make a reader stop and think, which slows the pace and feels serious.

So when you write about a text, do not stop at the label. The author uses a metaphor is a beginning, not an analysis. Go on: the metaphor compares the city to a hive, and the vehicle suggests both energy and danger, so the passage feels exciting and slightly threatening at once. Three moves: name the figure, unpack the vehicle, state the effect on tone. Every good paragraph about figurative language has all three.

Try it on Frost. When the speaker sees his neighbor as an old-stone savage armed, the figure is a metaphor. The vehicle is a prehistoric man carrying weapons. The effect is to make the neighbor seem not evil but stuck in the dark, and to make the speaker sound a little superior, which the poem may or may not approve of. That last uncertainty is fine. Tone is often mixed, and saying so honestly is stronger than forcing one answer.

Think it through
Two Descriptions of One Storm

A. The storm arrived at six and dropped two inches of rain in an hour. B. The storm came in like an army, drumming on the roofs and flinging the trees around by the hair.

Which version has tone, and what is it? Name the figures in B, unpack one vehicle and state its effect in a single sentence using the three moves.

Words to know
tone
the writer's attitude toward the subject, shown through word choice and figures of speech
mood
the feeling a passage creates in the reader
evasive
avoiding a direct answer; slippery
Check yourself

1. What does the hive metaphor add to the sentence about the city?

Why: Both versions report the same facts; the hive adds attitude and mood.

2. What are the three moves in analyzing a figure of speech?

Why: The label alone is only a beginning; the analysis lives in the vehicle and the effect.

3. What does the lesson say about a tone that seems mixed?

Why: Frost's speaker sounds both perceptive and superior; a careful reader can say both.
41.11

Using Figures in Your Own Writing

Main ideaA fresh, consistent figure strengthens your writing, while a cliché or a mixed metaphor weakens it.

A student writes: At the end of the day, the team hit the ground running and gave 110 percent. Every phrase is a figure of speech, and every one is dead. A is a figure so overused that it no longer makes a picture. The reader slides over it. Nobody sees a ground or a runner. Compare: the team came out like it had been waiting behind a door all week. The reader sees the door. Freshness is the difference between a figure that works and one that fills space.

The second danger is the , two vehicles that cannot share a sentence. We will iron out the bottlenecks. You cannot iron a bottle. The ship of state has hit a bump in the road. Ships do not travel roads. Mixed metaphors happen when a writer uses figures without seeing them. The cure is to see them. Before you keep a metaphor, picture it. If the picture is absurd, change one half.

Figures belong in analysis too, in moderation. Writing that Frost’s speaker circles the wall like a lawyer circling a witness makes a point vividly. But an analytical essay should mostly explain figures, not pile up its own. One well-placed comparison in an introduction or conclusion is enough. Let the text you are analyzing supply the poetry, and let your sentences supply the clarity.

A short practice. Take a plain sentence from your last essay. Find its most important noun. Ask what it is like, and reject the first two answers, since they will be the clichés. Take the third, check that the vehicle fits, and rewrite. Then read the new sentence aloud. If you can see the picture and it sounds like you, keep it. If it sounds like a greeting card, you have found a cliché, and you already know what to do.

Think it through
Fix the Sentence

Draft: When the rubber meets the road, our student council will grab the bull by the horns and sail through the storm to reach new heights.

Count the figures. Which are clichés, and where do the vehicles collide? Rewrite the sentence with one fresh, consistent figure and no more.

Words to know
cliché
a figure of speech so overused that it no longer creates a picture
mixed metaphor
two incompatible vehicles combined in one figure, such as ironing out a bottleneck
vivid
producing a clear, strong picture in the mind
Check yourself

1. Why does a cliché weaken writing?

Why: Readers slide over hit the ground running without seeing anything.

2. Which sentence contains a mixed metaphor?

Why: Ironing and bottlenecks are two vehicles that cannot combine; the others keep one picture.

3. What does the lesson advise about figures in an analytical essay?

Why: The essay's job is to explain the text's figures clearly, not to compete with them.
