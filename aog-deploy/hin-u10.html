Epics, Puranas and Poets

Smriti and Genre

The Vedas were guarded by a small group of priests. Most Hindus, though, learned their faith through stories and songs. They learned it from the two great epics, the Ramayana and Mahabharata, from the Puranas, and from poet-saints who sang in the languages of the market and the field.

In this unit you will study the epic as a genre, called itihasa. You will see how stories sit inside other stories and how the epics teach dharma through hard choices. Then you will meet the Puranas, with their avatars and goddesses, and the bhakti poets: the Alvars and Nayanars, Mirabai, Kabir, Tulsidas and Tukaram.

By the end, you will be able to name the books of each epic, explain what a Purana is, and describe how bhakti poets brought devotion to people of every background. You will also cite passages with their translations.

The big question

How did epics, Puranas and poet-saints carry Hindu teaching to people of every language and background?

Itihasa: Epic as Story and Teaching

The Epics
Big questionHow do the Ramayana and Mahabharata tell stories and teach dharma at the same time?
The story

A Story Inside a Story

A storyteller opens one story, and inside it another, and inside that another.

In a forest called Naimisha, many sages have gathered for a long ritual. A traveling bard named Ugrashravas arrives. The sages welcome him and ask for a story. "Tell us the great tale," they say, "the one you heard at King Janamejaya's snake sacrifice."

So the bard begins. He tells how, years before, a sage named Vaishampayana told the tale to King Janamejaya. And Vaishampayana had learned it from his own teacher, Vyasa, who composed it. Vyasa was also a grandfather of the heroes in the story.

Inside the tale of the heroes, characters stop to tell stories of their own. A sage tells a sad prince about a king named Nala who lost everything at dice. A wife tells of Savitri, who followed Death. A grandfather lying on a bed of arrows teaches for days.

This is how the Mahabharata opens, according to its first book. Stories sit inside stories, like boxes inside boxes. Readers sometimes lose track of who is speaking. Yet each layer reminds them that the story was handed from voice to voice.

The Mahabharata even says of itself that what is found here may be found elsewhere, but what is not here is nowhere. Many readers take this as a proud claim that the epic holds all of life, from love and war to grief and wisdom.

Talk about itWhy might a storyteller put stories inside other stories? What does it do for the listener?
Section 1

What Is Itihasa?

19.1

So It Was

Main ideaHindus call the Ramayana and Mahabharata itihasa, meaning 'so indeed it was', a genre of sacred history and epic.

When a Hindu grandparent begins the Ramayana, they often speak as if telling true history. The Sanskrit word for the two great epics is . It comes from iti ha asa, which means ’so indeed it was.’

Many Hindus read the epics as history: real events in a distant age. Others read them mainly as sacred stories that teach dharma. Scholars study them as , long poems about heroes, gods, war and duty, which grew over centuries.

Itihasa belongs to smriti, the remembered texts. Yet the epics are hugely important. The Mahabharata calls itself a fifth Veda. For many Hindus, the epics are the most loved of all their texts, told at bedtime, on stage and on TV.

19.2

Dates and Authors

Main ideaTradition names Valmiki and Vyasa as authors, while scholars date the epics' growth to about 400 BCE–400 CE.

Hindu tradition says the sage composed the Ramayana and is honored as the first poet. It says the sage composed the Mahabharata and also arranged the Vedas. In the traditional view, the events took place in very ancient ages.

Scholars see the epics as growing over many centuries. Most date their main growth to roughly 400 BCE to 400 CE. They point to layers in the text: some parts seem older, some added later.

Both views are held with care. A Hindu reader may honor Valmiki as the poet while also enjoying the work of scholars. This course names both views and does not decide between them. Remember to use ’c.’ for these dates.

19.3

Frame Stories

Main ideaBoth epics use frame stories, where one story is told inside another.

A is a story that holds other stories, like a picture frame. The Mahabharata has at least three frames. A bard tells sages in the Naimisha forest. Inside, Vaishampayana tells King Janamejaya. Inside that, the heroes’ story unfolds.

The Ramayana has a frame too. In its first book, Valmiki hears Rama’s story from the sage Narada. Later he sees a hunter kill a bird, and his grief flows out in a new kind of verse, the shloka. The god Brahma then asks him to tell Rama’s story in that verse.

Frames do several jobs. They show who passed the story down. They let characters pause to teach. They also remind readers that the tale is being told, and that listening is part of it.

Inside the Books

19.4

The Eighteen Parvas

Main ideaThe Mahabharata is arranged in eighteen books, called parvas, that trace a family feud from its start to its end.

The Mahabharata tells of two sets of cousins, the Pandavas and the Kauravas. They quarrel over a kingdom. The epic follows them from birth, through a dice game and exile, to a great war and its sad aftermath.

The story is arranged in eighteen , or books. The first is the Adi Parva, the book of beginnings. The Sabha Parva holds the dice game. The Bhishma Parva holds the Bhagavad Gita. The last books tell of the heroes’ final journey.

Eighteen shows up again and again. The war lasts eighteen days, eighteen armies fight, and the Gita has eighteen chapters. Many readers notice this pattern. Scholars also note a list of a hundred smaller sections, the upaparvas.

19.5

The Seven Kandas

Main ideaValmiki's Ramayana has seven kandas, or books, that follow Rama from birth to his reign.

Valmiki’s Ramayana has seven . The Bala Kanda tells of Rama’s childhood. Ayodhya Kanda tells of his exile. Aranya Kanda, the forest book, includes Sita’s capture. Kishkindha Kanda tells of the monkey kingdom.

Sundara Kanda, the beautiful book, follows Hanuman to Lanka. Yuddha Kanda tells of the war. Uttara Kanda, the last book, tells of Rama’s reign and of Sita’s later trials. Many scholars think the first and last books were added later than the middle five.

Many Hindus read the Sundara Kanda on its own as a devotional text. Some families recite it in times of trouble. This shows that a part of an epic can take on its own life in worship.

19.6

Stories Inside the Mahabharata

Main ideaThe Mahabharata holds many side stories, like Nala and Damayanti and Savitri, that teach about love and duty.

During the Pandavas’ forest exile, sages tell them stories to comfort and teach them. In one, King loses his kingdom at dice and wanders the forest. His wife never gives up, and at last they are reunited.

The Pandavas, too, have lost everything at dice. So the story mirrors their own, and it gives them hope that loss is not the end. Readers see how the epic uses side stories to hold up a mirror to the main characters.

Another tale tells of , who follows Yama, the lord of death, as he carries away her husband’s life. With wisdom and persistence, she wins it back. Many Hindu women honor Savitri in a yearly fast called Vat Savitri.

Section 3

Teaching Through Story

19.7

Hard Choices About Dharma

Main ideaThe epics show characters facing hard choices about dharma, and they often leave readers to think it through.

In the Sabha Parva, Yudhishthira, the eldest Pandava, gambles away his kingdom, his brothers, himself and finally his wife, . She is dragged into the hall. She asks the elders: did he lose himself first? If so, could he still wager me?

The elders fall silent. Even the wise Bhishma says dharma here is subtle. The epic does not give an easy answer. It lets the question hang in the air. The Mahabharata often says that dharma is subtle and hard to know.

The Ramayana tends to show clearer models, like Rama keeping his father’s promise. Yet it has hard moments too. Both epics invite readers to argue about what the right action was. Many Hindu families and scholars do exactly that.

19.8

Kings, Friends and Loss

Main ideaBesides dharma, the epics teach about kingship, friendship and grief.

The epics are full of friendships: Rama and Hanuman, Krishna and Arjuna, Rama and the monkey king Sugriva. They also show friendships that lead to harm, like Duryodhana and Karna, bound by loyalty even to a wrong cause.

The epics teach about kingship. The Shanti Parva, the book of peace, has Bhishma, dying on a bed of arrows, teach Yudhishthira how to rule. In the Ramayana, Rama’s reign, , became an ideal of just rule. Gandhi used the word for a fair society.

The epics do not hide grief. After the war, the Mahabharata’s women weep over the dead in the Stri Parva. Readers often say the epic’s ending feels like mourning, not victory, even for the winners.

Main ideaK.M. Ganguli's full English Mahabharata (1883–96) and Griffith's Ramayan (1870–74) are public-domain translations.

Many Ramayanas

Main ideaThe Ramayana has been retold in many languages, including Kamban's Tamil version and Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas.

Valmiki’s Sanskrit Ramayana is not the only one. In the 12th century (c.), the poet retold it in Tamil as the Ramavataram. Kamban’s Rama is more clearly divine, and his poetry is loved across Tamil Nadu.

In the 16th century, retold it in Awadhi, a form of Hindi, as the , the lake of Rama’s deeds. It is full of bhakti, loving devotion to Rama. In North India, it is the Ramayana many families know best and recite at home.

There are versions in Bengali, Telugu, Malayalam and many other languages, and in Thailand, Indonesia and beyond. Jain and Buddhist tellings exist too. Scholars speak of many Ramayanas, each with its own emphasis.

Puranas and the Bhakti Poets

Devotion
Big questionHow did the Puranas and the bhakti poets bring Hindu devotion into everyday languages and lives?
The story

The Queen Who Sang

A royal woman leaves the palace singing, and people across India sing her songs back.

Rajasthan, in the early 1500s. Stories say a young woman named Mira was married into a royal family. Since childhood she had loved Krishna. She called him her true husband and sang to him day and night.

Her in-laws were not pleased. A royal wife was supposed to stay behind palace walls. Mira danced in temples with wandering holy people. She sang in the everyday language of the people, not in Sanskrit. Stories say her family tried to stop her, even to harm her, but she was protected.

At last, the stories say, Mira left the palace. She traveled to Vrindavan, the land of Krishna's childhood, and later to Dwarka on the western coast. Wherever she went, she sang, and crowds gathered to listen and sing with her.

Historians can confirm only some details of Mira's life. The songs, though, are real and alive. Hundreds of songs carry her name. People across North India sing them at home, at temples and in concerts today. They call her Mirabai.

Mirabai was one of many poet-saints of bhakti, loving devotion. Some were kings and queens. Others were weavers, tailors, farmers or cobblers. They sang in their own languages, and their songs traveled farther than any palace order.

The Puranas

20.1

Old Stories, Big Books

Main ideaThe Puranas are large collections of stories, cosmology and worship, composed over many centuries.

Where does the story of Prahlad come from? Or Krishna lifting a mountain? Or Durga defeating a buffalo demon? Many come from the . Purana means old, and these texts tell ancient tales.

Tradition counts eighteen major Puranas, the Mahapuranas, and many minor ones. They include creation stories, family lines of gods and kings, descriptions of the universe, and rules for worship and pilgrimage. Tradition often names Vyasa as their arranger.

Scholars date most of the Puranas to about 300–1500 CE, with much growth during and after the Gupta period. Each one often favors one deity. Some center on Vishnu, some on Shiva, some on the Goddess. They are part of smriti.

20.3

The Ten Avatars of Vishnu

Main ideaThe Puranas describe Vishnu descending as avatars, and a common list names ten.

The Gita says that whenever dharma declines, the Lord comes into the world to protect the good (Gita 4.7–8). The Puranas tell how Vishnu does this as an , meaning one who descends.

A common list names ten avatars: Matsya the fish, Kurma the tortoise, Varaha the boar, Narasimha the man-lion, Vamana the dwarf, Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, the Buddha or Balarama, and Kalki, who is yet to come.

Lists differ. The Bhagavata names more than twenty. Some lists include the Buddha, others Balarama. Vaishnavas honor these avatars as Vishnu himself, and some see Krishna as the source of all avatars.

Bhakti in the South

20.4

What Is Bhakti?

Main ideaBhakti is loving devotion to God, and it became a movement of poet-saints across India.

The Gita already praises , loving devotion. From about the 6th century CE, it became a wave of song. Poet-saints sang to Shiva, Vishnu, Krishna, Rama and the Goddess, in their own languages rather than Sanskrit.

Bhakti poets often spoke to God like a friend, a parent, a child or a beloved. They taught that love matters more than ritual rules or high birth. Many came from groups that were barred from Vedic learning.

Bhakti began in the Tamil south and spread north over centuries. It shaped temple worship, festivals and home devotion. Much Hindu practice today, like singing bhajans or kirtan, grows from this movement.

20.5

The Alvars and Nayanars

Main ideaIn Tamil lands, the Alvars sang to Vishnu and the Nayanars sang to Shiva, c. 6th–9th centuries CE.

Between about the 6th and 9th centuries CE, twelve sang to Vishnu in Tamil. Their songs were gathered into the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, the four thousand divine hymns. Sri Vaishnavas, followers of Ramanuja, honor it like a Tamil Veda.

At the same time, sixty-three sang to Shiva. Their hymns form part of the Tirumurai, a sacred collection for Tamil Shaivas. Songs by Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar are sung in Shiva temples today.

These poet-saints came from many backgrounds. Andal was a woman poet among the Alvars. Tiruppan Alvar came from a caste of singers who were kept out of temples. Karaikkal Ammaiyar was a woman among the Nayanars.

20.6

Across Caste Lines

Main ideaBhakti poets often challenged caste barriers, though caste did not disappear.

Stories of the poet-saints often show devotion crossing caste lines. Tiruppan Alvar was not allowed into the temple at Srirangam. A story says a priest carried him in on his shoulders at the god’s command.

In Maharashtra, the poet-saint Chokhamela was from a caste treated as untouchable. His songs ask why he must stand outside the temple. Ravidas, a leatherworker poet in North India, sang that God loves the humble. His songs are in the Sikh Guru Granth Sahib.

Historians note that bhakti did not end caste. Many temples still barred people for centuries. But the poet-saints gave voice to people often left out, and later reformers drew on their songs.

20.7

Mirabai

Main ideaMirabai, a 16th-century Rajput woman devoted to Krishna, is one of the best-loved bhakti poets.

lived in Rajasthan in the 1500s. Tradition says she was married into a royal family but gave her heart to Krishna. She sang in a mix of Rajasthani and Braj, languages the people spoke.

Her songs speak to Krishna as her beloved. They show longing, joy and courage. Many describe her refusal to follow rules that kept her from worship. Scholars note that many songs under her name were added by later singers.

Mirabai became a symbol. Gandhi admired her courage. Women across India have seen her as a model of following one’s calling. Her bhajans are sung today in homes, temples and concert halls, including in Illinois.

20.8

Kabir, Claimed by Many

Main ideaKabir, a 15th-century weaver poet of Varanasi, is claimed by Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs.

was a weaver in Varanasi in the 1400s. Tradition says he was raised in a Muslim family and learned from a Hindu teacher, Ramananda. He sang short, sharp verses called dohas.

Kabir mocked empty ritual in both Hindu and Muslim practice. He sang that God is found within, not in temple or mosque. He often called God Ram, but he meant a God beyond all forms and names.

Many groups claim Kabir. Hindus sing his songs. Muslims honor him. His verses are in the Sikh Guru Granth Sahib. The Kabir Panth follows him as its founder. A story says Hindus and Muslims argued over his body, and found only flowers.

Think it through

Tulsidas and Tukaram

Main ideaTulsidas in Hindi and Tukaram in Marathi carried bhakti to millions of homes.

Tulsidas (c. 1532–1623) wrote the Ramcharitmanas in Awadhi. It retells the Ramayana as a song of devotion to Rama. The Hanuman Chalisa, forty verses to Hanuman that many Hindus recite daily, is traditionally credited to him.

Both poets wrote for ordinary people. Tulsidas said he wrote for his own heart’s joy. Tukaram’s songs were so loved that a story says his notebooks, thrown into a river, returned undamaged. Their songs remain part of daily Hindu life.
