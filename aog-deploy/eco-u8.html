Markets in Action

Markets

Why does a new sneaker sell out in a minute and then show up online for double the price? Why does gas cost more in summer? Why does a store with rivals down the street keep cutting prices? Each of these is a market in action. Buyers and sellers meet, and a price comes out of their choices.

In this unit you will read small tables of prices and quantities. You will find the price where buyers and sellers agree. You will tell a shift of a curve from a slide along it. Then you will step inside a business. You will add up revenue, subtract costs and find the profit, using a school store as the example.

By the end, you will be able to explain a real price change in three steps. You will also be able to say why more sellers usually help buyers, what a monopoly is, and why governments watch for businesses that block competition.

The big question

How do buyers, sellers and competition work together to set prices and decide what gets made?

The story

The Sneaker Drop

At 10 a.m. a new sneaker goes on sale, and by noon the resale price has doubled.

Maya set three alarms for Saturday. A sports brand was releasing a new sneaker online at 10 a.m. sharp. The store price was $120 (an example price). Maya had saved her birthday money and her dog-walking pay for two months. At 9:58 she was on the website, finger ready over the button.

At 10:00 the page froze. At 10:01 it said SOLD OUT. The company had made only a few thousand pairs in each size. Many more people than that wanted them. Maya refreshed the page again and again. The button stayed gray.

By noon, the same sneakers showed up on a resale app. People who got a pair were selling them for $240 (an example price). That is double the store price. Maya's older brother said, "That's a rip-off." Her friend Jordan said, "No, that's just what people will pay."

Maya did not buy the resale pair. But she kept wondering. Why did the store charge $120 if people would pay $240? Why did the price jump in just two hours? And would it fall again next month? This chapter gives you the tools to answer her questions.

Section 1

What Buyers Want

15.1

The Law of Demand

Main ideaWhen the price of a good goes up, people buy less of it; when the price goes down, they buy more.

Picture a lemonade stand at a summer park. At $3 a cup, the stand sells 100 cups on a hot day. When the owner drops the price to $2, it sells 150 cups. At $1, it sells 220 cups. These are example numbers. The pattern is what matters: a lower price, more cups.

Economists call this the . When the price of a good rises, the amount people want to buy falls. When the price falls, the amount people want to buy rises. The amount people are willing and able to buy at one price is the .

Why does this happen? At a higher price, some buyers decide the lemonade is not worth it. Others buy one cup instead of two. Some switch to a water fountain. At a lower price, new buyers join in. Notice the key words: willing AND able. Wanting a thing is not enough. You must also have the money to buy it.

15.2

Reading a Demand Schedule

Main ideaA demand schedule is a table that shows how many units buyers want at each price.

Economists often put demand in a small table. The table is called a . It has two columns. One column lists prices. The other lists the quantity demanded at each price. Here is an example for a school’s frozen yogurt cups: at $4, 40 cups; at $3, 70 cups; at $2, 100 cups; at $1, 140 cups.

Read it from top to bottom. As the price falls by $1 each step, the quantity rises. From $4 to $3, quantity goes up by 30 cups. From $2 to $1, it goes up by 40 cups. Every row tells the same story as the law of demand.

You can also turn the table into a graph. Put price on the side (the vertical axis). Put quantity along the bottom (the horizontal axis). Mark a dot for each row and connect the dots. The line slopes down from left to right. That line is the . High prices sit at the top left with small quantities. Low prices sit at the bottom right with big quantities.

15.3

What Shifts Demand

Main ideaA change in income, tastes, related goods or the number of buyers moves the whole demand curve.

Sometimes buyers want more at EVERY price. Say a famous athlete wears a certain backpack in a big game. Before the game, 500 backpacks sold each week at $50 (an example). After the game, 800 sell at that same $50. The price did not change. Something else did. When buyers want more at every price, the demand curve moves to the right. That is an .

Four main things shift demand. Income: when families earn more, they often buy more of many goods. Tastes: a trend or ad can make a good more popular. Related goods: a is a good used in place of another, like a scooter for a bike. If scooters get cheaper, demand for bikes falls. The number of buyers: a new neighborhood brings more customers.

A drop in demand moves the curve to the left. If a news story says a snack is unhealthy, fewer people want it at every price. Buyers’ expectations matter too. If people expect a price to rise next week, they may buy more today.

15.4

Shift or Slide?

Main ideaA change in the good's own price slides along the curve; a change in anything else shifts the curve.

This is the most common mistake in all of economics. Look closely at two events. Event one: a store cuts the price of hot chocolate from $3 to $2, and sales rise from 50 to 80 cups a day (example numbers). Event two: a cold snap hits, and at the same $3 price sales rise from 50 to 90 cups. Both show more cups sold. But they are different.

In event one, only the price changed. Buyers moved to a new point on the SAME demand curve. Economists call this a . It is a slide along the curve. In event two, the price stayed at $3. The cold weather made buyers want more at every price. That is a . The whole curve shifted right.

Here is a simple test. Ask: did the price of this good change? If yes, it is a slide along the curve. If something else changed, like weather, income or a trend, it is a shift. Never say a price change "shifts demand." A price change never moves the demand curve for that good.

Section 2

What Sellers Offer

15.5

The Law of Supply

Main ideaWhen the price of a good goes up, sellers offer more of it; when the price goes down, they offer less.

Now switch sides. Imagine you bake cookies to sell at school. If cookies sell for $0.50 each (an example), you might bake 20. It is not worth much of your time. If they sell for $2 each, you might bake 100. You would give up a movie night to bake. A higher price makes the work worth it.

This is the . When the price of a good rises, sellers offer more of it. When the price falls, they offer less. The amount sellers are willing and able to sell at one price is the .

Why? A higher price means more money for each unit. It can cover the extra costs of making more, like more flour, more hours and more oven time. It also pulls new sellers in. If cookies sold for $2, your friends might start baking too. A low price does the opposite. Some sellers decide it is not worth it and stop.

15.6

A Supply Schedule

Main ideaA supply schedule is a table that shows how many units sellers offer at each price.

Supply fits in a table too. It is called a . Here is an example for a pottery club that sells mugs at a craft fair: at $4, 20 mugs; at $6, 35 mugs; at $8, 50 mugs; at $10, 60 mugs. As the price rises, the club is willing to make more mugs.

Read the change row by row. From $4 to $6, the club offers 15 more mugs. From $8 to $10, it offers only 10 more. Making extra mugs gets harder as the club runs short of clay, kiln space and members’ time. Costs rise, so each price jump brings a smaller increase.

Put the table on a graph with price on the side and quantity on the bottom. The dots rise from left to right. The line through them is the . It slopes the opposite way from the demand curve. That difference matters, because the two curves will cross.

15.7

What Shifts Supply

Main ideaA change in costs, technology or the number of sellers moves the whole supply curve.

Supply can shift too. Say a pizza shop makes 300 pizzas a week at $12 each (an example). Then the price of cheese doubles. Each pizza now costs more to make. At the same $12 price, the shop makes only 220 pizzas. When sellers offer less at every price, the supply curve moves left. That is a .

Three main things shift supply. : the prices of things used to make a good, like cheese, wages or rent. Higher costs shift supply left. Technology: a faster oven lets the shop make more pizzas for the same cost, so supply shifts right. The number of sellers: when new shops open, supply shifts right.

Nature and events matter too. A drought can shrink a corn crop in Illinois, shifting corn supply left. Remember the same rule as before. A change in the good’s OWN price is a slide along the supply curve. Only other causes shift the whole curve.

Section 3

Where Buyers and Sellers Meet

15.8

Finding Equilibrium

Main ideaThe market price settles where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied.

Put buyers and sellers together. Here is an example market for school spirit T-shirts. At $5: buyers want 90, sellers offer 30. At $10: buyers want 60, sellers offer 60. At $15: buyers want 40, sellers offer 80. Look for the row where the two numbers match. At $10, both sides agree on 60 shirts.

That point is . The price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied is the . Here it is $10. The amount bought and sold there, 60 shirts, is the equilibrium quantity. On a graph, it is the spot where the demand curve and the supply curve cross.

No one sets this price by a rule. Buyers and sellers find it by trading. If the price is too high, sellers are stuck with extra shirts, so they cut the price. If it is too low, shirts sell out and sellers raise the price. Prices keep adjusting until the two sides balance.

15.9

Surplus and Shortage

Main ideaA price above equilibrium makes a surplus; a price below equilibrium makes a shortage.

Go back to the spirit shirts. Suppose the club charges $15. Buyers want 40, but the club has 80. That leaves 80 minus 40, or 40 extra shirts. When quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded, there is a . The fix is a sale. Lower prices draw more buyers and cut how much sellers want to offer.

Now suppose the club charges only $5. Buyers want 90, but the club offers just 30. That leaves 90 minus 30, or 60 shirts missing. When quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied, there is a . Lines form and items sell out. Sellers can raise the price, and some buyers drop out.

Both gaps push the price toward equilibrium. A surplus pushes the price down. A shortage pushes the price up. This is Maya’s sneaker story. The store price of $120 was below what the market would pay. The shortage sent the resale price up.

15.10

Real Prices, Explained

Main ideaTo explain a price change, ask which curve shifted, which way, and what that does to the price.

Now you can explain real price changes. Use three steps. First, ask: did demand or supply shift? Second, which way? Third, trace the result. When demand shifts right, price and quantity both rise. When supply shifts left, price rises and quantity falls.

Try gas prices. Gas often costs more in summer, because more families take road trips. That is an increase in demand. If a hurricane shuts down refineries on the Gulf Coast, less gas gets made. That is a decrease in supply. Either shift pushes the price up. When both happen together, prices can jump fast.

Now the video game at launch. On day one, many fans want the game. Demand is high. A year later, most fans already own it, and demand falls. The price often drops. A price change is not magic or greed alone. It is buyers and sellers reacting to a change.

Section 1

Money In and Money Out

16.1

Revenue and Costs

Main ideaRevenue is the money a business takes in; costs are the money it spends to run.

Start with the school store’s cash box. Every sale adds money. Say the store sells 150 bags of chips at $1 each and 40 bottles of water at $1.50 each (example prices). Chips bring in 150 times $1, or $150. Water brings in 40 times $1.50, or $60. Together that is $210.

The money a business takes in from sales is its . To find it, multiply the price by the number sold, then add up every product. Revenue is NOT the same as the money the business gets to keep. That is a common mistake, and it was Devin’s mistake in the story.

A business also pays . Costs are the money spent to make and sell its goods. The store pays for the chips it buys to resell, the rent for the room and the paint for the sign. Some costs stay the same no matter how much you sell, like rent. These are . Others grow with each sale, like the chips themselves. These are variable costs.

16.2

Profit and Loss

Main ideaProfit is revenue minus costs; if costs are bigger than revenue, the business has a loss.

Now use Priya’s receipts. The store brought in $900. Its costs were $300 for snacks, $450 for hoodies, $50 for rent and $25 for the sign. Add the costs: 300 plus 450 plus 50 plus 25 equals $825. Then subtract: $900 minus $825 equals $75.

That $75 is the store’s . Profit is what is left after a business pays all its costs. The rule is short: profit equals revenue minus costs. If the costs had been $950, the store would have a of $50. A loss means the business spent more than it took in.

Profit sends a signal. A big profit tells other sellers that people want this product, so more may enter the market. A loss tells a business to change something: cut costs, change prices or sell something else. Profit is also the reward owners get for taking a risk with their money and time.

16.3

Pricing for Profit

Main ideaA higher price does not always mean more revenue, because buyers purchase fewer units.

Should the store just raise prices? Try it with example numbers. At $1 a bag, the store sells 150 bags of chips, so revenue is $150. At $2 a bag, students buy only 60 bags, so revenue is 60 times $2, or $120. The higher price brought in LESS money. Many students bought cookies or brought chips from home.

Remember the law of demand. When the price goes up, the quantity demanded goes down. A business must think about both parts of revenue: price and quantity. Raising the price helps only if sales do not fall too much.

How much buyers react depends on the good. If there are many substitutes, buyers switch fast. If there are few substitutes, like water on a hot field day, buyers react less. Businesses test prices, watch what sells and adjust. That is why stores run sales and try new prices.

Section 2

Who Owns a Business

16.4

One Owner or Partners

Main ideaA sole proprietorship has one owner; a partnership has two or more who share the business.

Think of a neighbor who runs a lawn-mowing business alone. She owns it all. She makes every choice and keeps every dollar of profit. This is a , a business owned by one person. It is the most common kind of business in the United States, and it is easy to start.

But there is a big risk. If the business owes money it cannot pay, the owner may have to pay it from her own savings. This is called unlimited liability. Liability means legal responsibility for debts. Her car or savings could be at risk if the business fails.

Now picture two friends who open a bike repair shop. They each put in money and share the work and the profit. This is a , a business owned by two or more people. Partners can pool money and skills. But they must agree on choices, and in a general partnership each partner can be responsible for the business’s debts.

Think it through
Going It Alone?

Jaden bakes great cakes and wants to sell them. His cousin Rosa is good with numbers and has $2,000 saved (an example amount). She offers to be his partner and split the profit half and half.

What would Jaden gain from a partnership? What would he give up? Would you take the deal?

Words to know
sole proprietorship
a business owned and run by one person, who keeps the profits and is responsible for the debts
partnership
a business owned by two or more people who share the work, profits and debts
liability
legal responsibility for a business's debts
Check yourself

1. A dog-walker runs her business alone and keeps all the profit. What kind of business is it?

Why: A business owned by one person is a sole proprietorship.

2. What is one big risk of a sole proprietorship?

Why: Sole proprietors have unlimited liability, so personal money can be used to pay business debts.

3. Why might two people form a partnership?

Why: Partners pool money, skills and work. They still share risk and profit.
16.5

Corporations

Main ideaA corporation is a business that is its own legal unit, owned by shareholders who risk only what they invested.

Big companies that make phones, cars or cereal are usually corporations. A is a business that the law treats as separate from its owners. It can own property, sign contracts and borrow money in its own name.

The owners of a corporation are its . They own small pieces of the company called shares, or stock. Say a company has 1,000 shares and you buy 10 (an example). You own 10 divided by 1,000, or 1% of the company. If it earns a profit, it may pay part of it to shareholders.

The big advantage is limited liability. If the corporation fails, shareholders can lose the money they put in, but not their homes or savings. Selling shares also raises large amounts of money. The trade-offs: corporations cost more to set up, must follow more rules and file more reports, and owners may have little say in daily choices.

Read the numbers

What percent of the businesses on this example street are sole proprietorships? Why might small shops choose that type?

Words to know
corporation
a business the law treats as separate from its owners, able to own property and borrow in its own name
shareholder
a person who owns shares, or stock, in a corporation
limited liability
owners can lose only the money they invested, not their personal property
Check yourself

1. A company has 2,000 shares. You own 20 (example numbers). What percent of the company do you own?

Why: 20 divided by 2,000 equals 0.01, which is 1%.

2. What is the main advantage of limited liability for shareholders?

Why: Limited liability protects shareholders' personal property if the business fails.

3. Which is a trade-off of forming a corporation?

Why: Corporations gain limited liability and easier fundraising, but they face higher setup costs and more rules.
Section 3

Competition and Monopoly

16.6

Why More Sellers Help Buyers

Main ideaCompetition among many sellers usually pushes prices down and quality up.

Back to the story. The eighth graders down the hall open a second store. Right away, things change. The first store sold water for $1.50 (an example price). The new store sells it for $1.25. Students walk down the hall to save a quarter. Soon the first store cuts its price to $1.25 too.

This is : sellers trying to win the same buyers. When buyers can choose, each seller has a reason to offer lower prices, better products or faster service. A store that charges too much or treats customers poorly loses sales to its rival.

Competition also pushes new ideas. The first store starts selling fruit cups, which the other store does not have. Competition is not always comfortable for sellers. Some lose money and close. But for buyers, more sellers usually mean lower prices and more choices.

From the source
Adam Smith on Self-Interest
It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.
Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776, Book I, Chapter 2

Benevolence means kindness. In your own words, why does the baker sell you bread? How does competition turn a seller's self-interest into good deals for buyers?

Words to know
competition
the struggle among sellers to attract buyers by offering better prices, products or service
rival
another business that tries to sell to the same buyers
Check yourself

1. A town has one pizza shop. Three more open nearby. What will most likely happen to pizza prices?

Why: More sellers compete for the same buyers, which usually pushes prices down.

2. Which is a way businesses compete besides price?

Why: Sellers can win buyers with quality, new products or better service, not only lower prices.

3. What did Adam Smith mean about the butcher and baker?

Why: Smith argued that sellers serve buyers because it helps themselves, and trade benefits both sides.
16.7

When There Is Only One Seller

Main ideaA monopoly is a market with only one seller, which can charge more and has less reason to improve.

Now imagine the school allows only ONE store. There is no other place to buy water at lunch. The store raises water from $1.25 to $2 (example prices). Some students skip water, but many still buy it. They have no other choice nearby.

A market with only one seller of a good that has no close substitutes is a . A monopoly can often charge higher prices and sell less than a competitive market would. With no rival, it also has less reason to improve quality or service.

Some monopolies happen for good reasons. A company may hold a , a right from the government to be the only seller of a new invention for a set time. That reward encourages people to invent. A town often has one water system, because building two sets of pipes would waste money. Governments often set rules on the prices such services can charge.

Think it through
The Only Gas Station

A small town far from any highway has one gas station. The nearest other station is 40 miles away. The owner charges 50 cents a gallon more than stations in the city (an example).

Why can this owner charge more? What might happen if a second station opened in town?

Words to know
monopoly
a market with only one seller of a good that has no close substitutes
patent
a government grant that gives an inventor the only right to make and sell an invention for a set time
Check yourself

1. Which is the best example of a monopoly?

Why: A monopoly has only one seller with no close substitutes, like a single town water service.

2. Compared with a competitive market, a monopoly usually has:

Why: With no rivals, a monopoly can charge more and faces less pressure to improve.

3. Why does the government give patents, which create short-term monopolies?

Why: A patent lets an inventor be the only seller for a set time, which rewards the work of inventing.
16.8

Keeping Markets Fair

Main ideaGovernments use antitrust laws to stop businesses from blocking competition.

Sometimes businesses try to escape competition on purpose. Say the two school stores secretly agree to both charge $2 for water instead of $1.25 (example prices). Neither one loses customers, and students pay more. An agreement like this among rivals to fix prices hurts buyers.

Adam Smith warned about this back in 1776. The United States passed the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890. are laws that ban price-fixing and other ways of blocking competition. The government can also study a merger. A is when two companies join into one. If it would leave buyers with too few choices, the government can try to stop it in court.

Economists debate how much to step in. Some say big firms can be efficient and lower costs, so the government should act only when buyers are clearly harmed. Others say giant firms can crush new rivals and should be watched more closely. Both sides agree that price-fixing among rivals hurts buyers.

From the source
Adam Smith's Warning
People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices.
Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776, Book I, Chapter 10

A conspiracy is a secret plan; a contrivance is a trick. What was Smith worried sellers might do when they meet? Which law today tries to stop it?

Words to know
antitrust laws
laws that ban price-fixing and other actions that block competition
merger
when two companies join together to form one company
price-fixing
a secret agreement among rival sellers to charge the same high price
Check yourself

1. Two rival airlines secretly agree to raise fares on the same day. What is this called?

Why: Rivals agreeing on prices is price-fixing, which antitrust laws ban.

2. In what year did the United States pass the Sherman Antitrust Act?

Why: The Sherman Antitrust Act became law in 1890.

3. Why might the government try to block a merger of the two biggest companies in a market?

Why: Antitrust officials act when a merger would cut competition and hurt buyers.
Section 4

Starting and Staffing a Business

16.9

Entrepreneurs Take the Risk

Main ideaAn entrepreneur starts a business, takes on risk and earns profit as the reward if it works.

Every business starts with someone willing to try. An is a person who starts a business and takes on the risk. Say a teen named Leo spends $200 of his savings on a used snow blower (an example). If it snows a lot, he earns $600 clearing driveways. If it barely snows, he might earn only $100 and lose money.

That chance of losing is . The possible profit is the reward. Leo’s profit in the snowy winter would be $600 minus $200, or $400. In the dry winter, $100 minus $200 is a loss of $100. Entrepreneurs start businesses because they believe the reward is worth the risk.

Starting a business also has an opportunity cost. Leo could have kept his $200 or spent his time on something else. Many new businesses fail. But entrepreneurs who succeed bring new products and jobs. They spot a need, like shoveled driveways on a busy street, and fill it.

Think it through
The Phone Case Idea

Aisha wants to design and sell phone cases online. She needs $300 for supplies (an example). She could instead use that $300 for a summer camp she really wants.

What is Aisha's risk? What is her opportunity cost? What would help her decide?

Words to know
entrepreneur
a person who starts a new business and takes on the risk of losing money
risk
the chance of losing money or something of value
opportunity cost
the next-best thing you give up when you make a choice
Check yourself

1. Leo spends $200 on a snow blower and earns $600. What is his profit?

Why: Profit equals revenue minus costs: $600 minus $200 equals $400.

2. What is the reward an entrepreneur hopes for in exchange for risk?

Why: Profit is the reward for taking on the risk of starting a business.

3. Aisha spends her $300 on phone case supplies instead of camp. What is her opportunity cost?

Why: Opportunity cost is the next-best choice given up, which is the camp.
16.10

A Plan for the School Store

Main ideaA business plan lists what you will sell, to whom, at what price, and how you expect to earn a profit.

Before month two, Ms. Ortiz’s class writes a . A business plan is a written guide to how a business will work and make money. Theirs answers five questions. What will we sell? Who will buy it? What will we charge? What will it cost? How much profit do we expect?

The class uses its month-one numbers. They expect to sell 300 snacks at $1.25 each, or $375 (example numbers). The snacks cost $0.60 each, so 300 cost $180. Rent stays $50. Expected profit on snacks: $375 minus $180 minus $50 equals $145. They decide to order hoodies only after taking pre-orders, so none are left over.

A plan also names the competition, in this case the other store, and how to stand out. The class plans to sell fruit cups and open five minutes earlier. A plan does not guarantee success. But it makes owners check their numbers before they spend real money.

a written guide that explains what a business will sell, to whom, at what price, and how it expects to earn a profit

the total money a business takes in from sales; price times quantity sold
Check yourself

1. In the example plan, what would the expected profit be if rent rose from $50 to $80?

Why: $375 minus $180 minus $80 equals $115.

2. Why did the class decide to take pre-orders for hoodies?

Why: Pre-orders match the number made to the number wanted, so the store avoids unsold stock.

3. Which question does a business plan NOT need to answer?

16.11

Workers, Wages and Unions

Main ideaWages are set mostly by the supply of and demand for workers; unions let workers bargain as a group.

The school store needs cashiers. Workers are part of a market too. A is the payment a worker earns for work, often counted per hour. Say a shop pays $15 an hour for 20 hours a week (an example). The worker earns 15 times 20, or $300 a week before taxes.

Wages follow supply and demand. When many people can do a job and few businesses need it, wages tend to be lower. When a job needs rare skills, like a nurse or electrician, and many employers need them, wages tend to be higher. Training and education often raise what a worker can earn.

A is a group of workers who join together to bargain with an employer about wages, hours and safety. Bargaining as a group is called collective bargaining. Supporters say unions give workers a stronger voice and better pay. Critics say they can raise costs, which may lead firms to hire fewer workers. Economists study and debate both effects, including the effects of the federal minimum wage, which has been $7.25 an hour since 2009.

Think it through
Two Job Offers

Carlos gets two summer job offers (example wages). A grocery store pays $14 an hour for 25 hours a week. A lifeguard job pays $17 an hour for 20 hours, but he must first take a certification class.

Which job pays more per week? Why might the lifeguard job pay a higher hourly wage?

Words to know
wage
the payment a worker earns for work, often counted per hour
labor union
a group of workers who join together to bargain with employers about pay, hours and working conditions
collective bargaining
talks between a union and an employer to agree on wages and working conditions
Check yourself

1. A worker earns $16 an hour and works 25 hours (example wage). What does she earn before taxes?

Why: 16 times 25 equals $400.

2. Why do jobs that need rare skills usually pay higher wages?

Why: A small supply of skilled workers and a high demand for them push wages up.

3. What is one argument critics make about unions?

Why: Critics argue that union-won wage gains raise costs, which can reduce hiring. Supporters stress better pay and voice.
