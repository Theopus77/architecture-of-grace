Renewable is about the rate

A forest regrows a certain amount each year. That rate is the only income this resource has.

“Renewable” is not a property of the resource — it is a statement about the RATE you use it at. A forest cut faster than it regrows is being mined, and groundwater pumped faster than it recharges is too.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

Ozone is a pollutant, so more ozone is always worse.

Same molecule, opposite verdicts depending on altitude. At ground level it damages lungs and crops. In the stratosphere it blocks UV and we need it. Location decides, not the formula.

Bad at the ground, good up high.

⚠️ People often think…

Population growth is the main driver of environmental impact today.

Global growth is now under 1% a year and still slowing. The lever that has kept climbing is CONSUMPTION per person — which is also the one policy can actually reach.

Consumption per person is the moving number.

⚠️ People often think…

Solar and wind are still too expensive to matter.

Costs fell far enough that in most places they are now the cheapest new generation to build. Their real problem is timing — the sun sets and the wind stops — which is a storage and grid question, not a price one.

The problem is the clock, not the price.

⚠️ People often think…

Plastic’s environmental problem is its carbon footprint.

Its defining problem is PERSISTENCE — it does not break down for centuries. A life-cycle analysis has to count the right impact for the right material, or you end up swapping one product for another that is worse in the way that actually matters.

Count the right impact for the material.

Withdrawals, footprints, fixes, rules

1 Watch one

Fertilizer from a thousand farms pollutes a river. Why is that harder to fix than a factory pipe?

  1. A factory pipe is a POINT source — one place, one owner, one permit.
  2. A thousand fields are a NONPOINT source — no single outlet to measure or cap.
  3. You cannot put a meter on rain running off a field.
  4. So the tool changes: point sources you regulate, nonpoint sources you persuade and subsidize. ✓

Helpful Hints

🪜 Environmental science — the ladder

Every resource is a withdrawal from one of Earth’s four spheres → renewable if it regrows faster than we take it; nonrenewable if it doesn’t → impact = population × consumption per person × technology, and population growth is slowing while consumption rises → fossil fuels are dense and cheap and leave carbon; solar and wind are cheap and clean and intermittent → air, water, soil and species each have measured damage and measured fixes → the tragedy of the commons is why shared resources need rules → the rule works when the tool matches the source, and a finding is judged by checking its evidence, mechanism and conclusion one at a time.

Withdrawals, footprints, fixes, rules — and the data behind each.

🤝 The whole idea

This subject is where the debates live, so the habit that matters most is asking for the number: how much, compared with what, measured how. Every position in this unit comes with one.

Ask for the number.

⚠️ Tricky ones

  • “Renewable” is about the RATE. A forest cut faster than it regrows is being mined.
  • Population is not the main lever anymore — growth is under 1% and slowing. CONSUMPTION per person is.
  • Solar and wind are now CHEAP. Their problem is the clock and the calendar, not the price.
  • Ozone: bad at the ground, good in the stratosphere. Same molecule.
  • A point source you regulate; a nonpoint source you persuade. The tool follows the geometry.
  • “Rules never work” is refuted by three measured cases: 78% (criteria pollutants), 90% (sulfur), a closing ozone hole.
  • Life-cycle: count mining to disposal, and count the RIGHT impact — plastic’s problem is persistence, not carbon.

🎯 How the test will ask

  • A graph of population and a resource over decades — “describe the trend and what it implies.”
  • “Classify these sources as renewable or nonrenewable and justify the borderline ones.”
  • “Explain the tragedy of the commons with an example, and propose a fix.”
  • A short policy text — “identify the claim, the evidence and the mechanism.”
  • “Compare two energy sources on carbon, reliability and one other impact.”
  • “Evaluate a proposed regulation: which tool, which source, what would you measure to know it worked?”
