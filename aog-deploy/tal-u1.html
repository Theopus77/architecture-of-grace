A Very Big Book of Questions

The Book

The Talmud is a very big set of Jewish books. It fills a whole shelf. It is full of teachers asking questions and answering each other. In this unit, we find out what is inside it.

We start with a shelf of tall brown books. Then we look at the Torah, the scroll with the rules. One short rule, rest on the seventh day, leads to a very big question: what is work?

By the end, you will know what the Talmud is. You will know what a mitzvah is. And you will see how asking a good question is a way of learning.

The big question

Why is the Talmud so big, and why is it full of questions?

The story

The Longest Shelf in the Library

Sam looks for one book and finds a whole shelf with the same name.

Sam went to the big library with Grandma. Sam wanted a book about Jewish teachers. The librarian smiled. "Come with me," she said. She walked to a long, low shelf by the window.

Every book on the shelf was tall and brown. Every book had the same word on its back: Talmud. Sam started to count. One, two, three, four. Sam kept walking and kept counting.

At twenty, Sam stopped. The shelf was still going! "Is this all one book?" Sam asked. "It is one big set," said the librarian. "It has many parts. People read it a little at a time."

Grandma opened one book. The page had words in the middle. It had more words all around the edge. "Who wrote all this?" Sam asked. "Many teachers," Grandma said. "They asked a lot of questions."

Talk about itWhy do you think one set of books needs so many parts?
Section 1

A Shelf of Books

1.1

Many Books, One Name

Main ideaThe Talmud is a very big set of Jewish books, not just one book.

The is a set of Jewish books. It is very big. In many libraries it fills about twenty tall books. All of them share one name.

The Talmud is old. Historians say it was finished about 1,500 years ago. It was written in two languages, Hebrew and Aramaic.

The Talmud is split into parts. Each part is about one topic. One part is about prayers. One part is about holidays. One part is about lost things.

3. How is the Talmud split up?

1.2

Teachers Called Rabbis

Main ideaThe Talmud is full of Jewish teachers, called rabbis, talking to each other.

A is a Jewish teacher. The Talmud is full of rabbis. They lived a long time ago. They lived in the land of Israel and in Babylonia.

The rabbis had many names. Rabbi Akiva. Rabbi Meir. Rav. Abaye. Rava. The Talmud tells what each one said. It tells when they agreed. It tells when they did not.

Jewish tradition says the rabbis passed on teaching from teacher to student. One rabbi learned from another. Then he taught the next one. The chain went on and on.

From the source
A Chain of Teachers
Pirkei Avot begins with a chain. It says Moses received the Torah at Sinai and passed it to Joshua. Joshua passed it to the elders. The elders passed it to the prophets. The prophets passed it to a group called the Men of the Great Assembly.
Mishnah Avot 1:1, retold from Charles Taylor, Sayings of the Jewish Fathers (1877) (paraphrased)

Who is first in the chain? Who comes next?

Words to know

Check yourself

2. Which name belongs to a rabbi in the Talmud?

3. How did Jewish tradition say teaching was passed on?

Section 2

Questions and Answers

1.3

Asking Out Loud

Main ideaIn the Talmud, rabbis ask questions and answer each other.

The Talmud is full of . One rabbi asks. Another rabbi answers. Then a third rabbi asks a new question. It goes on like a long talk.

The rabbis did not always agree. The Talmud writes down both sides. It keeps the first answer. It keeps the second answer too. Readers can see both.

The very first page asks a question. It asks: when does the evening prayer time begin? Rabbis give different answers. That is how the Talmud works.

From the source
The First Question
The Talmud starts in a book called Berakhot, which means Blessings. The first words ask a question: from what time may a person say the evening Shema prayer? Then the rabbis give answers.
Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 2a, retold from Michael L. Rodkinson's translation (1896) (paraphrased)

Why might a book start with a question instead of an answer?

Words to know
question
something you ask because you want to know; the Talmud is full of them
Check yourself

1. What does the Talmud do when rabbis do not agree?

Why: The Talmud keeps both answers so readers can see both sides.

2. What does Berakhot mean?

Why: Berakhot means Blessings. It is the first book of the Talmud.

3. How does the Talmud begin?

Why: The first page of Berakhot asks a question about the evening prayer.
1.4

Words in the Middle, Words Around

Main ideaA Talmud page has old words in the middle and helpers' words all around.

Look at a page of the Talmud. The middle has the oldest words. This is the and the Gemara. The Mishnah is short. The Gemara talks about it.

Around the middle are more words. These were written later by helpers. One helper was named Rashi. He lived in France about 950 years ago. He explained hard words.

So one page holds many voices. Old rabbis in the middle. Later helpers around the edge. A reader can go back and forth between them.

Think it through
A Page in Words

Picture a big page. In the middle is a block of words. On the inside edge is a thin column in small letters. On the outside edge is another thin column. The middle block is the Mishnah and Gemara. The thin columns are helpers like Rashi.

If you wanted the oldest words, where on the page would you look?

Words to know

Check yourself

Why: The Mishnah and Gemara sit in the middle. They are the oldest words.

2. Who was Rashi?

Why: Rashi lived in France and wrote explanations that sit around the edge of the page.

3. What is the Mishnah?

Why: The Mishnah is the short, old teaching. The Gemara talks about it.
Section 3

Studying Together

1.5

Two by Two

Main ideaPeople study the Talmud in pairs and talk out loud.

Many people study the Talmud with a partner. The partner is called a . Two people sit with one page. They read it together.

They do not read quietly. They talk out loud. One reads a line. The other asks, "What does that mean?" They try to work it out.

A study room can be loud. Many pairs talk at once. That is not rude. That is how the Talmud has been studied for a very long time.

From the source
Get a Friend to Learn With
In Pirkei Avot, a teacher named Joshua ben Perachyah gives advice. He says: find yourself a teacher, and get yourself a friend to learn with.
Mishnah Avot 1:6, retold from Charles Taylor, Sayings of the Jewish Fathers (1877) (paraphrased)

Why might learning with a friend help more than learning alone?

Words to know
chavruta
a study partner; two people who read one page together and talk it out
Check yourself

2. How do study partners read the Talmud?

3. Why is a Talmud study room often loud?

Why: Many pairs talk out loud at the same time. That is the normal way to study.
1.6

Who Reads It?

Main ideaJewish people study the Talmud, and anyone can learn about it.

Jewish people have studied the Talmud for hundreds of years. Some study it in a school. Some study it at home. Some read one page every day.

Reading one , or page, a day is called Daf Yomi. The Babylonian Talmud has about 2,700 pages. So one page a day takes more than seven years!

You do not have to be Jewish to learn about it. Anyone can. In this course we learn what the book says. We learn how people read it.

1.7

How We Talk About It

Main ideaWe say "the rabbis said" and "Jewish tradition" to tell whose words they are.

In this class we talk about the Talmud carefully. We say, "The rabbis said." We say, "Jewish holds." This tells whose idea it is.

Jewish people read the Talmud in many ways. Some read every word as a rule. Some read it for its stories. Some study it for history. All of these are real ways.

We do not say who is right. We learn what the book says. We learn how people read it. That is what study means.

Think it through
Whose Words?

Here are two ways to say one thing. Way one: "Rest on the seventh day." Way two: "The rabbis said to rest on the seventh day." The second way tells you who said it.

Which way tells you whose idea it is? Try saying something your teacher said the second way.

The Torah and Its Rules

Torah and Mitzvah
Big questionWhy does a rule from the Torah need so many questions?
The story

Is Coloring Work?

One short rule from the Torah starts a big argument in Room 12.

Ms. Ortiz read a rule to the class. "The Torah says: rest on the seventh day. Do no work." She put down the book. "What counts as work?" she asked.

Hands went up. "Digging is work," said Leo. "Cooking is work," said Priya. Then Mia asked, "Is coloring work?" The room went quiet. Then everyone talked at once.

"Coloring is fun, so it is not work," said Leo. "But you make something new," said Priya. "Making things is work." Mia was not sure. "What if I color for school?" she asked.

Ms. Ortiz smiled. "Long ago, rabbis asked this same kind of question. They filled many books with their answers. That set of books is the Talmud."

Talk about itDo you think coloring is work? What would make it work or not work?
Section 1

A Scroll Called the Torah

2.1

Five Books on a Scroll

Main ideaThe Torah is the first five books of the Bible, kept as a long scroll.

The is the first five books of the Bible. The first book is Genesis. The last book is Deuteronomy. Together they are one long story with many rules.

In a synagogue, the Torah is kept as a scroll. A scroll is a long roll of paper or animal skin. It has two wooden rods. You roll it to find your place.

Every week, Jewish people read a part of the Torah out loud. By the end of the year, they finish all five books. Then they start again.

Think it through
A Scroll in Words

Picture a long roll of tan skin. It is wrapped around two wooden rods. The writing is in Hebrew, in neat black columns. To read the next part, you roll one rod and unroll the other. The scroll is heavy. Two people carry it.

How is a scroll different from the books in your classroom?

Check yourself

1. What is the Torah?

3. How often do Jewish people finish reading the whole Torah?

2.2

Moses on the Mountain

Main ideaJewish tradition says Moses received the Torah at Mount Sinai.

Jewish tradition says the Torah came from God. It says Moses received it at . Mount Sinai is a mountain in a desert.

The Torah tells the story. The people camp at the foot of the mountain. Moses goes up. He comes down with rules for the people to keep.

Different people read this story in different ways. Some read it as history. Some read it as a holy story. In this class, we say what the Torah tells and what tradition holds.

From the source

A Rule Is a Mitzvah

2.3

What a Mitzvah Is

Main ideaA rule in the Torah is called a mitzvah.

A rule in the Torah is called a . More than one are called mitzvot. Some mitzvot say, "Do this." Some say, "Do not do this."

Here are some mitzvot. Honor your father and mother. Do not steal. Leave some of your crops for poor people. Rest on the seventh day.

Rabbinic tradition counts 613 mitzvot in the Torah. That is a lot of rules! Many of them are about being fair and kind to other people.

2.4

Shabbat, the Seventh Day

Main ideaShabbat is a day of rest that comes every week on the seventh day.

is the seventh day of the week. The Torah says to work six days and rest on the seventh. Shabbat starts Friday at sunset. It ends Saturday night.

Many Jewish families light candles on Friday evening. They eat a special meal. They sing. They do not go to work or school. It is a day to rest and be together.

The Torah says everyone rests. Parents rest. Children rest. Helpers rest. Even the animals rest. Rabbinic tradition says Shabbat is a gift.

Section 3

What Counts as Work?

2.5

A Rule Needs Explaining

Main ideaA short rule like "do no work" makes people ask what work means.

The Torah says, "Do no work on Shabbat." That sounds simple. But what is work? Is carrying a bag work? Is writing work? Is lighting a lamp work?

The Torah does not give a list. So people asked. The gave an answer. It listed thirty-nine kinds of work. Planting is one. Cooking is one. Writing is one.

A rule can be short. Living by it takes more words. That is why a short rule can lead to a very long book.

From the source
Forty Less One
The Mishnah lists the kinds of work not done on Shabbat. It calls them forty less one. The list starts with planting and plowing. It has baking, weaving, tying a knot, and writing two letters. It ends with carrying something from one place to another.
Mishnah Shabbat 7:2, retold in simple words (paraphrased)

Pick one kind of work from the list. Why might the rabbis have called it work?

Words to know
Mishnah
the short, old teaching that lists rules, like the thirty-nine kinds of work
Check yourself

1. Why did people ask what work means?

Why: The Torah says do no work but does not list what work is, so people asked.

2. How many kinds of work does the Mishnah list?

Why: The Mishnah lists thirty-nine kinds of work, called forty less one.

3. Which of these is on the Mishnah's list?

Why: Writing two letters is one of the thirty-nine kinds of work in Mishnah Shabbat 7:2.
2.6

The Rabbis Worked It Out

Main ideaThe Talmud is where the rabbis asked and answered questions about the rules.

The Mishnah gave a list. But the list made new questions. What if you carry something small? What if you write one letter, not two? The rabbis asked all of this.

Their questions and answers were written down. That is the . The Mishnah and the Gemara together are the Talmud. Rule, question, answer, new question.

The rabbis cared about people. They asked: what if someone is sick on Shabbat? They said saving a life comes first. A rule about rest should not hurt anyone.

From the source
A Life Comes First
The Talmud teaches that if someone's life is in danger on Shabbat, you do what is needed to save them. The rabbis say Shabbat was given to people, not people to Shabbat. Saving a life sets the Shabbat rules aside.
Babylonian Talmud, Yoma 85b, retold in simple words (paraphrased)

What does this rule protect? Why might the rabbis have put it first?

Words to know
Gemara
the rabbis' questions and answers about the Mishnah
Check yourself

1. What is the Gemara?

Why: The Gemara is the rabbis' talk about the Mishnah. Together they make the Talmud.

2. What did the rabbis say if someone's life is in danger on Shabbat?

Why: The Talmud in Yoma 85b says saving a life comes before the Shabbat rules.

3. What two parts make up the Talmud?

Why: The Mishnah and the Gemara together are the Talmud.
2.7

A Good Question Is Learning

Main ideaAsking a good question is a way of learning.

The rabbis loved questions. A question is not a mistake. A question shows you are thinking. In the Talmud, a good question is praised.

Pirkei Avot is a small book of rabbis’ sayings. It says a shy person does not learn. That means: if you are afraid to ask, you miss out. So ask!

In this class, you can ask anything about the Talmud. What does that word mean? Why did they say that? Every question helps us learn.

From the source
Do Not Be Too Shy to Ask
In Pirkei Avot, Hillel says that a shy person cannot learn, and a quick-tempered person cannot teach.
Mishnah Avot 2:5, retold from Charles Taylor, Sayings of the Jewish Fathers (1877) (paraphrased)

Why would being shy make it hard to learn? Why would a bad temper make it hard to teach?

Words to know
Pirkei Avot
a small book of rabbis' sayings, part of the Mishnah
Check yourself

Why: In the Talmud, a good question is praised. It shows thinking.

3. What does Hillel say about a shy person?
