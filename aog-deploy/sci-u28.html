The Bass You Can Feel

At a summer block party, a soda can starts to shiver before you even hear the song.

It is a hot Saturday on a Chicago side street. Someone has set up two big speakers on a folding table. Kids are on the curb, grown-ups are at the grill, and a DJ is looking through a crate of records. A can of soda sits on the table next to the speakers.

The DJ drops the needle. The first thing you notice is not the music. It is the feeling. A low thump hits your chest. The table buzzes under your hand. The soda can shivers, and tiny rings dance across the top of the drink.

Walk to the end of the block and the song is still there, only softer. The high sounds, like the tss of the cymbals, seem to fade first. The deep thump carries farther. Nothing flew from the speaker to your ear. No wind blew. Yet something traveled a whole block and made your body feel it.

That something is sound. In this chapter you will find out what is really moving when a speaker plays, why some sounds are high and some are low, why some are loud and some are soft, and how your ear turns all of it into music.

Sound Starts With a Shake

61.1

Everything That Sounds Is Moving

Main ideaEvery sound starts with something vibrating, moving quickly back and forth.

Put two fingers on the front of your throat and hum. You can feel a buzz. Now stop humming. The buzz stops too. That buzz is a : a quick back-and-forth movement. Your vocal folds, two small flaps inside your throat, are shaking many times each second.

Every sound you have ever heard started this way. A drum skin shakes after the stick hits it. A guitar string shakes after it is plucked. A speaker has a cone that pushes out and pulls in. Even a clap makes your hands and the air between them shake for a moment.

Sometimes the shaking is too fast or too small to see. You can still find it. Sprinkle a little rice on a drum and tap the drum. The rice jumps. Put your hand on a speaker playing music. You feel it tremble. When the shaking stops, the sound stops. A is anything that vibrates and starts a sound.

61.2

A Chain of Pushes

Main ideaSound travels as a wave: each bit of air pushes the next bit, but the air itself stays close to home.

When a speaker cone pushes out, it squeezes the air right in front of it. That squeezed air pushes on the air next to it. Then that air pushes the next air. The push passes along like a line of people bumping shoulders. This traveling push is a sound .

Here is the surprising part. The air near the speaker does not fly to your ear. Each bit of air moves forward a tiny bit, then springs back. What travels is the pattern of pushes, and the it carries. At the block party, no wind came from the speaker. Energy came.

A sound wave has places where air is squeezed together and places where it is spread apart. Scientists call the squeezed places . The spread-out places are called rarefactions. A speaker making a steady note sends out compression, spread, compression, spread, over and over, as long as it keeps vibrating.

61.3

No Air, No Sound

Main ideaSound needs a medium, such as air, water or a solid, to travel through; in empty space there is no sound.

If sound is a chain of pushes, it needs something to push. The material a wave travels through is called its . For the block party, the medium was air. But sound also travels through water and through solids like wood, metal and the ground.

In the 1600s, scientists built pumps that could pull most of the air out of a glass jar. Robert Boyle, working in England, put a ticking watch inside such a jar. As he pumped the air out, the ticking grew fainter. When he let air back in, the sound returned. With less medium, less sound got out.

Outer space is almost a perfect , a space with nearly no particles at all. So astronauts cannot talk to each other through empty space the way we talk in a room. They use radios. A radio sends light-like waves, which do not need a medium, and the receiver turns them back into sound inside the helmet.

High and Low, Loud and Soft

61.4

Frequency Sets the Pitch

Main ideaThe faster something vibrates, the higher the pitch we hear.

Pluck a thick, loose guitar string and you hear a low note. Pluck a thin, tight one and you hear a high note. The difference is speed. The thin string vibrates back and forth many more times each second. How high or low a sound seems is its .

Scientists count vibrations per second. That count is the . It is measured in , written Hz. One hertz means one vibration each second. The A note that orchestras tune to is 440 Hz. The string making it moves back and forth 440 times in one second.

Double the frequency and you get the same note, only higher. Musicians call that jump an octave. The A above 440 Hz is 880 Hz. The A below is 220 Hz. On a drum machine, a deep kick drum is mostly low frequencies, and a tiny hi-hat is mostly very high ones.

61.5

Amplitude Sets the Loudness

Main ideaA bigger vibration makes a wave with a bigger amplitude, and we hear it as louder.

Tap a drum softly, then hit it hard. The pitch stays about the same, but the hard hit is louder. When you hit harder, the drum skin moves farther from its resting place. It squeezes the air more. The size of the vibration is its .

Loudness is how strong a sound seems. More amplitude means more energy in the wave, and it seems louder. Loudness is often measured in , written dB. The scale is not a simple count. Every 10 dB step means the sound carries about ten times more energy.

So pitch and loudness are two different things. Pitch comes from how fast something vibrates. Loudness comes from how big the vibration is. A soft high note and a loud high note have the same frequency. On the oscilloscope, a louder sound draws taller waves. A higher sound draws waves packed closer together.

61.6

Wavelength and the Speed of Sound

Main ideaSound moves at a set speed in each material, and its wavelength is that speed divided by the frequency.

Sound is fast, but not instant. In air at room temperature it travels about 343 meters each second. That is why you see lightning before you hear thunder. The light arrives almost at once. The sound takes about three seconds to cover each kilometer.

Sound moves even faster through water and faster still through steel. In solids and liquids the particles are packed close, so they pass each push along quickly. Put your ear on a long metal railing while a friend taps the far end. You may hear the tap through the metal first, then through the air.

The distance from one compression to the next is the . You can find it with a simple rule: wavelength equals speed divided by frequency. A 440 Hz note in air has a wavelength of about 343 divided by 440, or about 0.78 meters. Low bass notes have long waves, several meters long. That is part of why they carry around corners and through walls.

How We Hear

61.7

From Eardrum to Brain

Main ideaThe ear turns air vibrations into nerve signals, and the brain turns those signals into the sounds we notice.

Your outer ear is shaped like a funnel. It gathers sound waves and sends them down a short tube to the , a thin skin stretched tight. When a sound wave arrives, the eardrum vibrates in the same pattern as the wave.

Behind the eardrum sit three tiny bones, the smallest in your body. They pass the vibration along and make it stronger. The last bone presses on the , a snail-shaped tube filled with fluid. Inside the cochlea are thousands of tiny hair cells. Different hair cells respond to different pitches.

When the hair cells bend, they send signals along a nerve to the brain. The brain does the rest. It tells a voice from a drum, finds the beat, and notices your name in a noisy room. Brains differ. Some people notice small sounds that others miss. Some people hear best with a quiet room or with headphones. Knowing what helps your own ears is useful science.

61.8

The Sounds We Can Hear

Main ideaPeople can hear roughly 20 to 20,000 hertz, and many animals hear sounds outside that range.

Your ears do not hear every vibration. A young person with typical hearing hears from about 20 Hz, a very deep rumble, up to about 20,000 Hz, a thin whine. This span is your . As people get older, the top of the range usually drops.

Sounds too high for us are called . Dogs can hear some of them, which is how a dog whistle works. Bats send out ultrasound and listen for echoes to find insects in the dark. Doctors use ultrasound to make pictures of the inside of the body.

Sounds too low for us are called infrasound. Elephants can use very low rumbles to stay in touch across long distances. Very low bass, near the bottom of our range, is often felt in the chest as much as it is heard. That is the thump you felt at the block party.

61.9

Loud Enough to Hurt

Main ideaVery loud sound, or loud sound for a long time, can damage the hair cells in your ears for good.

Hair cells in the cochlea are delicate. A very loud sound, or a loud sound that goes on for hours, can bend and break them. Once they are gone, the body does not grow new ones. This is called . It can happen without any pain.

The U.S. government’s safety scientists say sound at 85 dB, about as loud as heavy city traffic, is safe for up to about eight hours a day. Each 3 dB louder cuts the safe time in half. At 100 dB, a level some headphones and concerts reach, the safe time is only about 15 minutes.

Protecting your hearing is simple. Turn it down. Take breaks. Step back from the speakers. Wear earplugs or ear defenders at loud events. Some people’s ears are extra sensitive to noise, and ear defenders help them stay comfortable. That is a smart tool, just like sunglasses on a bright day.

61.10

Seeing Sound on a Screen

Main ideaAn oscilloscope draws a sound as a line over time, so you can see its frequency and its amplitude.

A microphone works like an ear. Its thin plate vibrates with the sound, and it turns those vibrations into a changing electrical signal. An draws that signal as a line moving across a screen. Time runs from left to right. Up and down shows the push and pull of the air.

Now you can read a sound like a graph. Taller waves mean bigger amplitude, so a louder sound. Waves packed closer together mean higher frequency, so a higher pitch. The time for one full wave is the . A 440 Hz note has a period of about 2.3 thousandths of a second.

Different sources draw different shapes. A flute draws a smooth, rounded wave. A buzzy synthesizer might draw a sawtooth. A hi-hat draws a fuzzy scribble, because it mixes many high frequencies at once. The shape of a wave is part of why a trumpet and a violin sound different, even when they play the same note.

The story

The Warehouse and the Steady Kick

In Chicago in the late 1970s and early 1980s, a club and its DJ helped start a new kind of music built on a steady beat.

In the late 1970s, a club called the Warehouse opened on the West Side of Chicago. Its DJ was Frankie Knuckles. He did not just play records one after another. He blended them, stretched them and added beats so the dancing never had to stop.

Knuckles and other Chicago DJs began using drum machines under their records. A drum machine never gets tired and never speeds up by accident. It can play a kick drum on every beat, thump, thump, thump, thump, all night long. Dancers could lock onto that pulse and move together.

People started calling the music by the club's name. It became house music. From Chicago it spread to Detroit, to New York, to London and around the world. Much of the dance music on the radio today still stands on that steady kick.

62.1

A Beat Is a Repeating Event

Main ideaA beat is an event that repeats at an even spacing in time, like a clock's tick.

Listen to a clock. Tick, tick, tick. Each tick is an event, and the time between ticks is always the same. When something repeats at an even spacing, it has a . The steady feeling underneath the beat is called the .

Your body is full of beats. Your heart beats. You breathe in and out. When you walk, your feet land left, right, left, right at a fairly even pace. Music takes this idea and makes it something to share. Everyone can feel the same pulse and move with it.

A beat is a lot like a wave. Both repeat. A wave repeats in space and time, and a beat repeats in time. The time from one beat to the next is the beat’s period, the same idea you met with sound waves. A slow song has a long period between beats. A fast song has a short one.

62.2

Tempo in Beats per Minute

Main ideaTempo is the speed of the beat, measured in beats per minute (BPM).

How fast is the beat? Musicians call the speed of the beat the . They measure it by counting how many beats fit in one minute. That unit is , or BPM. A clock that ticks once a second is at 60 BPM.

BPM makes the math simple. At 60 BPM, each beat lasts one second. At 120 BPM, twice as many beats fit in the minute, so each beat lasts half a second. You can always find the time for one beat: divide 60 seconds by the BPM.

Different kinds of music tend to use different tempos. Slow songs might sit near 70 BPM. Many hip hop tracks sit roughly between 85 and 100 BPM. House music is often around 120 to 130 BPM. The Drum Machine has a tempo control, so you can hear the same pattern slow and fast.

Any single count can be a little off. You might start tapping late or lose one beat. Scientists handle this by repeating a and comparing. Count three times. If you get 29, 30 and 30 in 15 seconds, the tempo is about 120 BPM. The results agree, so you can trust them more.

You can measure other rhythms the same way. Find your pulse on your wrist and count for 15 seconds. Count a friend’s steps as they walk. Count the chirps of a cricket on a summer night. Every repeating event has a tempo you can measure.

Bars and Counting to Four

Main ideaMusic groups beats into bars; most popular music counts four beats in each bar.

Beats are easier to follow in groups. Musicians gather them into , also called measures. In most pop, hip hop and house music, each bar has four beats. You count them: one, two, three, four, one, two, three, four.

The first beat of each bar, the one, often feels strongest. It is where a new idea tends to start. The pattern of how many beats are in each bar is the . Four beats per bar is written 4/4, and it is so common that people call it common time.

Not all music counts in four. A waltz counts in three: one, two, three. Some music from the Balkans and from India uses longer, uneven groups. Counting is a tool. Once you can count the bars, you can tell where you are in a song, just like a map tells you where you are on a street.

Big questionHow can you design a beat for a purpose, then use measurements to show it does what you planned?
The story

A First Beat, Measured

Jada wanted a beat to help her class settle after lunch. Then she wanted proof it worked.

Jada's class came back from lunch loud and bouncy every day. Her teacher asked the class to design something to help everyone settle into reading time. Jada had one idea right away: a beat. A calm one, low and steady, that the class could breathe along with.

She opened the Drum Machine and set the tempo to 70 BPM, a little slower than a resting heart. She put a soft kick on the first beat of each bar and a quiet hi-hat on the off steps. No crash cymbals. No sudden sounds. She played it for her table. Two friends said it was too sleepy. One said it helped.

Jada wanted more than opinions. She played the beat into the Oscilloscope and looked at it. The kick drew slow, rounded waves. The hi-hat drew thin, fuzzy spikes. She checked that the loudest part was not too tall. She counted the time between kicks on the screen and got about 3.4 seconds, one full bar at 70 BPM.

Then she tested it. For one week the class heard her beat after lunch. For another week they did not. Each day, the teacher timed how long it took for the room to be ready to read. Jada had a claim, a plan and a way to measure. In this chapter, you will design and test a beat of your own.

64.1

Start With a Purpose

Main ideaGood design starts with a clear purpose and a list of what the design must do.

Engineers do not start by building. They start with a question: what is this for? Jada’s purpose was to help a class settle. Another student might want a beat for a dance, for a workout, or for walking the dog. The purpose shapes every choice.

Next come the , the things the design must do to succeed. For a calm beat, the criteria might be: slow tempo, steady pulse, no sudden loud sounds. Then come the , the limits you must work within. Maybe you have only 16 steps, one bar, and five minutes.

Writing these down first saves time later. When you are not sure whether to add a crash cymbal, check the criteria. No sudden loud sounds? Then leave it out. Criteria also tell you what to measure when you test. Design and science meet here: a clear goal leads to a fair test.

64.4

Reading a Waveform

Main ideaOn an oscilloscope, you can read a sound's loudness from the height of the waves and its pitch from how close they are.

Play your beat into the Oscilloscope. The line jumps each time a drum hits and settles back between hits. The picture is the beat’s . Reading it is like reading a graph: time runs left to right, and the height of the line shows the push and pull of the air.

Look for three things. First, how tall do the biggest waves get? That is the peak amplitude, the loudest moment. If the waves hit the top of the screen and flatten, the sound is , too loud for the system, and it will sound harsh. Second, how quickly does each hit fade? That is its . Third, how far apart are the hits? That is the rhythm.

You can adjust the screen. Stretching the time scale lets you zoom in on one kick to see its individual wiggles. Squeezing it lets you see a whole bar. Changing the height scale lets you see quiet sounds. A good scientist chooses the scale that shows what they want to measure.

64.5

Kick and Hi-Hat on the Screen

Main ideaA kick drum draws slow, big waves, and a hi-hat draws fast, small, noisy ones, which matches how low and high they sound.

Play a kick drum alone into the Oscilloscope. You see a few big, rounded waves spaced far apart, and then they fade. Those far-apart waves mean a , often around 50 to 100 Hz for a deep kick. Big height means strong amplitude.

Now play the hi-hat alone. The line turns into a thin, jittery smear with no smooth shape. A hi-hat is made of many high frequencies at once, jumbled together. Scientists call a jumble of many frequencies noise, even when it sounds nice. The hi-hat also fades quickly, so its decay is short.

Put them together, and you can see why a beat works. The kick fills the low end with big, slow energy. The hi-hat fills the high end with fast, light ticks. They do not crowd each other, because they live in different frequency ranges. Mixing engineers call this giving each sound its own space.

64.6

Timing Your Beat With Numbers

Main ideaYou can check a beat's tempo by measuring the time between hits and turning it back into BPM.

Your drum machine says the tempo is 90 BPM. Is it? Scientists check. Play the beat into the Oscilloscope and measure the time from one kick to the next kick on the main beats. That is the beat’s .

Turn the period back into tempo: BPM equals 60 divided by the period in seconds. If the kicks are 0.67 seconds apart, 60 divided by 0.67 is about 90. The measurement agrees with the setting. If you measure 0.60 seconds instead, the tempo is really 100 BPM, and something is set differently than you thought.

You can also check the steps. At 90 BPM, each of the 16 steps should last about 0.167 seconds. With swing on, the pairs of steps will not be equal; the first one will be longer. Measuring lets you see swing as numbers, not just a feeling. Record your measurements in a , with the setting in one column and the measured value in the next.

64.7

Protect Your Hearing While You Create

Main ideaWhen you make music, keep volume moderate, take breaks and check your levels so your ears last a lifetime.

Making beats can mean hours in headphones. That is exactly when hearing damage can sneak up, because it does not hurt. Keep the volume at a comfortable level. A good check: if someone an arm’s length away has to shout for you to hear them, it is too loud.

Take . Every hour or so, take off the headphones for a few minutes. Your ears get tired, and tired ears hear less detail. After a break, you will often notice things in your beat you missed. Producers call these fresh ears.

Everyone’s comfort level is different. Some people find certain sounds, like sharp hi-hats or sudden crashes, painful or overwhelming. That is useful information for a designer. It is fine to turn those sounds down, choose softer ones, or wear ear defenders. A beat that works for more people is a better design.

Main ideaWhen you use a sound someone else made, give them credit and follow the rules for using their work.

A can mean a single measurement, but in music it also means a piece of another recording used in a new song. Hip hop was built on samples: a drum break from a funk record, a horn line from a jazz record, looped and flipped into something new.

The sounds in those old records were made by real people: drummers, singers, engineers. Their work is protected by , a law that gives creators rights over how their work is used. Professional musicians who sample a song usually ask permission and often pay the people who made it. This is called clearing a sample.

For school projects, you can use the sounds built into the Drum Machine, record your own sounds, or use sounds that are marked free to use. Either way, give : write down where each sound came from. Scientists do the same when they cite other people’s data. Credit is how we show respect for the work we build on.

Main ideaA strong explanation makes a claim, backs it with measured evidence and uses science to explain why.
