Enoch and the Watchers

1 Enoch

Genesis gives a man named Enoch only four verses. It says he walked with God, and then God took him. Centuries later, Jewish writers composed a long book in his name. That book, 1 Enoch, told a much bigger story about heavenly beings called Watchers who came down to earth before the flood. For hundreds of years the whole book was kept only in Ethiopia. Then in 1773 it reached Europe, and in the 1950s pieces of it turned up among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Michael Heiser, a scholar of the Hebrew Bible, did not treat 1 Enoch as Scripture. But he argued it is a window into how many Jews read Genesis 6 around the time of Jesus. In this unit you will learn what 1 Enoch is, how it survived, and what its Book of the Watchers says. You will compare it with Genesis 6 and with other readings. You will also look at Heiser's idea that the story answered Babylon's tales of wise beings before the flood.

By the end you will be able to name the five parts of 1 Enoch and tell where the book is Scripture. You will be able to retell the Watchers story calmly and accurately. You will be able to explain Heiser's reading and at least one other reading, and to check a claim about Enoch against the texts themselves.

The big question

What is 1 Enoch, and how does its story of the Watchers help readers understand Genesis 6 and the world of the New Testament?

The Book
Big questionHow did a book named after a man in Genesis 5 survive, and why did Michael Heiser think it matters for reading the Bible?
The story

A Lost Book Comes Home

In 1773 a Scottish traveler reached Europe with Ethiopian manuscripts, and one held a book scholars had only known in pieces.

James Bruce was a tall Scottish traveler who wanted to find where the Nile River begins. In 1769 he went to Ethiopia, which Europeans then called Abyssinia. He stayed for more than two years. He learned about the country's churches, kings and old books. When he left, he packed some things he valued more than gold: handwritten books on parchment.

The books were written in Ge'ez, an old language of Ethiopia. Priests in Ethiopia still read Ge'ez in worship, the way some churches in Europe once used Latin. Among Bruce's books were three copies of one work. Its title named a man from the first pages of the Bible: Enoch.

In Europe, scholars knew that a Book of Enoch had once existed. The New Testament letter of Jude quotes a line from it. A Greek writer named George Syncellus, around 800 CE, had copied a few passages into his history of the world. But no one in Europe had the whole book. Many believed it was lost forever.

In 1773 Bruce reached Europe. He gave one copy to the royal library in Paris, and another later went to Oxford. Scholars were excited. Here was a book that people in Ethiopia had kept, copied and read in church for many centuries. It took years before anyone could translate it into English.

Today the book is called 1 Enoch. Scholars study it to learn what Jewish readers believed a few centuries before Jesus. Michael Heiser, a scholar of the Hebrew Bible, read it closely. He did not treat it as Scripture. He argued that it helps explain things the Bible's writers and first readers already knew.

Talk about itWhy might a book survive whole in one country but disappear almost everywhere else? What does that tell you about the people who kept it?
Section 1

Enoch in Genesis

15.1

The Seventh from Adam

Main ideaGenesis 5 gives Enoch only four verses, but they are unusual: instead of saying he died, the text says God took him.

Genesis 5 is a , a list of family lines. It runs from Adam to Noah. Each entry follows a pattern. A man lives, has a son, lives more years, and then the text says, "and he died." The line repeats like a drumbeat through the chapter.

Enoch breaks the pattern. Genesis 5:21–24 says he had a son named Methuselah. Then he "walked with God" for three hundred years. His whole life was 365 years. The text does not say "and he died." It says he "was not," for God took him. Counting Adam as first, Enoch is the seventh name in the list.

Those few words made readers curious for centuries. What did it mean to walk with God? Where did Enoch go? Because Genesis says so little, later writers filled the gap. Many Jewish books told more stories about Enoch. The biggest of these is the book now called 1 Enoch.

15.2

What Does "God Took Him" Mean?

Main ideaReaders have explained Genesis 5:24 in different ways, and later books like Hebrews and Jubilees added their own understanding.

The phrase "God took him" is short, and readers have understood it in more than one way. Many Jewish and Christian readers believed Enoch went to God without dying. The New Testament book of Hebrews reads it this way. Hebrews 11:5 says Enoch was "translated," or carried away, so that he would not see death.

Other readers thought the phrase could be a gentle way to describe a death. They pointed out that Enoch’s life of 365 years is short compared with the others in Genesis 5. Some ancient Jewish interpreters leaned toward this view. The text itself does not explain what happened, so each reading fills a silence.

Later Jewish books made Enoch a great teacher and writer. The book of , from around 150 BCE, says Enoch was the first person to learn writing. It says he wrote down signs of the heavens and testified against the Watchers. In these books Enoch becomes a scribe who sees heavenly things and reports them.

15.3

Three Books Named Enoch

Main ideaThree different ancient books carry Enoch's name; this unit is about 1 Enoch, the oldest of them.

Because Genesis says so little about Enoch, writers in later centuries wrote books in his name. Scholars number them to keep them apart. They are 1 Enoch, 2 Enoch and 3 Enoch. The numbers do not mean they are volumes of one series. They are three separate books from different times and places.

is the oldest. Its earliest parts come from about 300 BCE. It survives whole in Ge’ez, the old language of Ethiopia, so it is sometimes called Ethiopic Enoch. 2 Enoch survives in Old Church Slavonic, a language of early Slavic churches, and is called Slavonic Enoch. 3 Enoch is written in Hebrew and comes from much later Jewish writers.

When the New Testament letter of Jude quotes Enoch, the words match 1 Enoch. When Michael Heiser wrote about Enoch and the Watchers, he meant 1 Enoch. So in this unit, "the Book of Enoch" always means 1 Enoch. Knowing which book is meant is the first step in checking any claim about it.

Section 2

A Book in Five Parts

15.4

Five Books Inside One

Main idea1 Enoch is really five shorter books joined together, and scholars name each part.

Open 1 Enoch and you will find 108 chapters. But scholars see that it is not one book written at one time. It is a collection of five parts. Each part has its own style and its own main subject. Later copyists put them together under Enoch’s name.

The first part is the , chapters 1–36. It tells of heavenly beings who came down to earth. Next come the , chapters 37–71, visions of a heavenly judge. The Astronomical Book, chapters 72–82, is about the sun, moon and a calendar. The Dream Visions, chapters 83–90, retell history as a dream. The Epistle of Enoch, chapters 91–108, is Enoch’s advice to his children.

Knowing the five parts helps you read carefully. A claim about 1 Enoch might be true of one part but not another. For example, the story of the Watchers comes from the first part. The Parables are the part missing from the Dead Sea Scrolls. You will learn more about that in a later lesson.

15.5

Written Over Three Centuries

Main ideaThe five parts of 1 Enoch were written at different times, from about 300 BCE to the first century CE.

Scholars date ancient books by clues. They look at the oldest copies, at the events a book seems to know about, and at its language. For 1 Enoch these clues point to a long period of writing. The parts were written over roughly three hundred years.

The Book of the Watchers and the Astronomical Book are the oldest. Many scholars date them to around 300–200 BCE. The Dream Visions seem to know about the Maccabean revolt, a Jewish uprising in the 160s BCE. So they are dated to about that time. The Epistle of Enoch is probably from the 100s BCE.

The Parables are the youngest part. Many scholars date them to the late first century BCE or the first century CE. That is close to the time of Jesus. Scholars still discuss the exact dates, so books give ranges. When you read a date for 1 Enoch, ask which part it describes.

15.6

Writing in an Ancient Hero's Name

Main ideaScholars think 1 Enoch was written by later Jewish writers who used Enoch's name, a common practice in their time.

1 Enoch is written as if Enoch himself is speaking. Enoch lived before the flood in the Genesis story. But the book’s oldest parts come from about 300 BCE. So most scholars think later Jewish writers composed it and put it in Enoch’s voice.

Writing in the name of a famous figure from the past was common in the ancient world. Scholars call such books , which means "falsely titled" in Greek. The word is a technical label, not an insult. Writers used a great name to show that their message fit an honored tradition. Other examples include books written in the names of Moses, Baruch and Ezra.

Why choose Enoch? Genesis says he walked with God and was taken. That made him a perfect person to tell heavenly secrets. A man who had been close to God could describe heaven, angels and the end of days. Readers today can study the book while knowing who probably wrote it and when.

Section 3

How the Book Survived

15.7

Kept in Ethiopia

Main ideaThe Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church kept 1 Enoch in its Bible, which is why the whole book survives.

Ethiopia has one of the oldest Christian traditions in the world. Its church became Christian in the 300s CE. Early on, Christians there translated many books into Ge’ez. One of those books was Enoch. Scholars think it was translated from a Greek version, perhaps around 400–600 CE, though estimates vary.

The counts 1 Enoch as part of its Bible. So does the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Scribes there copied it by hand, generation after generation. Because they kept copying it, the whole book survived there when it was lost elsewhere.

A book that belongs in a church’s Bible is part of its . Different churches have different canons. Ethiopian Christians read 1 Enoch in their worship. For them it is not a lost book or a strange book. It is part of the Scripture they have always had.

15.8

From Ethiopia to Europe

Main ideaJames Bruce brought 1 Enoch to Europe in 1773, and English translations followed, including R. H. Charles's.

Before 1773, European scholars knew only bits of 1 Enoch. They had the quotation in Jude. They had Greek passages copied by George Syncellus, a writer from around 800 CE. The rest seemed lost. Then James Bruce, a Scottish traveler, returned from Ethiopia with copies in Ge’ez.

Few people in Europe could read Ge’ez, so translation took time. In 1821 Richard Laurence, a scholar at Oxford, published the first English translation. Later the Irish scholar studied many more manuscripts and made a careful new translation. He published it in 1912, and a popular edition followed in 1917.

Charles’s 1917 translation is now in the public domain. That means anyone may copy and quote it freely. This course uses it when it cites chapter and verse in 1 Enoch. Modern translations exist too, based on even more manuscripts, including pieces found later in the Dead Sea Scrolls.

15.9

Pieces in the Caves

Main ideaAramaic pieces of 1 Enoch found among the Dead Sea Scrolls show the book was read in Judea before Jesus's time.

Between 1947 and 1956, people found ancient scrolls in caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea. These are the . Most were torn into small pieces. In Cave 4, found in 1952, scholars identified pieces of about eleven copies of Enoch writings, written in .

Aramaic was a common language of Judea in that period. Scholars think Aramaic was probably the original language of much of 1 Enoch. The scholar J. T. Milik published these Aramaic pieces in 1976. Some of the copies are among the oldest scrolls found, from around 200 BCE.

One part was missing. No piece of the Parables, chapters 37–71, has been found at Qumran. Scholars treat this as one clue that the Parables are later than the other parts. The finds showed that Jewish readers knew the Book of the Watchers well before the New Testament was written.

Section 4

Why Heiser Read Enoch

15.10

In Some Bibles, Not Others

Main idea1 Enoch is Scripture only in the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches, though early Christians discussed it and Jude quotes it.

1 Enoch is not in the Jewish canon, the Hebrew Bible. It is not in the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox or Protestant Bibles either. It is canonical only in the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo churches. Other communities read it as an important ancient writing, not as Scripture.

Still, New Testament writers knew it. The letter of , verses 14–15, quotes 1 Enoch 1:9. Jude calls Enoch "the seventh from Adam" and repeats his warning that the Lord comes with many thousands of holy ones. Readers disagree on what Jude meant by quoting it. Some say he honored the book; others say he only used a line his readers knew.

Early Christian writers also disagreed. Tertullian, around 200 CE, defended the book. Augustine, in the 400s, did not accept it as Scripture. Over time most churches left it out. The debate shows that deciding a canon was a long conversation, not a single moment.

15.11

Heiser: A Window, Not Scripture

Main ideaHeiser did not treat 1 Enoch as Scripture, but he argued it shows what New Testament writers and readers already knew.

Michael Heiser studied the Hebrew Bible and ancient Semitic languages. He wrote about 1 Enoch in Reversing Hermon (2017) and in A Companion to the Book of Enoch (2020). In those books he said plainly that he did not consider 1 Enoch Scripture. He read it as a Jewish text from the , about 500 BCE to 70 CE.

Heiser argued that the book is a window. It shows how many Jews read Genesis 6 around the time of Jesus. He pointed to New Testament passages that seem to assume the Watchers story. Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4 speak of angels who sinned and are kept in chains for judgment. 2 Peter uses the Greek word for their prison, a word also found in the Greek of 1 Enoch.

Other scholars agree that 1 Enoch shaped some New Testament language. They differ on how much. Some think the links are few and loose. Heiser thought the links were many and important. You can test the idea yourself by reading the passages side by side.

One Line Becomes a Chapter

A student reads Genesis 6 and 1 Enoch 6 side by side and notices how much the second one adds.

Maya had a project for her history class: compare two ancient texts about the same event. Her teacher had suggested Genesis 6 and 1 Enoch 6. Maya opened both on her phone, one in each half of the screen. On the left was Genesis in the King James Version. On the right was R. H. Charles's 1917 translation of 1 Enoch.

Genesis 6:1–4 took her less than a minute to read. The sons of God saw that human women were beautiful and married them. The Nephilim were on the earth in those days. Their children became famous mighty men of old. Then the story moved straight on to Noah and the flood. There were no names, no places and no reasons.

The Enoch side kept going. It named a leader called Semjaza. It said the heavenly beings made a promise together on a mountain called Hermon. It counted them: two hundred. It listed the names of their chief leaders. Then, in the next chapters, it told what they taught people and what happened to the earth.

Maya drew a line from each verse in Genesis to the parts of Enoch that expanded it. One short phrase in Genesis became many verses in Enoch. She wrote in her notebook: "Genesis leaves a gap. Enoch fills it in." Then she wrote a question she wanted to answer: "Why would someone fill it in like this?"

That is the kind of question scholars ask too. Michael Heiser spent years studying it. His answer, in his book Reversing Hermon (2017), took him far from Jerusalem, to the very old stories of ancient Babylon.

Section 1

Genesis 6 and Its Retelling

16.1

Four Short Verses

Main ideaGenesis 6:1–4 tells, very briefly, of the sons of God, human women and the Nephilim just before the flood.

Genesis 6:1–4 comes right before the story of Noah. People were growing in number. The saw that the daughters of humans were beautiful, and they took wives. Then God said that humans would live 120 years. The passage ends by mentioning the and the mighty men of old.

The passage is short and puzzling. Who are the sons of God? The Hebrew phrase is bene ha’elohim. In Job 1:6 and 38:7 the same kind of phrase clearly names heavenly beings. Heiser argued that Genesis 6 uses it the same way. In his reading, heavenly beings crossed a line God had set between heaven and earth.

Who are the Nephilim? The Hebrew word is not fully clear. Some connect it to a verb meaning "to fall." When Jewish scholars translated Genesis into Greek, in the , they used the word gigantes, meaning giants. That is why the King James Version says "there were giants in the earth in those days."

From the source
Genesis 6:2
That the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair; and they took them wives of all which they chose.
Genesis 6:2, King James Version (1611)

16.2

Enoch Fills the Gap

Main idea1 Enoch 6–11 expands Genesis 6:1–4 into a longer story with names, numbers, a place and consequences.

The Book of the Watchers retells Genesis 6 at length. In 1 Enoch the heavenly beings are called . The name also appears in Daniel 4, where a watcher, a holy one, comes down from heaven. In 1 Enoch the Watchers who came down are heavenly beings who leave their proper place.

Genesis 6:2 says the sons of God took wives. 1 Enoch 6–8 adds a leader, an oath, a mountain, a number and a list of names. It adds what they taught. It adds what happened to the earth. Then chapters 9–11 tell how heaven responded. This kind of retelling is called an .

Scholars debate which came first. Most think Genesis 6 is older and 1 Enoch expands it. A few have suggested that both draw on an even older tradition. Either way, reading them side by side shows how Jewish writers in the Second Temple period understood Genesis 6.

16.3

Other Readings of Genesis 6

Main ideaHeiser read the sons of God in Genesis 6 as heavenly beings; other readers see them as men from Seth's line or as kings.

Heiser argued that the sons of God in Genesis 6 are heavenly beings. He pointed to Job, to the Septuagint and to 1 Enoch. He noted that 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6 seem to know this reading. Many Jewish writers in the Second Temple period read the passage this way too.

Later, other readings grew popular. Many Christian writers, including Augustine, read the sons of God as godly men from the family of Seth, Adam’s son. In this view the daughters of men came from Cain’s family. Genesis 6 then describes two human families mixing. This is often called the .

Some ancient Jewish interpreters read the sons of God as rulers or nobles. In that view, powerful men took any women they wanted. That is sometimes called the royal view. Each reading tries to fit the passage with the rest of the Bible. Heiser gave reasons for his view and explained why he found the others less convincing.

Think it through
Three Readings, One Verse

Reading A: the sons of God are heavenly beings who crossed into the human world (Heiser, Second Temple Jewish writers). Reading B: they are men from Seth's line marrying women from Cain's line (Augustine and many later Christians). Reading C: they are kings who took whatever they wanted (some Jewish interpreters).

The Oath on Mount Hermon

16.4

Two Hundred on the Mountain

Main idea1 Enoch 6 says two hundred Watchers, led by Shemihazah, swore an oath together on Mount Hermon in the days of Jared.

1 Enoch 6 names the leader of the Watchers. Charles’s translation spells his name Semjaza. Many scholars write it , closer to the Aramaic. He fears he alone will be blamed. So all the Watchers swear an oath together, binding each other to the plan.

The book says there were two hundred of them. They came down in the days of Jared, Enoch’s father. The Hebrew name Jared sounds like the verb yarad, "to go down." They came down on . The book links the name Hermon to a Hebrew word for a solemn oath or ban.

Mount Hermon is a real mountain. It rises about 9,200 feet at the north edge of ancient Israel, on today’s border of Lebanon and Syria. It is often covered with snow. Heiser gave Mount Hermon a central place in his book Reversing Hermon (2017), because this story begins there.

16.5

What the Watchers Taught

Main idea1 Enoch 7–8 says the Watchers taught people forbidden knowledge, such as weapon-making, cosmetics, sorcery and star-reading.

In 1 Enoch the Watchers do more than marry. They teach. Chapter 8 names , also spelled Asael. He teaches people to work metal and to make swords, knives, shields and armor. He also teaches jewelry-making, makeup for the eyes and the use of colored dyes.

Other Watchers teach other things. Shemihazah teaches charms and the cutting of roots. Some teach how to read signs in the stars, the moon and the sun. In the book’s view, this knowledge was given before people were ready. It spread harm, because weapons made fighting easier.

The story is not saying that metal, jewelry or learning are bad in themselves. The book’s worry is about knowledge given the wrong way, by the wrong teachers, for the wrong reasons. Scholars call this theme . Many ancient stories, from Greece to Mesopotamia, ask where human skills came from.

In the Greek story, Prometheus gives fire to humans against the will of Zeus. In 1 Enoch 8, Azazel teaches metalwork and weapons. In Genesis 4:22, a man named Tubal-cain is the first worker of bronze and iron, with no heavenly teacher mentioned.

Compare the three. How is each one explaining where a human skill came from? What does each seem to think about that skill?

16.6

Giants and a Hurting Earth

Main idea1 Enoch 7 says the Watchers' children were giants who used up the earth's food and harmed people and animals.

1 Enoch 7 says the Watchers’ wives had children who grew into giants. The book links them to the Nephilim of Genesis 6. The giants ate everything people had grown. When the food ran out, they turned against people and creatures. The book describes the earth as suffering under them.

The story’s main point is not the giants’ size. It is how the world went wrong. In the book, the earth itself is like a person crying out for help. Violence spreads, and people call to heaven. This helps explain why, in Genesis, God saw the earth "filled with violence" before the flood (Genesis 6:11).

Other Jewish writings of the time also mention the giants. Jubilees retells the story. A scroll found at Qumran, called the , tells more about them. Heiser read these texts as evidence of how widely Jews in the Second Temple period knew the story.

Section 3

Heaven Answers

16.7

Four Angels Hear the Cry

Main ideaIn 1 Enoch 9 the angels Michael, Uriel, Raphael and Gabriel see the harm on earth and bring the cry of the earth to God.

1 Enoch 9 turns to heaven. Four angels look down and see what is happening on the earth. Charles’s translation names them Michael, Uriel, Raphael and Gabriel. They hear the cry of people and bring it before God. They say that God knows everything and ask what should be done.

Two of these names are in the Hebrew Bible. Michael appears in Daniel 10 and 12 as a great prince. Gabriel appears in Daniel 8 and 9 and again in Luke 1. Raphael appears in the book of Tobit, which is in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles. Uriel appears in 1 Enoch and other Jewish writings.

In 1 Enoch these four are faithful angels. They stand in contrast to the Watchers who broke their oath to God’s order. Heiser described the Bible’s heavenly world as a , God’s heavenly family and court. In 1 Enoch, loyal and rebel members of that heavenly world are shown side by side.

16.8

Bound Until Judgment

Main ideaIn 1 Enoch 10 God sends angels to bind the Watchers in the earth until a final judgment, an idea echoed in 2 Peter and Jude.

In 1 Enoch 10 God answers. An angel is sent to warn Noah that a flood is coming, so he can be kept safe. Raphael is told to bind Azazel and place him in darkness in the desert. Michael is told to bind Shemihazah and the others. They are held under the earth for seventy generations, until the day of .

Gabriel is sent against the giants, and the book says they destroy one another. Then the earth is to be healed. The chapter looks ahead to a time when the earth is clean, people are righteous, and the land gives plenty. The tone moves from sorrow to hope.

The New Testament uses similar pictures. Jude 6 speaks of angels who did not keep their first place, held in chains until the great day. 2 Peter 2:4 says God put sinning angels in chains of darkness in Tartarus. Heiser argued these writers assumed their readers knew the Watchers story. Others think the writers simply shared common Jewish language.

16.9

Enoch the Go-Between

Main ideaIn 1 Enoch 12–16 the Watchers ask Enoch to plead for them, and in a vision of heaven he learns their request is refused.

In chapters 12–16, Enoch enters the story. The Watchers, now facing judgment, ask him to write a request to God for mercy. Enoch writes it. He goes to the waters of Dan, near Mount Hermon, reads it, and falls asleep. Then he has a .

In the vision Enoch is taken up to heaven. Chapter 14 describes a great house of fire and ice and a high throne. God speaks to Enoch. The answer is that the Watchers will have no peace. They were meant to live in heaven, but they left their place. Enoch must carry the answer back to them.

Here Enoch acts as an , someone who pleads for others. His request is refused. The scene shows the book’s view that the Watchers’ choice was very serious. It also shows Enoch as a trusted messenger who can stand near God’s throne, which fits Genesis 5:24.

Section 4

Heiser and the Sages of Babylon

16.10

The Apkallu of Mesopotamia

Main ideaMesopotamian stories told of apkallu, wise beings who brought knowledge to people before the great flood.

Long before 1 Enoch, people in Mesopotamia told stories about a great flood. Mesopotamia is the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, in today’s Iraq. Their stories also told of the . These were seven wise beings who lived before the flood. They were said to have taught people how to build cities and live in an orderly way.

In Mesopotamian art, apkallu often appear as men wearing fish-skin cloaks, or with bird heads and wings. A Babylonian priest named , writing in Greek around 290 BCE, described a fish-like teacher called Oannes. He said Oannes came up from the sea and taught people writing, farming and law.

Some Mesopotamian texts also say that after the flood, later sages were said to be partly human and partly apkallu. In those stories, wise beings and humans mixed. The apkallu were seen as sources of the knowledge that made kings great. Babylon’s kings were proud of this heritage.

Think it through
A Carving from Assyria

In many museums you can see Assyrian stone carvings from about 900–700 BCE. Some show tall figures with wings and bird heads, or men draped in a cloak shaped like a fish. Scholars identify many of these figures as apkallu, carved to guard palaces.

If a king placed apkallu carvings in his palace, what message might he be sending about where his power and wisdom came from?

16.11

Genesis 6 as an Answer

Main ideaHeiser argued that Genesis 6 and 1 Enoch answered Babylon's apkallu stories by treating those heavenly teachers as rebels, not heroes.

In Reversing Hermon (2017), Heiser argued that Genesis 6:1–4 is a , a text that argues against another view. He built on the work of other scholars, such as Amar Annus. They noticed that the Watchers story looks like the apkallu stories turned upside down.

In Babylon’s stories, the apkallu were honored teachers who brought civilization. In 1 Enoch, heavenly beings also bring knowledge before the flood. But here they are rebels, and their teaching spreads harm. Heiser argued that Jewish writers, some of whom lived in exile in Babylon, were answering Babylon’s pride. Babylon’s great wisdom, in this reading, came from a rebellion.

Not every scholar agrees. Some think the similarities are loose or come from shared ancient ideas. Others focus on Greek or Canaanite parallels instead. Heiser also warned against modern claims that such stories describe aliens or giants who built pyramids. He said the right way to test any idea is to read the ancient texts carefully.

From the source
Heiser's Argument in Brief
Heiser argued that Jewish writers knew Babylon's stories of wise beings who brought knowledge before the flood. They retold those events so that the heavenly teachers became rebels, and Babylon's proud learning became the result of a wrong choice.
Michael S. Heiser, Reversing Hermon: Enoch, the Watchers, and the Forgotten Mission of Jesus Christ (2017), paraphrased (paraphrased)
