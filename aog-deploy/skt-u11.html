Grinds and Slides

How To and Science

Walk into almost any skatepark and you will hear it: the long, rough scrape of metal trucks on a steel-capped ledge, or the hiss of a deck sliding across a waxed rail. Grinds and slides began on the rounded coping of empty pools in the late 1970s. After skaters learned to ollie on flat ground in the early 1980s, they carried those tricks to curbs, ledges and rails everywhere.

This unit has two halves. The first teaches the tricks step by step, starting on a low painted curb or flat bar with a helmet, wrist guards and knee pads: the 50-50, the 5-0, the boardslide, the noseslide and the tailslide, plus how to approach, where to look and how to bail. The second half explains the physics underneath every grind: the friction force, the normal force, how fast a grind slows and how far it goes.

By the end, you will be able to explain each trick's steps with front foot and back foot, and use f = μN, a = μg and d = v²/(2μg) to predict how far a board will slide. You will also see why a little wax or a little more speed can make a grind go so much farther.

The big question

How do skaters use balance, speed and friction to ride along an edge, and how can physics predict how far they will go?

The story

The First Scrape

A skater waxes a painted curb and hears her trucks grind for the very first time.

Marisol kneels beside the low painted curb at the edge of the skatepark's flat area. The curb is about as tall as her shoe is long. She pulls a small block of skate wax from her pocket and rubs it along the top edge in short strokes. A thin, shiny film builds up on the yellow paint. She tests it with a fingertip. It feels slick, almost greasy.

She buckles her helmet and checks that it sits low on her forehead, two fingers above her eyebrows. Wrist guards, knee pads, flat shoes. For weeks she has practiced her ollie on the open concrete until she could pop it while rolling every time. Today she wants to put that ollie on top of the curb.

She pushes off slowly and rides beside the curb, close and almost parallel, the way her coach showed her. On the first three tries she only ollies up onto the curb top and rolls off again. Her coach nods and says that is exactly the right first step. On the fourth try she aims her board at the edge itself, keeps her knees bent and looks at the far end of the curb instead of at her feet.

Both trucks land on the corner of the curb. For a moment she hears a long, rough scrape, metal on painted concrete, and feels a buzz come up through her shoes. Then the board slows and tips off the side. She steps off with both feet, jogs a few steps and laughs. It lasted barely a meter, but it was a grind. She walks back to the start, rubs on a little more wax and lines up again.

Talk about itMarisol practiced ollying onto the curb before she tried to grind it. Why might that smaller step help her feel ready?
Section 1

Getting Ready to Grind

21.1

Grinds, Slides and a Safe Start

Main ideaA grind rides on the trucks and a slide rides on the deck, and both start on something low with your gear on.

Skaters use two words for riding along an edge. In a , the metal trucks scrape along the edge. The axle and the hanger, the T-shaped metal part that holds the axle, take the contact. In a , the wooden underside of the deck rubs along the edge instead. Both tricks use a , a long edge like a curb or a concrete block, or a , a metal bar or pipe.

Start low. A painted curb at a skatepark or a flat bar a few inches off the ground is the right first obstacle. A flat bar is a straight metal rail that sits level, close to the ground, on short legs. If you fall from a low curb, you only drop a short way. Stairs, handrails and tall ledges come much later, at a skatepark, when a skater has years of control.

Before you try, put on a helmet that fits snugly and is buckled, plus wrist guards and knee pads. Wear flat-soled shoes that grip the grip tape. Pick a dry, smooth place away from cars and away from people walking. Most grinds and slides need a steady ollie while rolling, so practice that first. If your ollie gets you a few inches off flat ground every time, you are ready to begin.

21.2

Wax on the Edge

Main ideaSkate wax makes an edge slicker so the board slides farther, and a thin coat is enough.

New concrete and fresh paint can grab a board and stop it short. Skaters fix this with . Skate wax comes in small blocks sold for skating. Some skaters use plain paraffin, the kind of wax used in candles. Rub the block along the top corner of the ledge in short strokes, then rub it in with the side of the block until the edge looks shiny.

A thin coat works best. Too little and the trucks still catch. Too much and the edge gets so slick the board can shoot out from under you, or it builds up in sticky lumps. Try one run, then add a little more if the board stops short. Wax wears off as people skate, so a ledge may need a fresh coat after a session.

Wax only edges where skating is allowed, like the curbs and ledges at a skatepark. Wax leaves marks and can make a public step slippery for people walking. A good habit is to bring your own small block, wax just the edge you are using, and leave the area as clean as you found it. Wax also works on a flat bar, though metal rails are already smoother than concrete.

21.3

Practice Without Rolling

Main ideaSetting the board on the edge while standing still lets you feel the balance point before you add speed.

Before rolling, learn what a grind feels like with the board still. Set your board on the curb so both trucks sit on the top corner and the wheels hang over the side you will ride from. Hold a friend’s shoulder or a fence. Step your back foot over the back bolts, then your front foot over the front bolts. The bolts are the screw heads that hold the trucks to the deck.

Now feel where your weight must go. Lean too far toward the ground side and the board tips off. Lean toward the top of the curb and the wheels drop onto it. Somewhere in the middle, with your knees bent and your shoulders level, the board balances on the edge. That middle spot is the feeling you are hunting for when you are moving.

Next, try a rolling drill that is not a grind at all. Ride slowly next to the curb, almost parallel, and ollie up onto the flat top of the curb. Roll along the top and off the end. This teaches you to judge the distance and the timing. When that feels easy ten times in a row, start aiming a little lower, so your trucks meet the edge itself.

Section 2

Grinding on the Trucks

21.4

The 50-50

Main ideaIn a 50-50, both trucks grind the edge at once with your weight centered between them.

The is the first grind most skaters learn. Both trucks grind the edge at the same time, so the weight is shared fifty-fifty. It came from pool skating in the late 1970s, when skaters ground the coping, the rounded edge at the top of a pool wall. On flat ground it is done on curbs, ledges and flat bars.

Here are the steps. First, roll toward the curb at a slow, steady speed, nearly parallel to it, with your feet in ollie position: back foot on the tail, front foot just behind the front bolts. Next, when your front wheels are close to the edge, ollie up and a little toward it. Then guide the board so both trucks land on the corner at once. Bend your knees as they land and keep your weight centered between the trucks.

Ride the edge with your shoulders lined up with the curb. At the end, let the board roll off the end and land with bent knees, rolling away. A common problem is landing with only the back truck on, which tips the nose down onto the top. The fix is to ollie a little higher and level the board in the air. Another is landing too far onto the top. Then come in closer to the edge before you pop.

Think it through
A 50-50, Step by Step

Step 1: Roll slowly beside the curb, almost parallel. Step 2: Feet in ollie position, knees bent. Step 3: Pop the tail with the back foot and slide the front foot up. Step 4: Level the board in the air and steer it toward the edge. Step 5: Both trucks land on the corner. Step 6: Knees stay bent, eyes on the far end. Step 7: Roll off the end and ride away.

21.5

The 5-0

Main ideaA 5-0 grinds on the back truck only, balanced like a manual with the nose up.

In a (say five-oh), only the back truck grinds. The nose stays up in the air and the front truck hangs above the edge. If you have practiced a , balancing on the back wheels while rolling on flat ground, you already know the feeling. A 5-0 is a manual done on an edge instead of on the ground.

The steps begin like a 50-50. Roll toward the curb nearly parallel at a slow, steady speed. Ollie up toward the edge, but keep a little more weight on the back foot as you land, so the back truck meets the corner first and the nose stays up. Your back foot sits over the back bolts, not on the very end of the tail. Your front foot stays light over the front bolts, steering the nose level.

The hard part is the balance. If you lean too far back, the tail drags on the curb and the board stops. If you lean too far forward, the front truck drops and the trick turns into a 50-50. Aim to keep the nose a few inches up and steady. At the end, press the front foot down a little as the board rolls off, so both trucks come down together on the ground.

21.6

Angle and Speed

Main ideaCome in at a shallow angle and a steady, comfortable speed so the trucks lock on and carry to the end.

The is the path you ride toward the obstacle. For a 50-50 or 5-0, come in at a shallow angle, nearly parallel to the edge, but slightly toward it. A good picture is a door opened only a little. If you ride straight at the curb, the board will hit the edge and stop, or fly over the top. If you ride exactly parallel and far away, you cannot reach the edge when you pop.

Speed matters as much as angle. A friction force slows the board the whole time it grinds, so a board that arrives slowly stops partway. A board that arrives too fast is hard to steer onto the edge and hard to control at the end. Start at an easy rolling speed, a little faster than a brisk walk, and add a little speed on each try once you can land on the edge.

You can also choose a side. In a grind your chest and toes face the edge as you approach. In a grind your back and heels face it. Both are normal, and each skater may feel more comfortable on one side first. Pick one side, practice it until it feels steady, then learn the other.

Section 3

Sliding on the Deck

21.7

The Boardslide

Main ideaIn a boardslide the board turns across the rail and the middle of the deck slides along it, between the trucks.

In a , the board sits across the rail, crosswise, with the rail between the two trucks. The middle of the deck’s underside slides along the metal. A low flat bar is the classic place to learn it, because the bar is narrow and slick. The board points across the bar while you slide along it.

Here are the steps. First, roll toward the flat bar nearly straight on, but just to one side of its end. Next, ollie and turn your shoulders and the board a quarter turn, so the board crosses over the bar. Then land with the bar under the middle of the deck, between your feet. Keep your knees bent and your weight right over the bar, with your head above it.

To come off, turn your shoulders back a quarter turn at the end of the bar, so the board points forward again, and roll away. A common problem is leaning away from the direction you are sliding. Your feet then shoot forward and you sit down. The fix is to keep your weight over the bar and your eyes on its end. Another is landing with a truck on the bar, which hangs up the board; ollie a little higher to clear it.

21.8

The Noseslide

Main ideaIn a noseslide, the board turns a quarter turn and only the nose slides on the ledge.

The is the front end of the deck, past the front truck. In a , you ollie up, turn the board a quarter turn, and set only the underside of the nose on the ledge. The rest of the board hangs off the ground side, and the back wheels stay in the air. A waxed ledge works better than a round rail for learning it, because the nose sits flat on top.

Here are the steps. First, roll toward the ledge nearly parallel at an easy speed. Next, pop an ollie and turn your shoulders and the board a quarter turn toward the ledge. Then press your front foot onto the nose so the nose lands on the top edge. Most of your weight should sit over the front foot. Keep your back foot light on the tail and your knees bent.

To come off, push lightly with the front foot and turn your shoulders back a quarter turn, so the board lines up with your path and rolls away. A common problem is not getting enough weight on the nose. The board then slips off right away. The fix is to lean forward over the front foot and look toward the end of the ledge. Another is turning too far, past a quarter turn, so you come off pointing the wrong way.

21.9

The Tailslide

Main ideaA tailslide sets the tail on the ledge with the board turned a quarter turn and the weight over the back foot.

The is the back end of the deck, past the back truck, the part you pop for an ollie. A is the back-end partner of the noseslide. You ollie, turn the board a quarter turn, and set the underside of the tail on the top edge of the ledge. The front of the board points out over the ground and the front wheels hang in the air.

Here are the steps. First, ride toward the ledge nearly parallel at an easy, steady speed. Next, ollie a little higher than usual, because the tail must reach the top of the ledge. Then turn your shoulders and the board a quarter turn and press the tail down onto the edge with your back foot. Keep most of your weight over the back foot, with your front foot steadying the board over the front bolts.

To come off, turn your shoulders back a quarter turn and let the board drop to the ground pointing forward, with knees bent. A common problem is the tail landing below the top of the ledge, against its side. The board then stops hard. The fix is to pop higher and keep the front knee bent so the board levels out. Many skaters find the tailslide harder than the noseslide, because the slide happens behind them, where they cannot see it as easily.

Section 4

Eyes, Exits and Bails

21.10

Look Where You Want to Go

Main ideaKeeping your eyes on the end of the ledge keeps your shoulders, hips and weight lined up for the whole grind.

Your body tends to follow your eyes. If you stare down at your front foot during a grind, your head drops and your shoulders tip forward. That moves your weight off the balance point, and the board slides off early. Coaches tell skaters to look at the end of the ledge or rail instead, the spot where they plan to roll off.

Looking ahead also lines up your . For a 50-50 or 5-0, your shoulders should point along the ledge, the same direction as your path. For a boardslide, noseslide or tailslide, your shoulders turn with the board for the slide, then turn back for the exit. Your head and eyes lead the turn, and your shoulders and hips follow.

The is how you leave the obstacle. On a low curb, the simplest exit is rolling off the end and landing with bent knees, both feet over the bolts. Plan your exit before you start the trick, and look at that spot from the very beginning. A skater who knows where the trick ends rides it with more confidence than one who is only thinking about getting on.

21.11

Bailing Safely

Main ideaA good bail means stepping off and running it out when you can, and falling low and rolling when you cannot.

To is to leave the board on purpose when a trick goes wrong. Every skater bails many times while learning a grind. The safest bail is a step-off: as soon as the board stops or tips, step both feet onto the ground on the side away from the obstacle and jog a few steps. The board may roll away, so check that no one is in its path.

Sometimes there is no time to step off. Then fall small. Bend your knees and get low, so you drop a short distance. Tuck your chin. Let your body roll onto the meaty side of your shoulder, your hip or your back instead of stopping all at once. Slide on your knee pads if you fall forward. Do not throw out locked, straight arms to catch yourself. Wrist injuries are among the most common skateboarding injuries, and wrist guards help prevent them.

Fear is a normal signal, not a weakness. If a trick scares you, make it smaller. Go back to the standing drill, lower the speed, or practice ollying onto the curb top again. A short practice session with many small, controlled tries builds a grind faster than a few big, scary ones. Stop when you are tired, because tired legs land badly.

21.12

A Step-by-Step Practice Plan

Main ideaGrinds and slides come in a sensible order, each one building on the skills of the one before.

Grinds and slides build on each other, so the order you learn them matters. A common path starts with a steady rolling ollie, then ollying onto the curb top, then the 50-50. The 50-50 teaches you to land on an edge with your weight centered. Next comes the 5-0, which adds the manual’s balance on the back truck.

Slides usually come after you are comfortable on your trucks. A boardslide on a low flat bar teaches the quarter turn on and off. The noseslide and tailslide add turning plus putting most of your weight over one end. Each new trick reuses a skill you already have: the ollie, the quarter turn, a manual’s balance, or looking at the end of the obstacle.

Keep each practice short and focused. Choose one trick and one side, frontside or backside, for a session. Warm up with ollies on flat ground. Count your clean tries instead of your falls. When one trick works on a low curb most of the time, it is ready for a slightly longer or slightly higher ledge at the skatepark, one small change at a time.

The story

Same Speed, Twice as Far

Two skaters hit the same ledge at the same speed, and one slides twice as far.

Deshawn and Ana take turns on a long, low concrete ledge at their neighborhood skatepark. They have both learned the 50-50. They want to know who can grind farther. A friend stands beside the ledge with a tape measure and a phone timer.

To make it fair, they mark a starting line on the ground with chalk and push the same number of times. The friend times each skater over the last two meters before the ledge. Both arrive at about 3 meters per second, a little faster than a jog. Deshawn grinds first and comes off after a little over a meter. Ana grinds the next section of the same ledge and slides about twice as far.

Nothing about the skaters changed. Their speed was the same and their weight hardly mattered. What changed was the surface, and with it the friction between the trucks and the edge. The measuring tape showed a pattern a physicist would recognize: cut the friction in half, and the slide distance doubles.

Section 1

The Friction Force

22.1

What Friction Does

Main ideaFriction is a force between two touching surfaces that pushes against the sliding, so it slows a grind.

When metal trucks scrape along a concrete edge, they slow down. The force doing this is , a force between two surfaces that are touching. Friction always points against the sliding. If the board slides forward along the ledge, friction on the trucks points backward. That backward push is what eventually stops every grind and slide.

Physicists split friction into two kinds. Static friction holds two surfaces still against each other, like the grip between your shoe and the grip tape. Kinetic friction acts while two surfaces slide past each other, like the trucks on a ledge. For most pairs of surfaces, kinetic friction is a bit weaker than the most static friction can give. That is why a stuck board is harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.

Friction comes from the surfaces themselves. Up close, even a smooth rail has tiny bumps and pits. As the truck slides, those bumps catch, scrape and break, and the surfaces briefly stick together. A rough surface catches more. A slick coating, like wax, fills the pits and lets the surfaces slip. Isaac Newton’s first law explains the rest: the board would keep moving if no force pushed against it.

From the source
Newton's First Law
Every body keeps on in its state of rest, or of moving at a steady speed in a straight line, unless forces acting on it make it change that state.
Isaac Newton, Principia (1687), Law I; paraphrased from Andrew Motte's English translation (1729) (paraphrased)

22.2

The Normal Force

Main ideaThe normal force is the edge pushing up on the board; on a level ledge it equals the skater's weight.

When you stand on a board on a ledge, gravity pulls you down. The ledge pushes back up. That upward push is called the . Normal here means at a right angle, because the force points straight out of the surface. On a level ledge, with the skater not bouncing, the normal force exactly balances the weight.

Weight is the pull of gravity on a mass. It equals mass times g, where g is 9.8 meters per second squared near Earth’s surface. Weight is a force, so it is measured in newtons, written N. One newton is about the weight of a small apple. Mass is measured in kilograms and stays the same anywhere; weight would change on the Moon.

Here is a worked example. A skater and board together have a mass of 60 kilograms. Weight = m times g = 60 kg times 9.8 m/s squared = 588 N. On a level ledge, the normal force is also 588 N. If the skater pushes down hard or lands from an ollie, the normal force jumps above 588 N for a moment, and so does the friction.

22.3

Friction Equals Mu Times N

Main ideaKinetic friction force equals the coefficient of friction times the normal force: f = μN.

Experiments show a simple pattern for sliding friction. Press two surfaces together twice as hard and the friction roughly doubles. Scientists write this as f = μN. Here f is the friction force, N is the normal force, and μ, the Greek letter mu, is the . The coefficient is a plain number with no unit. It describes how grabby a pair of surfaces is.

The coefficient belongs to a pair of surfaces, not to one. Steel trucks on waxed steel have one value. The same trucks on rough concrete have a larger one. For example, a waxed metal rail might be around 0.2 and bare rough concrete around 0.5. These are example numbers, not fixed constants; real values change with wax, dirt, heat and wear.

Here is a worked example. The 60 kg skater from before grinds a ledge with an example coefficient μ = 0.30. The normal force is 588 N. Friction f = μN = 0.30 times 588 N = 176.4 N, about 176 N. That backward push is a little less than a third of the skater’s weight. Notice that the size of the trucks’ contact patch does not appear in the formula. For many surfaces, friction depends on the push, not on the area touching.

Section 2

How Fast a Grind Slows

22.4

Slowing Equals Mu Times g

Main ideaOn a level rail, a grinding board slows at a = μg, so the skater's mass cancels out.

Newton’s second law says force equals mass times acceleration, F = ma. Acceleration is how fast speed changes, in meters per second, each second. When friction is the only push along the ledge, it causes a , a slowing down. Divide the friction force by the mass and you get how quickly the board loses speed.

Put the pieces together. Friction f = μN, and on a level ledge N = mg. So f = μmg. The slowing is a = f divided by m = μmg divided by m. The m on top and the m on the bottom cancel, leaving a = μg. A heavier skater pushes harder on the ledge and gets more friction, but also has more mass to slow down. The two effects cancel exactly.

Here is a worked example. With μ = 0.30, the slowing is a = 0.30 times 9.8 m/s squared = 2.94 m/s squared. Each second, the board loses about 2.94 meters per second of speed. A board that starts the grind at 3.0 m/s stops after t = 3.0 divided by 2.94, about 1.0 second. That matches what skaters see: a grind on a bare ledge lasts about a second unless they bring more speed.

22.5

How Far a Grind Goes

Main ideaA grind that starts at speed v slides a distance d = v² divided by (2μg) before it stops.

If you know how fast the board starts and how quickly it slows, you can find how far it goes. For steady slowing from speed v down to zero, the is d = v squared divided by 2a. On a level ledge, a = μg, so d = v squared divided by (2μg). Here v is the speed in meters per second as the grind starts.

Here is a worked example, step by step. A skater starts a 50-50 at v = 3.0 m/s on a ledge with an example μ = 0.30. First, square the speed: 3.0 times 3.0 = 9.0. Next, find the bottom: 2 times 0.30 times 9.8 = 5.88. Then divide: d = 9.0 divided by 5.88 = 1.53 meters. The board grinds about one and a half meters before friction stops it.

The formula also works backward. Suppose you want to grind a ledge 2.0 m long with an example μ = 0.25. Solve for v: v squared = 2 times μ times g times d = 2 times 0.25 times 9.8 times 2.0 = 9.8. So v = the square root of 9.8, about 3.1 m/s. Arrive slower than that and the board stops before the end.

22.6

Why Speed Carries a Grind

Main ideaSlide distance grows with the square of speed, so doubling your speed makes a grind go four times as far.

Look at the formula again: d = v squared divided by (2μg). The speed is squared. That means distance does not grow in step with speed. It grows much faster. Double the speed and the distance becomes 2 times 2 = 4 times as long. Triple the speed and it becomes 3 times 3 = 9 times as long.

Here is a worked example with μ = 0.30, so 2μg = 5.88. At 1.0 m/s, d = 1.0 divided by 5.88 = 0.17 m. At 2.0 m/s, d = 4.0 divided by 5.88 = 0.68 m. At 4.0 m/s, d = 16 divided by 5.88 = 2.72 m. Going from 2 to 4 m/s, the speed doubled and the distance went from 0.68 m to 2.72 m, four times as far.

This is why skaters say speed is your friend on a long ledge, and why the same idea also calls for care. More speed carries a grind farther, but it also means more energy to manage if the trick goes wrong. A safe plan adds a little speed at a time. If your grind keeps stopping halfway, a small increase in speed often fixes it, because a small change in speed makes a bigger change in distance.

22.7

Wax Halves the Mu

Main ideaLowering the coefficient of friction makes a grind go farther; halving μ doubles the slide distance.

In the formula d = v squared divided by (2μg), μ sits on the bottom. When the bottom gets smaller, the answer gets bigger. Cut μ in half and the distance doubles. This is the pattern Deshawn and Ana measured: same speed, same ledge, but the waxed half let the trucks slide about twice as far.

Here is a worked example. Both skaters arrive at 3.0 m/s, so v squared = 9.0. On bare concrete, with an example μ = 0.40, the bottom is 2 times 0.40 times 9.8 = 7.84, so d = 9.0 divided by 7.84 = 1.15 m. On the waxed half, with an example μ = 0.20, the bottom is 2 times 0.20 times 9.8 = 3.92, so d = 9.0 divided by 3.92 = 2.30 m. Half the μ, twice the distance.

Wax lowers μ by putting a soft, slippery layer between the metal truck and the hard edge. The truck glides over the wax instead of catching on bumps in the concrete or paint. As skaters grind, the wax gets scraped away and pushed off the ends, so μ creeps back up. That is why the first grinds after waxing feel fast and later ones slow down.

Metal, Concrete and Paint

Main ideaDifferent edges have different friction: smooth metal is usually slick, bare concrete grabs, and paint changes as it wears.

Skateparks build edges from a few materials, and each grinds differently. Many ledges have a , a strip of steel angle iron along the top corner. Steel is smooth and hard, so trucks slide along it well, and the steel protects the concrete from chipping. Round steel rails and flat bars are also slick, especially after a little wax.

Bare concrete is rougher. Its surface has sand and small stones in it, which catch the trucks and raise the coefficient of friction. Fresh concrete can feel sticky until skaters wear it smooth and wax it. Concrete also crumbles at the corner over time, so a bare edge can get chipped, which makes the board hang up.

Paint changes over time. A newly painted curb can feel slick at first because the paint fills the rough pits in the concrete. As skaters grind it, the paint wears through in patches, and those bare spots grab. For example, a skater may feel the board slow suddenly when the trucks reach a chipped patch. Checking an edge with your hand before skating it is a smart habit.

22.9

Heat and Wear

Main ideaFriction turns the board's motion energy into heat and wears away the metal, wood and wax it rubs.

A moving board carries , the energy of motion, equal to one half times mass times speed squared: KE = ½mv². When friction stops a grind, that energy does not vanish. It turns into in the trucks and the edge, and a little into sound. That is the rough scraping noise of a grind. Rub your hands together fast and you feel the same effect.

Here is a worked example. A 60 kg skater starts a grind at 3.0 m/s. KE = 0.5 times 60 times 3.0 squared = 0.5 times 60 times 9.0 = 270 joules. A joule is the SI unit of energy. Friction does work equal to force times distance: 176.4 N times 1.53 m = about 270 joules. The two numbers match, because friction used up all the kinetic energy.

Friction also causes , the slow loss of material from rubbing. Trucks are usually made of aluminum, which is softer than steel, so grinding steel rails shaves tiny amounts off the hangers over time. Slides wear the wood under the nose, tail and middle of the deck. Wax wears off ledges. A worn deck can get thin or chipped, so checking your board for cracks is part of grinding safely.

Where the Model Stops

Main ideaThe formula is a good first model, but real grinds also depend on landing, slope, balance and changing friction.

Every physics formula is a , a simplified picture that matches reality in some ways. The formula d = v squared divided by (2μg) assumes a level edge, a steady μ and a board that slides the whole way. Real grinds break those rules in small ways. Knowing where a model stops helps you use it well.

Landing on the edge is one example. When the trucks hit the ledge after an ollie, the normal force spikes above the skater’s weight for a split second. Friction spikes with it, so the board loses extra speed right at the start. Balance is another. If the skater leans and the board tips, the grind ends early, no matter what the formula says.

Slope changes the math too. On a gently sloped flat bar at a skatepark, gravity pulls the board along the slope and partly cancels friction, so the board slows less. On a sloped edge going up, gravity adds to the slowing. The coefficient also changes along a real edge as wax thins and paint wears. Even so, the main ideas hold: more speed and less friction mean a longer grind.

Why: In a = μmg divided by m, mass cancels.

12. A board starts at 2.0 m/s with μ = 0.30 (2μg = 5.88). How far does it grind?

Why: d = 4.0 divided by 5.88 = about 0.68 m.

13. Two skaters arrive at the same speed. One edge has half the μ of the other. How do their grinds compare?

Why: μ is on the bottom of d = v²/(2μg), so halving μ doubles d.

14. What happens to the board's kinetic energy as friction stops a grind?

Why: Friction turns motion energy into heat and a little sound.

15. Why does a steel ledge cap usually give a longer grind than bare concrete?

Why: Concrete's sand and stones catch the trucks; smooth steel lets them slide.
