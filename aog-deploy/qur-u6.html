Prophets in the Qur'an

Stories

Muslims believe God sent many prophets, and the Qur'an tells their stories. Many of them are people the Bible tells about too: Adam, Nuh (Noah), Ibrahim (Abraham), Yusuf (Joseph), Musa (Moses), Maryam (Mary) and Isa (Jesus). In this unit you will read how the Qur'an tells these stories, and you will set them beside the Bible's telling.

You will find a boy in a well, a baby in a basket, a fire that turned cool, and a baby who spoke from the cradle. You will meet the only woman the Qur'an calls by name. And you will learn what the Qur'an says about Muhammad, whom Muslims believe was the last prophet.

By the end you will be able to name the main prophets in the Qur'an, tell what the Qur'an says about each, and compare a Qur'an story with a Bible story. You will practice saying "the Qur'an says" and "Muslims believe," the way people study holy books together with respect.

Big questionHow does the Qur'an tell the stories of the early prophets, and how are they like the Bible's stories?
The story

Eleven Stars, the Sun and the Moon

A boy has a strange dream, and his father tells him to keep it secret.

A boy wakes up early. He runs to find his father. "Father," he says, "I saw eleven stars in my dream. I saw the sun and the moon too. They were all bowing down to me." His father listens carefully. He does not laugh. He looks worried and proud at the same time.

"My son," the father says, "do not tell your brothers about this dream. They might grow jealous. They might plan something against you." The boy has eleven brothers. His father loves him very much, and the brothers know it. The father thinks the dream means something big is coming.

The boy is Yusuf (Joseph). His father is Yaqub (Jacob). The Qur'an tells this story in Surah 12, a chapter named Yusuf. It calls the story "the best of stories." It is the only long story the Qur'an tells from beginning to end in one surah. Muslims read it in Ramadan and at home.

The Bible tells about Joseph too, in the book of Genesis. In the Bible, Joseph also dreams of eleven stars, the sun and the moon. Jews, Christians and Muslims all know this boy. But each book tells his story in its own way. In this chapter, you will read how the Qur'an tells about Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim and Yusuf.

Section 1

Many Prophets, One Message

11.1

What a Prophet Is

Main ideaMuslims believe God sent many prophets to bring people the same message, and the Qur'an names 25 of them.

A is a person who brings a message from God. Muslims believe God sent prophets to every people. The Qur’an says that some prophets are named in the book and some are not. It names 25 prophets. Some are called messengers too, because Muslims believe they brought a book or a new law.

Many of the prophets in the Qur’an are people the Bible tells about. The Qur’an gives their names in Arabic. Adam is Adam. Nuh is Noah. Ibrahim is Abraham. Yusuf is Joseph. Musa is Moses. Isa is Jesus. Muslims believe the last prophet was Muhammad.

Muslims believe all the prophets taught one message. The message is that there is one God, and people should worship God and be good. In the Qur’an, God tells the prophets’ stories to Muhammad. The Qur’an says the stories are lessons for people who think (12:111, Surah Yusuf).

11.2

Stories Shared by Three Books

Main ideaThe Qur'an, the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Bible tell about many of the same people, each in its own way.

Jews read the . Christians read the Bible, which has the Old Testament and the New Testament. Muslims read the Qur’an. All three books tell about Adam, Noah, Abraham and Joseph. That is one reason people call Judaism, Christianity and Islam "Abrahamic" faiths.

The books do not tell the stories the same way. The Bible tells a story in order, from start to finish, with many details. The Qur’an usually tells a short part of a story. It often tells the same story in more than one surah. It says what the story teaches.

The Qur’an says that God sent earlier books too. Muslims believe God gave the to Musa (Moses). They believe God gave the Gospel to Isa (Jesus). Muslims call Jews and Christians "People of the Book." In this chapter you will compare the tellings.

11.3

Adam, the First Person

Main ideaThe Qur'an says God made Adam, taught him the names of things, and told the angels to bow to him.

The Qur’an says God made Adam, the first person, and made him a on the earth. That word means someone who takes care of something for someone else. God taught Adam the names of all things. Then God told the to bow to Adam. They bowed (2:30–34, Surah Al-Baqarah).

One being did not bow. His name was . The Qur’an says he was proud. He said he was made of fire and Adam was made of clay. Muslims believe Iblis became Shaytan, the one who whispers to people to do wrong (7:11–18, Surah Al-A’raf).

Adam and his wife lived in a garden. God told them not to eat from one tree. Iblis tricked them, and they ate. They said sorry to God. The Qur’an says God forgave them and sent them to live on the earth (2:35–37; 7:19–25). The Qur’an does not give Adam’s wife a name. Muslim tradition calls her Hawwa (Eve).

11.4

Adam in Two Books

Main ideaThe Bible and the Qur'an both tell about Adam and the garden, but they tell some parts differently.

The Bible tells about Adam in the book of Genesis, chapters 2 and 3. There, a snake tells Eve to eat from the tree. Eve eats first and then gives some to Adam. God sends them out of the garden. Many Christians read this as the start of sin for all people.

The Qur’an tells the story in a shorter way. It says Shaytan whispered to both of them. It does not say the wife ate first. It says they both did wrong, both said sorry, and God forgave them. Most Muslim readers say each person is responsible for their own deeds, not for Adam’s.

Both books agree on a lot. Both say God made Adam. Both tell about a garden and a tree. Both say the first people did wrong. When you two tellings, look for what is the same and what is different. Then ask what each book wants its readers to learn.

11.5

Nuh and the Ark

Main ideaThe Qur'an says Nuh warned his people for a long time, built an ark, and was saved from a flood with those who believed.

The Qur’an tells about (Noah) in many surahs. One whole surah, Surah 71, is named Nuh. The Qur’an says Nuh told his people to worship one God. He warned them for a very long time. The Qur’an says he stayed among them 950 years (29:14, Surah Al-Ankabut). Most of them laughed at him.

God told Nuh to build an , a big boat. The Qur’an says people made fun of him while he built it. Then the flood came. Nuh took pairs of animals and the people who believed with him. The Qur’an says one of Nuh’s sons would not get in. He said he would climb a mountain. The water covered him (11:36–43, Surah Hud).

The Qur’an says the ark came to rest on a mountain called Judi (11:44). The Bible tells about Noah in Genesis, chapters 6 to 9. In the Bible, the ark rests on the mountains of Ararat, and all of Noah’s sons are saved. The Qur’an adds the son who would not believe. It uses the story to teach that each person must choose.

Ibrahim, the Friend of God

11.6

Ibrahim and the Idols

Main ideaThe Qur'an says Ibrahim broke his people's idols to show they had no power, and God kept him safe from the fire.

(Abraham) is one of the most important prophets in the Qur’an. His father’s people prayed to . Idols are statues that people treat as gods. The Qur’an says Ibrahim asked them why. The idols could not hear, see or help them (21:52–67, Surah Al-Anbiya).

One day, the Qur’an says, Ibrahim broke all the idols but the biggest one. When people asked who did it, he said, "Ask the big one!" They knew the idol could not talk. Ibrahim wanted them to see that the idols had no power. The people got angry and threw him into a fire.

The Qur’an says God told the fire to be cool and safe for Ibrahim, and he was not hurt (21:69). The Bible does not tell this story. But old Jewish stories, called midrash, tell of Abraham breaking idols too. The Qur’an calls Ibrahim a , a person who turned to the one God and away from idols.

11.7

The Test With His Son

Main ideaThe Qur'an says Ibrahim dreamed he must give up his son, both agreed to obey God, and God gave a ram in the son's place.

The Qur’an tells about a hard test. Ibrahim dreamed that he must give up his son as an offering to God. He told his son about the dream. The son said, "Father, do what you are told. You will find me patient" (37:102, Surah As-Saffat). They both agreed to obey God.

When Ibrahim laid his son down, God called to him. God said he had already done what the dream asked. The Qur’an says God gave a great animal to offer in the son’s place (37:103–107). Muslims believe this shows how much Ibrahim trusted God.

The Qur’an does not say which son it was. Most Muslim scholars say it was (Ishmael), Ibrahim’s older son. Some early Muslim scholars said it was Ishaq (Isaac). The Bible, in Genesis 22, names the son as Isaac. Jews and Christians read the story with Isaac. Muslims and Jews and Christians all see it as a story about trust in God.

Think it through
One Story, Two Names

Genesis 22 says God tells Abraham to take "your son, your only son Isaac" up a mountain. The Qur'an (37:100–111) tells the dream and the test but does not name the son. Right after the story, it says God gave Ibrahim good news of Ishaq. Many Muslim scholars read that to mean the son in the test was Ismail, born before Ishaq. Others have read it differently.

11.8

Building the Kaaba, and Eid al-Adha

Main ideaThe Qur'an says Ibrahim and Ismail built the Kaaba, and Muslims remember Ibrahim's test each year at Eid al-Adha.

The is a square stone building in Mecca. Muslims face it when they pray. The Qur’an says Ibrahim and his son Ismail raised its walls and prayed that God would accept their work (2:127, Surah Al-Baqarah). Muslims believe it is the first house built for worshiping the one God.

Every year, Muslims from around the world go to Mecca for the . It is a special trip. Many things they do there remember Ibrahim’s family. They walk between two hills. Muslim tradition says Ismail’s mother Hajar did that. She was looking for water for her baby.

At the end of the hajj comes , "the feast of the offering." Muslims everywhere celebrate it, not just in Mecca. Families who can afford it give an animal. They share the meat and give some to people in need. It remembers the day Ibrahim was ready to give up his son. In Chicago, families gather for morning prayers and visit each other.

One bag goes to the family, one to friends, and one to people who need food. Every part of the day points back to a story about Ibrahim.

Section 4

Yusuf, the Best of Stories

11.9

The Well and Egypt

Main ideaSurah 12 tells how Yusuf's brothers put him in a well, and how he was carried to Egypt and wrongly put in prison.

Surah 12 is the only surah that tells one story from start to finish. It has 111 ayahs. It begins by calling itself "the best of stories." Then it tells Yusuf’s dream, the one you read at the start of this chapter. His father, Yaqub, warned him not to tell his brothers.

The brothers were . They asked to take Yusuf out to play. Then they put him in a well. They came home crying and said a wolf ate him. They showed their father Yusuf’s shirt with fake blood on it. Yaqub did not believe them. He said, "Patience is beautiful" (12:18).

A stopped at the well for water and found Yusuf. They sold him in Egypt to a rich man. Later the man’s wife tried to make Yusuf do wrong. Yusuf refused. The Qur’an says his shirt was torn from the back, which showed he was running away. Even so, he was put in prison. The Qur’an does not name the man or his wife. The Bible calls the man Potiphar.

11.10

Dreams, Prison and the King

Main ideaIn prison, Yusuf explained dreams, and the king's dream about cows brought him out to save Egypt from hunger.

In prison, Yusuf met two men who had strange dreams. He told them what their dreams meant, and he told them about the one God. One man was set free, as Yusuf said. But that man forgot Yusuf for years, and Yusuf stayed in prison (12:36–42).

Then the king of Egypt had a dream. He saw seven fat cows eaten by seven thin cows, and seven green ears of grain and seven dry ones. Nobody could explain it. The freed man finally remembered Yusuf. Yusuf said there would be seven good years, then seven years of , when little food grows. He told them to store grain (12:43–49).

Yusuf asked that his name be cleared first. The wife who had lied said the truth: Yusuf had done nothing wrong (12:51). The king made Yusuf the one in charge of Egypt’s storehouses. In the Bible, in Genesis 41, Pharaoh has the same dream and Joseph is also put in charge. The Qur’an says "the king"; the Bible says "Pharaoh."

11.11

The Brothers Return

Main ideaThe Qur'an ends Yusuf's story with forgiveness: he tells his brothers there is no blame on them, and the family is together again.

The famine reached Yusuf’s family too. His brothers came to Egypt to buy grain. They did not know the man in charge was Yusuf. He knew them. He told them to bring their youngest brother, Binyamin (Benjamin), next time. When they did, Yusuf found a way to keep Binyamin with him (12:58–79).

At last Yusuf told them who he was. The brothers were afraid. Yusuf said, "There is no blame on you today. May God forgive you" (12:92). He sent his shirt home. When it touched his father’s face, Yaqub, who had cried until he could not see, could see again (12:93–96).

The whole family came to Egypt. The Qur’an says Yusuf’s parents and brothers bowed to him, and Yusuf said his childhood dream had come true (12:100). The Bible, in Genesis 45 to 47, also tells how Joseph forgave his brothers and brought the family to Egypt. Both books make the end of the story.

A Surah Read to a King

A small group of Muslims stands before a Christian king far from home, and one of them begins to recite.

Muslim tradition tells this story. It is about the year 615. In Mecca, the first Muslims were being treated badly. Muhammad told some of them to cross the sea to Abyssinia, a land in Africa. Today it is Ethiopia. A Christian king ruled there. Muslims call him the Negus. Muhammad said the king was fair and would not let anyone be harmed.

Leaders from Mecca followed them. They brought gifts to the king. They asked him to send the Muslims back. The king wanted to hear both sides. He called the Muslims to his court. He asked, "What is this new religion of yours?"

A man named Ja'far stood up. He was Muhammad's cousin. He said that they used to pray to idols and be unkind. Then God sent a messenger who told them to worship one God, tell the truth, and care for the poor. The king asked if Ja'far had any of the words from God. Ja'far began to recite Surah 19, the surah named Maryam.

The surah tells about Zakariya, about Maryam (Mary), and about the birth of her son Isa (Jesus). The story says the king wept as he listened. He said these words and the words of Jesus came from the same light. He would not send the Muslims back. This story comes from the sira, the Muslim tradition about the Prophet's life. It shows how early Muslims talked about Maryam and Isa with Christians.

In this chapter you will read what the Qur'an says about Musa (Moses). You will read about Maryam, Isa and Muhammad. You will see what the Qur'an says about Jews and Christians, the People of the Book. And you will see how three religions share a line of prophets. You will also see where their beliefs are different.

Section 1

Musa, the Prophet Named Most

12.1

A Baby in a Basket

Main ideaThe Qur'an says Musa's mother put him in the river to save him, and Pharaoh's own family took him in.

(Moses) is the prophet named most often in the Qur’an. His name appears more than 130 times. His story is told in many surahs. The Qur’an says that , the king of Egypt, was cruel to the Children of Israel, the people of Yaqub. He ordered their baby boys killed (28:4, Surah Al-Qasas).

The Qur’an says God told Musa’s mother what to do. When she was afraid, she should put her baby in the river and not worry. God promised to bring him back to her (28:7). She did it. Musa’s sister followed the basket along the shore to see where it went.

Pharaoh’s family found the baby. Pharaoh’s wife said, "Do not kill him. He may be a joy to us" (28:9). The baby would not drink from any nurse. Musa’s sister said she knew a family who could feed him. So Musa’s own mother got to nurse him, just as God had promised (28:12–13). The Bible tells this story in Exodus 2, where Pharaoh’s daughter finds the baby.

Think it through
Side by Side: Who Found the Baby?

Exodus 2 says Pharaoh's daughter came down to the river, saw the basket, and took the baby as her own son. The Qur'an (28:8–9) says Pharaoh's household picked him up, and Pharaoh's wife asked to keep him as a joy for them both. In both books, the baby's sister watches, and his own mother is brought to nurse him.

12.2

The Burning Bush and Pharaoh

Main ideaThe Qur'an says God spoke to Musa from a fire, gave him signs, and sent him with his brother Harun to Pharaoh.

When Musa was grown, the Qur’an says, he left Egypt and lived in Midian for years. One night, traveling with his family, he saw a fire on a mountain. He went toward it. God spoke to him from the fire in a holy valley called Tuwa (20:9–16, Surah Ta-Ha).

God gave Musa two . His staff turned into a snake, and when he put his hand under his arm, it came out shining white. God told him to go to Pharaoh. Musa asked God to send his brother (Aaron) with him, because Harun spoke well (20:17–36).

Musa and Harun went to Pharaoh and told him to let the Children of Israel go. Pharaoh called his magicians. They threw down ropes that looked like snakes. Musa threw down his staff and it swallowed them. The Qur’an says the magicians bowed and believed (20:65–70). Pharaoh would not. The Bible tells this in Exodus 3 to 7.

12.3

The Sea and the Mountain

Main ideaThe Qur'an says God parted the sea for the Children of Israel, and Musa later received God's words on the mountain.

The Qur’an says Musa led the Children of Israel out of Egypt by night. Pharaoh’s army chased them to the sea. The people were afraid. Musa said God was with him and would show him the way. God told Musa to strike the sea with his staff. It split, and each side stood like a great mountain (26:61–63, Surah Ash-Shu’ara).

The people crossed on dry ground. Pharaoh’s army followed, and the water came back over them. The Qur’an says Pharaoh cried out that he believed, but it was too late (10:90–92, Surah Yunus). The Bible tells the crossing in Exodus 14. Both books make it the moment the people are saved.

Then Musa went up the mountain to meet God. He was gone forty nights. The Qur’an says God gave him with teachings on them (7:142–145, Surah Al-A’raf). While he was away, the people made a golden calf and worshiped it. Musa came back angry. Muslims believe God gave Musa the . In the Bible, Moses also brings tablets down Mount Sinai.

Section 2

Maryam and Isa

12.4

A Surah Named for Maryam

Main ideaMaryam (Mary) is the only woman named in the Qur'an, and Surah 19 tells about her childhood and her son.

(Mary) is the only woman the Qur’an calls by name. Surah 19 is named for her. The Qur’an says her mother promised her baby to God’s service before she was born. When the baby was a girl, the mother named her Maryam and asked God to protect her (3:35–36, Surah Al Imran).

The Qur’an says Maryam grew up in a place of prayer. A prophet named took care of her. Whenever he visited, he found food with her. He asked where it came from. She said it came from God (3:37). Seeing this, Zakariya prayed for a child of his own, and God gave him Yahya (John).

The Qur’an says the angels told Maryam that God had chosen her above the women of the worlds (3:42). Muslims honor Maryam very much. Many Muslim girls are named after her. Christians also honor Mary, and she appears in the Gospels of Matthew and Luke in the Bible. The Bible does not tell the story of Maryam’s childhood in the temple.

12.5

The Birth of Isa

Main ideaThe Qur'an says an angel told Maryam she would have a son, Isa, and that he spoke as a baby to defend her.

Surah 19 tells that Maryam went away by herself to the east. God sent an angel, who looked like a man. She was afraid. He said he was a messenger from God, sent to give her a pure son. Maryam asked how she could have a son, since no man had touched her. The angel said it was easy for God (19:16–21).

The Qur’an says Maryam went far away, and the pain of birth came to her under a . She wished she had died. A voice told her not to grieve. God had put a small stream by her, and if she shook the tree, fresh dates would fall (19:22–26).

She carried the baby back to her people. They were shocked. Maryam did not speak; she pointed to the baby. The Qur’an says the baby spoke from the cradle. He said, "I am the servant of God. He has given me the Book and made me a prophet" (19:29–30). The Bible tells of Jesus’s birth in Luke 2, in a stable in Bethlehem, with Joseph beside Mary. The Qur’an does not mention Joseph.

Think it through
Two Birth Stories

Luke 2 says Jesus was born in Bethlehem, laid in a manger, and shepherds came to see him. Joseph was with Mary. The Qur'an (19:22–33) says Maryam was alone under a palm tree with a stream, and dates fell to feed her. When she came home, her baby spoke to defend her. Both books say the birth was a sign from God and that Mary had no husband when the child was conceived.

12.6

Isa, the Messiah, in the Qur'an

Main ideaMuslims believe Isa was a great prophet who healed and taught by God's power, but not the son of God.

The Qur’an calls Isa the , a son of Maryam, and a messenger of God. It says God gave him the Gospel. The Qur’an says Isa did wonders by God’s permission. He healed people who were blind and people with skin disease. He made a bird from clay and it came alive. He brought the dead to life (3:49, Surah Al Imran; 5:110, Surah Al-Ma’idah).

Here Muslims and Christians agree on some things and not on others. Both believe Isa was born of Mary without a father. Both believe he healed and taught. Most Christians believe Jesus is the Son of God and part of a Trinity. The Qur’an says God has no son and is only one (4:171; 112:1–4).

The Qur’an also speaks about the cross. It says the people who tried to kill Isa did not kill him, though it seemed so. It says God raised him up (4:157–158, Surah An-Nisa). Most Muslims read this to mean Isa was not crucified. Christians believe Jesus died on a cross and rose again. These are two different beliefs about the same person. In a public school, we learn what each group believes. We do not decide who is right.

Think it through
What Each Believes About Isa

Muslims and Christians both say: Isa was born of Maryam (Mary) without a father; he healed the sick; he taught people about God. Christians say: Jesus is the Son of God; he died on a cross and rose again. Muslims say: Isa was a prophet and messenger, not God's son; God saved him, and he was not killed on the cross.

Section 3

People of the Book

12.7

Jews and Christians in the Qur'an

Main ideaThe Qur'an calls Jews and Christians People of the Book, praises some, argues with some, and tells Muslims to speak with them kindly.

The Qur’an calls Jews and Christians the . That means people who were given a holy book from God before the Qur’an. The Qur’an says Muslims may eat the food of the People of the Book. It also allows Muslim men to marry their women (5:5, Surah Al-Ma’idah).

The Qur’an says different things in different places. It praises Jews and Christians who believe in God and do good. It says they will have their reward (2:62). It also argues with some Jews and some Christians about their beliefs. It tells Muslims to argue with the People of the Book only in the best way (29:46, Surah Al-Ankabut).

Scholars say many of these verses came from real events in Medina. Muhammad lived there near Jewish tribes, and he met Christians too. Some verses praise. Some warn. Some are about one group at one time. Muslim scholars have long discussed how to read them together. Today many Muslims, Jews and Christians in Chicago meet to study each other’s books.

12.8

One Line of Prophets

Main ideaMuslims believe the prophets from Adam to Muhammad brought one message, and the three religions share many of the same figures.

Muslims believe God sent one message again and again, through many prophets. Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Dawud (David), Isa and Muhammad are all on one line. Muslims believe God gave books to some of them. The Torah went to Musa. The (Psalms) went to Dawud. The Gospel went to Isa. The Qur’an went to Muhammad.

Jews, Christians and Muslims do not agree on where the line ends. Jews honor Abraham, Moses and David, and do not count Jesus or Muhammad as prophets. Christians honor the Hebrew prophets and believe Jesus is more than a prophet. They do not count Muhammad as a prophet. Muslims count them all, and believe Muhammad was the last.

Even so, a of the three religions has many of the same names on it. When you study a figure like Abraham or Moses, ask three questions. What does the Hebrew Bible say? What does the Christian Bible say? What does the Qur’an say? Then you can compare without saying which is right.

Section 4

Muhammad, the Last Prophet

12.9

The Seal of the Prophets

Main ideaMuslims believe Muhammad was the last prophet, and the Qur'an calls him the seal of the prophets.

Muslims believe was the last prophet. The Qur’an calls him "the of the prophets" (33:40, Surah Al-Ahzab). A seal is a stamp that closes a letter. Muslims read this to mean that no prophet comes after him. Muslims say "peace be upon him" after his name.

The Qur’an names Muhammad only four times. It usually speaks to him without his name. It says "O Prophet" or "O Messenger." It calls him a and a bringer of good news. It calls him a mercy to all the worlds (21:107). Muslims believe the Qur’an came to him over about 23 years. That was from about 610 to 632.

The Qur’an says Muhammad was a person, not an angel, and that he did not know the future. It says he was sent to confirm the books that came before. So Muslims believe the Qur’an, the Torah and the Gospel are all from God. Jews and Christians do not share this belief about the Qur’an. Each group reads the others’ books in its own way.

12.10

Three Religions, One Timeline

Main ideaThe prophets of the Qur'an can be placed on one timeline that Jews, Christians and Muslims each read in their own way.

Put the prophets in order and you get a timeline that three religions share. Adam and Nuh come first. Then Ibrahim, whom all three call the father of their faith. Then Musa, who Jews say received the Torah. Then Dawud, whose Psalms Jews and Christians sing. Then Isa, whom Christians follow. Then Muhammad, whom Muslims follow.

Each religion stops at a different place. For Jews, the story of God’s prophets is told in the Hebrew Bible. For Christians, it leads to Jesus and the New Testament. For Muslims, it leads to Muhammad and the Qur’an. All three tell many of the same stories about the earlier prophets.

In this unit you read about Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim and Yusuf. You read about Musa, Maryam, Isa and Muhammad. You compared the Qur’an’s telling with the Bible’s. You learned to say "the Qur’an says" and "the Bible says." You learned to say "Muslims believe" and "Christians believe." That is how people study holy books together with .

  • Say "the Qur'an says," "the Bible says," "Muslims believe" or "Christians believe." Do not say which is right.
  • Find one thing that is the same and one thing that is different in the two tellings.
  • Use the unit's words: prophet, surah, People of the Book, compare.
  • End with what you think each book wants its readers to learn.
