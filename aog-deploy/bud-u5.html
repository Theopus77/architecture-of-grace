The Buddha's Life as the Texts Tell It

Life of the Buddha

Buddhists around the world know the story of the Buddha's life. They see it painted on temple walls, acted out at festivals and told to children. Pilgrims still visit the places where they believe it happened.

In this unit you will follow that story as the texts tell it. You will read about Lumbini, the Four Sights, the Great Going Forth and the awakening. Then you will follow the Buddha's teaching life, the first nuns, and his last journey.

You will also learn to ask where each part comes from. Some details are in the oldest suttas. Others come from later life stories. By the end, you will be able to retell the life and name the texts behind it.

The big question

How do the Buddhist texts tell the Buddha's life, and why do the tellings differ?

Early Life
Big questionHow do Buddhist texts tell the story of the Buddha's early life, and which parts come from which texts?
The story

One Last Look

At midnight a prince looks once at his sleeping baby son and then rides away.

Buddhist life stories tell this scene. It is midnight in the palace at Kapilavastu. Music has stopped. The dancers are asleep on the floor. Prince Siddhartha is awake.

He walks quietly to the room where his wife, Yasodhara, is sleeping. Their newborn son, Rahula, lies beside her. Her hand rests on the baby's head. The prince stands in the doorway.

The story says he wants to hold his son one time. But if he moves her hand, she will wake. If she wakes, he may not be able to leave. So he only looks. Then he turns away.

In the courtyard, his driver Channa waits with the white horse Kanthaka. The prince climbs on. The story says the gods muffle the horse's hooves so no one hears. The city gate swings open by itself.

By morning he is far from home. At a river he cuts off his long hair with his sword. He trades his fine clothes for a simple robe. Buddhists call this night the Great Going Forth.

Section 1

Birth of a Prince

9.1

Queen Maya's Dream

Main ideaBuddhist tradition says Queen Maya dreamed of a white elephant before Siddhartha was born.

Buddhist life stories begin with a dream. Queen Maya, wife of King Suddhodana, dreamed that a white elephant came to her. It carried a white lotus flower. Wise men said she would have a great son.

The king and queen ruled the Sakya people. Their city was Kapilavastu, near today’s border of India and Nepal. The family name was Gotama. That is why Buddhists also call the Buddha "Gotama" or "Gautama."

In many parts of Asia, a white elephant is a sign of honor and good luck. Temple paintings often show Maya’s dream. It is one of the first scenes in many picture stories of the Buddha’s life.

9.2

Born in Lumbini

Main ideaTradition says Siddhartha was born in a garden at Lumbini, a place Buddhists visit today.

The stories say Queen Maya was traveling to her parents’ home to give birth. She stopped in a garden called . There, holding the branch of a tree, she gave birth to a son.

Later stories add wonders. They say the baby took seven steps, and lotus flowers bloomed under his feet. Buddhists in many lands celebrate his birthday. Many pour sweet tea or water over a small statue of the baby Buddha.

Lumbini is in Nepal today. In about 249 BCE, King Ashoka set up a stone pillar there. Its writing says the Buddha was born at that place. Pilgrims still visit the garden.

9.3

Old Texts and Later Stories

Main ideaThe oldest suttas say little about the Buddha's childhood; fuller life stories came later.

Many details you know come from later books. The oldest suttas mostly record the Buddha’s teachings. They mention his early life only in short bits. For example, one sutta has him recall a calm moment as a boy under a rose-apple tree.

Full life stories came later. The , "Acts of the Buddha," is a long Sanskrit poem. Tradition names its author Ashvaghosha. Scholars date it to about the 1st or 2nd century CE. The Pali commentary on the Jatakas also tells the whole story.

Scholars compare these texts to see how the story grew. Buddhists read them as honored accounts of the Buddha’s life. Both kinds of readers find meaning in the details.

From the source
A Memory Under a Tree
The Buddha recalls a day in his childhood. His father was working in the fields. He sat in the cool shade of a rose-apple tree, and his mind grew quiet and happy.
Majjhima Nikaya 36 (Mahasaccaka Sutta), paraphrased (paraphrased)

The Four Sights and Leaving Home

9.4

Four Sights

Main ideaLife stories say an old man, a sick man, a dead man and a wandering holy man led Siddhartha to leave home.

The life stories say King Suddhodana kept his son in comfort. A wise man had predicted the boy would become a great king or a great teacher. The king wanted a king. So he kept sad sights away.

On trips outside the palace, the prince saw four things. He saw an old man, a sick man and a dead body. Then he saw a calm wandering holy man. Buddhists call these the .

An older sutta tells this same story, but about an earlier Buddha named Vipassi (Digha Nikaya 14). Later biographies tell it about Siddhartha. Buddhists read the Four Sights as showing the problems every person faces.

9.5

The Great Going Forth

Main ideaTradition says Siddhartha left home at night on his horse Kanthaka to seek an answer to suffering.

Siddhartha was 29, the stories say. He had a wife, Yasodhara, and a baby son, Rahula. One night he left with his driver Channa and his horse . Buddhists call this the Great Going Forth.

An older sutta tells it more simply. In Majjhima Nikaya 26, the Buddha says he shaved his head and put on a robe while his parents wept. There is no secret midnight ride in that telling.

Buddhists honor the going forth as a great sacrifice. Some readers today also think about Yasodhara, left behind. Later texts tell that she and Rahula both joined the Buddha’s community of followers.

Section 3

The Search and the Awakening

9.6

Teachers and Fasting

Main ideaThe texts say Siddhartha studied with teachers and then tried hard fasting for years before finding it did not work.

The suttas say Siddhartha first studied with two teachers of meditation. They were Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta. He learned all they taught. But he felt it did not end suffering.

Next he joined five other ascetics. For about six years he tried hard practices. He fasted until, one sutta says, he could feel his backbone through his stomach. Statues from Gandhara show him very thin.

At last he decided hard fasting did not lead to wisdom. He ate food again, and the stories say Sujata offered him rice cooked in milk. The five companions thought he had given up. They left him.

9.7

The Middle Way

Main ideaBuddhists say Siddhartha found a middle way between too much comfort and too much hardship.

Siddhartha had lived in two ways. In the palace he had every comfort. As an ascetic he had almost nothing. The texts say neither one brought the answer he wanted.

Buddhists call the path between them the . It is not a lazy path. It asks for steady effort, like tuning a string not too tight and not too loose.

The Buddha later began his first sermon by naming two extremes to avoid. One is chasing pleasure. The other is harming yourself. Between them, he said, is the middle way.

9.8

Awakening at Bodh Gaya

Main ideaBuddhists believe Siddhartha awakened under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya and became the Buddha.

Siddhartha sat beneath a fig tree near the town now called Bodh Gaya. The stories say he vowed not to rise until he understood. A tempter named Mara sent fear and desire to stop him.

Many images show him touching the earth with his right hand. The stories say he called the earth to witness his many good lives. Mara’s armies fled. Buddhists believe that, that night, he reached .

From then on he was the Buddha, "the awakened one." The tree became the Bodhi tree. Many scholars place these events in the 5th century BCE, but dates vary.

Teaching, the Sangha and the Last Journey

Teaching Life
Big questionHow do Buddhist texts describe the Buddha's forty-five years of teaching and his last days?
The story

The Mustard Seed

A mother carrying her sick child goes door to door looking for a mustard seed.

Buddhist commentaries tell this story. A young mother named Kisa Gotami had one little son. He grew sick and died. Kisa Gotami could not accept it. She carried him from house to house, asking for medicine.

People shook their heads. Some thought her grief had made her lose her way. At last, someone kindly told her, "Go to the Buddha. He may be able to help you."

She found the Buddha and asked him for medicine. The story says he answered gently. "Bring me a mustard seed from a house where no one has ever died." Mustard seeds were common. Every house had some.

She went from door to door. Each family wanted to help. But every family said, "We have lost someone too." A grandmother. A father. A child. By evening, she had no seed.

Kisa Gotami sat down, still holding her son. The story says she understood that loss comes to every family. She laid her son to rest. Then she returned to the Buddha and, the texts say, became a nun.

The First Sermon

10.1

In the Deer Park

Main ideaBuddhists say the Buddha gave his first sermon to five companions in the Deer Park at Sarnath.

After his awakening, the Buddha wondered if anyone could understand what he had found. The texts say he decided to teach. He walked to a Deer Park near Varanasi, a place now called .

There he met the five ascetics who had left him. They agreed to listen. The Buddha taught them the middle way and the Four Noble Truths. This talk is Samyutta Nikaya 56.11.

Buddhists call it "Setting the Wheel of the Dharma in Motion." The texts say one listener, Kondanna, understood right away. He became the first follower to see the truth for himself.

10.2

The Three Jewels

Main ideaBuddhists take refuge in the Three Jewels: the Buddha, the Dharma and the Sangha.

With the first followers, Buddhists say, the Three Jewels were complete. The first is the Buddha, the teacher. The second is the , his teaching. The third is the , the community.

Buddhists around the world recite words of "taking refuge" in these three. Refuge means a safe place. In Pali they say, "Buddham saranam gacchami," I go to the Buddha for refuge. Then they name the Dharma and the Sangha.

Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhists all honor the Three Jewels. The words and chants sound different in each land. The three jewels themselves are shared.

The Sangha Grows

10.3

Great Students

Main ideaThe texts name Sariputta, Moggallana and Ananda among the Buddha's most honored students.

The Sangha grew quickly, the texts say. Two friends, and Moggallana, joined together. The suttas praise Sariputta for wisdom. They praise Moggallana for special powers of mind.

Ananda, the Buddha’s cousin, became his helper for about 25 years. Upali, once a barber, became the expert on the rules. People from many backgrounds joined. The Buddha accepted people of any caste, the texts say.

In Buddhist temples today, statues of Sariputta and Moggallana often stand on either side of the Buddha. Buddhists honor them as model students. Visitors often see them there.

10.4

The First Nuns

Main ideaThe Vinaya says Mahapajapati, the Buddha's foster mother, led women to become the first Buddhist nuns.

Gotami was Queen Maya’s sister. She raised Siddhartha after his mother died. The Vinaya says she asked the Buddha to let women become nuns. At first he said no.

She and many women shaved their heads and put on robes anyway. They walked a long way to find him. Ananda spoke for them. The Vinaya says the Buddha then agreed, with eight extra rules for nuns.

Buddhists read this story in different ways today. Some see the extra rules as fitting their time. Others argue for equal treatment. In some Theravada lands, full ordination for nuns has been restored only recently, and debate continues.

10.5

Forty-Five Years on the Road

Main ideaTradition says the Buddha walked and taught for about 45 years, staying in one place during the rains.

Tradition says the Buddha taught for about 45 years. He walked from town to town in northern India. He spoke with kings, farmers, merchants and outcasts.

During the rainy season, he and the monks stayed in one place. Kind supporters gave them parks to live in. One famous park, Jetavana, was given by a merchant named Anathapindika. Many suttas were taught there.

He taught in ways that fit each listener. Sometimes he used stories. Sometimes he asked questions. The story of Kisa Gotami shows him teaching through something a person could do and see.

The Last Journey

10.6

Kisa Gotami's Lesson

Main ideaIn the mustard-seed story, Kisa Gotami learns that loss comes to every family.

The story of is told in the commentaries on the Dhammapada and on the Therigatha. The Therigatha is a book of verses by early Buddhist nuns. Kisa Gotami’s own verses are in it.

In the story, the Buddha does not scold her. He sends her on a search. Along the way, many families share their own losses. Buddhists say she learned that no one is alone in grief.

Teachers often tell this story when talking about loss. Buddhists see it as showing the Buddha’s kindness. Hospice workers and grief groups sometimes share it too.

10.7

The Buddha's Last Days

Main ideaThe Great Discourse on the Final Nirvana tells of the Buddha's death at Kushinagar at age 80.

Digha Nikaya 16 tells of the Buddha’s last journey. It is called the Mahaparinibbana Sutta, the Great Discourse on the Final . The Buddha was 80 years old and ill.

He lay down between two sala trees near Kushinagar. He asked the monks if they had any last questions. None did. Then he gave his final words. Buddhists call his death the parinibbana, the final passing.

Statues of the reclining Buddha show this scene. He lies on his right side, calm, head resting on his hand. Buddhists in Thailand, Sri Lanka and Myanmar have built very large reclining images.

Decay is inherent in all component things! Work out your salvation with diligence!

10.8

Four Places of Pilgrimage

Main ideaIn Digha Nikaya 16, the Buddha names four places for followers to visit: his birth, awakening, first sermon and death.

In Digha Nikaya 16, the Buddha names four places worth visiting. They are where he was born, where he awakened, where he first taught and where he died. These are Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kushinagar.

Buddhist pilgrims have visited these places for more than 2,000 years. King Ashoka set up pillars at some of them. Chinese pilgrims like Xuanzang wrote about them in the 600s CE.

Today Buddhists from Sri Lanka, Thailand, Japan, Tibet and America visit them. Many walk around the sites quietly, offer flowers and light lamps. Some come as families.

Unit test

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Who were the Buddha's parents, says tradition?

Why: Tradition names King Suddhodana and Queen Maya.

2. What did Maya dream about, say the life stories?

Why: The stories say she dreamed of a white elephant.

3. Which ancient object names Lumbini as the birthplace?

Why: Ashoka's pillar inscription names Lumbini as the birthplace.

4. Which text tells the Four Sights about Vipassi?

Why: Digha Nikaya 14 tells the pattern about the earlier Buddha Vipassi.

5. Who was Kanthaka?

Why: Kanthaka was the white horse of the Great Going Forth.

6. In Majjhima Nikaya 26, how does he leave home?

Why: This older sutta has no midnight ride; his parents weep.

7. What did Siddhartha find after years of fasting?

Why: He found hard fasting did not work and chose the middle way.

8. What does the earth-touching gesture remember?

Why: The stories say he touched the earth when Mara challenged him.

9. Where was the first sermon?

Why: The first sermon was in the Deer Park at Sarnath.

10. What are the Three Jewels?

Why: Buddhists take refuge in the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha.

11. Who led the first women to become nuns?

Why: The Vinaya says Mahapajapati led the first nuns.

12. What did the Buddha ask Kisa Gotami to find?

Why: Her search taught her that every family knows loss.

13. Where did the Buddha die, says Digha Nikaya 16?

Why: The Mahaparinibbana Sutta places his death near Kushinagar.

14. What is the Buddhacarita?

Why: Ashvaghosha's Buddhacarita tells the full life, centuries after the Buddha.

15. Which is one of the four pilgrimage places?

Why: The four places are Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kushinagar.

1. (Unit 4) What are the Jatakas?

Why: Buddhists tell the Jatakas as the Buddha's earlier lives.

2. (Unit 4) What is the oldest dated printed book known?

Why: The Dunhuang Diamond Sutra is dated 868 CE.

3. (Unit 4) Which basket holds the Buddha's talks?

Why: The Sutta basket holds the talks.

4. (Unit 4) Where does tradition say the Pali Canon was first written?

Why: The Sri Lankan chronicles name Aluvihara.

5. (Unit 4) How many verses does the Dhammapada have?

Why: It has 423 verses. 547 is about the number of Jatakas.

Write it

The Great Going Forth is told two ways. Compare the midnight ride in the later life stories with Majjhima Nikaya 26. Cite both, explain what each says, and suggest why Buddhists value each telling.

  • Name each text you cite, with its number.
  • Say "the story says" or "Buddhists believe" for each belief.
  • Compare the tellings; do not judge which is true.
  • Use unit words like ascetic, Sangha and middle way.
