Old Books and God's Council

Sources and the Divine Council

This unit starts with the old writings themselves. You will meet scrolls from caves near the Dead Sea, clay tablets from the city of Ugarit, and old copies and translations of the Bible. Then you will meet the scholars who study them, like Mark Smith, Loren Stuckenbruck and Michael Heiser.

You will learn the Hebrew word elohim. It can mean Israel's God, and it is used for other spirit beings too. Then you will read the Bible's council scenes in Psalm 82, Psalm 89, 1 Kings 22, Job 1–2, Isaiah 6 and Daniel 7. Many scholars call this picture the divine council, God's heavenly court.

Readers do not all agree about these passages. You will learn at least two readings of Psalm 82. By the end, you will be able to tell a primary source from a secondary source, explain a passage, name who said what, and compare two readings fairly.

The big question

What do the old writings say about God's heavenly council, and how do researchers read them?

The Verse That Stopped Her

A college student reads one short verse in Hebrew and has to read it again.

Nina sat in the college library with a thick book open in front of her. It was a Hebrew Bible. The letters ran from right to left. She had studied Hebrew for one year. Now her teacher had asked the class to read Psalm 82 on their own.

The first verse was short. Nina found the word for God. Then she found the same word again, a few words later. The first time, it meant God. The second time, it seemed to mean many beings. The verse seemed to say that God stands in a council and judges among the gods.

Nina frowned. She checked her dictionary. She checked her grammar notes. The word was elohim both times. The grammar showed that the first one was one being and the second one was many. She raised her hand and asked, "Who are the other elohim?"

Her teacher smiled. "That is a very old question," she said. "Start with the psalm itself. That is your primary source. Then read the scholars. Some compare it with old clay tablets from Syria. Some, like Michael Heiser, say this psalm surprised them too. They do not all agree."

Nina wrote two lists in her notebook. One said Old Writings. The other said Scholars. At the top of the page she wrote her question. She wanted to know what the first people who heard this psalm thought it meant.

The Old Writings Themselves

7.1

Primary and Secondary Sources

Main ideaA primary source is the old writing itself; a secondary source is what a later scholar writes about it.

In 1947, shepherds found old scrolls hidden in a cave near the Dead Sea. One was a long scroll of the Book of Isaiah, made of animal skin. It was copied by hand more than 2,000 years ago. A scroll like this is a . It is the old writing itself.

Other primary sources are clay tablets, carved stones and the books of the Bible. They come from the time being studied, or they are careful copies from long ago. They let a reader see the very words the old writers chose.

A is what a later person writes about an old writing. A scholar’s book about Isaiah is a secondary source. It can help a lot. But good researchers always go back and check the primary source first.

7.2

Copies Made by Hand

Main ideaThe Bible's first writings are lost; what survive are manuscripts, hand-made copies that scholars compare.

Before printing machines, every book was copied by hand. A copy written by hand is called a . The very first writings of the Bible are lost. What survive are manuscripts, copies of copies, made by careful .

Some manuscripts are very old. The Great Isaiah Scroll was copied about 125 BCE. Codex Sinaiticus, a Greek Bible, was copied in the 300s CE. The Leningrad Codex, from 1008 CE, is the oldest complete Hebrew Bible that survives.

Scholars lay manuscripts side by side. Most of the time the copies agree closely. Sometimes one word is different. Then scholars ask which reading is older. Later in this course you will see a famous difference in Deuteronomy 32:8.

7.3

Clay Tablets From Ugarit

Main ideaClay tablets from the old city of Ugarit are primary sources that show words and ideas Israel's neighbors knew.

In 1928 a farmer in Syria hit a stone while plowing. It led to the ruins of an old city called Ugarit. Beginning in 1929, diggers found thousands of there. Scribes had pressed wedge-shaped marks into wet clay, about 3,300 years ago.

Some Ugarit tablets tell stories about their gods. In them, the high god El leads a gathering of gods, called his . The gods in it are called the sons of El. Some of the same kinds of words for a heavenly gathering also appear in the Hebrew Bible.

Scholars read these tablets beside the Bible. E. Theodore Mullen (1980) and Mark S. Smith (2001) are two who compared them closely. Many say Bible writers used words their neighbors knew. In the Bible’s council scenes, Israel’s God is at the head, not El or Baal.

Old Words in New Languages

7.4

Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek

Main ideaThe Bible was first written in three languages: mostly Hebrew, a little Aramaic, and Greek for the New Testament.

Most of the Hebrew Bible, or Old Testament, was written in . Hebrew is written from right to left. Its alphabet has 22 letters, all consonants. Marks for the vowels were added much later by scribes called the Masoretes.

A few parts are in , a sister language of Hebrew. Parts of Daniel and Ezra are in Aramaic. Later, Aramaic became an everyday language in Israel. Some old pieces of the Book of Enoch, found near the Dead Sea, are in Aramaic too.

The New Testament was written in . By then Greek was a shared language around the eastern Mediterranean Sea. So a reader of the whole Bible meets three languages. Scholars who study these texts learn all three.

7.5

Carrying Words Into English

Main ideaA translation carries old words into a new language, and translators must choose how to say each word.

A carries words from one language into another. In the 200s BCE, Jewish scholars in Alexandria, Egypt, began putting the Hebrew Bible into Greek. This Greek translation is called the .

Translators must make choices. In Psalm 8:5, the Hebrew says people were made a little lower than elohim. The Septuagint chose the Greek word for angels. The King James Version of 1611 said angels too. Some newer English Bibles say God or heavenly beings instead.

Translations also carry books outside the Bible. The Book of Enoch survived whole in Ge&#x27;ez, an old language of Ethiopia. R. H. Charles put it into English in 1917. Comparing translations shows where a word could go more than one way.

One Word to Study: Elohim

7.6

One Word, Many Beings

Main ideaThe Hebrew word elohim can mean Israel's God, and it is also used for other beings of the spirit world.

Psalm 82:1 is a short verse. It says God stands in a council and judges among the gods. In Hebrew, the word for God and the word for gods are the same word: . English Bibles have to pick two different words.

Elohim appears about 2,600 times in the Hebrew Bible. Most of the time it means Israel’s God, as in Genesis 1:1. But 1 Kings 11:33 uses it for the god of the . Psalm 82:1 uses it for the beings in God’s council.

So one Hebrew word points to many beings. In English, God with a capital G names one being. In Hebrew, elohim is a wider word. Readers use the rest of the sentence to see which being is meant.

7.7

Scholars Explain It Differently

Main ideaScholars explain the word elohim in different ways, and each one points to the text.

Why does elohim fit so many beings? Scholars give different answers. Michael S. Heiser, in The Unseen Realm (2015), said elohim is a word about . It tells where a being lives: the spirit world. It does not tell what the being is like.

Heiser compared it to a word like Illinoisan. A baby and a farmer can both be Illinoisans. For him, Israel’s God is an elohim, but no other elohim is like him. Exodus 15:11 asks this too: who among the gods is like the LORD?

Other scholars explain elohim in other ways. In Exodus 22:8, the King James Version even says judges for elohim. Mark S. Smith (2001) studied how Israel’s writers spoke about God and other gods over time. Readers still discuss the word today.

7.8

One or Many? Context Tells

Main ideaElohim looks plural, but the words around it show whether it means one being or many.

In Hebrew, the ending -im often makes a word , meaning more than one. Elohim ends in -im. So how can it mean one God? Hebrew readers look at the words around it. When elohim goes with a verb for one person, it means one being.

Genesis 1:1 uses elohim with a verb for one person. So it means one God, who creates. But in Psalm 82:1, the second elohim is in a phrase that needs more than one. God judges in the midst of the elohim. You can only stand in the midst of many.

English has a word like this. The word sheep can mean one sheep or many sheep. You know which one from the sentence. The sheep is eating means one. The sheep are eating means many. Scholars of every view use this kind of clue.

Scholars and the Book of Enoch

Main ideaResearchers found, translated and studied the old copies of the Book of Enoch, starting from the manuscripts themselves.

Genesis 5:24 says Enoch walked with God, and then he was gone, because God took him. Much later, other writers told longer stories about Enoch. These were gathered into the Book of Enoch, also called .

7.10

The First Readers' World

Main ideaResearchers try to read an old text the way its first readers would have understood it.

Think of a text message from today. Someone reading it 3,000 years from now might be confused. What is a phone? What does a smiley face mean? They would need to learn about our world first. Old writings are like that too.

The first readers of the Hebrew Bible lived in the . That is the name for lands like Israel, Egypt, Syria and Iraq long ago. People there had their own ideas about kings, gods, temples and the sky. Bible writers used words those readers knew.

So researchers ask what a word meant back then, not only what it means now. They read the Bible beside other old writings, like the Ugarit tablets and 1 Enoch. This helps them hear a text the way its first readers may have heard it.

Main ideaA careful reader checks a big claim by finding the old text it points to and reading what it really says.

Some books and videos make big claims about the Bible. One claim says that space aliens visited the ancient world. Another says giants built the pyramids. These claims often borrow old words from ancient texts.

Scholars read those same texts in their first languages. Heiser was one who wrote that the ancient-alien claims do not match what the texts say. Many other scholars agree. The claims often use words in ways the ancient writers never did.

The story

A Field Trip to City Hall

A fourth-grade class watches a city council meeting, and one student remembers a psalm.

Ms. Ortiz's fourth-grade class rode the bus to City Hall. They sat in rows at the back of a big room. At the front was a long curved table. The mayor sat in the middle. Council members sat on each side, with name signs in front of them.

The mayor called the meeting to order. A council member spoke about fixing a park. Another asked about the cost. A woman from the town came to the microphone and shared an idea. The mayor listened to everyone. Then the council voted, and the mayor read the result.

On the bus ride home, Sam looked out the window. "That was like a picture I read in the Psalms," he said. "In Psalm 82, God stands in a council. There are others there too, and God is in charge."

Ms. Ortiz nodded. "Many scholars call that the divine council," she said. "They compare it with old clay tablets from Syria that describe a council of gods. Other readers see Psalm 82 in another way. Let's read the psalm first, and then look at both readings."

Sam thought about the mayor. The mayor did not need the council members to be the mayor. But the mayor still worked with them. He wondered why a king, or God, would have a council at all.

A Court in Heaven

8.1

Psalm 82: God Judges the Gods

Main ideaPsalm 82 shows God standing in a divine council and judging the elohim for being unfair.

Psalm 82 is only eight verses long. It opens with a scene. God stands in the . That means a gathering of heavenly beings with God at the head. In the middle of them, God judges. The Hebrew calls the members elohim, which English Bibles often translate as gods.

Then God speaks. He asks how long they will judge unfairly. He tells them to protect the poor, the weak and children with no parents. But the members have not done it. The psalm says they do not understand and they walk in darkness.

God says he called them gods and sons of the Most High. But now, he says, they will die like humans. The psalm ends with a prayer. The writer asks God to rise up and judge the earth, because all the nations belong to him.

8.3

Like a King's Court

Main ideaDaniel 7 and other passages picture God as a king with a court; Heiser said God shares his work with the council, not because he needs help.

In the ancient world, a king sat on a throne. Around him stood his , officials and messengers who carried out his plans. Daniel 7:9–10 pictures God this way. A is set. Thousands serve him, and the court sits to judge.

Many scholars say the Bible borrows this picture of a royal court to show God’s rule. Michael Heiser, in The Unseen Realm (2015), asked why God would have a council at all. He said God does not need anyone. But God chooses to include others in his work.

Heiser also saw a link to people. He wrote that God wanted a family on earth too, made of humans, to share his work. Other readers see the court as a picture of God’s greatness more than a team. Later units look closely at these ideas.

Section 2

Council Scenes in the Bible

8.4

Micaiah Sees the Throne

Main ideaIn 1 Kings 22, the prophet Micaiah describes God on his throne asking the host of heaven for a plan.

First Kings 22 tells about King Ahab of Israel. He wanted to go to war at a town called Ramoth-gilead. About 400 of his prophets told him he would win. But another king asked for one more prophet. So they called a named Micaiah.

Micaiah told what he had seen. He saw the LORD sitting on his throne. All the stood on his right and on his left. The LORD asked who would lead Ahab to go to battle. One said one thing, and another said something else. Then a spirit came forward with a plan, and the LORD sent it.

Many scholars point to this scene as a clear picture of the council at work. God asks, the members talk, and God decides. The members speak, but God has the last word. Micaiah’s message was a warning. The story says Ahab did not listen, and he did not come home from the battle.

8.5

The Sons of God in Job

Main ideaThe Book of Job opens with the sons of God coming before the LORD, including one called the satan.

The Book of Job begins on earth with a good man named Job. Then the scene moves to heaven. Job 1:6 says the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD. That means they came to stand before him, like officials reporting to a king.

One member came too. The Hebrew calls him . The word satan means adversary, or one who challenges. It has the word the in front, so it works like a title. He questions whether Job is good only because life has gone well for him.

Readers explain the satan in Job in more than one way. Many scholars read him as a member of the council acting as a challenger. Much Jewish tradition sees him as an angel who serves God as an accuser. Many Christians read him as the devil. Job 38:7 shows another scene: the sons of God shouting for joy when the world was made.

8.6

Isaiah and the Seraphim

Main ideaIn Isaiah 6, Isaiah sees God's throne with seraphim and hears God ask, 'Who will go for us?'

Isaiah 6 begins in the year King Uzziah died, around 740 BCE. Isaiah sees the Lord on a high throne. The edge of his robe fills the temple. Above him are . The name likely comes from a Hebrew word for burning, so it may mean burning ones.

Each seraph has six wings. With two it covers its face. With two it covers its feet. With two it flies. They call to one another: holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts. The whole earth is full of his glory.

Then God asks, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" Some scholars, like Heiser, read the word us as God speaking to his council. Many Christians see a hint of the Trinity. Others say it is a royal way of speaking. Isaiah answers, "Here am I; send me."

Section 3

Reading Psalm 82 Two Ways

8.7

Heavenly Beings

Main ideaMany scholars read the gods in Psalm 82 as heavenly beings, and they give reasons from the text.

Who are the elohim that God judges in Psalm 82? Many scholars say they are heavenly beings, members of God’s council. Mullen (1980), Mark S. Smith (2001) and Heiser all read the psalm this way. They give reasons from the text. A reason from the text is called .

First, verse 7 says they will die like humans. Why tell humans they will die like humans? The line makes more sense, these readers say, if the listeners are not human. Second, Psalm 89 places the council in the heavens.

Third, verse 8 says God will judge all the . Judges in Israel did not rule over every nation. Heiser linked this verse to Deuteronomy 32:8, where God divides the nations. For him, heavenly beings had been given the nations and ruled them badly.

Think it through
Weighing a Clue

Psalm 82:6–7, retold: I said you are gods, children of the Most High. But you will die like humans. Some scholars ask: if these listeners were already human, why would God say they will die like humans?

Explain this clue in your own words. Does the line make you think of humans or heavenly beings? Why?

Words to know
evidence

nations

Check yourself

1. Who do many scholars say the elohim in Psalm 82 are?

Why: Scholars such as Mullen and Smith read them as heavenly members of the divine council.

2. Which verse says the elohim will die like humans?

Why: Psalm 82:7 says they will die like humans.

3. Why do these readers point to Psalm 82:8 and the nations?

Why: Verse 8 speaks of all the nations, which Israel's human judges did not rule.
8.8

Human Judges

Main ideaOther Jewish and Christian readers have said the gods in Psalm 82 are human judges or rulers.

The heavenly reading is not the only one. Over many centuries, some Jewish and Christian readers have said the elohim in Psalm 82 are human or rulers. The reformer John Calvin, in the 1500s, read the psalm this way. Some old Jewish readings do too.

These readers have reasons as well. The psalm is about being fair in court. It talks about protecting the poor and the weak. That was the job of human judges in Israel. Rulers and judges acted for God, so these readers think the psalm could call them gods as an honor.

Readers of this view say verse 7 is a warning. You have an honored title, but you are still humans, and you will die. Both readings look closely at the same eight verses. They come to different answers about who is being judged.

Think it through
Two Readings Side by Side

Reading A (many scholars): The gods are heavenly beings in God's council who ruled the nations unfairly. Reading B (some Jewish and Christian readers, like Calvin): The gods are human judges who were honored with a high title but ruled unfairly.

What do both readings agree on? Where do they differ? Name one clue each reading uses.

8.9

Jesus Quotes Psalm 82

Main ideaIn John 10:34, Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6, and readers on both sides use this passage.

Psalm 82 also appears in the New Testament. In John 10, some people are upset with Jesus. They say he is claiming to be God. In John 10:34, Jesus answers with Psalm 82:6: "Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?" That is the King James Version.

In the next verse, Jesus says Scripture called them gods, unto whom the word of God came. Readers who hold the human-judge view point to this line. They say it shows the gods were humans who received God’s word.

Readers who see heavenly beings in Psalm 82 explain Jesus’ words in a different way. The of John does not stop to explain the psalm. So readers keep studying this passage. It is one reason Psalm 82 is discussed so much.

8.10

Comparing Readings Fairly

Main ideaA fair reader can explain each reading, with its clues, without making fun of either one.

You have now seen two ways to read Psalm 82. How can you compare them fairly? Start by saying each reading in words its own readers would agree with. Then name the clues each one uses. That is called stating a view .

Next, check each clue in the primary source itself. Find verse 7. Find verse 8. Read Psalm 89:5–7 and John 10:34–35. Ask what each passage says before you ask what it means. Every careful researcher starts there.

Last, remember that readers have discussed these verses for a very long time. Thoughtful people have reached different answers. You can explain what one scholar argued and what others argued. You do not need to make fun of anyone to do it well.

Think it through
A Fair Report

Student A writes: Many scholars read the gods in Psalm 82 as heavenly beings, because of verses 7 and 8 and Psalm 89. Student B writes: That reading is right and anyone else is silly. Student C writes: Some readers, like Calvin, see human judges because the psalm is about fair courts.

Which reports are fair? What would you change in the one that is not?
