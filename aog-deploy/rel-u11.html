One Question, Many Lenses

Comparative

By now you have read the major traditions one at a time: the Torah and the prophets, the Gospels and Paul, the Qur'an and the Hadith, the Upanishads and the Gita, the Dhammapada, the Analects and the Daodejing, the Guru Granth Sahib, and traditions carried in story and ceremony. This unit turns the course sideways. Instead of asking what one tradition teaches, it asks a single human question and lets each tradition answer it in its own words.

The questions are the oldest ones. How should a person live, and what do people owe one another? What happens when we die, and why is there suffering at all? Every student in the room already has some answer, from a family, a congregation, a book or their own thinking. Reading the traditions side by side shows where their answers meet, such as the Golden Rule and care for the poor. It also shows where they part, over God, the self and what comes after death.

By the end of the unit you will be able to set two or three sacred texts beside each other, attribute each teaching to its tradition, explain the reason each text gives, and show the variety inside a tradition as well as the differences between them. You will practice the discipline at the heart of this course: comparing carefully, in the traditions' own terms, without declaring a winner.

The big question

When the world's traditions answer the same great questions, what do they share, where do they differ, and why?

What Is a Good Life? What Is Justice?

Ethics Compared
Big questionWhen the world's traditions answer the question of how to live well and fairly, where do their answers meet and where do they part?
The story

One Question on the Board

A teacher writes seven words on a whiteboard, and six students discover that they have each been handed an answer before they ever heard the question.

The teacher wrote the question in blue marker and capped the pen: What is a good life, and what is justice? Then she asked six volunteers to answer, not from their opinions, but from a text their family or community treats as sacred, or from a philosopher if they had no such text. She gave them ten minutes and a stack of library copies. The room got quiet in the way a room does when people realize the assignment is harder than it looked.

Miriam found the passage in Leviticus that her grandfather quoted at every holiday meal, about loving your neighbor as yourself, and then a line from the prophet Micah about doing justice and loving mercy. Daniel opened Matthew to the Sermon on the Mount and read the Beatitudes twice, once for the words and once for the order. Yusuf asked whether he could bring in a verse from the Qur'an about standing firm for justice even against your own family. The teacher said that was exactly the point.

Priya had a copy of the Bhagavad Gita that had belonged to her aunt. She marked the lines in which Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna to do his duty without grasping at the reward. Kenji, whose grandmother kept a small Buddhist altar, wrote out the eight steps of the path and circled three of them: right speech, right action, right livelihood. Marcus said his family was not religious, so he went to the philosophy shelf and came back with Aristotle and a slim book by John Stuart Mill.

When the six read their answers aloud, something unexpected happened. Nobody had said the same thing, but nobody had said something the others found strange. Miriam's neighbor and Daniel's enemy-love and Yusuf's justice and Priya's duty and Kenji's right speech and Marcus's greatest happiness all seemed to be looking at the same mountain from different trails. Then Kenji asked whether the trails really led to the same place. Priya said the Gita and the Dhammapada disagreed about whether there is a self at all, so how could they agree about a good life for it?

The teacher did not answer. She uncapped the marker, drew a table with six columns, and said that the rest of the chapter would be spent filling it in. The rule was simple. Every answer had to be attributed to a text and a tradition, every belief had to be described rather than judged, and no column was allowed to be declared the winner. The bell rang before anyone had written a word, and six students walked out arguing in the hall.

Talk about itThe six answers seemed alike until someone pressed on the details. Why might the surface of an ethical teaching look similar across traditions while the reasoning underneath differs?
Section 1

Asking the Question Well

21.1

Two Questions, Not One

Main ideaA good life asks what a person should become; justice asks what people owe one another, and every tradition answers both.

Look again at the sentence on the whiteboard. It contains two questions that people often blur together. What is a good life? is a question about a single person: what habits, aims and character make a human life go well. What is justice? is a question about relationships: what one person owes another, what a ruler owes the ruled, what a community owes the stranger at its gate. A tradition can answer one without saying much about the other, and the differences between traditions often show up in how they connect the two.

Scholars call the study of these questions , from the Greek word for character or custom. Religious ethics is ethics grounded in a tradition’s texts, practices and picture of the world. When the Torah commands Israel to leave the corners of a field unharvested for the poor, it is answering the justice question. When the Buddha describes a mind free of craving, he is answering the good-life question. Most sacred texts do both, and often in the same passage.

This chapter reads each tradition’s answer in its own words before it compares anything. That order matters. A comparison built on summaries tends to flatten every teaching into the same shape, and then the comparison proves nothing. A comparison built on texts, with the reasoning attached, lets real agreements and real differences show. By the end you should be able to state what a tradition teaches about living well, name the text it comes from, and explain the reason that text gives.

Notice what the chapter does not do. It does not decide which answer is correct. In a public school, the academic study of religion describes what adherents believe and why, attributes each claim to its source, and leaves the judgment to the student and the family. That restraint is not a weakness of the method. It is what lets a Jewish student, a Muslim student and a student with no religion read the same page and each find it fair.

21.2

Comparing Without Ranking

Main ideaFair comparison describes each tradition in its own terms, attributes every claim, shows variety within the tradition, and refuses to declare a winner.

In 1893 the World’s Parliament of Religions met in Chicago, on the lakefront where the Art Institute now stands. For seventeen days in September, representatives of Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Judaism, Islam, Christianity in many forms, and other traditions spoke from the same platform. It is often described as the first large gathering of its kind. Some speakers used the stage to argue that their own faith was best. Others, like the Hindu monk Swami Vivekananda, argued that the traditions should learn from one another. Both impulses are still with us, and this chapter chooses the second, in an academic form.

Fair comparison follows a few rules. First, describe each tradition in the terms its own scholars and adherents use: dharma rather than "Hindu law," mitzvah rather than "Jewish rule." Second, attribute every claim, so that a sentence reports what a text says rather than asserting that it is so. Third, show the tradition; Sunni and Shia Muslims, or Orthodox and Reform Jews, may read the same verse differently, and a description that hides this is inaccurate. Fourth, do not rank. A student may hold private views, but the comparison itself stays neutral.

There is a fifth rule that is easy to forget: compare like with like. Setting a tradition’s highest ideal against another tradition’s worst failure is a common trick, and it proves nothing. If you compare the Sermon on the Mount with Islamic teaching, compare it with the Qur’an’s teaching on mercy, not with a headline. If you compare Buddhist compassion with Judaism, compare it with the prophets, not with a stereotype. The table the teacher drew has six columns, and every column gets the same kind of entry: a text, a teaching and a reason.

One more thing before the reading starts. Sizes differ. Christianity and Islam together count more than half the world’s people; Sikhism counts perhaps thirty million; Jainism a few million; the traditions of many Indigenous peoples count in the thousands. A tradition’s size says nothing about the depth or interest of its answer, and this chapter gives each answer room. The data below shows why the comparison cannot be settled by counting heads.

21.3

The Torah's Neighbor

Main ideaIn Jewish tradition the good life is a life of mitzvot, and justice begins with the command to love the neighbor and protect the stranger, the widow and the poor.

In the middle of Leviticus, among rules about harvests and honest scales, stands one sentence. Jewish tradition has treated it as a summary of the whole law: you shall love your neighbor as yourself. The verse (Leviticus 19:18) does not float free. It ends a list of concrete commands. Do not steal. Do not lie. Do not hold back a worker’s wages overnight. Do not curse the deaf. Do not favor either the poor or the powerful in court. Love of neighbor, in the Torah’s way of speaking, is a set of actions before it is a feeling.

The Hebrew word for one of these commands is , plural mitzvot. Rabbinic tradition counts 613 of them in the Torah, ranging from Sabbath rest to returning a lost animal. To live a good life, in this understanding, is to live inside the , the binding agreement between God and Israel made at Sinai, by keeping the commandments. The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20) are the best known, but Jewish teachers have always stressed that they are a beginning, not the whole.

Justice in the Torah has a particular shape. Again and again the text names three vulnerable figures, the stranger, the orphan and the widow, and ties their protection to Israel’s own memory: you were strangers in Egypt. Farmers must leave the corners of the field and the fallen grain for the poor (Leviticus 19:9–10). Every seventh year debts are released. The Hebrew word , which came to mean giving to the poor, shares its root with the word for justice. In Jewish thought, giving is not charity in the sense of an optional kindness; it is owed.

Jews do not all read these laws the same way. Orthodox Jews hold that the commandments bind in full and that rabbinic interpretation over centuries defines how. Reform Judaism, which took shape in nineteenth-century Germany and America, treats the ethical commands as central and many ritual commands as matters of informed choice. Conservative Judaism stands between. Yet across these movements, the phrase from Leviticus and the prophets’ demand for justice remain the common ground, and the story of the Exodus remains the reason: a people freed from slavery is commanded to remember what it was like.

21.4

The Prophets Demand Justice

Main ideaAmos, Micah and Isaiah taught that worship without justice for the poor is worthless, giving Judaism and Christianity a shared language for social ethics.

Around 750 BCE a herdsman named Amos walked north from Judah to the sanctuary at Bethel in the kingdom of Israel and began to shout. The kingdom was prosperous, its festivals crowded, its altars busy. Amos said that none of it mattered. In the words of the King James Version, God says, "I hate, I despise your feast days," and then demands instead that judgment run down like water and righteousness like a mighty stream (Amos 5:21–24). The offense Amos names is specific: the rich sell the poor for a pair of shoes, cheat with false weights and crush the needy in court.

A , in the Hebrew Bible, is not mainly a predictor of the future. The word means one who speaks for another, and the prophets speak for God to a people who have, in their view, broken the covenant. Their charge is nearly always the same: the powerful have forgotten the poor, the widow and the stranger, and ritual cannot cover it. Isaiah asks what use are sacrifices when hands are full of blood, and tells the people to seek justice and defend the orphan (Isaiah 1:11–17). Micah compresses the whole message into one line, quoted below.

This prophetic tradition shaped both Judaism and Christianity. In synagogues the prophets are read each Sabbath after the Torah portion, in a reading called the , and Reform Judaism in particular has made the prophets’ social justice central to its identity. In churches, Jesus is shown quoting Isaiah at the start of his ministry (Luke 4), and Christian movements from abolition to the civil rights movement have preached from Amos. Martin Luther King Jr. quoted Amos 5:24 in his speech at the March on Washington in 1963.

Read carefully, the prophets are not against worship. Amos and Isaiah assume the festivals and sacrifices will continue. Their claim is that worship and justice are one system, and that a community cannot keep the first half while breaking the second. That claim, that how you treat the poor is a religious matter and not only a political one, is one of the Hebrew Bible’s distinctive contributions to the question on the board.

21.5

The Sermon on the Mount

Main ideaIn Matthew 5–7 Jesus blesses the poor and the merciful, commands love of enemies, and gives the Golden Rule, which Christians read as the shape of life in the Kingdom of God.

The Gospel of Matthew gathers a long stretch of Jesus’s teaching into a single scene. He climbs a hill, sits down as a teacher would, and speaks to his disciples while a crowd listens (Matthew 5–7). Christians call it the . It opens with the , a series of blessings on people the world does not usually count as fortunate. They are the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek, those who hunger for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted. Matthew lists eight or nine, depending on how they are counted. The Gospel of Luke gives a shorter set of four, paired with four warnings to the rich and the comfortable (Luke 6:20–26).

The sermon then takes up the Torah directly. Jesus says he has come not to destroy the law but to fulfill it, and six times he quotes a command and pushes it inward: not only do not kill, but do not nurse anger; not only love your neighbor, but love your enemy. The passage on enemies, quoted below, is among the most discussed lines in Christian ethics. It asks not merely for restraint but for active good toward those who do harm. Christians have read it as a personal command, as a command for churches, and, in some traditions such as the Mennonites and Quakers, as a reason to refuse all violence.

Near the end comes the sentence often called the : whatever you would have others do to you, do to them, "for this is the law and the prophets" (Matthew 7:12). That last phrase matters. Jesus is presented as summarizing the Hebrew Bible, not replacing it, and Jewish teachers of the same era, such as Hillel, taught a closely related rule. The sermon also teaches the Lord’s Prayer, warns against showing off piety, and tells the listener to store treasure in heaven rather than on earth.

What is the good life in the sermon? Christians describe it as life in the : a way of living that trusts God for tomorrow, forgives as it hopes to be forgiven, and treats mercy as the measure by which one will be measured. Christians differ on how literally its hardest lines apply. Roman Catholic teaching has often read the sermon as counsel of perfection alongside the commandments; Martin Luther read it as showing how far everyone falls short and needs grace; Anabaptists read it as a plain rule for the church. All agree that it sets the standard.

21.6

Justice and Mercy in the Qur'an

Main ideaThe Qur'an commands justice even against oneself and one's kin, pairs it with God's mercy, and makes care for the poor a pillar of the good life.

Every chapter of the Qur’an but one opens with the same phrase: in the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful. Muslims recite it before meals, journeys and tasks. The two words for mercy come from the Arabic root , related to the word for a mother’s womb, and Muslim scholars have long noted that the Qur’an names God’s mercy far more often than God’s anger. The good life, in this picture, begins with recognizing a merciful Creator and responding with gratitude and obedience, which is what the word islam, submission, means.

Justice, &#x27;adl in Arabic, is commanded with unusual force. One verse tells believers to stand firm for justice as witnesses for God, even if it goes against themselves, their parents or their relatives, and whether the person concerned is rich or poor (Qur’an 4:135). Another says that hatred of a people must not push believers into injustice, because being just is closer to piety (5:8). A third, often recited at the end of Friday sermons, says that God commands justice, the doing of good and generosity to relatives, and forbids indecency, wrongdoing and oppression (16:90). Justice is not softened by mercy in these verses; the two are demanded together.

Concrete duties follow. Zakat, one of the Five Pillars, requires Muslims with savings above a threshold to give a fixed share each year, commonly reckoned at 2.5 percent, to the poor and other named categories. Beyond zakat, voluntary giving called is praised throughout the Qur’an and the Hadith. Honest weights, kindness to orphans, fair treatment of workers and the release of debtors recur as marks of the righteous, often in language close to the Hebrew prophets, whom the Qur’an also honors as messengers of God.

Muslims have developed these commands into detailed law, and the schools of differ on many particulars. Sunni scholars follow several schools and Shia scholars others. Sufi teachers have stressed the inner side of mercy and justice, the purifying of the heart. But the question on the board gets a recognizable answer across all of them. A good life is a life lived in grateful submission to a merciful God. Justice is what that God commands, without exception for the powerful or for oneself.

Duty, Path and Way

21.7

Dharma and the Gita's Answer

Main ideaHindu traditions describe the good life as living one's dharma, and the Gita teaches that right action is done as duty and offering, without grasping at its reward.

The Bhagavad Gita opens on a battlefield. The warrior Arjuna looks across at the opposing army, sees his cousins, teachers and friends, and drops his bow. He would rather be killed than kill them. His charioteer, who is the god Krishna in human form, answers him over eighteen chapters. The Gita is part of the vast epic Mahabharata, which scholars think was composed in stages over several centuries, roughly 400 BCE to 400 CE, and it has become for many Hindus the single most read statement of how to live.

The first word Krishna reaches for is . The term has no single English equivalent. It means duty, right order, the way things are meant to go, and the responsibilities of a person’s role and stage of life. Arjuna is a warrior. Krishna tells him that a warrior’s dharma is to fight a just war, and that shrinking from it out of grief is a failure of duty. Hindu ethics has always held that duties differ by role: a student’s dharma is not a householder’s, and a ruler’s is not a priest’s. Historically this idea was tied to the caste system. Modern reformers since the nineteenth century, including many devout Hindus, have argued that the ethical core of dharma can be kept while the hereditary hierarchy is rejected.

Krishna’s most famous teaching answers the good-life question directly. You have a right to your actions, he says, but never to the fruits of your actions; do your duty because it is your duty, and let go of the craving for reward. In Edwin Arnold’s 1885 verse translation, the counsel is to find full reward of doing right in right itself. This is , the path of action without attachment. Krishna adds two more paths: jnana yoga, the path of knowledge, in which one realizes that the true self is not the body; and , the path of loving devotion to God, which many readers, though not all, take the Gita finally to name as the highest.

How does this bear on justice? The Gita’s picture is that a person acting from duty rather than self-interest becomes a steady point in the world, doing what the role requires and offering the result to God. Gandhi, who read the Gita daily, drew from it a discipline of selfless service and, unlike many earlier readers, a commitment to nonviolence. Other Hindus have drawn from the same text a defense of a warrior’s duty. Both readings exist within the tradition, and a fair account names them both.

21.8

The Eightfold Path and Compassion

Main ideaBuddhists describe the good life as walking the Eightfold Path, whose ethical steps are right speech, right action and right livelihood, and whose fruit is compassion for all beings.

The Buddha’s first teaching, as tradition records it, was given to five companions in a deer park near Varanasi. He laid out Four Noble Truths: life as ordinarily lived involves dukkha, suffering or unsatisfactoriness; its cause is craving; it can end; and the way to its end is the . The path is the Buddhist answer to the good-life question, and it is a practice rather than a creed. Its eight parts are right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration.

Buddhist teachers group the eight into three trainings. Wisdom covers view and intention; concentration covers effort, mindfulness and meditation; and , ethical conduct, covers speech, action and livelihood. This middle group is where justice lives. Right speech means no lying, no slander, no harsh words, no idle chatter. Right action is spelled out in the Five Precepts that lay Buddhists undertake: to refrain from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech and intoxicants. Right livelihood rules out trades that harm, and early texts name dealing in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants and poisons.

What ties ethics to the rest of the path is the claim that harm and craving are linked. The Dhammapada, a collection of the Buddha’s sayings in verse, opens by saying that all we are is the result of what we have thought. A mind full of hatred produces harmful acts and further suffering; a mind trained toward , loving-kindness, and , compassion, produces the opposite. Buddhists cultivate these deliberately in meditation, extending goodwill first to oneself, then to friends, then to strangers, then to enemies, then to all beings.

Across Buddhist schools the emphasis shifts. Theravada Buddhism, strong in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, stresses the individual’s discipline on the path. Mahayana Buddhism, dominant in East Asia, holds up the , one who delays final liberation to help all beings, and makes compassion the highest ideal. Engaged Buddhists in the twentieth century, such as the Vietnamese monk Thich Nhat Hanh, applied the precepts to war, poverty and the environment. In all of these, the good life and justice are the same training seen from inside and from outside.

When another student asked about ren, Confucius said simply that it is to love people. Ren is the inner quality of a good person, and the Analects spend most of their pages on how it is formed.

It is formed, Confucius taught, through , ritual and proper conduct, and through xiao, filial devotion. Xiao is the respect a child owes to parents and ancestors. A person learns to be humane at home, by serving parents, and carries that care outward to the ruler, the friend and the stranger. The ideal is the , the noble person, whose nobility comes from character rather than birth. Justice, for Confucius, is what follows when rulers are junzi. A ruler who is upright leads without needing to command. A state governed by virtue and ritual rather than by punishment produces people with a sense of shame who correct themselves.

The Daodejing, a short text attributed to Laozi and probably compiled around the fourth century BCE, reads almost like a reply. Confucius builds the good life from ritual, learning and role. The Daodejing warns instead that when the great , the Way, is abandoned, people start talking about humaneness and righteousness. It praises , acting without forcing. Its favorite image is water, which benefits all things, does not compete, and settles in the low places that people avoid. The best ruler, it says, is one whose people barely know he exists.

Are these religions or philosophies? Scholars answer both. Confucianism has temples, rites for ancestors and a canon; Daoism developed priests, temples and practices for long life. In East Asia most people historically drew on both, along with Buddhism, without choosing. For the question on the board, the two give different answers within a shared world. Confucius: become humane through relationship and ritual, and justice will follow from good rulers. Laozi: let go of striving, return to simplicity, and the world will order itself.

Ethics Without Scripture

Main ideaSecular ethics answers the same questions by reason alone, through virtue, duty or consequences, and belongs in the comparison beside the religious answers.

The chapter treats these views the way it treats every other column: describe, attribute, do not rank.

Three secular approaches have shaped Western thinking. Aristotle, writing in Athens in the fourth century BCE, taught that the good life is , flourishing. It is reached by practicing the virtues, stable habits such as courage, honesty and generosity, each a middle between two extremes. Immanuel Kant, in Germany in 1785, argued that morality rests on duty. Act only on a rule you could will everyone to follow, and always treat persons as ends, never merely as means. John Stuart Mill, in England in 1861, argued for . The right act is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number.

Notice how each maps onto the religious answers. Aristotle’s virtues resemble Confucian self-cultivation and the Buddhist training of the mind. Kant’s rule sounds like a philosophical version of the Golden Rule, though Kant himself insisted that his test was stricter and not the same. Mill’s concern for the sum of happiness echoes the prophets’ and the Qur’an’s insistence that the poor count. The resemblances are real. So are the differences: a secular thinker grounds the answer in reason, experience or human nature, not in revelation, and does not connect it to God, karma or an afterlife.

Modern gathers these threads into a stance. Human beings, it holds, can live ethically and find meaning through reason, compassion and responsibility to one another, without belief in the supernatural. The first Humanist Manifesto was published in 1933. Humanist groups today run ceremonies, chaplaincies and charities. For the question on the board, the humanist answer is that a good life is one lived thoughtfully and kindly in the one life a person can be sure of. Justice is what fair-minded people would agree to. Whether that is enough, or whether it borrows from the religions it sets aside, is itself a debate worth having in class, with both sides described fairly.

Main ideaThe traditions converge on care for the vulnerable and a version of the Golden Rule, but they differ on the source of the rule, the nature of the self, and how justice relates to God.

Nearly every one states a form of the Golden Rule: Leviticus, Matthew, the Analects, a saying of the Prophet Muhammad in the Hadith that one is not a believer until one wants for one’s brother what one wants for oneself, and the Mahabharata’s teaching not to do to others what would cause pain to oneself. Scholars call this the rule’s near-universal reach.

Look more closely, and the reasons diverge. Why should I not hate my enemy? The Torah answers: because the LORD commands it and remembers Egypt. Matthew answers: because God sends rain on the just and the unjust, and the children should be like the Father. The Dhammapada answers: because hatred cannot end hatred, as a matter of how minds work. Confucius answers: because reciprocity is the rule of a humane person. Kant answers: because you could not will a world of hatred. The same conduct, five different foundations. A student who reports only the conduct has missed the most interesting part.

The deepest difference concerns the self. The Abrahamic traditions speak of a single person, created by God, who lives once, is judged and is answerable. Hinduism speaks of an eternal atman that passes through many lives, and Sikhism of a soul that passes through births until it merges with God. Buddhism denies a permanent self altogether and grounds ethics in the reduction of suffering wherever it arises. Confucianism locates the person in a web of relationships. These are not small variations. They change what a good life is for, and they change what justice is owed to, whether a soul, a stream of experience, an ancestor or a citizen.

A second difference concerns God. In the Torah, the Gospels and the Qur’an, justice is what God demands and God will finally establish. In Buddhism and Confucianism no such lawgiver stands behind the rule, and in secular ethics none is needed. Yet a Muslim, a Buddhist and a humanist may work side by side in the same food pantry on the same Saturday morning in Chicago, each with a full account of why. The study of religion does not ask which account is true. It asks you to be able to state all of them accurately, in their own words, and to notice, with respect, that they are not the same.

Judaism: This World and the World to Come

Main ideaThe Hebrew Bible focuses on life in this world, while later Jewish tradition taught resurrection and the world to come, and Jewish movements still differ on how to understand them.

Most of the Hebrew Bible says little about life after death. The dead go down to , a shadowy place of silence, and the Psalms ask whether the dead can praise God at all. Near the end of the biblical period the picture changes. Hinduism: The Soul Changes Clothes

Main ideaMany Hindus believe the atman passes through many lives in samsara, shaped by karma, until it reaches moksha, release from rebirth.

At the burning ghats of Varanasi, on the banks of the Ganges, cremation fires burn day and night. Many Hindus hope to die in Varanasi or to have their ashes placed in the Ganges, a river they regard as holy. A Hindu funeral, , usually ends in cremation, often lit by the eldest son. Rites for the dead continue for about ten to thirteen days, varying by region and community, and families later offer food and prayers for their ancestors in rites called shraddha.

The belief behind the fire is that the body dies but the self does not. Hindu texts call the true self the . In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna that the self is never born and never dies, and that it casts off worn-out bodies as a person casts off worn-out clothes, as shown below. The atman moves through , the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, taking many forms across many lives.

What shapes each new life is karma, the principle that actions have consequences that follow a person, in this life and the next. Good actions lead to better births; harmful ones to harder births. Karma in this sense is not a judge’s sentence. Hindu teachers describe it more like a law of nature, the way a seed produces its own kind of plant. It explains, in this view, why lives begin in such different circumstances.

The goal is not endless rebirth but release from it, called . Hindu paths describe moksha differently. For the Advaita Vedanta school, it is realizing that atman is Brahman, the one ultimate reality, like a drop recognizing it is the ocean. For many devotional traditions, devoted to Vishnu, Krishna or Shiva, it is an eternal life of love in God’s presence. Both views agree that death is a passage, not an end, and that how one lives now shapes where the passage leads.

Buddhism: Rebirth Without a Soul

Main ideaBuddhists teach rebirth driven by karma but deny a permanent self that passes from life to life, and the goal is nirvana, the ending of the cycle.

Buddhists share the picture of samsara with their Hindu neighbors, but with a striking change. The Buddha taught , non-self: there is no permanent, unchanging soul inside a person. What we call a self is a changing stream of body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations and consciousness. So what is reborn? A Buddhist text called the Questions of King Milinda answers with an image of a lamp lit from another lamp. The flame is neither the same flame nor a different one.

What carries over, Buddhists teach, is the momentum of karma, intentional action and its results. A life shaped by craving, hatred and delusion tends toward rebirth in painful conditions; a life shaped by generosity, kindness and wisdom toward better ones. Traditional Buddhist cosmology describes several realms of rebirth, including humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings and gods. None of them is permanent, and even the heavens eventually end.

The goal is to stop the cycle altogether. means blowing out, as a flame goes out when its fuel is gone. The fuel, in Buddhist teaching, is craving. A person who reaches nirvana is not reborn at death. The Buddha declined to describe what that state is like in ordinary terms, and Buddhist schools have explained it in different ways. Theravada teachers stress the ending of suffering. Many Mahayana traditions add paths such as rebirth in the Pure Land of the Buddha Amitabha.

Buddhist funerals reflect these teachings. Monks chant texts on impermanence, and families make offerings and gifts in the name of the dead to transfer merit to them. In Tibetan Buddhism, the Bardo teachings describe a passage of up to forty-nine days between death and rebirth. Jainism and Sikhism: Two Paths to Release

Main ideaJains teach that the soul frees itself from karmic matter through strict nonviolence, while Sikhs teach that liberation comes through God's grace and remembering God's name.

Jainism, the tradition taught by Mahavira in the sixth or fifth century BCE, has the most physical picture of karma in Indian religion. Jains hold that every living being has a , an eternal soul, by nature pure and all-knowing. Karma, in Jain teaching, is a fine kind of matter that sticks to the soul through passions such as anger, greed and pride and through harmful action. Weighed down by it, the jiva is reborn again and again.

Liberation comes by stopping new karma and burning off the old. That is why Jains practice ahimsa, nonviolence, with such care, avoiding harm even to insects, and why they value fasting and meditation. A soul entirely freed of karma rises to the top of the universe and dwells there in endless knowledge and bliss. Jains call such liberated souls siddhas. Near the end of life, some devout Jains take a vow of , gradually giving up food with the approval of their community and teachers. Jains describe it as a peaceful, conscious death, and its practice has been debated in Indian courts.

Sikhism, founded by Guru Nanak (1469–1539), also teaches rebirth driven by karma. But in Sikh teaching, karma is not the last word; God’s grace is. Liberation, called , comes when a person, by God’s grace, remembers the divine Name, lives truthfully and serves others. The self then merges with God, as Sikhs describe it, like water joining water. Sikhs stress that mukti can be experienced in this life, not only after death.

Sikh funerals reflect this. The body is usually cremated, the evening hymn Kirtan Sohila is recited, and the ashes are placed in running water. The family often arranges a complete reading of the Guru Granth Sahib over the following days. Sikh teaching discourages loud wailing, since death is understood as the will of God and a return to God. Both Jains and Sikhs, then, share samsara and karma with Hindus and Buddhists but draw very different maps of the way out.

The Ancestors Remain

Main ideaIn many African and Chinese traditions the dead become ancestors who stay connected to the living, who honor them and ask for their help.

In early April, many Chinese families go to the cemetery for , the Tomb-Sweeping Festival. They clear weeds, repair the grave, set out food and tea, burn incense and paper offerings, and bow. In many homes an altar holds wooden tablets bearing ancestors’ names, and families light incense there on important days. Honoring ancestors in this way is older than Confucius, and Confucian teaching made it central to xiao, filial devotion.

rests on the idea that the dead continue as members of the family. They are remembered, fed and consulted, and in turn they watch over the living. Chinese popular religion also recognizes spirits with no one to care for them. During the Ghost Festival in the seventh lunar month, families make offerings for these neglected dead. Confucius himself was reserved about the spirit world. When asked about death, he answered that one who does not yet understand life can hardly understand death.

Many African traditions hold a similar picture. Among the Yoruba of West Africa, ancestors are honored at family shrines and, in some communities, in Egungun festivals, where masked dancers are understood to embody the returning dead. Libations, pourings of water or drink on the ground, are offered to ancestors in many African societies. The Kenyan scholar John Mbiti described recently departed ancestors as the living-dead: still remembered by name, still part of the household.

In these traditions death does not end a relationship; it changes it. The ancestor’s role depends on being remembered, which makes the living responsible for the dead, and the dead for the living. Scholars note that such traditions often focus less on a final judgment than on the ongoing health of the family and community. Many Christians, Muslims and Buddhists in Africa and East Asia also keep forms of ancestor remembrance, blending older practices with their faith in varied ways.

Job and the Whirlwind

Main ideaThe Book of Job rejects the idea that suffering is always punishment, and God's answer from the whirlwind points to the limits of human understanding.

The Book of Job opens with a man who is blameless and upright. In a single day he loses his herds, his servants and his ten children. Then his body breaks out in sores. His first response, quoted below, is famous. But then Job sits in the ashes for seven days in silence with three friends, and when he finally speaks, he curses the day he was born. The book that follows is one of the most searching treatments of suffering in world literature.

Job’s friends offer the standard explanation. God is just, they say, so suffering must be punishment for sin; Job should confess and be restored. Job insists he has done nothing to deserve this and demands a hearing with God. The book takes Job’s side against the friends. At the end, God says that the friends have not spoken rightly, as Job has. The question of how a good and powerful God can allow suffering is called , and Job refuses the easy answer.

When God finally answers, it is from a , and not with an explanation. Instead God asks Job a long series of questions. Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Can you command the morning, or feed the lion? The speech paints the vastness of creation, far beyond human understanding. Job answers that he has spoken of things too wonderful for him. His fortunes are then restored.

Readers have never agreed on what the ending means. Some Jewish and Christian readers find in it trust: suffering is real, but the world’s order is beyond human sight. Others note that Job is never told why he suffered, and they take the book as permission to protest to God. Jewish thinkers after the Holocaust returned to Job for exactly that reason. What most readers agree on is that Job refuses to blame the victim.

The First Truth and Karma

Main ideaBuddhism begins by naming suffering as a fact of life, and karma explains much suffering in Indian traditions, though teachers warn against using it to blame the suffering.

A young mother named Kisa Gotami, according to a Buddhist story, carried her dead child from house to house, begging for medicine. At last she came to the Buddha. He told her to bring a mustard seed from a household where no one had ever died. She went door to door. Every family had lost someone. By evening she understood that death comes to all. She laid her child to rest and became a nun.

The story illustrates the Buddha’s : life as ordinarily lived involves dukkha, suffering or unsatisfactoriness. Birth, aging, sickness and death are dukkha; so is being separated from what one loves. The Buddha did not ask why a creator would allow this, because Buddhism does not teach a creator God. Instead the Second Truth names suffering’s cause, craving, and the Third and Fourth teach that it can end and how.

In Hindu, Buddhist and Jain thought, karma explains why suffering falls where it does. Actions have results, and some results ripen in later lives. That can make suffering seem less random: it has a cause, even when the cause cannot be seen. Hindu teachers add that suffering met with patience and devotion can purify the soul. For many believers the idea brings comfort, because in this view the universe is not unjust.

Teachers in all three traditions also warn against misusing karma. In one Buddhist sutta, the Buddha tells a questioner that not every painful feeling comes from past karma. Some come from bodily illness, the change of seasons, careless living or harm done by others. Modern Hindu and Buddhist teachers, and reformers who fought caste discrimination, have argued that karma must never be used to blame the poor or the sick or to excuse cruelty. The compassionate response, all three traditions teach, is to help. Here karma and Job meet: both warn against blaming the one who suffers.

Secular and Humanist Answers

Main ideaSecular thinkers and humanists generally hold that death is the end of the person and that meaning is found in this life, in love, memory and the good one does.

Nearly three in ten American adults now describe themselves as having no religion, and many of them, though not all, believe that death is simply the end of the person. That view is old. The Greek philosopher Epicurus, around 300 BCE, argued that death should not be feared, because where death is, we are not, and where we are, death is not. A person who no longer exists, he reasoned, cannot suffer. He wanted his students to live calmly and well.

Modern builds on this. Humanists hold that people can live meaningful, ethical lives without belief in God or an afterlife. They say meaning is not given from outside but made, in relationships, work, creativity and service. A humanist funeral, often led by a trained celebrant, centers on the life of the person who died. There are stories, music and tributes, and no prayers for the soul. Humanists speak of the dead living on in memory and in the difference they made.

On suffering, secular thinkers do not need a theodicy, since they do not believe in a God whose goodness must be defended. Some argue that the amount of suffering in the world is itself a reason to doubt such a God. This is the , an argument often traced back to Epicurus. Others simply treat suffering as part of nature, to be reduced through medicine, science and justice, and borne with courage when it cannot be.

The secular view shares ground with religious ones. Like Buddhists, many secular thinkers stress accepting impermanence. Like Jews, many emphasize this life over speculation about the next. Like everyone, they grieve, gather and remember. What differs is the claim underneath: that nothing of the person continues beyond memory and influence. Religious believers, in turn, often say that without a hope beyond death some suffering cannot be answered. A fair comparison lets each side state its answer in full.

Why: Zakat, one of the Five Pillars, is a required yearly gift from savings, commonly 2.5 percent.

5. In the Gita, what does Krishna teach about action?

Why: Krishna teaches karma yoga: act from duty and let go of attachment to the fruits.

6. Which Sikh practice serves a free meal to everyone who comes?

Why: The langar in every gurdwara serves a free meal to all, seated as equals.

7. Which three steps of the Eightfold Path make up ethical conduct?

Why: Buddhists group right speech, right action and right livelihood as sila, ethical conduct.

8. What distinguishes secular ethics from religious ethics?

Why: Secular ethics, such as Aristotle's, Kant's or Mill's, rests on reason and experience rather than on God or karma.

9. Which statement about Judaism and life after death is accurate?

Why: Rabbinic Judaism taught resurrection; the Reform movement set bodily resurrection aside in 1885.

10. In Islamic teaching, what happens on the Day of Judgment?

Why: The Qur'an teaches bodily resurrection, the weighing of deeds, and entry into jannah or jahannam.

11. Which tradition teaches rebirth but denies a permanent soul?

Why: Buddhism teaches anatta, non-self, while still teaching rebirth driven by karma.

12. How does Jainism picture karma?

Why: Jains describe karma as subtle matter that clings to the jiva and is removed by nonviolence and discipline.

13. What answer does the Book of Job give to the friends who say suffering is punishment?

Why: At the end of Job, God says the friends have not spoken rightly, as Job has.

14. Which pair matches a mourning practice to its tradition?

Why: Sikhs usually cremate and hold a complete reading of the Guru Granth Sahib; shiva is Jewish and burial facing Mecca is Muslim.

15. What is the fairest way to compare two traditions' answers to the same question?

Why: The unit's method is to describe, attribute, show variety within a tradition and compare like with like, without ranking.
