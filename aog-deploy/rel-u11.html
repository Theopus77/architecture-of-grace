The story

The King Who Carved His Regret

After a bloody war, an Indian emperor did something few rulers do: he said he was sorry, in stone.

In about 261 BCE, the armies of the emperor Ashoka marched into Kalinga, a land on the east coast of India. Ashoka ruled the Mauryan Empire, which stretched across most of the Indian subcontinent. His soldiers won. The cost was enormous. Ashoka's own words later said that about 100,000 people were killed and about 150,000 were carried away as captives.

Ashoka did not celebrate. Instead, he ordered words carved on rocks and tall stone pillars across his empire. In one of these messages, called Rock Edict XIII, the king says he feels deep sorrow over Kalinga. He writes that the only true conquest is a conquest by dhamma, a word that here means right conduct and moral teaching.

The edicts ask people to respect their parents, to be kind to servants and to animals, and to honor the teachers of every religious group. Ashoka had become a follower of the Buddha's teaching. Yet he urged respect for all sects, not only his own. His messages were written in local languages and scripts so ordinary people could hear them read aloud.

Some of these pillars still stand today. One pillar top, with four lions standing back to back, became the national emblem of modern India. Historians still debate how Ashoka governed after Kalinga. But his carved words remain one of the oldest records of a ruler trying to rule by a moral teaching.

Sacred Texts of Hinduism

21.1

The Vedas and the Upanishads

Main ideaThe Vedas are the oldest Hindu scriptures, and the Upanishads within them explore the link between the self and ultimate reality.

Before sunrise in many Hindu homes, someone chants a short verse in Sanskrit called the Gayatri Mantra. It comes from the Rig Veda, one of the oldest texts still used in worship anywhere. Hindus call these texts the , from a Sanskrit word meaning knowledge. There are four Vedas, and many Hindus regard them as revealed truth that ancient sages heard, not wrote.

Scholars date the oldest parts of the Rig Veda to about 1500 to 1200 BCE. For centuries, the Vedas were passed on by memory. Priests trained for years to chant every sound exactly. Only much later were they written down. The Vedas contain hymns to many deities, instructions for fire rituals and, in their later parts, deep questions about life.

Those later parts are the , composed from about 800 BCE onward. They ask: What is the real self? What is behind all things? Many Upanishads answer that the inner self, called Atman, is one with Brahman, the ultimate reality. Hindu schools of thought read this teaching in different ways, and they still debate it today.

Chandogya Upanishad 6.13 (the teaching of Uddalaka to Shvetaketu), paraphrase (paraphrased)

21.2

Two Great Epics

Main ideaThe Ramayana and the Mahabharata are long story-poems that teach Hindus about duty through heroes facing hard choices.

Every autumn, in towns across northern India, actors perform the story of Prince Rama. Crowds watch him lose his throne, go into the forest with his wife Sita and his brother Lakshmana, and rescue Sita from the demon king Ravana. This story is the , an epic poem traditionally credited to the sage Valmiki. Many Hindus see Rama as an image of perfect duty and honor.

The is even longer. Scholars call it one of the longest poems ever composed, with about 100,000 verses in its fullest form. It tells of a great war between two branches of one royal family. Both epics grew over many centuries, roughly between 400 BCE and 400 CE. Today many families know them through retellings, comics and TV series.

Inside the Mahabharata is the , or Song of the Lord. Just before battle, the warrior Arjuna does not want to fight his own relatives. His charioteer, Krishna, whom many Hindus worship as a form of God, teaches him. Krishna says Arjuna must do his duty without being attached to the results. Hindus have read this teaching in many ways, and millions study it closely.

21.3

Brahman and the Deities

Main ideaMany Hindus worship many deities while understanding them as forms or faces of one ultimate reality, Brahman.

Walk into a Hindu temple and you may see many sacred images, called murtis. One shows Ganesha, with an elephant’s head, whom many Hindus pray to before starting something new. Another shows Lakshmi, linked with good fortune. Another shows Shiva, dancing in a ring of fire. A visitor might ask: how many gods do Hindus worship?

Hindus answer in different ways. Many describe one ultimate reality, , beyond all names and forms. They see the many as forms or faces of that one reality. A common saying from the Rig Veda states that truth is one, but the wise call it by many names. Other Hindus focus devotion on one supreme God, such as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess, called Devi.

Families often have an ishta devata, a chosen deity they feel closest to. Followers of Vishnu are called Vaishnavas. Followers of Shiva are called Shaivas. Followers of the Goddess are called Shaktas. These are not separate religions. They are major paths within Hinduism, and they share many texts, festivals and ideas.

21.4

Dharma, Karma, Samsara, Moksha

Main ideaHindu teaching links duty, the results of actions, the cycle of rebirth and the hope of release into one picture of life.

A teenager in Chennai helps care for her grandfather, studies hard and tells the truth even when it costs her. Her family might say she is following her . Dharma means duty, or the right way to live. In Hindu teaching, a person’s dharma depends partly on their stage of life and role, such as student, parent or elder.

Hindus teach that every action has results that return to the one who acts. This is . Good actions tend to bring good results; harmful actions bring suffering. Many Hindus believe karma carries across lifetimes. The soul is reborn again and again in a cycle called , and karma helps shape each new birth.

The highest goal, many Hindus say, is , release from the cycle of rebirth. Hindu paths describe different ways toward moksha. The path of knowledge seeks insight. The path of devotion seeks loving surrender to God. The path of action does one’s duty selflessly. Many Hindus combine these paths in daily life.

Main ideaHindu worship happens at home and in temples through offerings, prayers and festivals that bring families together.

In the morning, a mother in Aurora, Illinois, rings a small bell at the family shrine. She offers a flower, lights a lamp and waves it in circles before an image of Krishna. This act of worship is called . Puja can be simple or grand. It often includes light, flowers, food, incense and prayer.

In a , or Hindu temple, priests care for the murtis each day, bathing and dressing them. Worshipers come for darshan, which means seeing and being seen by the deity. 21.6

Caste: History and Reform

Main ideaCaste was a social ranking system in India; many reformers, Hindu and others, worked to end discrimination based on it.

Ancient texts describe four social groups called : priests and teachers, rulers and warriors, merchants and farmers, and laborers. Over time, society in India also divided into thousands of smaller birth groups called jatis. People often married inside their group and did work linked to it. Scholars call this whole system .

Some groups were treated as outside the system and called untouchable. They faced harsh exclusion from temples, wells and schools. Many Hindu reformers rejected this treatment, citing the teaching that the same divine self lives in every person. Mahatma Gandhi campaigned against untouchability. Many people today use the name Dalit for these communities.

B. R. Ambedkar, born into a Dalit family, became a lawyer and led the writing of India’s Constitution. It took effect in 1950 and outlawed untouchability. In 1956, Ambedkar and hundreds of thousands of followers became Buddhists, seeking a path without caste. Discrimination based on caste has not fully disappeared, and Indians still debate how best to end it.

The Buddha and His Teaching

21.7

Siddhartha Becomes the Buddha

Main ideaBuddhist tradition tells how Prince Siddhartha left a life of comfort to seek an end to suffering and became the Buddha, the awakened one.

Buddhist tradition tells of a prince named Siddhartha Gautama, born in Lumbini, in what is now Nepal. Scholars often place his life in the 5th century BCE, though dates vary. His father kept him inside the palace, surrounded by comfort. According to the story, on trips outside he saw an old man, a sick man, a dead body and a calm wandering holy man.

Troubled, Siddhartha left home to seek an answer to suffering. For years he tried extreme fasting, but it did not bring the answer. He chose a between luxury and harsh self-denial. Seated under a tree at Bodh Gaya, he meditated until, Buddhists say, he reached awakening. From then on he was called the , which means awakened one.

The Buddha taught for about 45 years across northern India. He gathered a , a community of monks and nuns, and lay followers. Buddhists call the Buddha, his teaching (the Dharma) and the Sangha the Three Jewels. Many Buddhists recite that they take refuge in these three.

21.8

Four Truths and an Eightfold Path

Main ideaBuddhists teach that suffering has a cause and an end, and that the Eightfold Path is the way to reach that end, called nirvana.

Buddhist tradition says the Buddha’s first sermon took place in a deer park near Varanasi. There he taught the . First, life involves suffering, or dukkha, a word that also means dissatisfaction. Second, suffering comes from craving, which means wanting and clinging. Third, suffering can end. Fourth, there is a path that leads to its end.

That path is the : right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration. Buddhists often group these into three parts: wisdom, ethical conduct and meditation. Here right means wise or skillful, not a rule to obey out of fear.

The goal is , a word that means blowing out, as a flame is blown out. Buddhists describe it as the end of craving and suffering and freedom from rebirth. Like Hindus, Buddhists speak of karma and rebirth. But the Buddha taught that there is no permanent, unchanging self. This is one key point where Buddhist and Hindu teachings differ.

Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (Setting the Wheel of Dhamma in Motion), Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, paraphrase (paraphrased)

Section 4

Buddhism Spreads

21.9

Ashoka and the Silk Road

Main ideaBuddhism spread from India across Asia through rulers like Ashoka, monks, pilgrims and traders on the Silk Road.

Ashoka ruled the Mauryan Empire from about 268 to 232 BCE. After the war in Kalinga, he supported the Buddha’s teaching. His , carved on rocks and pillars, urged kindness, honesty and respect for all religious groups. According to Buddhist tradition, he sent teachers far and wide, and his son Mahinda carried Buddhism to Sri Lanka.

Over the following centuries, monks and merchants traveled along the , the network of trade routes linking China, Central Asia and India. Buddhism reached China by about the 1st century CE. From China it spread to Korea and then to Japan by the 6th century CE. It also traveled by sea to Southeast Asia.

Pilgrims carried texts home. The Chinese monk Xuanzang walked to India in the 600s CE and returned with hundreds of Buddhist texts to translate. In Afghanistan, huge statues of the Buddha were carved into the cliffs at Bamiyan. Each new land shaped Buddhism, blending it with local languages, art and customs.

The story

A Gate, a Bell and One Family

On a Japanese mountain path, one family visits a Shinto shrine and a Buddhist temple on the same day.

Early on New Year's morning, the Sato family climbs a stone path on a hillside in Japan. At the top stands a tall orange gate with two crossbeams, called a torii. It marks the entrance to a Shinto shrine. Before stepping through, the family bows. At a stone basin, they rinse their hands and mouths with a wooden dipper.

At the shrine, each person tosses a coin into a wooden box, rings a bell, bows twice, claps twice and bows once more. Shinto tradition teaches that sacred powers called kami dwell in places like this mountain. The family asks the kami for health and good fortune in the new year. They buy a small charm to hang at home.

Farther down the path is a Buddhist temple. On New Year's Eve, its great bell was struck 108 times. Many Japanese Buddhists say the number stands for 108 worldly desires or troubles. Now the family lights incense there and bows before an image of the Buddha. Later in life, many Japanese families hold funerals with Buddhist rites.

To the Satos, visiting both places on one morning does not feel odd. Many people in Japan take part in both Shinto and Buddhist customs. In China, too, many families have long blended Confucian, Daoist and Buddhist ways. This chapter explores those traditions, and two more from India: Sikhism and Jainism.

The Sikh Gurus

22.1

Guru Nanak and the Ten Gurus

Main ideaSikhism began with Guru Nanak in the Punjab, who taught devotion to one God and the equality of all people.

Sikh tradition tells that Guru Nanak, born in 1469 in the Punjab region of South Asia, went to bathe in a river one morning and disappeared for three days. When he returned, he said: There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim. Sikhs understand this to mean that God sees people beyond such labels. Nanak became the first , or teacher, of the Sikhs.

The word Sikh means learner or disciple. Guru Nanak taught that there is one God, the creator, who is present in all creation. He taught that people draw close to God by remembering God’s name, working honestly and sharing with others. He rejected ranking people by caste and taught that women and men are equal before God.

Nine more human Gurus followed Guru Nanak, from the 1500s to the early 1700s. The fifth Guru, Guru Arjan, compiled the scripture and built the Harmandir Sahib, known as the Golden Temple, in Amritsar. The tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, declared before his death in 1708 that the scripture itself would be the Guru from then on.

22.2

Scripture, Khalsa and Langar

Main ideaSikhs honor the Guru Granth Sahib as a living Guru, many join the Khalsa, and every gurdwara serves a free shared meal called langar.

In a , the Sikh house of worship, the rests on a raised platform under a canopy. A reader waves a whisk over it as a sign of respect. Its hymns are written in Gurmukhi script and are sung with music. The book contains hymns by Sikh Gurus and also by Hindu and Muslim holy poets.

In 1699, Guru Gobind Singh founded the , a community of initiated Sikhs. Members promise to live by Sikh teaching and keep five articles of faith called the five Ks: kesh (uncut hair), kara (a steel bracelet), kanga (a wooden comb), kachera (cotton undershorts) and kirpan (a small sword). Many Sikh men and some Sikh women wear a turban to cover their uncut hair.

Every gurdwara has a , a free community kitchen. Anyone may eat, of any faith or none. Guests traditionally sit together on the floor, so no one sits higher than another. Volunteers cook, serve and wash dishes as seva, or selfless service. The Golden Temple in Amritsar serves many thousands of people every day. Gurdwaras in the Chicago area, such as in Palatine, also serve langar.

The Jain Path

22.3

Mahavira and Ahimsa

Main ideaJains follow the teaching of Mahavira and the earlier Tirthankaras, which centers on ahimsa, doing no harm to any living being.

A Jain nun walks slowly, gently sweeping the ground ahead with a soft broom so she will not step on an insect. Some Jain monks and nuns cover their mouths with a cloth. These acts show , the principle of non-harm to any living thing. Jains teach that every living being, even a tiny insect, has a soul, called a jiva.

Jains honor 24 , teachers who crossed over the river of rebirth and showed others the way. The 24th is Mahavira, who lived in northern India around the same time as the Buddha, often dated to the 6th or 5th century BCE. Jain tradition says he left home at about 30, lived as a wandering ascetic and reached perfect knowledge.

Like Hindus and Buddhists, Jains speak of karma and rebirth. But in Jain teaching, karma is a fine substance that sticks to the soul when a person acts with harm or desire. Careful, nonviolent living removes it. The goal is liberation, when the soul is free of karma. Most Jains live in India today, and Jain temples also stand in the Chicago suburbs.

From the source

All beings are fond of life. They like pleasure and dislike pain. They shun destruction and wish to live. To all, life is dear. So one should not kill, harm or cause suffering to any living creature.
Acaranga Sutra, Book 1 (a Jain scripture; see the translation by Hermann Jacobi, 1884), paraphrase (paraphrased)

22.4

The Jain Vows

Main ideaJains take vows of non-harm, truth, not stealing, self-control and non-attachment; monks and nuns keep them strictly and lay people in a milder form.

Jains organize their way of life around five . They are ahimsa (non-harm), satya (truthfulness), asteya (not stealing), brahmacharya (self-control, especially in sexual life) and aparigraha ( to possessions). Monks and nuns take these as great vows and keep them strictly. Lay Jains take them as smaller vows suited to family life.

The vows shape daily choices. Jains are vegetarian, and many avoid root vegetables such as onions and potatoes, since pulling them up kills the whole plant and many tiny beings in the soil. Many Jains do not eat after sunset. Jain businesses often avoid trades that cause great harm to living things.

Jains practice anekantavada, the teaching of many-sidedness. It holds that truth is complex and any one view sees only part of it. Jains tell the story of blind men who each touch one part of an elephant and describe it differently. Jains are divided into two main groups, the Digambara and the Shvetambara, which differ on practices such as monks’ clothing.

Confucius and the Analects

22.5

Confucius and Ren

Main ideaConfucius taught that people become truly good through ren, humane care for others, practiced in everyday relationships.

Confucius, called Kongzi in Chinese, lived in China from about 551 to 479 BCE, according to tradition. It was a time of war between rival states. He worked as a teacher and adviser, hoping rulers would govern by good example instead of force. After his death, his students collected his sayings in the .

The central virtue in the Analects is , often translated as humaneness or goodness. Ren means caring for others and treating them well. Confucius taught that a person grows in ren through practice, starting with family and moving outward to the community and the state. He called a person of great virtue a junzi, an exemplary or noble person.

Scholars debate whether Confucianism is best called a religion, a philosophy or a way of life. Confucius spoke of Heaven, or tian, and honored ancestral rites. Yet he said little about gods or life after death. In China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam, Confucian ideas have shaped families, schools and government for more than two thousand years.

Analects 15.23, trans. James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1 (1861)

22.6

Li and Xiao

Main ideaConfucian teaching holds that proper conduct, li, and devotion to parents, xiao, build harmony in families and in society.

At a Chinese family dinner, grandparents are served first. The children greet each elder by name before eating. On the Qingming Festival in spring, many families sweep the graves of their ancestors and bring food and flowers. Confucian tradition sees these customs as more than politeness. They express , proper conduct and ritual.

Li includes ceremonies, like honoring ancestors, and everyday manners, like bowing or greeting elders. Confucius taught that practicing li with a sincere heart shapes a person’s character. He thought outer actions and inner feelings belong together. Li without ren, he warned, is empty.

One of the most important Confucian virtues is , or filial piety, which means devotion and respect toward one’s parents. Confucian teaching describes five key relationships: ruler and subject, parent and child, husband and wife, older and younger sibling, and friend and friend. Each side has duties. Critics, then and now, have debated how these roles treat women and younger people.

22.7

The Examinations

Main ideaFor about 1,300 years, China chose many officials through exams on the Confucian classics.

Imagine a young man in a tiny exam cell, one of thousands in a row. For days, he writes essays about Confucian texts that he has memorized since childhood. If he passes the highest level, his whole family rises in honor. This was the Chinese system.

The exams began under the Sui dynasty, around 605 CE. They grew larger under the Tang and Song dynasties. Candidates studied the Confucian classics, including the Analects. The idea was that officials should be chosen for learning and virtue, not only for noble birth. In practice, families with money for tutors had a great advantage, and women could not take the exams.

The system lasted until 1905, when the Qing government ended it. Through the exams, Confucian values shaped China’s government and schools for centuries. Korea and Vietnam adopted similar exams. Today, many people in East Asia still link hard study and respect for teachers with Confucian tradition.

The Dao, the Kami and Blending

22.8

Laozi and the Dao

Main ideaDaoists seek harmony with the Dao, the natural way of all things, through simplicity, balance and wu wei.

In a Chinese park at dawn, people move slowly through the gentle motions of tai chi. Many practitioners say the practice helps them feel balanced, like water flowing. This image of flowing water appears again and again in the , a short book of poems traditionally credited to Laozi. Scholars debate whether Laozi was a real person. Many date the book to about the 4th century BCE.

The Daodejing opens by saying that the Dao that can be spoken of is not the lasting Dao. means the way. Daoists describe it as the natural way of all things, beyond words. They teach , often translated as effortless action. Wu wei means acting in harmony with nature, without forcing things, like water that flows around a rock.

Daoists also speak of , two opposite forces that complete each other, like dark and light or rest and motion. Religious Daoism grew later, with temples, priests, rituals and many deities, and a hope for long life. Daoism and Confucianism often balanced each other in China: one stressing duties in society, the other harmony with nature.

From the source

The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence of water appears in its benefiting all things, and in its occupying, without striving (to the contrary), the low place which all men dislike.
Daodejing, chapter 8, trans. James Legge, Sacred Books of the East vol. 39 (1891)

22.9

Shinto: Kami and Shrines

Main ideaShinto, the traditional religion of Japan, honors the kami, sacred powers found in nature, ancestors and special places.

A huge old tree at a Japanese shrine has a thick straw rope tied around it, hung with white paper zigzags. The rope, called shimenawa, marks the tree as a sacred place. In , Japan’s traditional religion, such places are homes of the . Kami are sacred powers or spirits found in mountains, rivers, trees, ancestors and people of great virtue.

Shinto has no single founder and no single scripture. Its ancient stories are recorded in the Kojiki, compiled in 712 CE. It teaches purity and gratitude. Worshipers wash their hands at the shrine and bow. The gate marks the step from ordinary space into sacred space. The Grand Shrine at Ise, dedicated to the sun kami Amaterasu, is Shinto’s most honored shrine.

Many Shinto customs mark the seasons and the stages of life. Families bring newborns to a shrine. On Shichi-Go-San, children ages three, five and seven visit a shrine in bright clothes. Festivals called matsuri carry the kami through the streets in portable shrines. Many Japanese people say they take part in Shinto customs as tradition more than as belief.

22.10

Three Teachings and Two Ways

Main ideaIn China and Japan, many people blend several traditions, which makes simple religious labels hard to apply.

In a Chinese home, a family might keep a tablet honoring ancestors, a Confucian practice. On a holiday they might visit a Daoist temple to ask for good fortune. For a funeral they might invite Buddhist monks to chant. In China these are often called the : Confucianism, Daoism and Buddhism. Many people draw on all three.

In Japan, Buddhism arrived from Korea in the 6th century CE. Over time, Buddhism and Shinto blended closely. Some Buddhists taught that the kami were local forms of Buddhas. Temples and shrines often stood side by side. In 1868, the Meiji government ordered Shinto and Buddhism to be officially separated. Even so, many families still take part in both, as the Satos did.

This , or blending of traditions, makes counting adherents hard. A survey question like What is your religion? may not fit someone who follows several ways. Scholars say it is more accurate to describe what people practice than to fit them into one box. Chicago’s Chinatown and its Japanese American communities show some of these blended customs today.
