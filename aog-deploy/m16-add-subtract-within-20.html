Two things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

7 + 2 is 8, because you count “7, 8.”

The 7 is where you start, not a step you take. Say 7, and THEN count two more: 8, 9. Counting the start number is the most common wrong answer at this level, and it is always off by exactly one.

Say the start number, then start counting.

⚠️ People often think…

The word “more” always means add.

“How many MORE does Sam have than Ana?” is a comparison, and you subtract to answer it. Read the whole question, not one word in it.

Key words lie. Picture the parts and the whole.

Helpful Hints

🔟 Make a ten — the four steps

8 + 5: ask how many the big number needs to reach ten (8 needs 2) → break the small number into that and the rest (5 = 2 + 3) → add up to ten (8 + 2 = 10) → add what is left (10 + 3 = 13).

Need, break, ten, rest.

💡 The whole topic in one idea

Every problem has a WHOLE and two PARTS. Adding puts the parts together to find the whole. Subtracting starts with the whole and finds the part that is missing. Once you know which number is the whole, you know what to do.

Parts make the whole. Whole minus a part is the other part.

⚠️ Traps the test loves

  • Counting the start number as a step: 7 + 2 as “7, 8” = 8. Say 7, THEN count 8, 9.
  • Seeing the word MORE and adding. “How many more does Sam have?” is a compare: subtract.
  • Reading = as “the answer comes next.” 7 + 3 = 4 + 6 is true because BOTH sides are 10.
  • Pushing digits together: 4 + 4 = 44. Count or use the double: 8.
  • Forgetting the leftover in make-a-ten: 8 + 5 → 8 + 2 = 10 and stopping. Add the 3 that is left.
  • Subtracting to find a missing whole: ? − 5 = 9 is NOT 4. The blank is the whole, so add: 14.

🎯 How the test will ask

  • A bare fact — “8 + 6 = ?” Pick a strategy: make a ten or doubles plus two.
  • A story — “How many in all? How many are left? How many more?” Find the whole and the parts before you pick + or −.
  • A blank in the middle — “5 + __ = 12.” Count on from the part to the whole.
  • A blank at the start — “__ − 4 = 7.” That blank is the whole: add.
  • True or false — “6 + 4 = 3 + 7?” Work out each side; same number means true.
  • Three numbers to add — “4 + 6 + 3.” Find the ten first.
  • A fact family — “Write the four facts for 3, 9 and 12.” Two adds, two subtracts; the whole leads every subtract.

✅ Before the test, can you…

  • Add and subtract within 10 quickly, from memory?
  • Count on and count back without counting the start number?
  • Say all the pairs that make ten, and all the doubles to 10 + 10?
  • Make a ten to add 9 + 7 or 8 + 6?
  • Write the four facts of a fact family?
  • Tell a put-together story from a take-from story from a compare story?
  • Find a missing number anywhere in a number sentence, and say if an equation is true?
