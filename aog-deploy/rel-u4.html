What Is a Religion?

Method

People all over the world belong to religions. Your classmates, neighbors and friends may keep many different holidays. Some may follow no religion at all. In this unit you will learn how to study religion the way a fair scholar does.

First you will learn three parts most religions have: beliefs, practices and communities. You will learn what five symbols mean to the people who use them. Then you will fill a whole year with holidays. You will find out why some move and some stay put.

By the end, you will be able to describe a religion without judging it. You will use words like belief, tradition and sacred. And you will know when to say "Happy Diwali" or "Eid Mubarak."

The big question

How can we learn about the beliefs and holidays of others in a fair and kind way?

The story

Five Symbols on One Poster

A class makes a poster of five symbols and asks what each one means to the people who use it.

Ms. Reyes taped a big white poster to the wall. She drew five empty boxes on it. "Each group gets one symbol," she said. "Your job is to find out what it means to the people who use it." The class leaned in to see the cards she held up.

Omar's group got a cross. Priya's group got the word om, written in an old Indian script. Leo's group got a six-pointed star. Ana's group got a wheel with eight spokes. Jaspreet's group got a symbol with a sword in the middle, a circle and two curved swords.

The groups read library books. Some students asked family members. Omar said, "Christians use the cross to remember Jesus." Jaspreet said, "The khanda is a Sikh symbol. My grandpa has it on his car." Ana said her book called the wheel a sign of the Buddha's teaching.

Then Leo raised his hand. "What if a symbol means nothing to me?" he asked. Ms. Reyes smiled. "That is fine. You do not have to believe it. Our job is to learn what it means to others, and to say it fairly."

By Friday the poster was full. Each box had a symbol, a name and one sentence. Every sentence started the same way: "People who use this symbol believe..." The class read the poster out loud together.

Section 1

Three Parts of a Religion

7.1

What People Believe

Main ideaMost religions teach beliefs, ideas their followers hold to be true about life and what matters most.

Many religions answer big questions. Where did the world come from? How should people treat each other? What happens after we die? The answers a religion teaches are its . Followers hold these ideas to be true.

Beliefs are different in each religion. Jews, Christians and Muslims believe in one God. Many Hindus worship many gods and goddesses. Many Hindus also see them as forms of one great spirit. Many Buddhists focus on the Buddha’s teaching more than on a god.

People inside one religion do not always agree, either. Two Christians may believe some things differently. That is why we say "many Christians believe" and not "all Christians believe." Careful words help us be fair.

Jews say a prayer called the Shema that begins with this line. What belief does it share?

Words to know
belief
an idea a person holds to be true, such as an idea about God or about how to live
religion
a way of life shared by a group, with beliefs, practices and a community
Check yourself

3. Which three religions teach belief in one God?

7.2

What People Do

Main ideaReligions are also practices, things followers do, like praying, eating special foods and keeping holidays.

A religion is not only ideas. It is also things people do. Praying, singing, fasting and sharing food are . Practices are actions people repeat, often in the same way each time.

Many Muslims pray five times a day. Many Jewish families light candles on Friday night for Shabbat, the day of rest. Many Hindus do at home. Puja is worship with flowers, light and food offered to a god.

Some practices happen every day. Some happen once a year, like a holiday. Some happen once in a life, like a wedding. Watching what people do teaches a lot about what they believe.

7.3

Who They Do It With

Main ideaReligions are communities, groups of people who gather and help each other, often at a house of worship.

Most religions are shared. People pray together, eat together and help each other. A group like this is a . A community can be a small family or millions of people around the world.

Many communities meet in a . Christians gather in churches. Jews gather in synagogues. Muslims gather in mosques. Hindus and Buddhists visit temples. Sikhs gather in a gurdwara.

Chicago has houses of worship of every kind. A church, a mosque and a temple may stand on the same street. Each one is a place where a community meets.

The Cross and the Star

Main ideaA symbol is a picture that stands for an idea; the cross stands for Christianity and the six-pointed star for Judaism.

7.9

Describe, Do Not Judge

Main ideaAt school we learn about religions by describing them fairly, which is different from being part of one.

Learning about a religion is not the same as joining it. A student can learn how Muslims pray without praying. A student can learn Christmas stories without being Christian. At school, we study. We do not practice.

The main rule is to , not judge. To describe is to tell what people believe and do. To judge is to say they are right or wrong. We say "Sikhs believe..." We do not say "Sikhs are right" or "Sikhs are wrong."

Good questions help. Ask, "What does this mean to you?" Do not tease about food, clothes or holidays. Every student should feel safe and welcome in class.

Sun and Moon Calendars

8.1

Two Clocks in the Sky

Main ideaSome calendars count years by the sun and some count months by the moon, and a few use both.

People have always watched the sky to count time. Earth goes around the sun once a year. That takes about 365 days. A calendar that follows this trip is a . The calendar on most school walls is solar.

The moon changes shape as it goes around Earth. It goes from new, to full, and back to new in about 29 and a half days. That is about one month. A calendar that counts moon months is a .

Twelve moon months add up to about 354 days. That is about 11 days shorter than a sun year. Some calendars add an extra month now and then to catch up with the sun. These use both the moon and the sun.

8.2

Why Some Holidays Move

Main ideaHolidays set by the moon move on the school calendar, while Christmas stays on one date.

The Islamic calendar is lunar. Each year its months start about 11 days earlier on the school calendar. So Ramadan, the month of fasting, moves. Over about 33 years, it passes through every season.

Easter moves too. In many churches, Easter is the first Sunday after the first full moon of spring. So it can fall in March or April. Many Orthodox Christians use an older calendar. Their Easter is often a little later.

Christmas does not move on the school calendar. Many Christians keep it on December 25 each year. Many Orthodox Christians keep it on January 7. That date matches December 25 on their older calendar.

Main ideaMuslims fast in daylight during Ramadan, celebrate Eid al-Fitr at its end, and keep Eid al-Adha at the time of the pilgrimage.

Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar. Muslims believe the Qur’an was first sent down to the Prophet Muhammad in this month. Many adult Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. At sunset, families often break the fast with dates and water.

When the new moon ends Ramadan, begins. Eid means feast. Families pray together, wear new clothes and visit friends. Many give food or money to people in need.

About two months later comes Eid al-Adha. It comes at the time of the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca. It remembers the Qur’an’s story of Abraham’s obedience to God. Many families share meat with neighbors and people in need.

Vesak and Vaisakhi

Main ideaVesak honors the Buddha on a spring full moon, and Vaisakhi is a Sikh spring holiday remembering the Khalsa.

is a Buddhist holiday on a full moon in April or May. Many Buddhists remember the Buddha’s birth, his awakening and his death on this day. They bring flowers to temples. Many light lamps and give to people in need.

Some Buddhists keep these memories on separate days. Buddhists in Japan often mark the Buddha’s birth on April 8. This shows how customs differ inside one tradition.

A Year on One Wall

Main ideaPutting holidays on one calendar shows what each one remembers and how different calendars work together.

Look at a full year of holidays. Each one something. That means it remembers an event or honors a person. Passover remembers the Exodus. Christmas remembers Jesus’s birth. Vesak remembers the Buddha.

Some holidays last one day. Others last about a week or even more. Ramadan lasts a whole month. The length is one way a tradition shows what matters most to it.

A shared calendar helps a school be fair. Teachers can plan tests so they do not land on big holidays. Friends can learn when to say "Happy Diwali" or "Eid Mubarak."
