Comparison and Debate

Comparison

Comparison is one of the oldest ways to think. Chinese readers have compared Confucius and Laozi for more than two thousand years. Today students also set the Chinese classics beside Jewish, Christian, Hindu, Buddhist and Greek texts. Done well, comparison sharpens each text. Done badly, it flattens or ranks them.

In this unit you compare in two directions. First you look inside the Chinese tradition: ritual and naturalness, the legend of Confucius meeting Laozi, Analects 14.36 beside Daodejing 63, and the debate over whether Confucianism is a religion. Then you look outward: the Golden Rule in five traditions, the junzi beside Aristotle and the bodhisattva, Mencius and Xunzi beside Hobbes and Rousseau, and wu wei beside the Bhagavad Gita.

By the end you will be able to build a fair comparison from exact citations, name real differences and real overlaps, explain each tradition's grounding for its teaching, and write about several traditions without ranking them.

The big question

How can we compare the Chinese classics with each other and with other traditions honestly, without flattening them or ranking them?

Confucius and Laozi in Dialogue

Inside Comparison
Big questionWhere do the Confucian and Daoist classics truly disagree, where do they overlap, and how have Chinese readers held them together?
The story

Two Quotations on a Board

Two sayings about repaying injury go up side by side, and the class splits.

Ms. Lin writes two short sayings on the whiteboard of her twelfth-grade philosophy class in suburban Chicago. She does not say where they come from. The first: someone asks whether injury should be repaid with kindness, and the answer is, repay injury with justice, and kindness with kindness. The second: repay injury with kindness.

She asks the class to stand on the side of the room that matches the saying they find more convincing. About half the students walk to the window side, under the first saying. The rest go to the door side, under the second. A few stand in the middle and refuse to choose.

Then she reveals the sources. The first is Analects 14.36, a saying of Confucius. The second is from chapter 63 of the Daodejing. Several students are surprised. One says he thought the Daodejing was the book about fairness. Another says she assumed Confucius would be the gentle one.

Ms. Lin tells them the two lines have been read together for centuries. Some commentators think the Analects passage answers the Daodejing's line directly. Others doubt that and say the ideas were simply in the air. She asks each side to explain what the other side's saying gets right. The room gets quieter as they think.

Talk about itWhat does "repay injury with justice" protect that "repay injury with kindness" might risk? What does the second protect that the first might risk?
Section 1

Ritual and Naturalness

31.1

Two Starting Points

Main ideaThe Analects often starts from ritual and learning that shape a person, while the Daodejing often starts from naturalness that learning and ritual can spoil.

Analects 12.1 says ren is to subdue oneself and return to propriety, li. Analects 1.1 opens with the pleasure of constant learning. The Confucian picture often starts from shaping: people become good through study, ritual and good models. A person is carved and polished over time.

The Daodejing often starts elsewhere. Chapter 19 urges people to cast off "sageliness" and cleverness so the people benefit. Chapter 38 calls ritual the thin edge of loyalty and the start of disorder. Chapter 28 praises returning to the "uncarved block," , raw wood not yet shaped. Its picture often values what is natural before shaping.

These are tendencies, not walls. The Analects also warns that ritual without feeling is empty (3.3). The Daodejing also speaks of the sage’s care and "three treasures." Scholars warn against turning the books into cartoons of rule-follower and free spirit. The disagreement is real, but both books share concern for a good and peaceful life.

Think it through
Carved or Uncarved?

Analects 1.15 (paraphrased): a disciple quotes a poem about cutting, filing, carving and polishing jade, and the Master praises him for seeing it as a picture of self-cultivation. Daodejing 28 (paraphrased): return to the uncarved block.

What does each image value? Could a person hold both at once? How?

31.2

When Confucius Met Laozi

Main ideaSima Qian's Records of the Historian tells of Confucius visiting Laozi to ask about ritual, a meeting scholars treat as legend but which shaped how the two were seen.

The historian Sima Qian, writing around 100 BCE in the Shiji, the Records of the Historian, tells a story in chapter 63. Confucius travels to the Zhou capital to ask Laozi about ritual. Laozi tells him to set aside his proud airs and many desires. Afterward Confucius tells his disciples that Laozi is like a dragon, beyond his understanding.

Most modern scholars treat the meeting as a : a traditional story that may not be historical. Sima Qian himself admitted uncertainty about Laozi’s identity. Still, other early texts, including Confucian ritual records, also mention Confucius learning from a Lao Dan about rites. The story may preserve some old tradition.

The legend shaped how Chinese readers saw the two teachers: an elder sage and a younger one, not enemies. Artists in the Han dynasty carved the meeting on tomb stones. Religious Daoists told it with honor for Laozi. Confucian readers told it with honor for Confucius’s humility in asking. Each told it their own way.

From the source

After meeting Laozi, Confucius told his disciples that he knew how birds fly, fish swim and beasts run, and how each can be caught; but a dragon rides the wind and clouds up to heaven, and he could not know it. Laozi, he said, was like a dragon.
Sima Qian, Shiji ch. 63 (Biography of Laozi), c. 90s BCE; paraphrased for this course (paraphrased)

31.3

Confucius in the Zhuangzi

Main ideaThe Zhuangzi uses Confucius as a character, sometimes teasing him and sometimes making him voice Daoist ideas.

Confucius appears many times in the Zhuangzi as a character. In some stories he is corrected by hermits, fishermen or robbers. In chapter 29, the robber Zhi sends him away with a scolding. These stories poke fun at Confucian concern for rank, office and ritual.

In other stories Confucius becomes a Daoist teacher. In chapter 4 he teaches Yan Hui the "fasting of the mind," emptying oneself to meet the world. In chapter 6, Yan Hui tells Confucius he has learned "sitting and forgetting." Confucius is so impressed that he asks to become Yan Hui’s follower.

Readers explain this in different ways. Some see mockery. Some see respect: the Zhuangzi borrows Confucius’s authority to teach its own ideas. Some later scholars, including Guo Xiang, argued that the Zhuangzi secretly honored Confucius as the true sage. The book’s playful use of a real teacher is itself a kind of between schools.

Section 2

Repaying Injury

31.4

Justice for Injury

Main ideaIn Analects 14.36, the Master rejects repaying injury with kindness and says to repay injury with justice and kindness with kindness.

Analects 14.36 is a short exchange. Legge: "Some one said, ’What do you say concerning the principle that injury should be recompensed with kindness?’ The Master said, ’With what then will you recompense kindness? Recompense injury with justice, and recompense kindness with kindness.’"

The Master’s answer turns on a question of balance. If injury gets kindness, what is left for kindness? Legge’s "justice" translates zhi, which also means straightness or uprightness. Some translators write "uprightness" or "straightness." The idea is to respond to injury honestly and fairly, not with revenge and not with a show of favor.

Readers note that the questioner may be quoting a known saying. Some commentators link it to Daodejing 63. Others say the idea was common and cannot be traced to one book. Either way, 14.36 shows the Analects valuing : a response that fits what was done.

31.5

Kindness for Injury

Main ideaDaodejing chapter 63 includes repaying injury with kindness among the sage's ways of acting without forcing.

Chapter 63 is a list of paradoxes. Act without acting. Handle affairs without fuss. Taste the tasteless. Treat the small as great and the few as many. And, in the same list, repay injury with kindness, or with , virtue. The line sits among ways the sage handles things before they grow large.

The chapter goes on to say that hard things should be handled while they are easy, and great things while they are small. Read in context, repaying injury with kindness may be part of this: meeting harm early and gently so it does not grow. Some readers take it as a moral ideal. Others take it as practical wisdom.

Many readers have compared this line with the Christian teaching to love one’s enemies (Matthew 5:44) and with Buddhist teachings on returning hatred with non-hatred. Scholars caution that similar words can have different reasons behind them. Comparing them well means asking why each tradition says it.

31.6

Weighing the Two Lines

Main ideaReaders have long set Analects 14.36 beside Daodejing 63, and they disagree about whether one answers the other.

Put the two lines side by side. The Analects keeps separate responses for injury and kindness. The Daodejing gives kindness to both. On the surface they contradict. Many readers treat this pair as a clean example of a Confucian and a Daoist difference.

Commentators have gone further. Some said the questioner in 14.36 was quoting Laozi, so Confucius was answering him. Others noted that we cannot date the two texts precisely, so we cannot say who answered whom. Scholars today usually say the idea of repaying injury with kindness was known in the period and appears in both.

Readers also ask whether the lines are about the same situation. The Analects may address public justice and relationships among equals. The Daodejing may address a sage’s way of stopping conflict early. Reading each in its can soften the contradiction without erasing it. The class in the story was right to find both lines hard.

Rule, Religion and the Three Teachings

31.7

Virtue or Non-Interference?

Main ideaThe Analects pictures a ruler who leads by visible virtue; the Daodejing pictures one who governs by not interfering.

Analects 2.1 makes the ruler a pole star all others turn toward. Analects 12.19 says the junzi’s virtue is like wind and the people’s like grass: the grass bends when the wind blows. In both images the ruler is seen and followed. Leadership is a matter of .

Daodejing 17 prefers a ruler the people hardly know. Chapter 57 says the sage does not act and the people change by themselves. Chapter 60 warns against stirring the small fish. In these images the ruler steps back. Leadership is a matter of restraint.

Readers find common ground. Both books reject rule by force and fear. Both put the ruler’s own character first. Both, in different ways, trust that good order grows rather than being imposed. The difference is how visible the ruler should be. Later Chinese statesmen often drew on both.

31.8

Is Confucianism a Religion?

Main ideaScholars and practitioners disagree about whether Confucianism is a religion, and the answer depends partly on what "religion" means.

Consider the evidence people point to. Confucian temples stand in many cities, and ceremonies honor Confucius each year. Families offer rites to ancestors. The Analects speaks of Heaven, Tian. Yet the Analects also says the Master did not talk about spirits (7.20) and urges respect for spirits while keeping them at a distance (6.20).

Views differ. Legge, in The Religions of China (1880), treated Confucianism as a religion. The sociologist C. K. Yang described Chinese religion as "diffused": spread through family and state rather than gathered in one church. The reformer Kang Youwei, in the 1910s, tried to make Confucianism China’s state religion. Many scholars call it a philosophy or a way of life.

Practitioners disagree too. Some see their rites as religious devotion. Others see them as family memory and ethics. The question also depends on the word , which grew up in Europe with Christianity in mind. This course names these views and does not settle the question.

31.9

The Three Teachings as One

Main ideaMany Chinese people have practiced Confucian, Daoist and Buddhist teachings together, a view summed up in the phrase "the three teachings are one."

In many Chinese towns, one family might honor ancestors with Confucian rites, visit a Daoist temple for a festival, and invite Buddhist monks to chant at a funeral. Some temples hold images of Confucius, Laozi and the Buddha side by side. The phrase , "the three teachings are one," names this outlook.

Thinkers argued for it in different ways. Some said each teaching covered a different area: Confucianism for society, Daoism for the body, Buddhism for the mind. In the Ming dynasty, Lin Zhao’en founded a "Three-in-One" teaching that still has temples in Fujian and Southeast Asia. Others kept the teachings separate and argued for one over the others.

This outlook challenges the habit of treating religions as exclusive boxes. Scholars use terms like , the blending of traditions, but some think the word is too simple for Chinese practice. For many families, the question "which one are you?" does not fit how they live.

31.10

Comparing Without Ranking

Main ideaA good comparison of the two classics describes differences and overlaps accurately and lets each speak in its own terms.

Comparison can go wrong in two ways. One is flattening: saying the two books are really the same. The other is cartooning: making one book all rules and the other all freedom. Both miss what the texts say. A careful comparison names real differences and real overlaps.

A simple method helps. Pick one question, such as how to respond to injury or how to rule. Find a passage in each book. Quote or paraphrase each exactly. Describe what each says in its own terms. Then name one difference and one overlap, and describe how readers in each tradition have handled them.

What you should not do is declare a winner. This is not because the question is unimportant. It is because a public school course studies what traditions teach, not which is true. Your job is to make each position as clear and strong as its best readers would. Philosophers call this the principle of .

The Golden Rule and the Good Person Across Traditions

Ethics Compared
Big questionWhat can we learn by setting Chinese classical ethics beside other traditions, and how can we compare them without ranking?
The story

Five Rules on One Page

A student lines up five versions of the Golden Rule and notices one is in the negative.

Daniel is preparing a project for his world religions elective. He has found five sayings that people often call the Golden Rule. He prints them in a column on one page: from the Analects, from the Talmud, from the Gospel of Matthew, from the Mahabharata, and from a Buddhist text. He plans to show that every tradition says the same thing.

Then he reads them slowly. The Analects says: what you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others. Hillel, in the Talmud, says: what is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow. Matthew says: whatever you would want others to do to you, do so to them. Two tell you what not to do. One tells you what to do.

He notices other differences too. Hillel adds that this is the whole Torah and the rest is commentary, and then says: go and learn. Matthew adds that this is the law and the prophets. The Analects gives it as one word, reciprocity, for a whole life. Each saying sits inside its own book and its own reasons.

Daniel crosses out his title, "They All Say the Same Thing." He writes a new one: "Five Rules, Five Homes." His teacher asks what changed. He says the sayings are close, but each one belongs to a different house. He wants to show the rooms, not just the doors.

Section 1

The Golden Rule Compared

32.1

Reciprocity in the Analects

Main ideaThe Analects gives both a negative form of reciprocity (15.23) and a positive one (6.28), which readers often forget.

Analects 15.23 gives the best-known form. Legge: "What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others." The same words appear in 12.2, where the Master answers a question about ren. This is the negative form: a limit on what one does to other people.

A positive form appears in 6.28 in Legge’s numbering; some editions number it 6.30. Legge: "Now the man of perfect virtue, wishing to be established himself, seeks also to establish others; wishing to be enlarged himself, he seeks also to enlarge others." Here ren means actively helping others reach what one wants for oneself.

Legge argued that the negative form was weaker than Christianity’s positive rule. Many scholars since have answered that he overlooked 6.28. Others argue the negative form has its own strength: it respects that others may want different things than you do. The debate shows how can carry a hidden ranking.

Confucian Analects 6.28 (6.30 in some editions), trans. James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1, 2nd ed., 1893

32.2

Hillel and Matthew

Main ideaThe Talmud's Hillel gives a negative form and the Gospel of Matthew a positive form, each placed inside its own tradition's reasons.

The Babylonian Talmud, tractate Shabbat 31a, tells of a man who asks the sage Hillel to teach him the whole Torah while standing on one foot. Hillel answers, in paraphrase: what is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow; that is the whole Torah, the rest is commentary; go and learn it. Hillel lived around the turn of the first century BCE and CE.

The Gospel of Matthew, chapter 7, verse 12, gives a positive form in the Sermon on the Mount. In paraphrase: in everything, do to others what you would have them do to you, for this is the law and the prophets. Christians often call this the Golden Rule. The name itself became common in English in the 1600s.

Notice how each saying points beyond itself. Hillel says "go and learn," sending the man to study. Matthew ties the rule to "the law and the prophets," the Hebrew scriptures. The Analects ties it to shu and ren. Each rule is a of a larger body of teaching, and each tradition reads it through that body.

32.3

The Mahabharata and Buddhist Texts

Main ideaHindu and Buddhist texts also give reciprocity rules, each grounded in their own ideas of dharma, karma or compassion.

The Mahabharata, the great Sanskrit epic of India, gives a rule of reciprocity in its thirteenth book, the Anushasana Parva. In paraphrase: one should never do to another what one would find harmful to oneself; this, in brief, is dharma. means duty or the right way to live.

Buddhist texts also link self and others. The Dhammapada, verses 129–130, says in paraphrase that all beings fear punishment and love life, so, comparing others with oneself, one should not harm or kill. The reason is tied to Buddhist teaching on suffering and compassion for all beings.

So the five rules on Daniel’s page have five different homes. Confucian shu grows from ren and the family. Hillel’s grows from Torah. Matthew’s from the law and the prophets and Jesus’s teaching. The Mahabharata’s from dharma. The Dhammapada’s from compassion for all beings. Scholars call this : the reason a tradition gives for a rule.

The Junzi and Aristotle's Virtuous Person

Main ideaBoth the Analects and Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics describe virtue as built by practice and habit, though they differ on ritual, family and the goal of life.

Aristotle, a Greek philosopher of the fourth century BCE, wrote the Nicomachean Ethics. In book 2 he argues that virtues come from habit: we become just by doing just acts, as we become builders by building. He describes virtue as a between too much and too little, such as courage between rashness and cowardice.

Scholars often compare this with the junzi. The Analects also stresses practice: learning, rites, repeated effort. The Doctrine of the Mean, a Confucian classic, speaks of zhongyong, often translated as the mean or the constant center. Some scholars see real kinship. Others warn the two "means" are not the same idea.

The differences are also clear. Aristotle centers the good life on the city and on reason. The Analects centers it on family, ritual and relationships. Aristotle names happiness, eudaimonia, as the goal. The Analects speaks more of harmony and the Way. Comparing them sharpens each.

We become builders by building and harpists by playing the harp; in the same way we become just by doing just acts, self-controlled by doing self-controlled acts, and brave by doing brave acts.
Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics book 2, ch. 1, paraphrased for this course (paraphrased)

The Junzi and the Bodhisattva

Main ideaThe bodhisattva of Mahayana Buddhism and the Confucian junzi both serve others, but for different reasons and toward different goals.

In Mahayana Buddhism, a is one who seeks enlightenment in order to help all beings become free from suffering. Many Mahayana Buddhists take vows to save all beings. The bodhisattva Guanyin, honored across China for compassion, is among the most beloved figures in Chinese religious life.

The junzi also serves others. Analects 14.45 in Legge (14.42 in many editions) says the junzi cultivates himself to bring peace to others and to all the people. Both ideals move from self-cultivation to care for others. But the junzi works mainly within family, ritual and government. The bodhisattva’s care reaches all beings across many lifetimes.

Chinese thinkers argued about these ideals for centuries. Some Confucians criticized monks for leaving their families. Some Buddhists answered that their care was wider, not weaker. Others joined them, as in the three-teachings outlook. The comparison shows two ideas of service shaped by different pictures of the world.

32.6

Human Nature: Mencius, Xunzi, Hobbes, Rousseau

Main ideaThe Mencius–Xunzi debate on human nature is often compared with European debates, especially Hobbes and Rousseau, though the questions differ.

Mencius said human nature has good beginnings. Xunzi said it tends to selfishness and needs shaping by ritual and teachers. Centuries later in Europe, Thomas Hobbes, in Leviathan (1651), described life without government as a war of all against all. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in his Discourse on Inequality (1755), argued that people in a natural state were not wicked, and that society brought many vices.

It is tempting to line them up: Mencius with Rousseau, Xunzi with Hobbes. Some scholars find this useful. Others warn it misleads. Xunzi’s answer is ritual and teachers, not an all-powerful state. Mencius’s sprouts need cultivation within family and society, while Rousseau often blames society itself.

The comparison works best as a set of questions, not a match. What is a person like before training? What makes people good? What role do family, ritual, law and government play? Each thinker answers these differently. Laying out the answers side by side helps a reader see each clearly.

Think it through

Q1: What are people like before training? Q2: What makes people good? Q3: What role does government play? Mencius: good sprouts; cultivation; humane rule. Xunzi: selfish desires; ritual and teachers; rule by ritual. Hobbes: war of all against all; fear and agreement; a strong sovereign. Rousseau: not wicked; ______; ______.

32.7

Wu Wei and the Gita

Main ideaDaoist wu wei and the Bhagavad Gita's action without attachment both describe acting without grasping, but rest on different worldviews.

The Bhagavad Gita is part of the Mahabharata. In chapter 2, verse 47, Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna, in paraphrase: you have a right to your action, but never to its fruits; do not act for the fruits, and do not cling to inaction. Hindu readers call this , action without desire for its results.

Wu wei in the Daodejing is also a kind of acting without grasping. Chapter 2 says the sage acts but does not claim, achieves but does not dwell on it. Cook Ding in the Zhuangzi carves without forcing. Many readers see a family resemblance between the two ideas.

The worldviews differ, though. The Gita places action within duty, dharma, and devotion to Krishna. Wu wei places it within the Dao and naturalness, ziran. The Gita’s setting is a battlefield and a warrior’s duty. The Daodejing is wary of weapons and war. Comparing them means holding both the likeness and the difference.

Building a Comparison Chart

Main ideaA comparison chart built from exact quotations and clear citations lets readers see likenesses and differences without ranking.

A good chart has rows for traditions and columns for questions. For the Golden Rule, columns might be: the exact words, the form (positive or negative), the speaker and setting, and the grounding. Every cell cites its source: text, chapter and verse, and translation.

Use exact quotations only where a public-domain translation lets you. Legge’s Analects, Mencius, Daodejing and Zhuangzi can be quoted. For other texts, write a careful paraphrase and label it. Never mix a quotation and a paraphrase in one cell without marking which is which. This is what protects.

Last, check the chart for hidden ranking. Are positive forms always listed first, as if better? Does one tradition get more space or warmer words? Are your column headings drawn from only one tradition’s vocabulary? A fair chart gives each tradition the same questions and the same care.

32.9

Why Compare at All?

Main ideaComparison can deepen understanding of each tradition, but it can also flatten or rank them, so its purpose must be clear.

People compare traditions for many reasons. Some seek common ground for living together. Some want to understand their own tradition better by seeing another. Some scholars study how ideas travel. The comparative study of religion and philosophy has its own long history and debates.

Critics point out dangers. Comparison can take one tradition’s categories, often Western and Christian, and measure others by them. Legge’s view of the negative Golden Rule is one example. Comparison can also flatten, as in "all religions say the same thing." Many believers in each tradition reject that claim.

A careful comparison names its purpose, uses each tradition’s own terms, cites exactly and lets differences stand. Daniel’s new title, "Five Rules, Five Homes," does this. It shows the likeness and honors the homes. That is the stance of in this course.

32.10

The Classics in a Global Conversation

Main ideaChinese classical ethics now takes part in worldwide debates, from virtue ethics to care ethics to discussions of human rights.

In recent decades, philosophers have compared Confucian ethics with Western virtue ethics, which grew from Aristotle. Others have compared Confucian ideas about relationships with care ethics, a movement begun by feminist philosophers such as Nel Noddings. Some scholars write about Confucian views of human rights.

These conversations run both ways. Some Western thinkers draw on the Analects to rethink family and role. Some Chinese and East Asian thinkers use Western ideas to reread their classics. Scholars in Korea, Japan, Singapore and the United States all take part.

Critics warn of : reading today’s debates into ancient texts. Supporters answer that every reading comes from some present, and honest comparison can reveal new sides of old texts. A careful student names which present they read from and still cites the ancient text exactly.

Think it through
Old Text, New Question
