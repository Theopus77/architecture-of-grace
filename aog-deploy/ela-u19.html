Research and Writing

Writing

Every day you are asked to believe things. A post says a food cures headaches. A friend says a rule is unfair. A page says a cow burned down Chicago. Research is the set of habits that lets you check instead of guess: turn a vague topic into a question that can be answered, find sources that have earned trust, take notes that keep other people's words separate from yours, and put what you found together into an idea nobody handed you. Those habits are the difference between repeating and knowing.

Then comes the writing. An essay that works is not longer or fancier than one that does not. It makes a claim a reader could disagree with, supports it with evidence the reader can check, takes the other side seriously, opens with something worth reading and ends with something worth remembering. This unit walks through the kinds of essays high school asks for, analysis, argument, explanation and narrative, and the moves that make each one work, from the first sentence to the last cut.

By the end you will be able to take a question you actually care about, find and judge sources on it, cite them in MLA style without fear, and write an essay that says something true in your own voice. You will also be able to look at a draft that is asleep, yours or a classmate's, and know exactly how to wake it up.

The big question

How does a writer move from what other people have said to something true that is their own?

The steps
Day 1

A topic becomes a research question with limits on who, when and which angle

Day 2

Keyword searches on the web, then in a library database, turn up eight possible sources

Day 3

The Research Process

Research
Big questionWhen eight sources all claim to answer your question, how do you decide which ones deserve your trust?
The story

Eight Sources, Three Survivors

A student types a question into a search bar, gets eight promising results, and then does the hard part: asking each one to prove itself.

Marisol's question was simple to say and hard to answer: what really started the Great Chicago Fire of 1871? She had heard the story about Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicking over a lantern. Her uncle swore it was true. Her history teacher said nobody knows. Marisol wanted to find out for herself, so on a Tuesday night she opened a laptop and typed the question in. The screen filled with results in less than a second. She picked the first eight that looked useful and pasted their links into a document.

Then she went back and looked at each one slowly. Source one was a blog post with no author's name and no date, and it repeated the cow story as fact. Source two was a page selling Chicago tours; the fire was a paragraph between two ads. Source three was a video with dramatic music and a narrator who never said where his facts came from. Source four was a student essay someone had posted online. Marisol crossed all four out. Not one could tell her who wrote it, when, or why she should believe it.

Source five was a newspaper article from 1871, scanned and posted by a library. It was written days after the fire, by a reporter who was there. Source six was a book by a historian, published by a university press, with notes showing where every fact came from. Source seven was the website of the Chicago History Museum, which had a page on the fire's causes. These three could answer her hard questions. She kept them.

Source eight was the interesting one. It was a 1893 newspaper piece in which a reporter admitted he had helped invent the cow story to sell papers. Marisol was not sure at first whether to trust it. Then she found that the historian's book discussed the same confession. Two sources, independent of each other, pointed the same way. That is when a fact starts to feel solid.

Marisol's paper did not say the cow was innocent, because nobody can prove that either. It said the cow story was never proven, that the official inquiry in 1871 could not name a cause, and that a reporter later admitted helping to spread the tale. Every sentence pointed back to a source a reader could check. That is what research is: not collecting claims, but testing them, and then showing your work.

Talk about itMarisol threw out four sources in five minutes. What questions did she ask that let her decide so fast, and which of those questions could you ask about any source?
Section 1

From Topic to Question

44.1

Narrowing a Topic to a Question

Main ideaA topic is a subject; a research question is a specific thing you want to find out, and only the question can guide a search.

A student says, "My paper is about social media." That is a , and it is the size of an ocean. You cannot research an ocean. You can research one wave. A is a question about the topic that is narrow enough to answer and open enough to be worth answering. "Does social media use affect how well high school students sleep?" is a wave. It names who, what and which effect. It could be answered with evidence.

To narrow a topic, add limits. Limit the group: not everyone, but teenagers in the United States. Limit the time: not all of history, but the last ten years. Limit the angle: not every effect, but sleep, or grades, or friendship. Then turn the narrowed topic into a question that starts with how, why, to what extent, or does. A good question cannot be answered with a single word you already know. "Is social media popular?" is not worth ten pages. "Why did one Chicago high school ban phones, and what changed?" might be.

Test your question before you commit to it. Is it too big? If you could write a book on it, cut it down. Is it too small? If one sentence on a museum website answers it, open it up. Is it actually a question, or a claim you already believe? "Why is social media bad for teens?" has decided the answer before the research begins. "How does social media use relate to teen sleep?" leaves room for the evidence to surprise you. Good researchers want to be surprised. That is the whole reason to look.

44.2

Searching Like a Researcher

Main ideaGood searching means choosing precise keywords, using search tools on purpose, and knowing when to leave the open web for a library database.

Typing your whole question into a search box works badly. The search engine matches words, not meanings, so "why did the Chicago River get reversed" pulls in every page with the word river. Researchers search with , the two or four words that carry the meaning. For that question, the keywords are Chicago River reversal 1900. Add a word to narrow, drop a word to widen. If results are about the wrong thing, add a minus sign before the word you want gone: reversal -car keeps out pages about backing up a vehicle.

Quotation marks tell a search engine to find an exact phrase. Searching "Sanitary and Ship Canal" with the marks finds pages that use that exact name. Without the marks you get pages about canals, ships and sanitation separately. Search engines also let you limit results by date or by site. Adding site:.gov shows only government pages; adding site:.edu shows only college and university pages. These are not always the best pages, but they are a fast way to find sources with a name behind them.

For serious work, the open web is only the first stop. A is a searchable collection of published articles, books and records that a library pays for. Illinois students can search many databases free through their school library or through the state’s online library service. The articles there were edited and checked before they were published, which the open web does not promise. A good research day often goes like this: search the web to learn the vocabulary of your topic, then take that vocabulary into a database to find the sources you will actually cite.

Section 2

Judging Sources

44.3

Authority and Accuracy

Main ideaAsk who is behind a source and whether its claims can be checked; a source that hides its author or its evidence has not earned your trust.

The first question to ask a source is who wrote it, and why should I believe that person? This is the source’s . A historian who spent ten years in the Chicago archives has authority on the fire. A tour company does not, even if its page is well written. Authority is not the same as fame. A famous actor has no special authority on medicine. Look for an author’s name, the author’s job or training, and the organization that published the piece. If you cannot find any of those, the source is asking you to trust a stranger in the dark.

The second question is whether the source is accurate: are its facts right, and can I check them? A careful source shows where its information came from, through footnotes, links, a list of works cited, or phrases like "according to the 1871 inquiry." That trail lets you follow a claim back to its origin. A source with no trail may still be right, but you have no way to know. Try this test: pick one specific fact from the source, such as a date or a number, and check it against a second source you already trust. If it fails, the whole source is suspect.

Watch for signs of carelessness. Misspelled names, dates that do not match, and claims that are bigger than the evidence all warn you. So does language that sounds sure about things nobody can be sure of. "Mrs. O’Leary’s cow definitely started the fire" should make you suspicious, because the 1871 inquiry could not name a cause. Honest sources say what they know and admit what they do not. A source that admits uncertainty is usually more reliable than one that never does.

44.4

Currency and Purpose

Main ideaCheck when a source was made and why; a date tells you whether the information is still good, and a purpose tells you which way it might lean.

means how recent a source is. It matters more for some questions than for others. If your question is about the best treatment for a disease, a source from 1995 may be dangerously out of date. If your question is about what people believed in 1871, a source from 1871 is exactly what you need. Ask: has anything changed since this was written that would change its answer? Look for a publication date, and on web pages look for a "last updated" line. A page with no date at all is hard to judge, which is one reason to prefer pages that have one.

is the reason the source exists. Every source was made by someone who wanted something. A scientist wants to report what an experiment showed. A company wants to sell you a product. A campaign wants your vote. A comedian wants a laugh. None of these purposes makes a source useless, but each one shapes what the source says and leaves out. A page from a bottled water company may have real facts about tap water, but it will choose the facts that make bottled water look good. Knowing the purpose tells you where to push.

You can often find the purpose by asking who pays for the source and who benefits if you believe it. Look at the web address: .gov is a government site, .edu is a school, .org is often but not always a nonprofit, and .com is usually a business. Read the About page. Notice whether the source gives both sides of a question or only one. is a lean toward one side, and every writer has some. The problem is hidden bias, the kind that pretends to be neutral. A source that says up front what it wants is easier to use honestly than one that hides it.

44.5

Primary and Secondary Sources

Main ideaA primary source comes from the time or the person you are studying; a secondary source studies primary sources, and a strong paper uses both.

Frederick Douglass wrote his Narrative in 1845. He was there. He had been enslaved in Maryland and escaped. When he wrote, "You have seen how a man was made a slave; you shall see how a slave was made a man," that sentence is evidence straight from the person who lived it. That is a : a record made by someone who took part in or witnessed the events, or an original object from the time. Letters, diaries, speeches, photographs, interviews, lab data, court transcripts and newspaper reports from the day are all primary sources.

A historian’s book about Douglass, written in 2010, is a . It studies the primary sources, compares them, and builds an interpretation. Secondary sources are enormously useful. They give you context you would need years to gather yourself, and they tell you what experts have concluded. But they are one step removed. Every secondary source chose which primary sources to look at and how to read them. Two historians can look at the same letters and tell different stories.

Whether a source is primary or secondary depends on your question. A 1995 book about the Chicago fire is a secondary source if your question is about the fire. It is a primary source if your question is how historians in the 1990s wrote about disasters. A strong research paper uses both kinds. Primary sources let you see for yourself and quote the people who were there. Secondary sources tell you what that evidence has been taken to mean and where the arguments are. If you only have secondary sources, you are reporting other people’s conclusions. If you only have primary sources, you may be missing what everyone else already figured out.

Section 3

Working With Sources

44.6

Quote, Paraphrase, Summarize

Main ideaTake notes in three modes, quoting exact words, paraphrasing in your own words, or summarizing the whole, and mark which mode each note is in.

Here is a sentence from a museum page: "The fire began on the evening of October 8, 1871, in or near a barn on DeKoven Street, and burned for about two days." There are three ways to take a note on it. A copies the exact words inside quotation marks. A restates the idea in your own words and sentence shape, about the same length: The fire started near a DeKoven Street barn on the night of October 8, 1871, and lasted roughly two days. A boils a longer passage down to its main point in far fewer words: fire began Oct. 8, 1871, DeKoven St., two days.

Quote when the exact words matter: when the wording is striking, when you plan to analyze it, or when changing it might change the meaning. Paraphrase for most facts and ideas, because your paper should sound like you, not like a string of other people’s sentences. Summarize when you need the gist of a whole page or chapter. A common mistake is a paraphrase that only swaps a few words: The fire started on the evening of October 8, 1871, in or close to a barn on DeKoven Street. That is not a paraphrase. It is the source’s sentence wearing a hat. A real paraphrase closes the source, writes from memory, then checks the facts.

Mark every note with its mode. Put quotation marks around exact words the moment you copy them. Write P for paraphrase or S for summary at the top of the note. Write the page number on every note, even if you think you will remember. Weeks later, when you draft, those marks are the only way to know which words are yours. Students who get accused of copying are often students who took sloppy notes and forgot what was quoted. Careful notes are the cheapest insurance there is.

44.7

The Source Log

Main ideaRecord every source's full details the moment you find it, in one place, so that nothing you use is ever lost or uncitable.

Every researcher has lived this moment: the paper is due tomorrow, there is a perfect fact in the draft, and nobody remembers where it came from. The fix is boring and it works. A is a single list, on paper or in a file, where you record every source the moment you decide it might be useful. For each one, write the author, the full title, the publisher or website, the date, the page numbers you used, and the web address if there is one. Give each source a number or a short label, such as Museum or Smith 1995.

Then use that label on every note. A note that reads Smith 1995, p. 42, P: inquiry could not name a cause tells you three things at a glance: which source, which page, and that the words are your own paraphrase. When you draft, you pull the label into the sentence as a citation. When you build your works cited list, the log already has everything you need, in one place, and you do not have to go hunting through browser history at midnight.

The log also records your judgment. Add a line for each source saying what it is good for and what its limits are: strong on dates, weak on causes; written to sell tours; the only primary source I have on the inquiry. This turns your log into a map of your evidence. You can see at a glance if all your sources lean the same way, if you have no primary sources, or if one source is doing all the work. Those are the problems best caught early, while there is still time to search again.

44.8

Synthesis, Not Stacking

Main ideaSynthesis puts sources in conversation around your own idea; stacking just lines them up one after another with no thinking between them.

Read this paragraph from a first draft. Smith says the fire’s cause was never proven. The museum says the cause is unknown. The 1871 newspaper blamed the cow. A reporter later admitted he made the cow story up. That is four sources, one after another, like cans on a shelf. It is . Every sentence is true and cited, and the paragraph still says nothing, because the writer never appears. There is no idea holding the cans together.

Now read it again after revision. The cow story spread within days of the fire, appearing in the newspaper before the official inquiry had even begun, yet the inquiry could not name any cause, and both Smith and the Chicago History Museum treat the cause as unknown. The story’s staying power came less from evidence than from how good it was as a story, which a reporter admitted in 1893 when he confessed to helping invent it. The same four sources are there. But now the writer has a point, that the cow story outran the evidence, and the sources are arranged to support it. That is : combining sources to build an idea that none of them states alone.

To synthesize, look for the relationships between your sources. Where do they agree? That agreement is strong evidence. Where do they disagree? That disagreement is often your most interesting paragraph. Does one source explain something another only mentions? Does a primary source show what a secondary source claims? Write those relationships down before you draft. Then write sentences that start with your idea and bring the sources in to support it, instead of sentences that start with a source’s name. A paper that says "Smith says" at the start of every sentence is a book report. A paper that says "The evidence points two ways" is research.

Section 4

Giving Credit

44.9

What Plagiarism Is

Main ideaPlagiarism is presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own, on purpose or by accident, and the cure is careful notes and clear credit.

is using another person’s words or ideas without giving them credit, so that a reader thinks they are yours. The word comes from a Latin word for kidnapper. Copying a paragraph from a website is plagiarism. So is copying a sentence and changing three words. So is taking a source’s idea, stating it in your own words, and not saying where it came from. So is turning in a paper someone else wrote, or that a program wrote for you. The rule is about honesty, not about words alone.

Most student plagiarism is not a plan to cheat. It is an accident born of bad notes. A student copies a sentence into a notes file without quotation marks, forgets a month later that it was copied, and pastes it into the draft as her own. The reader cannot tell the difference between that accident and theft, and the consequences at most schools are the same. That is why the earlier lessons insisted on marking every note. Some students also plagiarize out of fear: they do not trust their own sentences, so they borrow better ones. Your own clear sentence, with credit, always beats a borrowed one.

What does not need a citation? : facts that any reader could find in many general sources, such as Chicago being in Illinois or the fire happening in 1871. Your own ideas, observations and arguments need no citation either. Everything else does: exact words, specific facts and numbers, and other people’s interpretations, even in your own words. When you are unsure, cite. No teacher has ever lowered a grade for a citation that was not strictly needed. Many have failed papers for one that was missing.

44.10

MLA In-Text Citations

Main ideaIn MLA style, a short note in parentheses, usually the author's last name and a page number, points the reader to the full entry in the works cited list.

stands for the Modern Language Association, a group of teachers of English and languages that publishes a style guide most high school English classes follow. Its system for giving credit has two parts that work together. In the body of the paper, a short points to a source. At the end, the works cited list gives the full details. The in-text part is small on purpose, so it does not interrupt the reading. Its job is only to say which source and where.

The basic form is the author’s last name and the page number in parentheses, with no comma between them and no p.: The inquiry could not settle on a cause (Smith 42). The period comes after the closing parenthesis. If you name the author in your sentence, leave the name out of the parentheses: Smith notes that the inquiry could not settle on a cause (42). If a source has no page numbers, such as a web page, give only the author: (Chicago History Museum). If there is no author, use a shortened form of the title in quotation marks or italics, whichever the works cited entry uses: ("Great Fire").

Quotations follow the same rules. Put the citation after the closing quotation mark and before the period: One witness wrote that "the city was a furnace" (Jones 3). Two authors get both names: (Lee and Park 17). Three or more get the first name and et al., a Latin phrase meaning and others: (Lee et al. 17). Every in-text citation must match the first word of an entry in the works cited list. That match is the whole point: a reader sees (Smith 42), turns to the list, finds Smith, and can go check page 42.

The Works Cited List

Main ideaA works cited list gives full details for every source cited, in alphabetical order, so a reader can find each one.

The list is the last page of an MLA paper. It is titled Works Cited, centered, and it includes every source you cited in the paper and nothing you did not. Entries are in alphabetical order by the first word, usually the author’s last name. Each entry uses a : the first line starts at the left margin and every line after it is indented. The list is double-spaced like the rest of the paper. The purpose is simple. Anyone who reads your paper should be able to find every source you used.

MLA builds each entry from the same set of pieces in the same order, using only the pieces that apply. Author. Title of the source. Title of the larger container it sits in, such as a website, a journal or a collection. Other contributors. Version. Number. Publisher. Date. Location, such as page numbers or a web address. A book by one author needs only a few of these: Smith, Karen. The Fire and the City. University of Illinois Press, 1995. A web page needs others: "The Great Chicago Fire." Chicago History Museum, 2023, www.chicagohistory.org/great-fire. Titles of whole works, like books and websites, are italicized. Titles of parts, like articles and web pages, go in quotation marks.

A magazine or journal article shows the container idea clearly: Lee, Ana. "Rumor and Fire." Journal of Urban History, vol. 22, no. 3, 2019, pp. 40-58. The article is the source; the journal is its container; the volume, issue, year and pages locate it. When a piece has no author, start with the title and alphabetize by it. When you are unsure of a format, look it up rather than guess; the MLA publishes examples, and most school libraries have the handbook. What matters most is not perfect commas but completeness: every source a reader would need, with enough detail to find it.

The Annotated Bibliography

Main ideaAn annotated bibliography adds a short paragraph under each citation that summarizes the source and judges how useful it is.

An is a works cited list with a paragraph under each entry. That paragraph is the . It usually does three things in about four to six sentences. It summarizes what the source says. It evaluates the source: who wrote it, how reliable it is, what its purpose and limits are. And it explains how the source will help your project. Teachers often assign one before the paper itself, because writing it forces you to actually read your sources and think about them instead of collecting links.

Here is a model annotation for an invented source. Smith, Karen. The Fire and the City. University of Illinois Press, 1995. Smith traces the fire from the first alarm through the rebuilding, using the 1871 inquiry transcripts and dozens of letters. She argues that the cow story survived because it gave a frightened city someone to blame. Smith is a professional historian, and her notes let a reader check every claim, though the book is now nearly thirty years old. This will be my main secondary source on the fire’s causes and the inquiry.

Notice what the annotation does not do. It does not praise the source in vague words like great or interesting. It does not just repeat the title. It names the evidence the source uses, states its argument in one sentence, gives a real reason to trust it and a real limit, and says exactly what job it will do. Write your annotations from your source log; the judgment lines you wrote there are the seeds. A good annotated bibliography is your research in miniature. Read it straight through and you should be able to see the shape of the paper you are about to write.

The Dictionary Opening

A student's essay begins the way ten thousand essays begin, with a definition. Then he rewrites the first paragraph, and the whole essay wakes up.

Devon's first draft opened like this: "Webster's Dictionary defines courage as the ability to do something that frightens one. Courage is an important theme in many works of literature. In this essay I will show how the main character shows courage in three ways." His teacher read it and wrote one word in the margin: asleep. Devon was annoyed. The paragraph was correct. It had a definition, a topic, a plan. What was wrong with it?

The teacher explained that nothing in it was his. The dictionary wrote the first sentence. The second sentence could open an essay about any book ever written. The third told the reader what was coming instead of making the reader want it to come. "Your essay is about a girl who walks into a burning house to get a photograph," the teacher said. "Why is your first sentence about a dictionary?"

Devon tried again. "The photograph was the only picture of her mother that existed, and it was upstairs, and the stairs were already burning. Nadia counted to three and went in. She did not feel brave. She felt sick. That difference, between feeling brave and acting anyway, is what the novel is really about." He read it aloud and did not recognize his own writing. It sounded like someone who had something to say.

The rest of the essay changed to match. The body paragraphs, which had listed three examples of courage like items on a receipt, now argued that the novel keeps separating feeling from action. The conclusion, which had repeated the introduction in slightly different words, now asked what the reader would have done at the bottom of those stairs. The essay was the same length. It was not the same essay.

This chapter is about that difference. Every kind of essay you will write in high school, analysis, argument, explanation and story, has a way of working and a way of only filling pages. The gap between them is not talent. It is a set of choices you can learn: what to claim, how to use evidence, where to begin, how to end, and which words to cut. By the end you should be able to look at a draft that is asleep and know how to wake it up.

Analyzing a Text

45.1

A Claim About How a Text Means

Main ideaA literary analysis argues a claim about how a text creates its meaning, not just what it is about.

Emily Dickinson’s poem begins, "Because I could not stop for Death – / He kindly stopped for me –." A student writes: "This poem is about death." True, and useless, because it is not a . A claim is a statement someone could disagree with, and nobody would disagree that the poem is about death. Here is a claim: "By making Death a polite gentleman caller, Dickinson turns the most feared event in life into a quiet social visit, and the calm tone is what makes the poem unsettling." A reader could push back on that. It says how the poem works, not just what it mentions.

is an essay that makes and supports a claim about how a text means: how its choices of word, image, structure, sound, character or point of view produce its effect. The key word is how. A summary tells what happens. An analysis explains what the writer did and why it works. To move from summary to claim, look for a choice the author made and ask what would change if the choice were different. What if Death had knocked the door down instead of stopping kindly? The poem would be a horror story. The politeness is the choice, and the claim explains its effect.

A useful claim usually has two parts: what the text does and what that doing accomplishes. Dickinson personifies Death as courteous (what the text does), which makes dying seem ordinary and therefore stranger (what it accomplishes). Put the claim at the end of your introduction, one or two sentences, in plain words. Then every body paragraph should give one reason the claim is true, backed by the text. If a paragraph does not help prove the claim, it does not belong, no matter how interesting it is.

45.2

Evidence and Commentary

Main ideaEach piece of textual evidence needs to be introduced, quoted precisely, and then explained; the explanation is where the analysis lives.

Here is a body paragraph that fails. "Dickinson makes Death polite. ’He kindly stopped for me.’ This shows Death is polite." The quotation is there, but nothing happens to it. The sentence after the quote repeats the sentence before it. Analysis needs three moves for every quotation, and most weak paragraphs skip the third. First, introduce the evidence so the reader knows where it comes from and what to look at. Second, quote only the words that matter. Third, write : explain what the quoted words do and how they support the claim.

Watch the fix. "Dickinson describes the speaker’s meeting with Death in the language of manners: ’He kindly stopped for me.’ The word kindly does two things. It makes Death sound like a neighbor offering a ride, and it hints that the speaker was too busy to stop for him, as if death were an errand she kept putting off. Both effects make dying seem ordinary, and that ordinariness is what unsettles the reader." The quotation is the same six words. The commentary is three sentences, and it does the work: it points at a specific word, explains two effects, and ties them back to the claim.

A good rule is that commentary should be at least as long as the quotation, and usually longer. Quote small: a phrase, a line, rarely more than two. Long quotations let the source do your talking. Commentary should name specific features: a word choice, an image, a sound, a sentence’s shape, a break in the pattern. Weak commentary says "this shows" and repeats the claim. Strong commentary says what the word does, what it might have said instead, and what changes because it did not. Keep asking yourself: could I say this about any quotation? If so, it is not yet commentary.

45.3

Organize by Idea, Not by Plot

Main ideaArrange body paragraphs by the reasons that support your claim, not by the order of events in the text.

The most common structure in weak literary essays is the retelling: first this happened, then this, then this, with a quotation in each paragraph. It feels organized because the text already did the organizing. But it forces the reader to walk through the plot again, and it buries the claim. The alternative is to organize by idea. Each body paragraph takes one reason your claim is true, and the paragraph pulls evidence from wherever in the text that reason shows up, beginning, middle or end.

Suppose the claim is that Kate Chopin’s "The Story of an Hour" treats freedom as something felt in the body before it is understood in the mind. A plot-order essay would go: paragraph one, she hears the news; paragraph two, she sits in her room; paragraph three, the ending. An idea-order essay would go: paragraph one, the story gives the body’s reactions before the mind’s words; paragraph two, the story describes the world outside the window as if it were entering her; paragraph three, the ending shows that the body’s truth cannot be taken back. Each paragraph has a job, and each job is a piece of the argument.

To find your idea order, write your claim at the top of a page and list every piece of evidence you have. Then group the evidence by what it proves, not by where it appears. Each group becomes a paragraph. Order the paragraphs so that the argument builds: often the simplest reason first and the most surprising or important last. Begin each paragraph with a that states the reason in your own words, with no quotation in it. A reader should be able to read only your topic sentences and follow the whole argument.

Argument, Explanation, Story

45.4

Argument With a Counterclaim

Main ideaAn argument essay states a claim, supports it with evidence and reasoning, and takes the strongest opposing view seriously before answering it.

An essay tries to convince a reader that a claim is true or that an action should be taken. It differs from literary analysis in its subject, which is usually a question about the world, and in one added part: the . A counterclaim is the strongest thing a smart person who disagrees with you would say. Weak arguments ignore it or pick a feeble version of it. Strong arguments state it fairly, in words the other side would accept, and then answer it. This is called a .

Suppose the claim is that our school should start later in the morning. The evidence: sleep researchers report that teenagers’ bodies shift toward later sleep, and schools that moved start times have reported fewer tardies and better attendance. The reasoning connects evidence to claim: if the body cannot sleep earlier, the only way to get more sleep is to wake later. Now the counterclaim, stated fairly: a later start pushes practices and jobs into the evening, and buses that serve several schools cannot all run late. The rebuttal: some districts solved the bus problem by flipping elementary and high school schedules, and an hour more of morning sleep is worth an hour less of evening free time for most students.

Notice what the rebuttal does not do. It does not call the other side stupid, and it does not pretend the bus problem is imaginary. It concedes what is true and shows why the claim still holds. A reader who disagrees with you will read your counterclaim paragraph looking for a reason to stop trusting you. Give the objection its full weight and you keep that reader. Structure often looks like this: introduction with claim, two or three paragraphs of reasons with evidence, one paragraph of counterclaim and rebuttal, and a conclusion. Some writers put the counterclaim early, to clear it away; either place works if the answer is honest.

45.5

Explaining With Structure and Precision

Main ideaAn informative essay explains a subject clearly by choosing a structure that fits the material and using precise, defined terms.

An or explanatory essay does not argue. It explains: how something works, what happened, what a thing is, how two things compare. Its test is whether a reader who knew nothing understands the subject at the end. That depends on two choices. The first is structure. Different subjects want different shapes. A process wants steps in order. A history wants time order. A comparison wants either a block for each thing or a point-by-point walk through both. A problem wants its causes, then its effects, then possible solutions. Pick the shape that matches the material, and tell the reader the shape early.

The second choice is . Vague words make readers guess. "The river was very dirty and caused a lot of problems" says almost nothing. "By the 1880s the Chicago River carried the city’s sewage into Lake Michigan, the source of its drinking water, and outbreaks of typhoid followed" tells the reader what, where, when and why it mattered. Precision means specific nouns, exact numbers where you have them, and technical terms used correctly and defined the first time. It does not mean big words. Sewage is precise. Effluent is just fancier.

Informative writing also uses signposts: headings if the piece is long, transitions like first, as a result and by contrast, and a sentence at the end of each section that says what the reader now knows. Add a concrete example for every general statement. If you explain that engineers reversed the river’s flow, show what that meant: water that once flowed east into the lake now flowed west toward the Mississippi. General statement, then example, is the basic beat of explanation. A paragraph with no example is a paragraph a reader will not remember.

Narrative Technique

Main ideaA narrative uses specific techniques, such as pacing, dialogue, sensory detail and reflection, to make a reader live an experience rather than hear about it.

A tells a story, real or invented, and its purpose is to make a reader experience something. The mistake most student narratives make is telling the reader what happened instead of showing it happening. "I was nervous before the game" is a report. "I retied my left shoe three times and could not remember whether I had already tied the right" puts the reader inside the nervousness. That is : what a character sees, hears, touches and does, chosen so the reader feels what the character felt without being told.

is how fast the story moves, and good narratives change speed on purpose. Long stretches of time can pass in one sentence: "The season went by." A single second can take a paragraph: the moment the ball leaves the hand. Slow down for the moments that matter and speed past the ones that do not. Dialogue is another tool, and it works best when people do not say exactly what they mean. A coach who says "You’re fine" while looking at his clipboard tells the reader more than a coach who says "I am worried about you." Keep dialogue short and let action carry the feeling.

The technique that separates a story from an anecdote is : the moment where the narrator, then or now, understands something. It can be one sentence, and it should not be a moral. Structure a narrative like an essay: a hook that drops the reader into a scene, rising tension, a turning point, and an ending that has changed something. Real events do not come with structure. Beginnings and Endings

45.7

Introductions That Earn Attention

Main ideaAn introduction should start with something specific, move toward the subject, and end with the claim; it should never start with a dictionary or a statement about all of history.

Readers decide in the first two sentences whether to pay attention. Three openings waste that chance so often that teachers can recite them: the dictionary definition ("Webster’s defines courage as"), the dawn of time ("Throughout history, people have always"), and the announcement ("In this essay I will discuss"). Each one could open any essay, and that is exactly the problem. An introduction that fits every subject fits none. The fix is to begin with something that belongs only to your subject.

Several kinds of openings do that. A scene: the burning stairs, the ball in the air. A specific fact that surprises: the 1871 inquiry never named a cause. A short quotation from the text, followed by what is strange about it. A question the reader cannot answer yet, as long as it is a real question and not "Have you ever wondered about courage?" A small story from the news or from life that the essay will explain. Whatever you choose, it should be concrete and it should point toward your subject, so the reader feels pulled rather than tricked.

From the opening, the introduction moves in a funnel: specific hook, then the subject and context a reader needs, then the claim at the end. Keep the whole thing short, usually four to six sentences in a school essay. Do not summarize the plot; assume your reader knows the text. Do not list your three body paragraphs; the topic sentences will do that. Many writers draft the introduction last, after they know what the essay argues. That is not cheating. It is the reason so many good introductions sound as if the writer knew exactly where they were going. They did, because they had already gone there.

45.8

Conclusions That Do More

Main ideaA conclusion should show what the argument adds up to, answering why it matters, rather than repeating the introduction in different words.

The weakest conclusion is the echo: "In conclusion, I have shown that Death is polite in the poem, that the tone is calm, and that this is unsettling." The reader just read that. Repeating it says you do not trust them to remember. A conclusion has a different job. The body paragraphs proved the claim piece by piece. The conclusion steps back and says what the proved claim means: why it matters, what it changes, what it lets us see that we could not see before.

Several moves work. Answer the question so what: now that we see Death as a courteous caller, what does the poem say about how we should face dying? Widen the frame carefully: connect the claim to a larger pattern in the author’s work or the world, without floating off into vagueness. Return to the hook: if you opened with the burning stairs, end there, with the reader now knowing something about that scene they did not know at the start. End on a specific image or sentence from the text, with a final line of commentary that lands. Or raise a real question the essay has made possible to ask.

A few rules. Do not begin with "In conclusion"; if the reader cannot tell it is the conclusion, the problem is elsewhere. Do not introduce new evidence; the conclusion interprets, it does not prove. Do not apologize or hedge ("This is just my opinion"). Do not swell into a claim about all of humanity. Keep it about as long as the introduction. The last sentence is the one the reader carries away, so write it three times and pick the one that sounds like an ending. Often the best final sentence is the shortest one.

3. Why does the lesson say not to introduce new evidence in the conclusion?

Why: Proof belongs in the body; new evidence at the end is an argument left unfinished.

Style and Finish

45.9

Sentences With Style

Main ideaStyle at the sentence level comes from choices you can control: strong verbs, concrete nouns, varied length, and putting the important word where the reader will feel it.

Read two versions of the same idea. "There are many factors that can be seen as contributing to the fact that the fire was able to spread quickly." "The fire spread fast because the city was built of wood and the summer had been dry." The second is shorter, and it also has a that does something: the fire spread. The first hides the action under there are, can be seen, and the fact that. Style begins with finding the real actor and the real action in a sentence and letting them stand at the front. Verbs like is, are, was and there are should make you look for a stronger verb hiding nearby.

The second tool is . Abstract nouns like factors, aspects, situation and things ask the reader to imagine nothing. Wood and a dry summer ask the reader to imagine something. When you catch an abstract noun, ask what it actually refers to and name that instead. The third tool is variety. A paragraph of sentences all the same length feels like a metronome. Follow a long sentence with a short one and the short one lands hard. Devon’s rewrite did this: after a long sentence about the burning stairs, "She did not feel brave."

The fourth tool is emphasis. Readers remember the end of a sentence more than the middle, so put the word you want remembered last. "The stairs were already burning, and the photograph was upstairs" ends on upstairs. "The photograph was upstairs, and the stairs were already burning" ends on burning. Same facts, different pressure. None of these tools requires a large vocabulary. They require rereading your own sentences and asking, for each one, who is doing what, can I see it, and where does the weight fall.

2. Why does a short sentence after a long one work?

Why: Variety in length creates emphasis; a metronome of same-length sentences creates none.

3. Where does the lesson say readers feel a sentence's weight most?

Why: The last word of a sentence is the one readers carry, so the important word belongs there.
45.10

Revising for Clarity and Concision

Main ideaRevision means cutting words that do no work and rebuilding sentences a reader could misread; it is a separate pass from proofreading.

is not fixing commas. It is re-seeing the draft as a reader would and changing what does not work. Do it in passes. The first pass is for the whole: does the claim hold, is every paragraph doing a job, is the order right? The second pass is for : could any sentence be misread? "After reading the novel, the theme became clear" says the theme read the novel. "The coach told the player that he was late" leaves the reader guessing who. Fix each by naming the actor and putting related words next to each other.

The third pass is for , saying the same thing in fewer words. Cut phrases that only take up space: due to the fact that (because), in order to (to), at this point in time (now), it is important to note that (nothing). Cut doubles: each and every, first and foremost. Cut the word very and the word really; if the noun needs help, find a stronger noun. Cut sentences that announce what the next sentence will do. A useful goal is to cut a first draft by ten to twenty percent without losing a single idea. Most drafts can lose that much and get stronger.

Read the draft aloud, or have a computer read it to you. Your ear catches what your eye slides over: the sentence you have to reread, the word used four times in one paragraph, the paragraph that runs out of breath. Then, and only then, proofread for spelling, punctuation and citations. Proofreading first wastes effort on sentences you will end up cutting. Keep the cut material in a separate file; knowing it is not gone makes you braver about removing it. The willingness to cut your own sentences is the single clearest sign of a writer who has grown up.

45.11

Writing on Demand

Main ideaA timed essay rewards a plan: read the prompt closely, budget minutes, outline in three lines, and leave time to reread.

A timed essay on a test is a different sport from a paper written over two weeks, and it rewards different habits. The first habit is reading the twice and underlining the task words. Analyze, argue, explain and compare ask for different essays. A prompt that says "analyze how the author builds tension" is not asking what happens or whether you liked it. Circle every part of the task; prompts often have two, and answering one well earns half credit at best. Note whether a source or passage is provided and whether you must use it.

The second habit is a . For a forty-minute essay: five minutes to read and plan, thirty to write, five to reread. Most students skip the five minutes at the start and regret it at minute twenty-five. Planning means writing your claim in one sentence and three reasons under it, one line each, with a piece of evidence beside each reason. That is the whole outline. It takes three minutes, and it means you never sit staring at a half-written paragraph wondering what comes next.

Then write, in order, without going back. A plain introduction that ends in the claim beats a clever one you spend eight minutes on. Body paragraphs open with a topic sentence and hold one piece of evidence with commentary. A short conclusion answers so what. If time runs short, finish the argument in two sentences rather than leaving a paragraph half done; a complete short essay scores higher than an incomplete long one. Spend the last five minutes rereading for sentences that do not say what you meant. On demand, clarity beats style, structure beats length, and a plan beats talent.

45.12

Building a Portfolio

Main ideaA writing portfolio collects your best work across kinds of writing, with reflections that show what you learned, so growth becomes visible.

A is a collection of a writer’s work chosen on purpose. It is not a folder of everything. It is a selection, usually four to six pieces across different kinds of writing, each with a short explaining why it is there and what it shows. Artists and architects keep portfolios because a single piece cannot show range. Writers keep them for the same reason, and for one more: a portfolio makes growth visible. A draft from September next to a draft from May tells a story no grade can.

Choose pieces that show different skills: a literary analysis with a real claim, an argument with a fair counterclaim, an explanation with structure, a narrative with a scene, maybe a timed essay to show what you can do under pressure. Include at least one piece with its early draft attached, so a reader can see the revision. Order the pieces on purpose: strongest first, or earliest to latest to show the arc. Give the portfolio a short introduction, one paragraph, that says who you are as a writer right now.

The reflections are where the learning shows. A weak reflection says "I worked hard on this and I think it is good." A strong one is specific: "My first draft opened with a definition of courage. My teacher wrote asleep in the margin. The revision opens with the burning stairs, and the whole essay had to change to match. I learned that the introduction is a promise and the body has to keep it." That paragraph proves growth better than any grade could. Keep the portfolio; add to it each year. Colleges and employers ask to see writing, and the writer who can show a range and explain their choices is the one they remember.
