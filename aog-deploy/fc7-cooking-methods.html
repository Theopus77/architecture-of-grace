Cooking Methods and Heat

Heat moves in only three ways, and every cooking method is built on one of them: dry heat that browns, moist heat that softens, and the two used one after the other. The four tabs hold 24 study cards, hints with a method chart, a three-tier quiz, and a workshop you can carry to the stove.

Water sets a ceiling

Three pieces, space between them. The steam escapes and the pan surface stays dry.

Browning has a name: the Maillard reaction. It needs a dry surface that is hotter than boiling water. As long as the surface is wet it stays at about 212 °F (100 °C), so a crowded or wet pan steams instead of browning.

Four things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

Turning the burner higher makes the boiling water hotter.

Boiling water stops at 212 °F (100 °C) at sea level. More heat does not raise that number — it only turns water into steam faster. For the food in the pot, a hard boil and a gentle simmer are nearly the same temperature.

The bubbles tell you it is boiling, not how hot it is.

⚠️ People often think…

Deep-frying is moist heat, because the food is covered in liquid.

Moist means water, not wet. Oil holds no water, so it climbs far past 212 °F (100 °C) and browns whatever it touches. Deep-frying is a dry-heat method, and the crust proves it.

If it browns, there was no water in the way.

⚠️ People often think…

Browning the outside seals the juices inside the meat.

A browned crust is not a seal. A seared piece of meat keeps losing water the whole time it cooks. Browning is worth doing for color and flavor, and that is the whole reason. Resting the meat is what keeps the juice in it.

Browning buys flavor. Resting buys juice.

⚠️ People often think…

Chicken with no pink left in it is cooked.

Color is not a temperature. Chicken can lose its pink well before it is safe, and it can stay a little pink after it is safe. The USDA number for all poultry is 165 °F (74 °C), read in the thickest part, away from bone.

Read the number, not the meat.

Helpful Hints

🧭 The unit in one line

Heat moves three ways — conduction (by touch), convection (carried by moving liquid or air) and radiation (as rays across a gap). Dry heat is hotter than water can get, so it browns. Moist heat stops at 212 °F (100 °C), so it softens. Braising and stewing do one, then the other. A thermometer, not a clock, tells you when it is done.

Touch, carry, rays. Dry browns, wet softens.

🔑 The one idea

Water cannot get hotter than its boiling point. Everything follows from that. A wet surface stays at 212 °F (100 °C) and stays pale; a dry surface climbs far past it and turns brown. So if you want color and flavor, get the water off first. If you want a tough cut to fall apart, keep the water on and wait.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • A boil is not hotter than a simmer for the food inside it. Both are water. Turning the burner to high only boils the water away faster.
  • Deep-frying is DRY heat. Oil holds no water, so it can pass 212 °F (100 °C) and brown the food.
  • A crowded pan does not sear faster. It steams. Cook in two batches instead.
  • Browning does not ‘seal in the juices’. That is an old story. Browning makes flavor and color, nothing more.
  • Never put water — or wet food — into hot oil, and never move a burning pan. Turn off the heat and slide a lid over it.
  • A tough cut cooked fast and hot stays tough. Time and moisture, not more heat, are what soften it.
  • Color is not doneness. Chicken can look done and be under 165 °F (74 °C). Use a thermometer in the thickest part, away from bone.

🌡️ The numbers to know

The first three are about the water. The rest are USDA safe minimum internal temperatures — the point at which the food is safe to eat.

  • Boil: 212 °F (100 °C) at sea level. Simmer: about 185–205 °F (85–96 °C). Poach: 160–180 °F (71–82 °C).
  • All poultry — whole, in pieces, or ground: 165 °F (74 °C).
  • Ground beef, pork, veal and lamb: 160 °F (71 °C).
  • Steaks, chops and roasts of beef, pork, veal and lamb: 145 °F (63 °C), then rest 3 minutes.
  • Fish and shellfish: 145 °F (63 °C). Egg dishes: 160 °F (71 °C). Leftovers and casseroles: 165 °F (74 °C).
  • The danger zone is 40–140 °F (4–60 °C). Food should not sit there.
