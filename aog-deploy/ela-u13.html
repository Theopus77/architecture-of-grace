Grammar and Style

Language

Jada's morning, told in ten flat sentences, sounds like a robot counting. Told in two, it sounds like a girl running for the bell. A text that says Let's eat Grandma makes a family laugh; a comma turns it back into dinner. In both cases the words were fine. What was missing was the shape around them: the joins, the stops, the small marks that tell a reader how the words go together.

This unit is about that shape. The first chapter takes a sentence apart and puts it back together: phrases and clauses, the four kinds of sentences, how to combine short ones and repair broken ones, and the choices inside a sentence, such as who does the action, what mood the verb takes, which pronoun fits, and where a description attaches. The second chapter turns to the marks and the words: commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, apostrophes, quotation marks, capitals, the words that sound alike, the spelling patterns, and the guides that settle the arguments.

By the end you will be able to name any sentence by counting its clauses, fix a fragment or a run-on four different ways, tell its from it's and affect from effect in one second, and explain why one comma can change who is insulting whom. None of it is about sounding fancy. All of it is about being understood the first time.

The big question

How do the joins inside a sentence and the marks around it control what a reader understands?

The story

Ten Sentences, Then Two

The same rainy morning, told twice: once like a robot counting, once like a person hurrying.

Read this in your head, the way a tired reader would: The bus was late. It was raining. Jada had no umbrella. She stood under the awning. The awning leaked. A drop hit her neck. She checked her phone. It was 7:52. First bell rang at 8:00. She started to run. Every fact is there. Every sentence is correct. And it sounds like a robot counting.

Now read the same facts joined: Because the bus was late and the rain would not stop, Jada stood under a leaking awning with no umbrella, feeling a drop slide down her neck. When her phone said 7:52, eight minutes before first bell, she started to run. Same girl, same rain, same clock. Two sentences instead of ten. And now you can feel her hurry.

Nothing was added. The writer of the second version only decided which ideas were big and which were small. The lateness and the rain became the reason. The awning and the umbrella became the setting. The clock became the trigger, and the run became the main event. Grammar is the set of tools that lets a writer make those decisions on the page.

That is what this chapter is for. You will learn the names of the parts: phrases, clauses, the four kinds of sentences. You will learn to spot a sentence that is broken and one that never stops. Then you will learn the moves that make writing sound like a person thinking: choosing who does the action, choosing the mood of a verb, keeping pronouns clear, and keeping a description attached to the thing it describes.

None of this is about sounding fancy. Short sentences are not wrong; a good writer uses them on purpose, for a punch. The problem with the first paragraph is not that its sentences are short. It is that they are all the same. Variety is what tells a reader which idea to hold onto.

Talk about itWhich version of Jada's morning would you rather read, and what does the second version tell you that the first one does not?
Section 1

The Building Blocks

30.1

Phrases and Clauses

Main ideaA clause has a subject and a verb; a phrase is a group of words that does not.

Look at these two groups of words: under the leaking awning, and Jada shivered. The first one paints a place, but nobody does anything in it. The second one has a person and an action. That difference is the first thing to see in any sentence. A group of words with a subject and a verb is a . A group of words without that pair is a .

Phrases do jobs inside sentences. A prepositional phrase starts with a word like under, after, with or beside and tells where, when or how: after the last bell, with her brother. A verb phrase is the verb plus its helpers: had been waiting. A noun phrase is a noun plus the words that describe it: the old yellow bus. Phrases add detail, but a phrase alone can never be a sentence.

To test a group of words, ask two questions. Who or what is this about? That is the . What does the subject do, or what is it? That is the . If you can answer both, you have a clause. In the awning she had trusted, the words she had trusted have a subject and a verb, so that part is a clause. Under the leaking awning has no doer and no action, so it stays a phrase.

Why does this matter? Because every sentence you will ever write needs at least one clause. A writer who can find the subject and verb can fix almost any broken sentence, join two ideas without a mess, and tell when a description has wandered away from the thing it describes. Everything else in this chapter builds on this one test.

30.2

Independent and Dependent Clauses

Main ideaAn independent clause can stand alone as a sentence; a dependent clause has a subject and verb but needs a main clause to lean on.

Read this clause out loud: because the bus was late. It has a subject, bus, and a verb, was. It is a real clause. But say it to a friend and stop there, and the friend will wait for more. Because promises a result that never comes. Now say: the bus was late. That one is finished. Both are clauses, but only one can stand alone.

A clause that makes a complete thought by itself is an . A clause that starts with a word like because, when, although, if, since, while, until or after is a . Each of those starting words is a . It hooks the clause to a bigger idea. Subordinate means lower in rank, and that is exactly the job: the dependent clause serves the main one.

A quick trick: cover the first word. Although the store was closed becomes the store was closed, a complete sentence. That tells you although is a subordinating conjunction and the whole thing is dependent. Relative pronouns do the same trick: who, which and that. The man who fixed our sink is not a sentence until you add what the man did. The man who fixed our sink forgot his wrench.

Dependent clauses are not mistakes. They are how writers show that one idea depends on another. When the storm passed tells the reader that the next part happened after the storm. The mistake is leaving the dependent clause by itself with a period, as if it were finished. You will practice fixing that in the section on fragments.

1. Which is an independent clause?

Why: The lights went out is a complete thought. When, because and until make the others lean on something else.

2. What does the word because do to a clause?

30.3

Simple and Compound Sentences

Main ideaA simple sentence is one independent clause; a compound sentence joins two independent clauses with a comma and a coordinating conjunction, or with a semicolon.

The pot boiled over. That is a : one independent clause, one complete thought. A simple sentence can still be long. The tall pot of noodles on the back burner boiled over onto the clean stove. It has phrases stacked on it, but there is still one subject, pot, and one verb, boiled. Length does not change the kind of sentence. The number of clauses does.

Now join two simple sentences: The pot boiled over, and Dad grabbed a towel. Two independent clauses, each able to stand alone, tied with a comma and the word and. That is a . Each joining word is a : for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. Many students remember them by their first letters, FANBOYS. Coordinate means equal in rank, and that is the point: both halves matter equally.

There is a second way to join. A can replace the comma and conjunction when the two ideas are close cousins: The pot boiled over; Dad grabbed a towel. The reader supplies the and. Use the semicolon when you want the two clauses to sit side by side with no comment about how they connect.

Watch for a common trap. Dad grabbed a towel and wiped the stove is not compound. There is one subject, Dad, doing two things. It is a simple sentence with a compound verb, and it needs no comma. The test is always the same: can each side of the and stand alone as a sentence? Wiped the stove cannot, so this stays simple.

1. Which is a compound sentence?

Why: Two independent clauses joined by a comma and and make a compound sentence. The first choice has one subject with two verbs.

2. Which word is a coordinating conjunction?

3. Why is Lena ran the race and won a medal a simple sentence?

Section 2

Four Kinds of Sentences

30.4

Complex Sentences

Main ideaA complex sentence joins an independent clause with at least one dependent clause; put a comma after the dependent clause when it comes first.

When the gate opened, the crowd rushed in. Two clauses, but they are not equals. The crowd rushed in can stand alone. When the gate opened cannot. One independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses makes a . Complex here does not mean hard. It means the sentence has a main part and a leaning part.

Order matters for the comma. Dependent clause first: When the gate opened, the crowd rushed in. The comma marks where the leaning part ends and the main part begins. Main clause first: The crowd rushed in when the gate opened. No comma is needed, because the reader hits the main idea first and the when clause simply adds the time.

The power of a complex sentence is that it tells the reader how two ideas relate. Because shows cause. Although shows contrast. If shows a . While shows two things at once. Compare: The bus was late. Jada ran. That leaves the reader to guess the link. Because the bus was late, Jada ran. Now the link is on the page. Choosing the conjunction is choosing the meaning.

A also makes a sentence complex. The player who scored last is my cousin. The clause who scored last hangs on the noun player and tells which player. It cannot stand alone, so the sentence is complex. You will see in the next chapter that some of these clauses take commas and some do not.

Check yourself

1. Which is a complex sentence?

Why: After the rain stopped is a dependent clause attached to the independent clause we went out.

2. When does a complex sentence need a comma between its clauses?

Why: The comma marks the end of an opening dependent clause. When the main clause comes first, no comma is needed.

3. Which conjunction shows contrast in She smiled ___ she was tired?

Why: Although sets two ideas against each other: she smiled even though she was tired.
30.5

Compound-Complex Sentences

Main ideaA compound-complex sentence has at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause; count the clauses to name any sentence.

Although the field was muddy, the game went on, and the fans stayed until the final whistle. Count the pieces. Although the field was muddy is dependent. The game went on is independent. The fans stayed is independent. Until the final whistle is a phrase telling when. Two independent clauses plus one dependent clause makes a , the biggest of the four kinds.

Naming sentences is really just counting. One independent clause and nothing else: simple. Two or more independent clauses and no dependent ones: compound. One independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses: complex. Two or more independent plus one or more dependent: compound-complex. The names do not measure length or difficulty. A compound-complex sentence can be eleven words long.

Long sentences carry a risk. The reader has to hold every clause in mind until the period. If the ideas belong together, that is fine. If they do not, the sentence becomes a suitcase with too much in it. A good test is to read it aloud in one breath. If you cannot, cut it into two sentences, or drop a clause that is not earning its place.

Writers use compound-complex sentences for moments with several moving parts: a sports report, a science explanation with a cause and a result, the of a story where two people act at once. Use them on purpose, and follow them with something short. The short sentence after a long one lands hard.

30.6

Combining for Variety

Main ideaCombining short sentences with conjunctions, dependent clauses and phrases makes writing flow; varying length tells the reader which idea is big.

Here is a paragraph from a student’s draft. The car was old. It was blue. It had a dent. Grandpa loved it. Every sentence is a simple sentence of three or four words. Read it aloud and you can hear the flat beat, like a drum with one note. The fix is not to make every sentence long. The fix is to decide which ideas belong together and join them.

There are three main ways to sentences. Use a coordinating conjunction: The car was old and blue, and it had a dent. Use a dependent clause: Although the car was old, blue and dented, Grandpa loved it. Fold a sentence into a phrase: Grandpa loved the old blue car with the dent in its door. The last version is one clause and twelve words, and it says everything the four sentences said.

Combining also lets you cut repeated words. The car, it, it, it: four subjects for one car. In the combined version the car appears once. This is why combining usually makes writing shorter, not longer. It also makes writing clearer, because the reader no longer has to connect the pieces alone.

means mixing lengths and shapes on purpose. A long sentence gathers details. A short one delivers a point. Try this pattern on your next draft: after a sentence of twenty words or more, write one of five or fewer. Then read both aloud. You will hear the short one land.

Section 3

Broken Sentences

30.7

Fragments

Main ideaA fragment is a piece of a sentence punctuated as if it were whole; fix it by attaching it to a neighbor or adding what is missing.

Read this as a teacher would: We finally reached the lake. After walking for three hours in the heat. The second group of words starts with a capital letter and ends with a period. It looks like a sentence. But it has no subject doing the walking, and after leaves the thought hanging. It is a : a piece of a sentence dressed up as a whole one.

Fragments come in a few common shapes. A dependent clause alone: Because the store had closed. A phrase alone: Running down the hall. A subject with no verb: The kid in the red hoodie. A verb with no subject: Slammed the locker and left. Each one is missing something the reader needs before the period.

There are two fixes. the fragment to the sentence beside it: We finally reached the lake after walking for three hours in the heat. Or add the missing part: We had walked for three hours in the heat. Choose the one that reads better. The first fix is often best, because the fragment was usually meant to belong to its neighbor anyway.

Writers sometimes use fragments on purpose, for punch. Not today. Never again. In a story or a speech that can work, because the reader can tell the choice was made. In an essay, a lab report or a job application, a fragment reads as an error. The rule of thumb is simple: use a fragment only when a reader would see that you meant it.

30.8

Run-ons and Comma Splices

Main ideaA run-on jams two independent clauses together with nothing or only a comma; fix it with a period, a semicolon, a conjunction, or by making one clause dependent.

Try to read this in one breath: The fire alarm went off everyone grabbed their coats the teacher counted heads twice we stood in the cold for twenty minutes. You ran out of air, and you probably lost track of where one event ended. This is a : two or more independent clauses with no proper stop between them. Length is not the crime. The missing stops are.

The sneakiest run-on uses a comma as the stop: The alarm went off, everyone grabbed their coats. A comma alone cannot hold two independent clauses apart. That error is a . To splice is to join two ends together, and a comma is too weak a join for two whole sentences. Readers feel it as a stumble even when they cannot name it.

There are four fixes, and each changes the feel a little. A period: The alarm went off. Everyone grabbed their coats. A semicolon: The alarm went off; everyone grabbed their coats. A comma with a coordinating conjunction: The alarm went off, and everyone grabbed their coats. A dependent clause: When the alarm went off, everyone grabbed their coats. The last one says the most, because it tells the order of events.

To find run-ons in your own draft, read it aloud and mark every place you naturally stop to breathe. At each mark, check what sits on either side. If both sides could be sentences and there is only a comma or nothing between them, you have found one. Then pick the fix that says what you mean.

Verbs, Pronouns and Modifiers

30.9

Active and Passive Voice

Main ideaIn active voice the subject does the action; in passive voice the subject receives it. Prefer active unless the doer is unknown or unimportant.

Compare: The goalie blocked the shot. The shot was blocked by the goalie. Same event, same people. In the first, the subject, goalie, does the action. That is . In the second, the subject, shot, has the action done to it. That is . Passive uses a form of be, such as was or is, plus a like blocked, and the doer moves to the end or disappears.

Active voice is usually shorter, clearer and stronger. Six words became eight, and the goalie, who did the work, got pushed to the end. Readers process the doer-action-receiver order fastest. When you find a lot of passive verbs in a draft, ask who is doing each action and put that person first.

But passive is not an error, and sometimes it is the honest choice. Use it when the doer is unknown: My bike was stolen last night. When the doer does not matter: The bridge was built in 1930. When the receiver is the real topic: The Declaration of Independence was signed in Philadelphia. Science reports often use passive so that the experiment, not the scientist, stays in front.

Watch for passive that hides responsibility. Mistakes were made. The window was broken. Who made them? Who broke it? Politicians and kids both reach for this trick. When you catch it in your own writing, decide: are you hiding the doer for a good reason, or because naming the doer is uncomfortable?

30.10

Verb Moods

Main ideaA verb's mood signals whether the sentence states, commands, asks, imagines a condition, or expresses a wish or demand.

The same verb can do five different jobs depending on its . You close the door. Close the door. Do you close the door? If you closed the door, the room would warm up. I wish you would close the door. State, command, ask, imagine, wish. Mood is the grammar word for the attitude a verb takes toward what it says.

The mood states facts or opinions: The door is open. It is the mood of almost every sentence you read. The gives a command or request, and its subject, you, is usually left out: Close the door, please. The interrogative mood asks a question, often by moving a helping verb in front of the subject: Did you close the door?

The mood talks about what would happen under some condition, usually with would, could or might: If it rained, the game would move inside. The expresses a wish, a demand, or something contrary to fact. Its clearest sign is were where you would expect was: If I were taller, I would play center. I wish it were Friday. It also appears after verbs of demand: The coach insisted that he be on time.

The subjunctive is fading in everyday speech, and many people say if I was. In formal writing, if I were is still the standard form when the idea is imaginary. Knowing the moods helps in a practical way: a persuasive essay that mixes commands, conditions and wishes without meaning to sounds unsure. Pick the mood that matches what you want the reader to do.

30.11

Pronoun Case and Agreement

Main ideaChoose I or me, he or him, by the pronoun's job in the sentence, and make every pronoun match the noun it stands for.

Me and Tasha went to the game. Most people say it, and most teachers mark it. Here is the test that settles it. Take Tasha out: Me went to the game. Nobody says that. I went to the game. So the sentence is Tasha and I went to the game. The pronoun’s form depends on its job, and grammar calls that job its .

Subject case pronouns do the action: I, you, he, she, it, we, they. Object case pronouns receive it or follow a preposition: me, you, him, her, it, us, them. The coach picked her and me. Between you and me, not between you and I, because between is a preposition and takes the object case. The take-one-out test works every time a pronoun has a partner.

is the second rule. A pronoun must match its , the noun it stands in for, in number. Each player brought his or her own water. Each is singular, so the pronoun is singular. The players brought their own water. Plural noun, plural pronoun. The mismatch to watch for is everyone, somebody, each and nobody: they feel plural but are grammatically singular.

Usage is shifting here, and it is worth saying so honestly. Many writers now use they for one person whose gender is unknown or not stated: Someone left their umbrella. Major style guides accept this. In a formal test answer, the safest path is to make the antecedent plural if you can: Students left their umbrellas. Whatever you choose, keep it consistent, and make sure every pronoun points clearly to one noun.

Dangling Modifiers

Main ideaA modifier must sit next to the word it describes; a dangling modifier describes something not in the sentence, and a misplaced one attaches to the wrong word.

Running to catch the bus, my backpack fell open. Read it slowly. Who was running? The sentence says the backpack was. The opening phrase, running to catch the bus, is a : words that describe something else. It wants to describe a person, but the only noun nearby is backpack. It has nothing to hold on to, so grammar calls it a .

The fix is to put the doer right after the comma: Running to catch the bus, I felt my backpack fall open. Or give the phrase its own subject: As I ran to catch the bus, my backpack fell open. Either way, the reader now knows who ran. The rule is that an opening phrase describes the subject that comes right after it.

A cousin of the dangling modifier is the , which sits next to the wrong word. She served cake to the kids on paper plates. The kids are not on paper plates; the cake is. Move the modifier: She served the kids cake on paper plates. Single words can be misplaced too. I almost ate the whole pizza means you ate none. I ate almost the whole pizza means you ate most of it.

These errors are easy to make because you know what you meant, so your brain fills the gap. Readers do not have that advantage. When you edit, look at every sentence that opens with an -ing phrase or a phrase like after finishing or to win. Ask: who or what does this describe? Then check that the answer is the very next noun.

The Comma That Saves Grandma

One family group chat, one missing comma, and a grandmother hiding behind the couch.

A text goes out to a family group chat: Let's eat Grandma. Grandma reads it and, being a good sport, replies with a picture of herself hiding behind the couch. Everyone laughs. But look at what happened. One missing comma turned an invitation to dinner into a threat to eat a relative. Let's eat, Grandma, is a call to the table. Let's eat Grandma is a horror movie.

The joke is old, and it works because it is true. Punctuation is not decoration on top of words. It is part of the meaning. The comma in Let's eat, Grandma does the job your voice does when you speak: the tiny pause that says I am talking to you, not about you. Writing has no voice, so it needs marks to stand in for pauses, stops, questions, and who said what.

The teacher, said the student, is lazy. Two commas, and the insult changes direction. I'm sorry I love you is an apology. I'm sorry. I love you. is a declaration. In each pair, nobody changed a word.

The Comma

31.1

Series and Introductory Commas

Main ideaUse commas to separate items in a series and to set off an introductory word, phrase or clause from the main sentence.

Read this shopping list in sentence form: We need eggs milk bread and peanut butter. Your eye stumbles. Now with commas: We need eggs, milk, bread, and peanut butter. Each item stands apart. A list of three or more items inside a sentence is a , and commas are the walls between the items.

The comma before the and in a series has a name, the , and it is a real argument among editors. Newspapers usually skip it. Books usually keep it. Both are accepted styles, but the safe rule for school is to use it, because it prevents misreadings. Compare: I thanked my parents, Ms. Ruiz and Mr. Bell. Without the last comma, a reader might think Ms. Ruiz and Mr. Bell are your parents.

The second job of the comma is to mark the end of an , whatever comes before the main clause. A single word: However, the bus was late. A phrase: After the last bell, the halls emptied. A dependent clause: When the storm hit, the lights went out. The comma tells the reader: the setup is over, here comes the main idea.

For very short introductory phrases, the comma is optional: At noon we ate. Many writers still include it, and no one will mark it wrong. When in doubt, read the sentence aloud. If you hear a pause after the opener, put the comma there. If a missing comma could cause a stumble, as in After eating the dog fell asleep, the comma is not optional at all.

31.2

Extra Information Commas

Main ideaSet off with commas any clause or phrase that only adds information; leave out the commas when the words are needed to tell which one.

Two sentences, one comma apart. My brother who lives in Peoria is a nurse. My brother, who lives in Peoria, is a nurse. In the first, you have more than one brother, and the clause tells the reader which one: the Peoria brother. In the second, you have one brother, and the clause simply adds a fact about him. The commas change how many brothers you have.

A clause that is needed to identify the noun is . It restricts, or narrows, which one you mean. No commas. A clause that only adds information is . You could lift it out and the sentence would still point at the same person or thing. Commas on both sides, like handles on a basket.

A quick test: cross out the clause and ask if the sentence still means what you meant. Students who cheat will fail. Cross out who cheat and you get Students will fail, which is not the claim. Restrictive, no commas. The gym, which was built in 1962, needs a new roof. Cross out the middle and you still know which gym. Nonrestrictive, commas.

Many careful writers use that for restrictive clauses and which, with commas, for nonrestrictive ones: the book that I lost; the book, which I lost. This is a common style rule, not an iron law, but following it makes your meaning clearer. The same comma logic applies to an , a noun that renames another noun: My friend Sam is here (which friend: Sam) versus Sam, my oldest friend, is here (extra information).

31.3

Commas Between Adjectives

Main ideaPut a comma between two adjectives when each describes the noun equally and could be joined by and; do not use one when the first adjective describes the second plus the noun.

A cold, gray morning. A big red truck. Why does one pair get a comma and the other not? Say and between them. A cold and gray morning sounds fine. A big and red truck sounds odd. Now swap them. A gray, cold morning works. A red big truck does not. When both tests pass, the adjectives are , equal partners, and they take a comma.

When the tests fail, the adjectives are : they stack. Big describes red truck as one unit. The first adjective is really describing the second plus the noun. Cumulative adjectives usually follow an order that English speakers feel without thinking: opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material. A lovely little old round blue Italian wooden box. No commas anywhere.

Two more comma rules follow the same logic of equal partners. Do not put a comma between the last adjective and the noun: a cold, gray, morning is wrong. And do not put a comma between a subject and its verb, no matter how long the subject is: The long, tired line of students outside the cafeteria, finally moved is wrong. Subject and verb are a team, not a list.

Why does it matter? Comma-heavy writing feels choppy, and a comma in the wrong spot makes readers hunt for a list that is not there. Comma-light writing lets adjectives blur together. The and test takes two seconds and works almost every time. Use it whenever you have stacked two describing words.

31.4

Semicolons and Colons

Main ideaA semicolon joins two closely related independent clauses; a colon follows a complete sentence to introduce a list, an explanation or a quotation.

The power went out; the whole block went quiet. A period would work here, but it would separate two moments that belong together. A comma alone would be a splice. The sits between: a stop, but a soft one, used between two independent clauses. Test it by covering the semicolon. If both sides could be sentences, the semicolon is legal.

Semicolons have a second job: sorting a list whose items already contain commas. We visited Springfield, Illinois; Madison, Wisconsin; and Des Moines, Iowa. With commas alone, a reader might count six places instead of three. The semicolons act as bigger walls between the bigger pieces.

The points forward. It says: here comes what I promised. Before it, a complete sentence. After it, a list, an explanation or a quotation. She packed three things: a flashlight, a map and her father’s compass. He had one rule: never lie to the crew. The colon says pay attention, what follows is the payoff.

The most common colon error is putting it after a verb or a preposition, where nothing is complete yet: She packed: a flashlight, a map and a compass. The words before the colon are not a sentence, so the colon has nothing to stand on. Take it out, or rewrite the opener as a full sentence. And do not use a semicolon where a colon belongs; a semicolon never introduces a list.

Dashes and Parentheses

Main ideaDashes and parentheses both set off an interruption; a dash makes it loud and sudden, while parentheses make it quiet and aside.

Three ways to drop the same fact into a sentence. My uncle, who never cooks, made dinner. My uncle (who never cooks) made dinner. My uncle—who never cooks—made dinner. The commas are neutral. The whisper the fact, as if the writer leaned over to mutter it. The pair shouts it. The words are the same; the marks set the volume.

A dash is typed as two hyphens with no spaces, or as the long line called an . Use a pair to set off an interruption in the middle of a sentence. Use a single dash to add a sharp turn at the end: The test had twenty questions—all essay. A dash breaks the flow on purpose. Use it when you want the reader to feel the break.

Parentheses hold information the sentence could live without: a date, a definition, a quick example, a side comment. The museum opened in 1893 (the year of the World’s Fair) on the lakefront. If the words inside form a whole sentence on their own, put the period inside. If not, the period goes after the closing mark. Nothing inside parentheses should change the grammar of the sentence around it.

Both marks lose their power when overused. A page full of dashes reads like someone who cannot finish a thought. A page full of parentheses reads like someone who keeps whispering. Try this: use no more than one dash pair per paragraph, and ask of every parenthesis whether the fact belongs in the sentence, in a footnote, or nowhere.

31.6

Apostrophes

Main ideaAn apostrophe shows ownership or a missing letter; it never makes a word plural.

The dogs bowl. The dog’s bowl. The dogs’ bowl. Three versions, three meanings. The first is a mistake: it says the dogs bowl, as in a bowling alley. The second is one dog and its bowl. The third is a bowl shared by several dogs. The little mark moves, and the number of dogs changes. That mark is the , and it has exactly two jobs.

Job one is , showing that something belongs to someone. Add an apostrophe and s to a singular noun: the girl’s coat, Chris’s locker. For a plural noun that already ends in s, add only the apostrophe: the girls’ coats, the players’ bench. For a plural that does not end in s, add apostrophe and s: the children’s room, the men’s team.

Job two is the : the apostrophe stands where letters were removed. Do not becomes don’t. It is becomes it’s. They are becomes they’re. This is where the most common error in English lives. It’s always means it is or it has. Its, with no apostrophe, is the possessive, like his and her: The dog wagged its tail. If you can swap in it is, use the apostrophe. If not, do not.

The one thing an apostrophe never does is make a plural. Signs everywhere get this wrong: Banana’s 99 cents. Two DVD’s. The bananas do not own anything. Plain s makes plurals: bananas, DVDs, the 1990s. When you see an apostrophe, ask which of the two jobs it is doing. If it is doing neither, delete it.

Quotation Marks

Main ideaQuotation marks wrap a speaker's exact words and the titles of short works; commas and periods go inside the closing mark, and a new speaker starts a new paragraph.

Look at a line of dialogue: "We’re going to be late," Maya said, grabbing her keys. The wrap only the words Maya actually spoke. The comma after late sits inside the closing mark, which is the American rule for commas and periods. The , Maya said, tells who spoke and stays outside the marks. This is : her exact words.

Now the same idea reported instead of quoted: Maya said that they were going to be late. No quotation marks, because these are not her exact words; the pronoun changed from we to they. This is . Students often put marks around indirect speech. The test is simple: could a recorder have picked up these exact words? If not, no marks.

When the tag comes first, a comma introduces the quote: Maya said, "We’re going to be late." When a quoted question ends the sentence, the question mark goes inside: Maya asked, "Are we late?" If the whole sentence is a question but the quote is not, the mark goes outside: Did Maya really say "we have time"? Each time a new person speaks, start a new paragraph, even if the line is one word.

Quotation marks also mark the titles of short works: a poem, a song, a short story, an article, an episode. Longer works that stand alone, such as a book, a movie, an album or a newspaper, are set in italics in print, or underlined by hand. So the poem "The Road Not Taken" appears in a book of Frost’s poems. The marks say: this is a small piece that lives inside something bigger.

31.8

Capital Letters

Main ideaCapitalize the first word of a sentence, the pronoun I, and proper nouns, the names of specific people, places and things; leave common nouns lowercase.

My aunt is a doctor at a hospital on the west side. My aunt, Dr. Amara Bell, works at Rush University Medical Center on the West Side of Chicago. In the first sentence every noun is general: an aunt, a doctor, a hospital, a side of town. In the second, each has become a specific name. A general noun is a and stays lowercase. A specific name is a and gets a capital.

Proper nouns include people, cities, states, countries, streets, buildings, days, months, holidays, languages, nationalities, brand names and organizations. Tuesday, October, Halloween, Spanish, the Chicago Bears, Lake Michigan. Seasons stay lowercase: fall, winter. Directions stay lowercase when they mean a direction, drive west, but take a capital when they name a region: the West Side, the Midwest.

Titles before a name are capitalized: President Lincoln, Coach Diaz, Aunt Rosa. The same word after the name or on its own is not: the president spoke, my coach, my aunt. Family words follow the same rule. Mom is a name when you use it as one: I asked Mom. It is a common noun when a word like my sits in front of it: I asked my mom.

For titles of works, capitalize the first word, the last word, and every important word in between. Short words such as a, an, the, of, and, to and in stay lowercase unless they come first: The Call of the Wild; A Tale of Two Cities. Finally, the pronoun I is always capitalized, and so is the first word of a quoted sentence: She said, "Wait for me."

31.9

Words That Sound Alike

Main ideaWords that sound alike, such as affect and effect or than and then, are chosen by meaning and job, not by sound; a short test settles each one.

The weather affected the game. The weather had an effect on the game. Both are correct, and they mean about the same thing. But swap them and both become wrong. is almost always a verb, an action: to change something. is almost always a noun, a thing: the result of a change. If you can put the in front of it, it is effect. If you can put will in front of it, it is affect.

Than and then get mixed up because in fast speech they sound the same. Than compares: taller than, more than, rather than. Then tells time or order: first this, then that. If the sentence is comparing two things, than. If it is telling what happened next, then. A pattern to remember: than has an a like compare; then has an e like next.

Each family causes trouble because the ear cannot help. Their, there, they’re: their shows ownership, their bikes; there points to a place or starts a sentence, over there, there is; they’re is they are. To, too, two: to points toward, too means also or more than enough, two is the number. Your and you’re: your shows ownership, you’re is you are. For every one with an apostrophe, expand it. If you’re does not become you are in the sentence, it is wrong.

A few more that trip up good writers. Accept means to receive; except means leaving out. Lose has one o and means to misplace or be beaten; loose has two and means not tight. Who’s is who is; whose shows ownership. None of these can be settled by ear. Each needs the test, and the test takes a second. Keep a list of the ones you personally mix up and check for them last, after everything else in a draft is done.

31.10

Spelling Patterns

Main ideaMost English spelling follows patterns, such as dropping a silent e or doubling a final consonant before -ing; learn the pattern, then memorize the few words that break it.

Hope, hoping. Hop, hopping. Why does one word drop a letter and the other add one? Because each is obeying a rule that keeps the vowel sound steady. The in hope makes the o say its name. Drop the e before adding -ing and the o still sounds long, because the i takes over the job. Hop has a short o. Doubling the p walls off the vowel so it stays short. Without the doubling, hoping and hopping would look identical.

So the two rules: drop a final silent e before an ending that starts with a vowel (make, making; but hope, hopeful keeps the e because -ful starts with a consonant). Double the final consonant of a short one-syllable word with one vowel before adding -ing or -ed (run, running; stop, stopped). For longer words, double only if the last syllable is the : begin, beginning; but open, opening.

Two more patterns handle most of the rest. Change y to i when adding an ending, unless the ending is -ing: cry, cried, crying; happy, happier. And the old rhyme, i before e except after c, works for most words with the ee sound: believe, receive, ceiling. It fails for weird, seize and a few others. Rhymes like this are tools, not laws. Learn the tool, then learn each by name.

Spelling in English is messy because the language borrowed from French, Latin, Greek and German, and each brought its own habits. That is why a good speller does two things. First, knows the patterns, which cover most words. Second, keeps a personal list of the words that break them, because those are the ones spell-check will not always catch. Their and there are both spelled correctly. Only you know which one you meant.

Style Guides and Meaning

Main ideaWhen rules are contested, a style guide settles them for a publication; whatever guide you follow, every mark you place changes what a reader takes from the sentence.

Two teachers, two answers. One says always use the serial comma. One says never. Who is right? Both, in a sense. Where usage is genuinely divided, publications settle the question with a : a rulebook that picks one option and asks every writer to follow it, so the whole newspaper or book or website reads as one voice. Most American newspapers follow the Associated Press guide, which skips the serial comma. Most book publishers follow the Chicago Manual of Style, which keeps it.

A style guide also decides things grammar does not: whether to write 9 or nine, e-mail or email, whether to capitalize internet. Your school may have one for essays. If it does, follow it. If it does not, choose a rule for yourself and keep it through the whole piece. is what readers notice. Switching styles mid-page looks like carelessness even when each choice is defensible.

Underneath all the rules is one idea: punctuation is meaning. The teacher said the student is lazy. The teacher, said the student, is lazy. Same six words, opposite insult; the commas changed who is talking. A woman without her man is nothing. A woman: without her, man is nothing. Each mark tells the reader how to group the words, and grouping is meaning.

So when you edit, do not ask only whether this comma is legal. Ask what this comma makes the reader think. Try the sentence with the mark and without it. Read both aloud. If the meaning shifts, choose the version that says what you meant. If a rule seems to forbid it, rewrite the sentence rather than let the wrong meaning stand. That is the whole art: rules in service of the reader, never the other way around.

One Sentence, Two Readings

A referee's note after a game: The player said the coach was out of line. Now move two commas in: The player, said the coach, was out of line.

Who is accusing whom in each version? Then find one sentence in your own writing where moving a comma would change the meaning.

Words to know
style guide
a rulebook a publication follows so that every writer makes the same choices on contested points
consistency
making the same choice the same way every time through a piece of writing
Check yourself

1. What does a style guide do?

Why: Where usage is divided, the guide picks one option and every writer for that publication follows it.

2. Why should a writer be consistent about the serial comma?

Why: Both styles are accepted, but mixing them in one piece looks like the writer was not paying attention.

3. The teacher, said the student, is lazy. Who is speaking?

Why: The commas set off said the student as the tag. The student is calling the teacher lazy.

1. Which sentence has a correct introductory comma?

Why: The comma marks the end of the introductory phrase, before the main clause.

2. Which sentence means the speaker has only one brother?

Why: The commas make the clause extra information, so it is not picking one brother out of several.

3. Which uses a semicolon correctly?

Why: Both sides are independent clauses. A semicolon never introduces a list or follows a dependent clause.

4. Which is correct?

Why: Its is the possessive. It's means it is, and its' is never a word.

5. Which is punctuated correctly?

Why: A comma links the quote to its tag and sits inside the closing quotation mark.

6. Which should be capitalized?

Why: Months are proper nouns. Seasons, plain directions and common nouns stay lowercase.

7. Which is correct?

Why: Affect is the verb. The noun would be effect, as in had a big effect.

8. What does a style guide settle?

Why: Grammar settles the first, second and fourth. A style guide picks one option where usage is divided.

Why: The kids, the subject, do the building. In the others the fort receives the action.

Why: Rest before the game is a command with the subject you left out.

Why: Both pronouns are subjects, so both take subject case: she and I.

Why: The book was not waiting for the bus. The opening phrase has no doer next to it.

Why: Commas separate the items in the series. No comma belongs after need or after and.

Why: Without commas, who lives in Rockford is restrictive: it picks out one uncle from several.

Why: She wanted one thing is a complete sentence, so the colon can introduce what follows.
