The path the thread takes decides whether the seam holds

Two pieces joined by one line of stitching: over, under, over. Pull one piece and the seam opens between the dashes.

Those two paths have names. The first is the running stitch — quick, and weak. The second is the backstitch — the strong one, because each stitch reaches back into the last and no gap is ever left unfilled.

Four things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

A longer thread means fewer stops, so the job goes faster.

Past about 18 in (45 cm) a thread tangles, knots on itself and wears thin from being dragged through the cloth so many times. You lose more time untangling than you saved.

About 18 in — fingertips to elbow.

⚠️ People often think…

A running stitch will hold a split seam if you make the stitches small enough.

The size is not the problem; the path is. A running stitch leaves the thread free between stitches, so pull one end and the whole row slides out. A seam that carries weight gets a backstitch.

Weight on the seam means backstitch.

⚠️ People often think…

A button should be pulled down tight against the cloth so it does not wobble.

On thick cloth a tight button leaves no room for the layer the buttonhole sits in, and the buttonhole will not close. Sew over a pin or a toothpick, pull it out, and wind the loose thread underneath into a short stem — a shank.

Thick cloth needs a shank.

⚠️ People often think…

The knot can go on whichever side you started sewing from.

Knots and loose ends belong on the wrong side — the back of the fabric. Start from the wrong side, end off there with two small stitches in the same place, and snip close.

If you can see the knot, you worked from the wrong direction.

Helpful Hints

🧭 The unit in one line

Open the kit → pick the needle → cut about 18 in of thread → knot it → put right sides together → choose the stitch the job needs → sew → end off on the wrong side → snip → count the needles and pins back in.

Kit, knot, stitch, end off.

🔑 The one idea

There is no best stitch. There is a stitch for each job. Ask one question first — does this have to come out later, does it have to hold hard, or does it have to disappear? — and the stitch chooses itself.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Long thread is not faster. Past about 18 in (45 cm) it tangles, knots and wears thin from being pulled through the fabric so many times.
  • Never cut paper with fabric shears. One cut through paper dulls the blades, and a dull blade chews fabric instead of cutting it.
  • A running stitch is not a repair. Pull one end and the whole row slides out. Seams that carry weight get a backstitch.
  • Do not sew a button flat against thick cloth. With no shank — the little stem of thread under it — the button pulls tight and the buttonhole will not close.
  • Knots and loose ends belong on the wrong side, the back of the fabric. If you can see your knot from outside, you sewed from the wrong direction.
  • Never hold pins or needles in your mouth, and never leave one loose on a chair or the floor. Count them out and count the same number back in.

📚 Go to the sources

These open another website and need the internet. Old sewing manuals are free because their copyright has run out — the stitches in them are the same ones you are learning. Museum collections let you look closely at stitches people made by hand long before machines.

  • Project Gutenberg — a search for free, out-of-copyright sewing and needlework books, many of them school textbooks with stitch diagrams
  • Library of Congress — photographs, prints and documents about sewing in American life, free to view
  • Smithsonian Learning Lab — search ‘quilt’ or ‘needlework’ to see stitched objects from the Smithsonian museums up close
  • The Metropolitan Museum of Art — samplers: the practice cloths on which children once learned these same stitches, one row at a time
