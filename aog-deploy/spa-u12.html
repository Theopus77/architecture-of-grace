Ser, Estar and Describing People

Description

Every day you describe people and things: a new teacher, your little brother, a pair of lost headphones, how you feel before a game. In Spanish, describing starts with two verbs that both mean to be, ser and estar, and with adjectives that change their endings to match what they describe.

This unit shows you how to choose between ser and estar: ser for who someone is, where they are from, what they are like, the time and events; estar for where someone is and how they feel or are doing right now. Then you will learn to make adjectives agree in gender and number, place them in a sentence, and compare people with más… que, menos… que, mejor, peor, mayor and menor.

By the end you will be able to describe a family member, a friend or a famous person in a clear paragraph, explain every verb you chose, and fix the most common mistakes English speakers make, like la casa blanco or la fiesta está en mi casa.

The big question

How do Spanish speakers describe who someone is, how they are right now, and how they compare to others?

Two Verbs for To Be

Ser and Estar
Big questionHow can one English verb, to be, need two Spanish verbs, and how do you choose the right one?
The story

Two Cousins at the Same Party

Mateo is always calm. Daniela is calm only today, and Spanish can hear the difference.

Mateo and Daniela are cousins, and both live in Little Village in Chicago. At their grandmother's birthday party, their aunt introduces them to a new neighbor. She points to Mateo and says, "Mateo es tranquilo" (Mateo is a calm person). Everyone nods. Mateo never raises his voice, even when his little brothers spill juice on his homework.

Then the aunt points to Daniela, who is usually loud, funny and full of energy. Today Daniela is sitting quietly in the corner with a plate of tamales. The aunt says, "Daniela está tranquila hoy" (Daniela is calm today). Daniela laughs, because she knows the word está means this is how she is right now, not who she always is.

The neighbor, a student learning Spanish, asks a question in English: "Why did you say es for Mateo and está for Daniela? Aren't they both just is?" The aunt smiles. "In English, yes. In Spanish we have two verbs: ser and estar. Ser tells what someone is like as a person. Estar tells how someone is at this moment, or where someone is."

Later the grandmother opens her gifts and says, "¡Estoy muy contenta!" (I am very happy!). Then she adds, "Soy de Michoacán" (I am from Michoacán). The neighbor listens closely and starts to notice a pattern. Feelings and places use estar. Where you come from and what you are like use ser.

By the end of the party the neighbor tries it herself: "Soy estudiante y estoy cansada" (I am a student and I am tired). Daniela gives her a thumbs up. One sentence, two verbs, two different kinds of being.

Talk about itThink of one thing about you that is always true and one thing that is true only today. Which one would use ser and which would use estar?
Section 1

Meet Ser and Estar

23.1

All the Forms of Ser

Main ideaSer is irregular: soy, eres, es, somos, sois, son, and you must memorize these forms.

Picture your first day in a new Spanish class. The teacher says, "Yo la profesora Ruiz" (I am Professor Ruiz). A student answers, "Yo soy Carlos." Both sentences use the verb (to be), and both tell who someone is. Ser is one of the most common verbs in Spanish, so you will hear it every day.

Ser is irregular, which means its forms do not follow a normal pattern. You cannot just drop an ending and add a new one. Here are all six present-tense forms: yo soy (I am), tú eres (you are, friendly), él, ella or usted es (he, she is, or you are, formal), nosotros somos (we are), vosotros sois (you all are, used in Spain), ellos, ellas or ustedes son (they are, or you all are).

Say them in order out loud: soy, eres, es, somos, sois, son. Notice that eres and es look alike but are different. Eres goes only with tú: "Tú eres mi amigo" (You are my friend). Es goes with one other person or with usted: "Ella es mi amiga" (She is my friend).

A common mistake is to say "yo es" because English uses is so often. In Spanish each subject has its own form. Yo always takes soy. Another mistake is to forget the verb completely: "Yo estudiante." Spanish needs the verb, so say "Yo soy estudiante."

Think it through
Introductions in the Hallway

Ana: Hola, yo soy Ana. ¿Tú eres nuevo? (Hi, I'm Ana. Are you new?) Luis: Sí, soy Luis. Mi hermana y yo somos de Texas. (Yes, I'm Luis. My sister and I are from Texas.) Ana: ¡Qué bien! Mis padres son de Puerto Rico. (Great! My parents are from Puerto Rico.)

Find every form of ser in the dialogue and name its subject. Then introduce yourself with two sentences using soy.

to be — for identity, origin, traits, time and dates; irregular: soy, eres, es, somos, sois, son
soy
I am — the yo form of ser: Soy estudiante (I am a student)
eres
you are — the tú form of ser: Eres simpático (You are nice)
Check yourself

1. Which form of ser completes the sentence? Nosotros ___ de Chicago.

Why: Nosotros (we) takes somos: Nosotros somos de Chicago.

2. Which sentence is correct?

Why: Tú always takes eres; es is for él, ella and usted.

3. What does Ellos son estudiantes mean?

Why: Son is the ellos form of ser, so the sentence means they are students.
23.2

All the Forms of Estar

Main ideaEstar is also irregular: estoy, estás, está, estamos, estáis, están, with accents on most forms.

Imagine texting your cousin after school. She writes, "¿Dónde ?" (Where are you?). You answer, "Estoy en casa" (I am at home). Both messages use the verb (to be), the verb Spanish uses for where you are and how you are right now.

Here are the six present-tense forms of estar: yo estoy (I am), tú estás (you are), él, ella or usted está (he, she is, or you are, formal), nosotros estamos (we are), vosotros estáis (you all are, Spain), ellos, ellas or ustedes están (they are, or you all are). Estoy ends in -oy, just like soy, voy and doy.

Watch the accent marks. Estás, está, estáis and están all have a written accent, and it changes where you put the stress: es-TÁ, not ES-ta. Without the accent, esta means this (esta casa, this house). So "Ella está aquí" (She is here) needs the accent, but "esta mesa" (this table) does not.

English speakers often drop the accent because English has none. In Spanish a missing accent can create a different word, so write it every time. Estamos is the one form besides estoy that has no written accent, because its stress falls naturally on -ta-.

Think it through
A Quick Text Thread

Prima: ¿Dónde estás? (Where are you?) Tú: Estoy en la biblioteca con Ana. Estamos cansadas. (I'm at the library with Ana. We're tired.) Prima: Mis padres están en el mercado. (My parents are at the market.)

List every form of estar in the texts and write its subject. Then write a text telling a friend where you are right now.

Words to know
estar
to be — for location, feelings and conditions; forms: estoy, estás, está, estamos, estáis, están
estoy
I am — the yo form of estar: Estoy en clase (I am in class)
estás
you are — the tú form of estar, with an accent on the a: ¿Cómo estás? (How are you?)
Check yourself

1. Which form completes the sentence? Mis amigos ___ en el parque.

Why: Mis amigos is ellos, which takes están, written with an accent on the a.

2. Which sentence is spelled correctly?

Why: The tú form is estás, with a written accent; está goes with él, ella or usted.

3. Which form of estar has NO written accent?

Why: Estoy and estamos have no written accent; estás, está, estáis and están do.
23.3

Why Spanish Has Two

Main ideaSer tells what something is; estar tells where it is or how it is right now.

Look at two sentences about the same cup of hot chocolate. "El chocolate es delicioso" means chocolate is delicious as a general fact. "El chocolate está caliente" means the chocolate is hot right now. English uses is for both. Spanish uses two verbs because it separates what something is from the state it is in.

A simple way to think about it: ser answers the question What is it? or What is it like? Estar answers Where is it? or How is it right now? This is a starting point, not a perfect rule, but it works for most sentences you will say in middle school.

Both verbs come from Latin. Ser comes mostly from the Latin verb esse (to be). Estar comes from stare, which meant to stand. That history still shows: estar describes where something stands and the condition it stands in at the moment.

Many learners try to choose based on whether something is permanent. That idea helps sometimes, but it fails with some examples. A person who has died "está muerto" (is dead), which is permanent, yet it uses estar because it is a condition. Learn the uses one by one instead of relying only on permanent versus temporary.

Think it through
Same Soup, Two Sentences

Abuela: La sopa de mi mamá es muy buena. (My mom's soup is very good, always.) Nieto: ¡Y hoy la sopa está muy caliente! (And today the soup is very hot!) Abuela: Espera un minuto. Ahora está perfecta. (Wait a minute. Now it's just right.)

Why does the grandmother use es in the first line and está in the last line? Write one sentence of each kind about your favorite food.

Words to know
caliente
hot (temperature) — often with estar: La sopa está caliente (The soup is hot)
delicioso
delicious — Los tacos son deliciosos (Tacos are delicious, in general)
ahora
now — a clue that you are describing a present state: Ahora estoy bien
Check yourself

1. Which question does estar usually answer?

Why: Estar is for location and present states; material, time and origin use ser.

2. What does El café está frío mean?

Why: Está describes the coffee's condition at this moment.

3. Why does La abuela está muerta use estar even though death is permanent?

Why: Spanish treats muerto as a resulting condition, so it takes estar; permanent versus temporary is only a rough guide.
Section 2

When to Use Ser

23.4

Identity and Profession

Main ideaUse ser to say who someone is, what they are, and what job they have.

At a family reunion someone asks, "¿Quién es él?" (Who is he?). You answer, "Es mi tío Roberto. Es enfermero" (He’s my uncle Roberto. He’s a nurse). Both answers use ser, because they tell who he is and what he does. Identity, relationships and jobs all take ser.

Notice something new: Spanish says "Es enfermero," not "Es un enfermero." When you name someone’s job, religion or nationality after ser without any description, you leave out un or una. "Mi mamá es profesora" (My mom is a teacher). English speakers often add un because English needs a.

If you add a description, the un comes back: "Es un enfermero excelente" (He is an excellent nurse). The description makes it about this particular person, so the article returns. You will see this pattern in school documents all the time.

Relationships also use ser: "Ella es mi hermana" (She is my sister), "Somos amigos" (We are friends). A frequent mistake is using estar here, as in "Está mi hermana." That sounds like she is present in a place, not who she is to you.

Think it through

Look at a family photo. "Esta es mi mamá. Es doctora. Este es mi papá. Es cocinero en un restaurante en Pilsen. Y este soy yo. Soy estudiante." (This is my mom. She is a doctor. This is my dad. He is a cook at a restaurant in Pilsen. And this is me. I am a student.)

23.5

Origin and Nationality

Main ideaUse ser de to say where someone or something is from, and ser with nationality words.

A new classmate says, "Soy de Guatemala" (I am from Guatemala). Another says, "Mi familia es de Chicago, pero mis abuelos son de Puerto Rico." Where people come from is part of who they are, so origin always takes ser plus de (from).

Nationality words also use ser: "Ella es mexicana" (She is Mexican), "Ellos son colombianos" (They are Colombian). In Spanish, nationality words are not capitalized. English writes Mexican with a capital M, but Spanish writes mexicana with a small m.

Things have origins too. "El café es de Colombia" (The coffee is from Colombia). "Estas tortillas son de una tortillería en Little Village" (These tortillas are from a tortilla shop in Little Village). Ser de can also mean made of: "La mesa es de madera" (The table is made of wood).

Compare origin with location. "Soy de Chicago" tells where you are from. "Estoy en Chicago" tells where you are now. A student from Chicago on vacation in Miami says, "Soy de Chicago, pero estoy en Miami."

Think it through

23.6

Traits and Characteristics

Main ideaUse ser with adjectives that describe what someone or something is like in general.

Think back to Mateo from the story: "Mateo es tranquilo." That sentence describes his personality, the kind of person he is. Traits like personality, looks, size and color usually take ser: "Mi hermano es " (My brother is tall), "Mi perro es " (My dog is friendly).

Walk through a description: Mi abuela es baja (short), es muy inteligente y es generosa. Each adjective tells what she is like, not how she feels today, so each uses ser. The adjectives also end in -a because abuela is feminine.

Things have traits too. "La casa es grande" (The house is big). "El libro es interesante" (The book is interesting). "La nieve es blanca" (Snow is white). These describe the thing as it normally is.

Learners sometimes use estar for looks: "Mi hermano está alto." A native speaker might hear that as He’s looking tall today, or He’s gotten tall. Use ser for normal traits unless you really mean a change.

Think it through

23.7

Time, Dates and Events

Main ideaUse ser to tell time, give dates and days, and say where an event takes place.

Your friend asks, "¿Qué hora es?" (What time is it?). You look at your phone: "Son las tres" (It’s three o’clock). Telling time uses ser. For one o’clock use es: "Es la una." For every other hour use son, because the hours are plural: "Son las dos, son las diez."

Dates and days use ser too. "Hoy es lunes" (Today is Monday). "Mañana es el 16 de septiembre" (Tomorrow is September 16), Mexico’s Independence Day. Notice that Spanish puts the day number before the month and does not capitalize days or months.

Here is a surprise: the location of an event uses ser, not estar. "La fiesta es en mi casa" (The party is at my house). "El concierto es en el parque" (The concert is in the park). An event takes place somewhere, and Spanish treats that as ser.

Compare two sentences. "La fiesta es en la escuela" (The party takes place at school). "Mis amigos están en la escuela" (My friends are at school). People and objects are located with estar; events happen with ser. Many English speakers use está for both, which is a very common error.

Section 3

When to Use Estar

23.8

Location of People and Things

Main ideaUse estar to say where a person or object is located.

You can’t find your backpack. Your mom says, "Tu mochila está en la cocina" (Your backpack is in the kitchen). Any time you say where a person or thing is, use estar. "Estoy en la escuela." "Chicago está en Illinois." "Mis llaves están en la mesa."

Even places that never move use estar for their location. "El lago Michigan está al este de Chicago" (Lake Michigan is east of Chicago). "Madrid está en España." The city is always there, yet Spanish still uses estar, because the sentence is about location.

Useful location words go after estar: (near), lejos de (far from), al lado de (next to), encima de (on top of), debajo de (under). "La biblioteca está cerca de mi casa" (The library is near my house).

The question word is dónde (where): "¿Dónde está el baño?" (Where is the bathroom?). A common mistake is "¿Dónde es el baño?" Remember: objects and people are located with estar, and only events use ser for place.

23.9

Feelings and Moods

Main ideaUse estar for emotions and moods: how someone feels right now.

The grandmother in the story opens her gifts and says, "¡Estoy muy contenta!" (I’m very happy!). Feelings are states that change during the day, so they take estar. "Estoy " (I’m nervous) before a test. "Estoy tranquilo" after it ends.

Common feeling words with estar: contento (happy), triste (sad), enojado (angry), nervioso (nervous), preocupado (worried), aburrido (bored), emocionado (excited). Each changes to match the person: "Mi hermana está preocupada." "Mis amigos están emocionados."

The question is "¿Cómo estás?" (How are you?). You might answer "Estoy bien" (I’m well) or "Estoy un poco triste" (I’m a little sad). Asking "¿Cómo eres?" is a different question: it means What are you like? and you would answer with traits.

One word breaks the pattern: feliz (happy). Many speakers say "Soy feliz" for a happy life and "Estoy feliz" for right now, and both are correct. For a safe choice, use estar contento for a feeling today.

23.10

Conditions and Health

Main ideaUse estar for physical conditions: sick, tired, broken, open, clean, full.

You wake up with a sore throat and text your teacher: "Estoy " (I’m sick). Health is a condition, so it takes estar. "Estoy cansada" (I’m tired). "Mi abuelo está mejor" (My grandfather is better).

Objects have conditions too. "La puerta está abierta" (The door is open). "La tienda está cerrada" (The store is closed). "Mi teléfono está roto" (My phone is broken). "El cuarto está limpio" (The room is clean). These describe the state things are in right now.

Watch how a condition can change while the object stays the same. "La ventana está abierta" in the morning, and later "La ventana está cerrada." The window did not become a different window; only its condition changed.

A mistake to avoid: "Soy enfermo" or "Soy cansado" for how you feel today. With ser, those can sound like a permanent description of who you are. For how you are today, use estar. Say "Estoy enferma" instead.

Section 4

Pairs That Change Meaning

23.11

Aburrido and Listo

Main ideaSome adjectives change meaning with ser or estar: es aburrido is boring, está aburrido is bored.

Your friend yawns during a movie and says, "Estoy aburrido." He means I’m bored, a feeling right now. If he said, "Soy aburrido," he would mean I’m a boring person. Same adjective, different verb, very different message.

Aburrido works like this: ser aburrido means boring (a trait), estar aburrido means bored (a feeling). "La película es aburrida" (The movie is boring). "Los estudiantes están aburridos" (The students are bored).

Listo is another pair: ser listo means clever or smart, estar listo means ready. "Mi prima es muy lista" (My cousin is very clever). "¿Estás lista?" (Are you ready?). Before a trip your mom might shout, "¡Estamos listos!" (We’re ready!).

These pairs make sense if you use the core idea. Ser gives the trait or identity. Estar gives the state right now. Being ready is a state; being clever is a trait. Once you see that, the meanings are easy to remember.

23.12

Rico, Malo and Verde

Main ideaMore pairs: ser rico is rich, estar rico is tasty; ser malo is bad, estar malo is sick; ser verde is green, estar verde is unripe.

At a taquería in Pilsen, your uncle bites into a taco al pastor and says, "¡Está !" (It’s delicious!). He is not calling the taco wealthy. With estar, rico describes how food tastes right now. With ser, "Es rico" usually means He is rich.

Malo follows the same idea. "Ese perro es malo" (That dog is bad, as in mean). "Mi abuela está mala" (My grandmother is sick) in many countries. The trait is bad; the state is unwell.

Colors can change too. "La manzana es verde" (The apple is green, a green apple). "El plátano está verde" (The banana is unripe, not ready to eat). The ser sentence names a color; the estar sentence describes its condition.

You do not need to memorize a long list. Ask yourself each time: am I describing what this is, or the state it is in right now? That question explains every pair in this lesson. Try it with any new adjective you meet.

Main ideaCheck ser or estar by asking: is this identity, origin, trait, time or event, or is it location, feeling or condition?

Section 1

Making Adjectives Match

24.1

Gender Agreement: -o and -a

Main ideaAn adjective ending in -o changes to -a to match a feminine noun: el gato negro, la gata negra.

Gabriel’s first mistake on the poster was writing perdido for a girl dog. In Spanish, an adjetivo (adjective), a word that describes a noun, must agree with that noun. Agree means it matches the noun’s gender (masculine or feminine) and its number (one or more than one).

Most adjectives you learn first end in -o. That is the masculine form. To describe a feminine noun, change the -o to -a. Walk through it: el perro negro (the black dog), la perra negra (the black female dog). El libro nuevo (the new book), la mochila nueva (the new backpack).

Look at the noun, not the person talking. A boy describing his grandmother says, "Mi abuela es bonita" (My grandmother is pretty), with -a, because abuela is feminine. A girl describing her uncle says, "Mi tío es alto," with -o.

English speakers often forget agreement because English adjectives never change: a tall boy, a tall girl. In Spanish that habit produces mistakes like "la casa blanco." Train yourself to look back at the noun every time you write an adjective.

24.2

Number Agreement: Plurals

Main ideaPlural nouns take plural adjectives: add -s after a vowel and -es after a consonant.

Gabriel’s second mistake was "orejas grande." Orejas (ears) is plural, so the adjective must be plural too: orejas grandes. Number agreement works the same way nouns make plurals. Plurals matter just as much as gender.

If the adjective ends in a vowel, add -s: alto → altos, bonita → bonitas, grande → grandes. If it ends in a consonant, add -es: azul → azules, fácil → fáciles, joven → jóvenes. Notice that joven gains an accent in the plural so the stress stays in the same place.

Now combine gender and number. There are four forms of an -o adjective: alto (masculine singular), alta (feminine singular), altos (masculine plural), altas (feminine plural). "Los chicos son altos. Las chicas son altas."

When a group mixes masculine and feminine nouns, use the masculine plural. "Mi hermano y mi hermana son simpáticos." Even one boy in a large group of girls makes the adjective masculine plural. This rule is the standard in Spanish grammar.

24.3

Adjectives Ending in -e or a Consonant

Main ideaAdjectives ending in -e or most consonants have one form for both genders: inteligente, fácil.

Your teacher says, "Marco es . Ana es inteligente." The adjective did not change. Adjectives that end in -e have the same form for masculine and feminine. Only the number changes: los estudiantes inteligentes.

Most adjectives ending in a consonant work the same way. "El examen es . La tarea es fácil" (The test is easy. The homework is easy). Other examples: difícil (hard), azul (blue), gris (gray), joven (young), popular (popular).

A common mistake is to invent a feminine form: "inteligenta" or "fácila." Those words do not exist. If the adjective does not end in -o, leave it alone for gender and change only for number.

There is one group that does change: nationality adjectives ending in a consonant, and a few ending in -or. Español becomes española; trabajador (hardworking) becomes trabajadora. "Mi mamá es muy trabajadora" (My mom is very hardworking).

24.4

Where Adjectives Go

Main ideaMost describing adjectives go after the noun; number words and a few like buen and gran go before.

English puts adjectives before the noun: a red car. Spanish usually puts them after: un carro . "Una casa grande" (a big house). "Un perro simpático" (a friendly dog). Putting them first, as in "una roja casa," is a very common English-speaker error.

Some words go before the noun: numbers (dos gatos), mucho (mucha gente, a lot of people), poco, and the question words. These tell how many rather than what kind. "Tengo tres hermanos" (I have three siblings) and "Hay muchos libros" (There are many books) follow this order.

Bueno and malo can go before a masculine singular noun, and then they drop the -o: buen and mal. "Es un amigo" (He is a good friend). "Es un mal día" (It’s a bad day). The feminine keeps its ending: "una buena amiga."

Grande becomes gran before any singular noun, and its meaning changes. "Una casa grande" is a big house. "Una gran casa" is a great house. "Es un gran presidente" means he is a great president, not a physically big one.

Describing People

24.5

Physical Description

Main ideaDescribe looks with ser plus an adjective and tener plus hair or eyes: Es alto. Tiene el pelo negro.

The neighbor recognized Canela from three details. Describing people works the same way. Start with ser for build and height: "Es alto" (He is tall), "Es baja" (She is short), "Es delgado" (He is slim). Choose the ending that matches the person.

For hair and eyes, Spanish often uses (to have): "Tiene el pelo rizado" (He has curly hair), "Tiene los ojos verdes" (She has green eyes). Notice the adjective agrees with pelo or ojos, not with the person. A girl "tiene el pelo largo," with -o, because pelo is masculine.

Useful words: el pelo corto (short hair), largo (long), rizado (curly), lacio (straight), castaño (brown hair), rubio (blond). For eyes: los ojos cafés or marrones (brown eyes), azules, verdes. Café and marrón are both correct; different countries prefer different words.

Watch the false friend: largo means long, not large. "Tiene el pelo largo" is long hair. For large, use grande. English speakers mix these up often. "Una casa grande" is a big house, and "una calle larga" is a long street.

24.6

Personality

Main ideaPersonality traits use ser and agree with the person: Es generosa, son divertidos.

24.8

More Than and Less Than

Main ideaTo compare, use más + adjective + que or menos + adjective + que, with the adjective agreeing.

Gabriel’s grandmother wrote, "Es más pequeña que un gato grande" (She is smaller than a big cat). That is a comparison. The pattern is más + adjective + que for more than, and menos + adjective + que for less than.

Walk through it: Ana es alta. Ana es más alta que Luis (Ana is taller than Luis). Luis es menos alto que Ana (Luis is less tall than Ana). English often uses -er (taller), but Spanish always uses más; "altera" is not a word.

The adjective still agrees with the first person or thing: "Mi hermana es más alta que mi hermano." Alta matches hermana, the subject, not hermano. "Soy más alto que mi hermano" (I am taller than my brother) follows the same rule.

A frequent mistake is using de or como instead of que: "más alto de Luis" or "más alto como Luis." For comparing two people or things, use que. (Más de is used only before numbers, like más de diez, more than ten.)

For age, Spanish uses (older) and (younger). "Mi hermana es mayor que yo" (My sister is older than I am). "Mi primo es menor que mi hermano" (My cousin is younger than my brother).

These four words do not change for gender. "Mi hermana es mayor" and "Mi hermano es mayor" use the same form. They only change for number: mejores, peores, mayores, menores. "Mis abuelos son mayores que mis padres."

The common mistake is "más mejor." Mejor already means more good, so adding más doubles it. Also, más grande and más pequeño are fine for physical size; mayor and menor are best for age.

Write it

Escribe un párrafo sobre una persona importante en tu vida. Write a paragraph in Spanish (6–8 sentences) that says who the person is and where they are from, describes their looks and personality, tells how they are or where they are today, and includes one comparison with más… que, menos… que, mayor or menor.

  • Use ser for identity, origin and traits; use estar for location and how the person feels today.
  • Check every adjective against its noun: -o or -a, singular or plural.
  • Put most adjectives after the noun, and use tener for hair and eyes.
  • Use que in comparisons, and never write más mejor.
  • Read it aloud, then read again looking only at endings and accents.
