The Mafia on Trial

Mafia

This unit follows one secret criminal society, the Sicilian Mafia, from the lemon groves around Palermo in the 1860s to courtrooms in Palermo and Chicago. The story reaches into our own time. You will meet the people who studied it, the people who feared it and the people who stood up to it. You will also meet Chinese American tongs and the triads, and see how their story differs.

The Mafia's strongest shield was omertà, a rule of silence. For more than a century it kept witnesses quiet and crimes unpunished. This unit is about how that silence was broken: by police reports, Senate hearings, new laws, brave insiders, judges who paid with their lives and ordinary neighbors acting together. Remember too that almost all Italian Americans and Chinese Americans had nothing to do with these groups, and many were their victims.

By the end, you will be able to explain where the Mafia came from and how it spread. You will be able to describe how the RICO Act and the Maxi Trial worked. You will also be able to weigh an insider's testimony, and to explain the real cost of organized crime to ordinary people.

The big question

How did people break the Mafia's rule of silence, and what did it cost them?

The story

Hands on the Screen

In March 1951, millions of Americans stop what they are doing to watch a Senate hearing on live television.

It is March 1951 in New York City. In a crowded federal courtroom, a committee of United States senators is asking questions about organized crime. Bright lights hang over the tables. Television cameras stand in the room. Across the country, people gather around small black-and-white sets at home, in bars and in store windows to watch.

The witness at the table is Frank Costello, a gambling boss the newspapers call powerful. He does not want his face shown on camera. So the camera crew points at something else: his hands. Viewers watch his fingers move on the table while he answers, dodges or refuses to answer the senators' questions.

The man leading the hearings is Senator Estes Kefauver of Tennessee. Since May 1950, his committee has held hearings in about 14 cities, including Chicago. It has heard more than 600 witnesses. The Senate's own history office estimates that about 30 million Americans tuned in to the New York hearings that month.

For many viewers, this is the first time they have seen the people behind the headlines. The hearings raise a question Americans will argue about for decades. Is organized crime a set of local gangs? Or is it one secret society, with rules, a name and a reach across the whole nation?

Where the Mafia Began

27.1

Protection in the Lemon Groves

Main ideaThe Sicilian Mafia grew in western Sicily after 1860 by selling violent protection where the new Italian state was weak.

In 1860, Giuseppe Garibaldi’s volunteers landed in Sicily. Soon the island joined the new Kingdom of Italy. The new government was far away in the north. It had few police in the countryside and little trust from the people. Around Palermo, in the west of the island, rich lemon and orange groves earned good money. Anyone who could guard that wealth, or threaten it, had power.

In this setting, armed men began to sell . A landowner paid them to keep thieves away from his trees and his water. But the same men could also send the thieves. Paying was safer than refusing. A deal where people pay a group so the group will not harm them is called a . It grew strongest where the state was weakest.

The name came into use in these same years. A popular play set in Palermo’s prison, I mafiusi di la Vicaria, was first staged in 1863. It made the word familiar to many people. In 1865 a government official in Palermo, Filippo Antonio Gualterio, used the word ’maffia’ in a report to his superiors. Historians usually call this the first official use.

Historians such as Salvatore Lupo and John Dickie place the Mafia’s beginnings in these decades, not in the Middle Ages. A popular legend says the Mafia began in 1282, or that its name is a secret code made of first letters. No record from that time supports either story. They are legends told much later.

27.2

Franchetti's Inquiry

Main ideaIn 1876 Leopoldo Franchetti studied Sicily on his own, and his 1877 report argued that violence there had become a business.

In 1876, two young men from Tuscany, Leopoldo Franchetti and Sidney Sonnino, traveled through Sicily. No one sent them. They paid their own way and asked their own questions. They wanted to understand why the island was so violent. They also wanted to know why the new national government seemed unable to stop it.

They published what they found in 1877, in two volumes called La Sicilia nel 1876. Sonnino wrote about the hard lives of peasant farmers. Franchetti wrote about politics and government. A careful study of a problem, built on interviews and evidence, is called an . The Italian parliament held its own official inquiry into Sicily in those same years.

Franchetti’s main idea was striking. He described what he called an . In his view, violence in western Sicily was not wild or random. It was a service that some people bought and others sold, much like any trade. Landowners, estate managers and armed guards all took part in this market.

Many historians today build on Franchetti’s idea. Diego Gambetta’s 1993 book The Sicilian Mafia describes the Mafia as a business in private protection. Other historians, such as Salvatore Lupo and Letizia Paoli, add that it is also a , with rituals, loyalty and political ties. Both views use the same evidence. They stress different parts of it.

27.3

The Sangiorgi Report

Main ideaFrom 1898 to 1900, Palermo's police chief Ermanno Sangiorgi mapped Mafia groups in detail, showing they were organized long before later trials.

Ermanno Sangiorgi was the , the chief of police, of Palermo in the late 1890s. From November 1898 to February 1900 he sent 31 reports to the government in Rome. Together they run to about 485 pages. They describe the Mafia around Palermo in more detail than anyone had put on paper before.

Sangiorgi described eight Mafia groups. Sicilians called these groups ; one group is a cosca. Each group had its own territory in the citrus gardens around the city. He named and profiled 218 men. He wrote about their rules, their leaders and the ceremony that brought in new members.

For about a century, the report sat almost forgotten in the state archives in Rome. The historian Salvatore Lupo published it in full in 2011. The historian John Dickie described it for English readers. It matters because it shows that the Mafia’s groups, rules and ritual already existed in the 1890s.

Sangiorgi’s work did not end the Mafia. Police knowledge alone was not enough when witnesses stayed silent and powerful friends protected suspects. But his report is a , a record made at the time by someone involved. Historians can compare it with what later witnesses said, and they find the same kind of structure.

Silence, Ships and New Cities

27.4

Omertà, the Rule of Silence

Main ideaOmertà is the rule of silence toward police and courts, and fear of breaking it let Mafia crimes go unpunished for generations.

On 1 February 1893, Emanuele Notarbartolo was stabbed to death on a train near Palermo. He was a former mayor of the city. He had also run the Bank of Sicily and tried to clean up its dealings. Suspicion fell on a powerful member of parliament, Raffaele Palizzolo.

The case dragged through courts in Milan, Bologna and Florence. In 1902 a court in Bologna convicted Palizzolo, but a higher court threw out that verdict. In 1904 a court in Florence acquitted him for lack of evidence. Through all of it, Notarbartolo’s son Leopoldo kept pushing for justice, while many witnesses said little or nothing.

That silence has a name: . It is the Mafia’s rule that members, and people around them, never help the police or the courts. Breaking it could bring punishment or death. Many ordinary people stayed silent, not out of loyalty, but out of fear. A crime with no witness willing to is very hard to prove in court.

This is why omertà runs through every part of this unit. The state could not win while people stayed quiet. Every real victory came when someone chose to speak, and when others protected them. Watch for that pattern in each story that follows, in Sicily and in America.

27.5

Leaving Sicily

Main ideaMillions of Italians came to America to work; a small number of criminals followed and preyed mostly on other immigrants.

Between about 1880 and 1920, about four million Italians moved to the United States. Most came from southern Italy and Sicily, and many were poor farmers. Sonnino’s 1877 study had described how hard life was for Sicilian peasants. In America they built railroads, worked in factories, opened shops and raised families in cities like New York and Chicago.

Almost all of these had nothing to do with crime. But a small number of criminals came too, or grew up in the new neighborhoods. From about 1903 to 1915, some sent threatening letters signed with a black hand. They demanded money and threatened harm. Getting money by threats is called . Police at the time said the Black Hand was a method used by many small gangs, not one society.

Most victims of the Black Hand were Italian immigrants themselves, and many fought back. In Chicago in November 1907, Italian Americans proposed a group called the White Hand Society. By early 1908 it had an office in the Masonic Temple building downtown. It offered rewards and pressed the police to act.

Prejudice made life harder. In New Orleans in 1891, after a trial ended without convictions in the murder of the police chief, a mob killed 11 Italian men. In 2019, the mayor of New Orleans formally apologized to Italian Americans. A , a fixed and unfair idea about a whole group, can do real harm.

27.6

The First American Families

Main ideaProhibition made gangs rich, and by the 1930s crime families built on the Sicilian pattern ran rackets in several American cities.

On 16 January 1920, national began. Making and selling alcohol was now against federal law. But millions of people still wanted to drink. Gangs of many backgrounds, including Irish, Jewish and Italian gangs, made fortunes supplying them. In Chicago, Johnny Torrio and then Al Capone built a large organization that later came to be called the Outfit.

Some of these gangs were organized like the Sicilian cosche. Each had a boss, other leaders and members who swore loyalty in a ceremony. In the early 1930s, after a violent struggle among New York bosses, leaders set up a system of families with a council to settle disputes. New York came to have five families. Chicago had one main group, the Outfit.

The families lasted after Prohibition ended in 1933. They moved into gambling, loan-sharking, labor unions and other , illegal businesses built on fraud or threats. Their power depended on bribing officials and on silence. For years, many officials said little about them. Some doubted a national organization existed. Others were paid not to look.

Remember what the families were not. They were never ’the Italians.’ Only a very small share of Italian Americans ever took part. Many Italian Americans became police officers, prosecutors, judges and reporters who fought organized crime. Historians also stress that gangs of many backgrounds ran rackets in these years.

America Finds a Name

27.7

The Kefauver Committee

Main ideaFrom 1950 to 1951, a Senate committee led by Estes Kefauver held hearings in about 14 cities, including Chicago, and put organized crime in the spotlight.

In May 1950, the United States Senate formed a to investigate organized crime that crossed state lines. A special committee is a group of senators set up for one task and for a limited time. Its chair was Estes Kefauver, a senator from Tennessee. Over the next year it held hearings in about 14 cities and heard more than 600 witnesses.

Chicago was one of those cities. Some Chicago testimony was taken in , meaning the public and the press were kept out. One witness was a police captain, Daniel ’Tubbo’ Gilbert, who was running for Cook County sheriff. He was asked how he had become so wealthy. His private testimony leaked to the press before the November 1950 election, and he lost.

The hearings also showed the danger to people who spoke up. On 25 September 1950, a former Chicago police lieutenant, William Drury, was shot and killed in Chicago. He had been expected to help the committee. His murder reminded everyone why so many witnesses were afraid to talk.

The committee held a , not a trial. Senators could question witnesses under oath and publish what they learned. They could not convict anyone of a crime. But hearings can change what the public knows. That can push police, prosecutors and voters to act.

27.8

Crime on Live Television

Main ideaLive TV in March 1951 reached millions, and the committee's report claimed a nationwide Mafia, a claim historians still debate.

In March 1951 the committee held hearings in New York, and they were shown live on television. The Senate Historical Office estimates that about 30 million Americans watched. Frank Costello, a New York gambling boss, did not want his face shown. So the cameras showed his hands instead. Many viewers remembered those hands for years.

On 1 May 1951 the committee issued its Third Interim Report, an meant to share results before the final one. It said there was a nationwide crime , a group of criminals working together, known as the Mafia. It said the Mafia had members in many large cities and controlled some of the most profitable rackets. It said the Mafia’s power rested on force and murder.

Not everyone agreed. The scholar Joseph Albini in 1971, and Dwight C. Smith in The Mafia Mystique in 1975, argued that the evidence was thin. They said the committee relied too much on rumor and focused too much on one ethnic group. Later evidence confirmed that Italian American crime families existed and worked together. It did not show that one Mafia ran all organized crime in America.

This debate is a good lesson in evidence. A committee’s , its stated conclusion, can be partly right and partly overstated. Historians test each part. Which claim has sworn testimony behind it? Which rests on police opinion? Which was later proved in court?

27.9

A Trooper's Hunch at Apalachin

Main ideaIn November 1957, a state police sergeant's roadblock at Apalachin, New York, exposed a meeting of crime leaders from many states.

Edgar Croswell was a sergeant in the New York State Police. He worked near Apalachin, a small town in upstate New York. He knew that Joseph Barbara, who owned a country estate there, had a criminal past. In November 1957 Croswell noticed signs that many out-of-town visitors were coming to Barbara’s estate. He decided to watch.

On 14 November 1957, Croswell and other officers set up a near the estate. When the guests learned that police were outside, many tried to leave by car or ran into the woods. Officers stopped and identified 58 men from many states. Many were known leaders of crime families. Sam Giancana, a leader of Chicago’s Outfit, is said to have escaped through the woods.

The police could not easily prove a crime. The men gave innocent-sounding reasons for being there. Some were later convicted of for lying about why they met. But in 1960 a federal appeals court overturned those convictions in United States v. Bufalino. Simply being at a meeting is not, by itself, a crime.

Still, Apalachin changed minds. Before it, the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover had given organized crime little attention. Right after it, the FBI began its Top Hoodlum Program to gather , meaning organized information, on crime leaders. One observant officer had shown that the families were in contact across the country.

27.10

Valachi Names Cosa Nostra

Main ideaIn 1963 Joseph Valachi testified on television and gave the public the name Cosa Nostra and an inside picture of the families.

Joseph Valachi was a low-ranking member of a New York crime family. By the early 1960s he was in federal prison. He came to believe his own boss wanted him dead, and he agreed to talk to the government. It was a rare break in omertà by a sworn member in the United States.

In September and October 1963, the Senate’s Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations held public hearings. A is a smaller group inside a larger committee. Its chair was Senator John McClellan of Arkansas. The hearings opened on 25 September, and Valachi first appeared before the cameras on 27 September. He testified on five days. Millions watched a sworn member describe the secret society from the inside.

Valachi said members called their organization , Italian for ’our thing.’ He described an initiation ceremony, ranks such as boss and soldier, and the five New York families. He named many members. Most Americans had never heard the name before. Newspapers soon spread it everywhere.

Valachi’s testimony had limits. He was a low-level member, so he saw only part of the organization. Some of his dates and details did not match other records. Investigators and historians treat him as an important , a person who gives inside information to the authorities. His account still has to be checked against other evidence.

Tongs and Triads Compared

27.11

Tongs: Brotherhood and Mutual Aid

Main ideaChinese American tongs began mostly as mutual-aid brotherhoods; some also ran gambling, but most members were ordinary workers and merchants.

In 1882, Congress passed the . It barred most new Chinese workers from entering the United States. Chinese immigrants already here faced unfair laws and violence. Many could not turn to the courts or city hall for help. So they built their own associations, based on family names, home districts and brotherhood.

A is one kind of these associations. The word means a hall or meeting place. Tongs offered : members helped each other find jobs, housing and lawyers. They lent money and settled arguments. Other Chinese associations, based on home districts, helped send the dead home for burial in China. For many men far from their families, the tong was a second home.

Some tongs also ran gambling halls and other illegal businesses. Rival tongs sometimes fought what newspapers called tong wars. In New York in the early 1900s, the Hip Sing and On Leong tongs fought around Doyers Street in Chinatown. These fights were real. But they were not the whole story, and most tong members never took part in them.

Chicago shows both sides. In 1926 and 1927 the On Leong Merchants Association built a grand building at 2216 South Wentworth Avenue, a meeting place for the Chinatown community. In 1988 FBI agents and Chicago police raided it and found an illegal casino, and the government seized the building. A church bought it in 1993, and in 1994 it became the Pui Tak Center, a community center. It is a Chicago Landmark.

27.12

Triads: Legend and Record

Main ideaTriads descend from the Heaven and Earth Society, founded around 1761 as a mutual-aid brotherhood, though its legend places its start a century earlier.

Triad members tell a founding . In the 1670s, it says, loyal monks of the Shaolin monastery were betrayed by the emperor. Five survivors swore to overthrow the Qing dynasty and bring back the old Ming rulers. The story appears in the society’s own ritual books. It gave members a heroic past and a cause.

Records tell a different story. Qing government files, studied by the historians Dian Murray and Qin Baoqi in 1994, show that the , called the Tiandihui, began around 1761 or 1762 in Zhangpu county, Fujian. It started as a brotherhood for poor men who moved around looking for work and needed each other’s help. Its anti-Qing slogans grew later.

The government learned much about the society after a crackdown in Taiwan in 1786 and a rising there in 1787 and 1788. Arrested members described it under questioning. Over time the society split into many groups, known together as the Hongmen. Some became political. In 1904 in Honolulu, the revolutionary Sun Yat-sen joined the Chee Kung Tong, a Hongmen group, to win support from Chinese people abroad.

The English word came later, from the society’s symbol joining heaven, earth and humankind. Some later triad groups, especially in Hong Kong, became criminal. Scholars say modern triads descend from the old society but are not the same thing. As with the Mafia, historians separate the legend from the records, and old roots from later crimes.

The story

Stickers in the Night

On one June night in 2004, a few young people in Palermo quietly change what their city talks about.

It is the night of 28 June 2004 in Palermo, the capital of Sicily. A small group of young friends walks through the old city center. They carry small stickers with a black border, like the notices Italians post to announce a death. They press them onto walls, poles and doors. By morning, stickers are all over the center of the city.

Each sticker carries one sentence in Italian: 'Un intero popolo che paga il pizzo è un popolo senza dignità.' In English it means that a whole people who pays the pizzo is a people without dignity. The pizzo is protection money. For generations, many Palermo shopkeepers had paid it to the Mafia, quietly, because refusing felt too dangerous.

The stickers did not name anyone. They did not threaten anyone. They simply said out loud what many people knew and few people said. Soon the stickers were in the news. Shopkeepers, customers and students began to talk about the pizzo in public, and some shopkeepers later joined the young people's campaign.

This was not the first time Palermo had broken its silence. Twelve years earlier, in 1992, two judges named Giovanni Falcone and Paolo Borsellino had been murdered by the Mafia. Their work, and their deaths, had changed what many Sicilians believed was possible. This chapter tells how new laws, brave witnesses and ordinary people fought back.

A New Law for an Old Problem

28.1

The RICO Act

Main ideaRICO, signed on 15 October 1970, lets prosecutors charge people for running or taking part in a criminal organization, not just for single crimes.

For decades, American prosecutors faced a problem. They could charge a gunman for a shooting or a collector for a threat. But the bosses who gave the orders rarely touched a gun or wrote anything down. A family could lose members to prison and keep running. The organization itself stayed out of reach.

On 15 October 1970, President Richard Nixon signed the Organized Crime Control Act. Title IX of that law is the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act, known as . Its main author was G. Robert Blakey, a lawyer who worked for the Senate and later taught law.

RICO made it a crime to run or take part in an , meaning any group of people working together, through a . A pattern means at least two related crimes of certain listed kinds within ten years. The list includes murder, extortion, bribery and illegal gambling.

This mattered for three reasons. Prosecutors could now try a boss for the whole pattern of an organization’s crimes. They could put many members on trial together and show a jury the big picture. And the government could take away property and money gained through the enterprise, a step called .

RICO in the Courtroom

Main ideaFrom the 1980s, prosecutors used RICO to try whole crime families at once, and even the leaders of several families together.

At first RICO was used slowly. Prosecutors and judges were still learning how it worked. In the 1980s that changed. Federal prosecutors began building large cases that treated a crime family as the enterprise. Court-approved and hidden microphones gave them the bosses’ own words.

In New York, prosecutors went further. In what became known as the , begun in 1985, they charged leaders from several of the city’s families together. They argued that the Commission, the council of bosses, was itself a criminal enterprise. In 1986 a jury convicted the defendants, and long prison sentences followed.

RICO also has a civil side. People harmed by an enterprise can sue for , money to make up for harm, and the law allows three times the loss. The government has also used civil RICO to take control of labor unions that crime families had captured, and to appoint monitors to clean them up.

Critics say RICO is very broad. Because an enterprise can be almost any group, prosecutors have used it against businesses, street gangs and public officials, not only the Mafia. Defenders say the breadth is the point: organized crime changes shape, so the law has to fit many shapes. You can weigh both sides.

Main ideaAddiopizzo, begun by young people in 2004, turned refusing protection money into something shops and customers could do together.

The strategy changed the math. One shop refusing alone is a target. Many shops refusing together, with customers behind them and police ready to act, are much harder to punish. Addiopizzo also supports shopkeepers who report extortion, helping them through the police and court process.

Can an Insider Be Trusted?

Main ideaWitnesses from inside a crime group are powerful but risky, so courts need corroboration from other evidence.

Courts deal with this through : evidence from another source that supports what a witness says. Falcone checked Buscetta’s words against bank records and other witnesses. In Chicago, the recordings Frank Calabrese Jr. made let the jury hear a defendant’s own voice.

Governments also protect witnesses who tell the truth. The United States set up a federal program under the same 1970 Organized Crime Control Act that contained RICO. It can give a witness a new name and a new home. Italy built its own system to protect collaborators of justice.

The Real Cost

Main ideaOrganized crime takes money, safety and trust from ordinary people, and those who resist it have paid the highest price.

Films often show crime families as clever, stylish and loyal. The record tells a different story. Think of a family that runs a bakery. Every month, part of what they earn goes to the pizzo. That money comes out of wages, prices and savings. Customers pay more. Honest competitors can be pushed out.

The costs spread wider. When officials are bribed, people stop trusting police and courts. When such as roads and schools are rigged, they cost more and may be built worse. When witnesses are killed, like William Drury in Chicago in 1950, others learn to stay silent. Fear becomes a kind of tax too.

Whole communities have paid in another way: . Italian Americans and Chinese Americans were often blamed as groups for the crimes of a few. In 1891 that prejudice led to a mob killing in New Orleans. Most victims of the Black Hand and of the pizzo came from the same communities the criminals claimed to belong to.

The heaviest price was paid by those who resisted: Emanuele Notarbartolo, Rocco Chinnici, Giovanni Falcone, Francesca Morvillo, Paolo Borsellino, their escort officers and Father Puglisi. Their stories show what this unit has traced from 1876 to today. Silence protected the Mafia. Speaking up, together and with protection, is what weakened it.

1. In which decades did the Sicilian Mafia first grow in western Sicily?

Why: Records place the Mafia's start in the decades after Italian unification in 1860.

2. What did Franchetti and Sangiorgi each provide?

Why: Franchetti's 1877 study and Sangiorgi's 1898 to 1900 reports are early written records of the Mafia.

3. What is omertà?

Why: Omertà is the Mafia's rule never to help the police or courts.

4. Which event exposed a meeting of crime leaders from many states?

Why: Sergeant Croswell's roadblock at Apalachin identified 58 men from many states.

5. Which statement about the Kefauver Committee is true?

Why: The committee held hearings in about 14 cities from 1950 to 1951, Chicago among them.

6. Who gave the American public the name Cosa Nostra in 1963?

Why: Valachi told the McClellan subcommittee that members called the organization Cosa Nostra.

7. How did most Chinese American tongs begin?

Why: Tongs began mostly as mutual-aid groups when exclusion laws and prejudice shut Chinese immigrants out.

8. What does RICO let prosecutors do?

Why: RICO targets running or taking part in an enterprise through a pattern of racketeering activity.

9. Why did the Palermo magistrates work as a pool?

Why: Chinnici's idea was that shared files meant no single death could stop the work.

10. What did Tommaso Buscetta explain to Falcone?

Why: Buscetta described families, districts and a ruling Commission.

11. What happened on 30 January 1992?

Why: The Court of Cassation upheld the verdict on 30 January 1992.

12. What did the Addiopizzo stickers say, in English?

Why: That is the English meaning of the Italian sentence on the 2004 stickers.

13. In Operation Family Secrets, what did Frank Calabrese Jr. do?

Why: Calabrese Jr. recorded conversations with his father and later testified in court.

14. Which is the best example of corroboration?

Why: Corroboration is evidence from another source, such as a bank record, that supports a witness.

15. Which statement best fits the evidence in this unit about Italian Americans?

Why: The unit shows most Italian Americans were not involved, and many were victims or fought back.
