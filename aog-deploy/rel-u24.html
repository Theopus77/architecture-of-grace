Capstone: The Sources Speak

Capstone

For eleven units you have read other people's sacred texts: the Sabbath commandment in Exodus, the Sermon on the Mount, the opening sura of the Qur'an, Krishna's counsel to Arjuna, the Buddha's first teaching, the sayings of Confucius, the hymns of Guru Nanak, and stories told by elders of the Great Lakes and West Africa. In the last unit six students answered one question from six traditions. Now the question is yours. You will choose it, find the texts, and argue what three traditions would say.

This unit has two chapters. The first follows a student named Maya from a blank index card to a stack of quoted, cited and carefully read primary sources. You will learn to shrink a topic into a researchable question, to find reliable translations, to read with author, audience and purpose, to cite each scripture in its own system, and to avoid stereotype. The second chapter follows her to the front of the room, where she writes a thesis, builds layered evidence, compares without ranking, and answers questions from her classmates.

The capstone is not a test of what you believe. Students of every faith and of none do the same work on equal terms. It is a test of whether you can read what others hold sacred accurately and fairly, and report what you found in a way a thoughtful member of each tradition would recognize. By the end you will be able to pose a comparative question, gather and cite primary texts, and argue from them in writing and aloud.

The big question

How can you let three traditions' own texts answer a question you care about, fairly, accurately and without ranking them?

Section 1

Choosing a Question Worth Asking

47.1

What Makes a Question Researchable

Main ideaA good capstone question is open, comparable across traditions, and answerable from primary texts.

In the last unit six students stood at the board and answered one question with six traditions. The capstone reverses that. You choose the question, and the texts do the answering. A has three features. It is open, meaning it cannot be answered yes or no. It is comparable, meaning more than one tradition has actually spoken to it in its texts. And it is answerable from evidence, meaning you can point to a verse, a teaching or a practice rather than to your own opinion.

Test each feature against a real example. Is war ever right? is open, and nearly every tradition has texts about war, so it is comparable. But it is enormous. A student who asks it will drown in material. Compare it with a narrower version: What do the Torah, the Qur’an and the Dhammapada say about the treatment of an enemy? That question points to three specific texts and one specific issue. It is still open, still comparable, and now answerable in ten pages.

The six starting topics on the board, work, family, forgiveness, the natural world, wealth and war, were chosen because every tradition studied in this course has spoken to each of them. A is a subject area. A question is a sentence that ends with a question mark and demands evidence. Wealth is a topic. What does each tradition say a rich person owes to the poor, and why? is a question. Your first job is to move from the one to the other.

One more test matters in a public-school course. The question must be about what traditions teach, not about which one is right. Which religion has the best view of forgiveness? cannot be answered by any text, because the texts do not compete in a contest. How do three traditions explain why a person should forgive? can be answered, and answered well, because each tradition has given its reasons in writing. Keep the question descriptive and the whole project stays on solid ground.

47.2

Narrowing a Topic to a Question

Main ideaShrink a topic step by step until the question names one issue that three traditions have addressed in texts.

Start with wealth. It is a fine topic and a hopeless question. Ask what about wealth actually puzzles you. Maybe it is this: some people in every tradition are rich, and every tradition seems to say something uneasy about riches. That is a real observation, and it can be turned into a question in stages. Stage one: What do religions say about wealth? Still too big. Stage two: What do traditions say a wealthy person should do with money? Better. Stage three: What do the Hebrew prophets, the Gospels and the Qur’an say a wealthy person owes to the poor, and why? Now you have a question.

Notice what changed at each stage. The word religions became three named traditions. The vague say about became owes to the poor, one specific duty. And the question gained a why, which forces you to look for the reasons a text gives, not just its rule. A rule without its reason tells you little. Deuteronomy commands leaving the forgotten sheaf and the gleanings of the harvest for the stranger, the orphan and the widow, and explains that Israel was once enslaved in Egypt. The Qur’an makes , the required giving of a share of wealth, one of the Five Pillars and ties it to purifying what one keeps. The reasons are where the traditions differ most, and where your paper gets interesting.

Here is the same process on family. Stage one: What do religions teach about family? Stage two: What do traditions teach that children owe to parents? Stage three: How do the Ten Commandments, the Analects and the Guru Granth Sahib describe what a grown child owes to aging parents? The last version is enough to answer because it names an age, a duty and three texts. The Analects speak at length of , often translated as filial piety, the respect and care a child owes to parents. The commandment to honor father and mother sits in Exodus 20. Sikh teaching includes service to family as part of an honest householder’s life.

A warning about narrowing too far. What does the Qur’an say in sura 2, verse 177? is narrow, but it is not a question you can compare across traditions. Stop narrowing when the question names one issue, asks for reasons, and can still be put to three traditions. Write the final version at the top of your notes and check every source you gather against it. If a source does not help answer that exact question, it goes in a separate pile for later, not in the paper.

47.3

Choosing Three Traditions

Main ideaPick three traditions that have each spoken to your question in their texts, and choose for range rather than for convenience.

Three is the number for a reason. One tradition gives you a report, not a comparison. Two invite a contest, this one versus that one. Three make a conversation, in which you can see two traditions agree while a third answers differently, and then ask why. The course has covered the Hebrew Bible, the New Testament, Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, Daoism, Sikhism, Jainism, and the oral traditions of West Africa and Native North America. Any three that have addressed your question will work.

Choose for . If all three of your traditions are Abrahamic, meaning they trace their story through Abraham, as Judaism, Christianity and Islam do, you will find many shared ideas and fewer surprises. Many strong papers pair one Abrahamic tradition with one tradition from India, such as Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism or Sikhism, and one from East Asia or from the oral traditions. On the question of rest, for example, the Sabbath, the Buddhist monastic day and the Daoist idea of , acting without forcing, give three very different answers to why a person should stop.

Check that each tradition has texts on your question before you commit. Some pairings look promising and turn out thin. A question about a written code of law will find rich material in the Torah, the Qur’an and the Analects, but an Ojibwe oral tradition does not keep its teaching in that form, and forcing it into a law-code comparison would misrepresent it. A question about what humans owe the land runs the other way: Native traditions of the Great Lakes have much to say, and you would be wise to include one, using sources the community itself has chosen to make public.

Size is not the test. Christianity has about 2.4 billion adherents by common estimates and Jainism perhaps four to five million, but a Jain text on nonviolence may answer a question about war more sharply than any larger tradition. Nor should you choose a tradition because you already belong to it or already dislike it. The teacher will ask you to name your three traditions and one text from each before you go further, which is a check that you have looked and not guessed.

Finding Primary Texts

47.4

Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Sources

Main ideaA primary source is the tradition's own text; a secondary source explains it; a tertiary source summarizes the explanations.

Open three tabs. In the first is Psalm 23 in the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation. In the second is a rabbi’s essay on what the psalm meant to shepherds in ancient Judah. In the third is an encyclopedia entry titled Psalms. These are three kinds of source, and the capstone treats them differently. The psalm is a : the text itself, as the tradition preserves it. The essay is a : a scholar or adherent interpreting the text. The encyclopedia entry is a : a summary of what secondary sources generally say.

The rule for the capstone is that every claim about what a tradition teaches rests on at least one primary source, quoted and cited. Secondary sources help you understand the primary text and tell you how adherents read it, and you should use them. Tertiary sources are for orientation only. They can point you to the right chapter of the Qur’an or the right book of the Analects, but they are not evidence, and a paper built on encyclopedia entries will read like one.

Primary sources come in more forms than you might expect. For Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism, Confucianism and Daoism there are written scriptures with chapters and verses. For Jainism there are the Agamas and the writings of later teachers. For the oral traditions of West Africa and Native North America, the primary source may be a story, a song or a ceremony as told by an authorized speaker, and it may exist only in a recording or a transcription the community approved. A transcription of an elder telling a creation story is a primary source; a professor’s book about creation stories is secondary.

Be careful with one common trap. A modern religious leader’s sermon quoting a verse is a secondary source about the verse, even though it is a primary source about that leader’s views. If your question is what the Gospels say about wealth, cite the Gospel. If your question is how twenty-first-century preachers in Chicago read the Gospel on wealth, the sermon becomes your primary source. Which kind of source a document is depends on the question you are asking of it.

47.5

Public-Domain Translations and Their Limits

Main ideaPublic-domain translations can be quoted freely, but each carries the language and assumptions of its own century.

This course quotes a specific set of English translations word for word because they are in the , meaning their copyright has expired and anyone may reproduce them. The King James Bible of 1611; the Jewish Publication Society Tanakh of 1917; J. M. Rodwell’s Qur’an of 1861 and E. H. Palmer’s of 1880; Max Müller’s Sacred Books of the East, fifty volumes published from 1879 to 1910, which include the Dhammapada, the Upanishads and the Daodejing; James Legge’s Analects of 1861; Edwin Arnold’s verse Bhagavad Gita of 1885; and Max Arthur Macauliffe’s six-volume work on the Sikh Gurus of 1909. You may quote any of these exactly.

Each of them is also old, and age has costs. The King James Bible uses thee and thou and words whose meanings have shifted. Rodwell rearranged the Qur’an’s chapters into what he guessed was chronological order, which no Muslim edition does, so his chapter numbers can mislead. Müller and Legge were Victorian scholars who sometimes described the traditions they translated as inferior to Christianity, and those attitudes leak into footnotes and introductions. Arnold turned the Gita into English poetry and added words for the sake of rhythm. Macauliffe was more sympathetic, working closely with Sikh scholars, but his English is formal and his volumes mix translation with retelling.

Modern translations are usually clearer and often more accurate, and you should read them. Most are under copyright, so you may quote only short passages with citation, which is normal academic practice, and you must say whose translation you are using. Better still, read your key verse in two translations, one old and one modern, and note where they differ. A difference between translations is not a problem to hide. It is evidence about the text, and a paragraph explaining it shows a reader that you understand every translation is an .

For traditions without a single scripture, translation raises a different problem. A Yoruba praise poem for an orisha, or an Ojibwe teaching about the land, was translated by someone, and that someone may have been an outsider with limited understanding. Prefer versions produced by or with the community itself, and say who produced them. When you cannot find a reliable version, say that too. Honest limits are part of good research.

47.6

Where Reliable Texts Live

Main ideaFind primary texts through libraries, universities, museums and the traditions' own publishers, and judge every website by who made it and why.

A search engine will return a verse of the Qur’an in a tenth of a second, and it may be wrong. It may be mistranslated, misnumbered, cut short or posted by a group hostile to Islam. The capstone asks you to know where your text came from. Start with the school and public library. Librarians can pull complete printed editions of every scripture in this course, along with scholarly commentaries. The Chicago Public Library and suburban systems such as Skokie’s hold many of them, and the Newberry Library downtown keeps rare early editions that you can request to see.

Online, prefer sites that show their sources. University digital libraries post scanned copies of the Sacred Books of the East and the 1917 JPS Tanakh with page images, so you can check the printed text yourself. Major museums, including the Art Institute of Chicago and the Field Museum, publish catalog entries for manuscripts and ritual objects with expert descriptions. Religious communities publish their own scriptures: many Sikh gurdwaras link to searchable editions of the Guru Granth Sahib with the page number, called the , beside every line. A tradition’s own edition tells you how the community itself presents the text.

Judge a website the way you judge any source: author, audience, purpose. Who made it, and what do they know? For whom, and in what tone? Why does it exist? A site run by a seminary, a university department or a religious body has a clear author and purpose. An anonymous page with no dates and heavy advertising has neither. Be especially careful with sites that quote another tradition’s text only to attack it. A page of Qur’an verses assembled to prove Islam violent, or of Bible verses assembled to prove Christianity absurd, is not a source for what the tradition teaches. It is a source for what its author thinks, and it belongs in a different paper.

Keep a from the first day. For each text write the title, the translator, the year, the edition or website, the date you looked at it and the exact location of the passage. If it is a website, save the address and a copy of the page. You will need all of this for your citations, and you will be surprised how quickly you forget where a good quote came from. A log also lets a reader, or a teacher, retrace your steps, which is what makes research .

47.7

Author, Audience and Purpose Again

Main ideaBefore a verse can count as evidence, ask who is speaking in it, to whom, and for what purpose.

The first unit of this course gave you a toolkit: author, audience, purpose. The capstone is where it earns its keep. Take a verse that students often choose for questions about justice, Micah 6:8 in the Hebrew Bible. In the King James translation the prophet says that God has shown what is good: to do justly, love mercy and walk humbly. Before you quote it, run the toolkit. The , as the tradition presents it, is a prophet named Micah, speaking in the kingdom of Judah in the eighth century BCE. The audience is his own people, whom the surrounding chapters accuse of cheating the poor. The purpose is to correct them: sacrifices without justice, he argues, are not what God wants.

Now the verse means more, and it means something more specific. It is not a general saying about being nice. It is a prophet’s answer to people who thought ritual could replace fairness. A paper that quotes Micah 6:8 and then explains that setting shows the reader that the student understands the text. A paper that quotes it as a free-floating motto does not. The same three questions work on every text in this course. Who speaks in the Gita? Krishna, to Arjuna, on a battlefield, to a warrior who has lost his nerve. Who speaks in the Analects? Usually Confucius, to named students, answering their questions about conduct and government.

Author is the hardest of the three, because traditions and scholars often answer it differently, and you must report both. Jewish and Christian tradition long attributed the Torah to Moses; most academic scholars describe it as compiled from several sources over centuries. Muslims hold that the Qur’an is the word of God revealed to Muhammad; academic study describes its collection into a written text in the decades after his death in 632. Neither of these is your claim to settle. Write both, attribute each, and move on. The rubric rewards accuracy about what people hold, not a verdict.

Purpose is where students most often go wrong, because it requires reading around the verse. A single line can look like a command when it is part of a story, or like a promise when it is part of a warning. Before you copy any quotation into your notes, read at least the whole paragraph or the whole chapter it sits in, and write one sentence about what that larger passage is trying to do. That sentence will end up in your paper, and it is the difference between quoting a text and understanding it.

47.8

Genre Changes the Meaning

Main ideaLaw, story, poetry, prophecy, letter and teaching each ask to be read differently, and a paper should say which kind of text it is quoting.

Two texts about rest. The first is a law: the Sabbath commandment in Exodus 20, counted fourth in Jewish and most Protestant numbering and third by Catholics and Lutherans, which orders a household, including servants, animals and foreigners living among them, to stop work on the seventh day. The second is a verse of teaching: the first line of the Dhammapada, which says that everything we are results from what we have thought. Both bear on a question about work and rest. But one is a , given as binding law to a people, and the other is a , offered to anyone who will consider it. Quoting them as if they were the same kind of sentence would mislead your reader.

is the kind of writing a text is. The first unit listed the main genres in sacred texts: law, story, poetry, prophecy, letter and teaching. Each asks for a different reading. A law tells you what a community was obligated to do, and often why. A story shows a value in action, through characters, without stating a rule. Poetry, like the Psalms or many hymns in the Guru Granth Sahib, expresses feeling and praise, and reading it as law produces nonsense. Prophecy calls a people back to something. A letter, like Paul’s to the Corinthians, answers particular problems in a particular town. Teaching, like the Analects or the Dhammapada, offers wisdom for a student to practice.

The Sabbath law has a reason built in. Exodus grounds it in creation: God rested on the seventh day. The parallel version in Deuteronomy 5 grounds it in memory: you were slaves in Egypt, and now your servants must rest too. That is two reasons from two passages of law, and a careful paper notes both. The Dhammapada’s verse belongs to a different genre and a different logic. It does not order rest. It describes how the mind works, so that a reader will choose to train it. When Maya compares them in her capstone, she must say: one tradition frames rest as commanded, the other frames the quieting of the mind as chosen. Genre is not a detail. It is part of the answer.

In your notes, write the genre next to every quotation: law, story, poem, prophecy, letter, teaching, or, for oral traditions, story, song or ceremony. When you write the paper, name it in the sentence that introduces the quote. In the Torah’s law of the Sabbath, Exodus commands. In a verse of teaching, the Dhammapada observes. In a parable, Jesus tells of. Those small phrases tell your reader you know what kind of text you are holding.

Main ideaEach scripture has its own citation system, and a paper must use the right one so a reader can find the passage.

Main ideaUse each tradition's own names and terms, attribute every belief, and replace loaded words with accurate, neutral ones.

Handling Difficult Texts

Main ideaWhen a text speaks of war, punishment or unequal roles, quote it accurately, give its context, and report how adherents have read it, without attacking or excusing.

Every scripture in this course contains passages that trouble some readers. The Gita is spoken on a battlefield, where Krishna urges Arjuna to fight. The Hebrew Bible contains laws of war. The Qur’an contains verses about fighting. Early Christian letters give instructions about slaves and wives that many readers today find hard. A capstone question about war, family or forgiveness will lead you to such passages. The worst choices are to skip them, which misrepresents the text, or to use them as weapons, which misrepresents the tradition.

Treat a difficult text exactly like any other: text, context, reading. Take Qur’an 2:190, which tells believers to fight those who fight them but not to overstep limits. The , as Muslim tradition presents it, is the conflict between the early Muslim community at Medina and its opponents from Mecca. Many Muslim scholars have read the verse as permitting fighting in defense and within limits. Interpretations have varied across fourteen centuries and among legal schools. Your paper can say all of that in four sentences, each attributed.

Readings of the Gita show how much interpretation matters. Some Hindu commentators read the battle as real and teach that a warrior’s duty, his , required him to fight. Gandhi, who practiced nonviolence, read the battlefield as an , a story in which the fighting stands for the inner struggle between right and wrong. Both readings come from within the tradition. A paper on war that quotes Krishna’s command without these readings has told half the story.

Two cautions keep a paper fair. Do not apologize for a tradition by hiding a hard verse, and do not prosecute it by quoting that verse alone. Also keep the text separate from the history. What a scripture says and what some members did in its name are different questions, and each needs its own evidence and dates. Describe violence, persecution and conflict factually, with places and dates, and let the reader weigh them.

Building the Written Essay

Main ideaThe essay moves from the question to the thesis, gives each tradition its own section of layered evidence, then compares them and names the limits of the study.

The written essay has five parts, in order. The poses the question, explains in a sentence or two why it matters, and ends with the thesis. Next come three body sections, one for each tradition. Each section follows the five moves from earlier in this chapter: claim, text, context, reading and warrant. Then comes a comparison section that sets the three answers side by side. The conclusion states the answer again in fresh words and names what the study could not settle. Last is a works-cited list with full information for every translation and secondary source.

There are two ways to organize a comparison. In , you finish one tradition before starting the next, then compare at the end. It is easier to write, and it keeps each tradition’s answer whole. The Oral Presentation

Main ideaA strong presentation states the thesis early, reads one key text per tradition accurately with its citation, and treats sacred words and objects with care.

A listener cannot flip back a page. That one fact shapes every choice in an . State the question and thesis in the first minute and show the thesis on screen. Use , spoken markers that tell listeners where they are, such as: that was the Torah’s answer; now the Gita’s. Give each tradition one key passage, not five. Reading a sacred text aloud is a responsibility. Practice every name and term until you can say it without stumbling: Bhagavad Gita, Analects, Guru Granth Sahib, Tanakh. Main ideaAnswer audience questions by returning to the texts, naming diversity within the tradition, and saying plainly when you do not know.

Reading the Rubric

Main ideaThe rubric weights evidence most heavily, and it rewards fairness, attribution and diversity within traditions as much as it rewards organization.

A is a scoring guide that lists what a piece of work will be judged on and describes each level of quality. The capstone rubric has six criteria, or standards: thesis, evidence and context, diversity and fairness, organization, citation, and delivery. Each has a number of points. Reading the rubric before you write is not gaming the assignment. It is reading the assignment carefully, the same way you read a sacred text with author, audience and purpose in mind.

The weights tell you what matters most. Evidence and context carry the most points because the whole project rests on them. A paper with a brilliant thesis and thin evidence has asserted, not argued. Diversity and fairness come second. That criterion asks whether you named branches and schools, and used qualifiers. It also asks whether you attributed every belief and avoided loaded words. Many students lose points here not through disrespect but through the small word all.

Each criterion has , often four: exceeds, meets, approaching and beginning. The descriptions make the difference concrete. For evidence, meets might read: quotes a primary text for each tradition, with context. Exceeds might read: quotes a primary text for each tradition, gives its context and reports how adherents have read it, including a disagreement within the tradition. The gap between the two is the third layer of evidence from earlier in this chapter.

Use the rubric twice. Before drafting, turn each criterion into a question for your notes: do I have a primary text for each tradition? a branch named for each? What the Study of Religion Is For

Main ideaThe academic study of religion helps people in a diverse society read carefully, understand their neighbors and disagree fairly, without asking anyone to believe or disbelieve.

A recent national survey found about six in ten Americans identify as Christian, nearly three in ten with no religion, and the rest with other traditions. 1. (Unit 23) What does zakat require?

Why: Zakat, one of the Five Pillars, is a required yearly gift from savings, commonly 2.5 percent.

2. (Unit 22) What does Maimonides rank as the highest level of tzedakah?

Why: Maimonides placed enabling someone to no longer need help above every other form of giving.

3. (Unit 21) What makes Ifa an example of an oral tradition?

Why: Babalawos train for years to memorize and recite the verses linked to the 256 odu.

4. (Unit 20) Why do many scholars hesitate to call Confucianism simply a religion or simply a philosophy?

Why: It has religious features and lacks others, and the Chinese word for religion, zongjiao, took on that meaning only in the late 1800s.

5. (Unit 19) According to Buddhist tradition, what four sights led Siddhartha to leave the palace?

Why: The traditional story says Siddhartha saw age, sickness, death and a renouncer, which set him on his search.

Write it

Choose a question about work, family, forgiveness, the natural world, wealth or war. Cite one primary text from each of three traditions studied in this course. Argue what each tradition would say and why, using the text, its context and how adherents read it, and end with a comparison that names both a parallel and a contrast.

  • Name the text, its chapter and verse or its tradition's own citation system, and the translation every time you quote.
  • Attribute every belief: according to the Torah, many Buddhists hold, in Sikh teaching.
  • Name the branch or school you mean, and report at least one disagreement within a tradition.
  • Compare rather than judge: show where the answers meet and where they part, without ranking.
  • Use the unit's terms: thesis, warrant, genre, primary source, interpretive tradition.
