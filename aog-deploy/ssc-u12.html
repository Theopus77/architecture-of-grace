Faiths, Empires and Exchange

World History

In 1324 a king from West Africa rode into Cairo with so much gold that prices fell and stayed down for years. In 1348 a cart rolled into an English village and, within a season, half its people were dead of plague. Both events happened because the world between 500 and 1500 was connected by roads, rivers, oceans and deserts that people had learned to cross. Goods moved along those routes. So did faiths, numbers, books and germs.

This unit follows those routes. You will start in Constantinople, the city that kept Rome alive for a thousand years, and in Mecca and Baghdad, where a new religion built an empire and a library. You will cross the Sahara by camel to the gold kingdoms of Ghana and Mali, and sail the monsoon winds to the stone cities of Zimbabwe and the Swahili coast. Then you will turn to the castles and cathedrals of Europe, the horsemen of Genghis Khan, the samurai of Japan, and the pyramids, floating farms and mountain roads of the Maya, Aztec and Inca.

Along the way you will practice reading history the way historians do: from a traveler's report, a law code, a tribute list, a coin dug out of a wall. By the end you will be able to explain how faith, law and trade held big societies together, what happened when disaster struck, and why the world of 1450 was ready to collide.

The big question

Between 500 and 1500, how did faith, law and trade connect the world's great societies, and who paid the price of that connection?

The story

The King Who Broke the Price of Gold

In the summer of 1324 a ruler from West Africa rode into Cairo with so much gold that the city talked about him for years.

The guards on the walls of Cairo saw the dust first. Then the line of camels came into view, and it did not end. Riders in fine cloth, servants on foot, soldiers with spears, and camel after camel loaded with bags. Inside the bags was gold dust. At the center of it all rode Mansa Musa, the ruler of the Empire of Mali, a land far to the southwest across the Sahara Desert. He was on his way to Mecca, the holy city of Islam, to make the pilgrimage every Muslim hopes to make once in life.

Cairo in 1324 was one of the biggest cities in the world, and its people had seen many rich visitors. They had never seen this. Mansa Musa gave gold to the officials who greeted him. He gave gold to the poor at the mosques. His followers bought silk, horses and slaves, and paid whatever price the sellers named. Gold poured into the markets faster than anyone could count it.

Here is what the merchants of Cairo learned the hard way. When there is suddenly a great deal of something, each piece of it is worth less. So much gold entered Cairo that summer that the price of gold fell. A writer named al-Umari, who talked to people in Cairo a few years later, reported that the value of gold there stayed low for more than ten years. One traveler's generosity had bent the economy of a great city.

Why does this story matter? Because it shows a world we do not always picture. In the year 1324, a Muslim king from West Africa could travel thousands of miles through Muslim lands, speaking to scholars in Arabic, using the same calendar and the same prayers, welcomed as a fellow believer. The desert that looks empty on a map was a highway. Gold, salt, books and ideas moved across it. This chapter follows that road, from the marble churches of Constantinople to the mud-brick libraries of Timbuktu.

Talk about itMansa Musa gave away gold to be generous and to show his power. What did Cairo gain from his visit, and what did it lose?
Section 1

The Empire That Did Not Fall

26.1

A City Between Two Seas

Main ideaWhen Rome's western half collapsed, its eastern half lived on for a thousand years from Constantinople, a city built on a narrow strait.

In the year 330 the Roman emperor Constantine chose a new capital. He picked an old Greek town on a narrow strip of water where Europe almost touches Asia. He renamed it after himself: Constantinople. The place was easy to defend and hard to starve. Water surrounded it on three sides, and thick walls closed the fourth. Ships from the Black Sea and the Mediterranean had to pass right beside it, and each one paid a tax.

In 476 the last Roman emperor in the West lost his throne, and the western empire broke into small kingdoms. The eastern half did not break. Its people still called themselves Romans, but historians call their state the Empire, after Byzantium, the old name of the city. It lasted from 330 until 1453, more than eleven hundred years. Its people spoke Greek, kept Roman law and followed Christianity.

Constantinople was the largest city in Europe for most of that time, with perhaps several hundred thousand people. Its markets sold silk from China, furs from Russia, spices from India and grain from Egypt. Its gold coin, the solidus, was so trusted that merchants used it from Britain to India. A traveler in the year 1000 who wanted to see the richest city in the Christian world went to Constantinople, not Rome or Paris.

How do we know? The walls still stand in the city now called Istanbul, in Turkey. Byzantine writers left histories, law books and church records. Coins turn up in graves as far away as Sweden. And the city’s enemies wrote about it too, from Arab armies who failed to take it to Vikings who came to serve as the emperor’s guards.

26.2

Justinian Writes Down the Law

Main ideaEmperor Justinian collected a thousand years of Roman law into one clear code, and that code still shapes laws in much of the world.

Justinian became emperor in 527. He was born a peasant’s son in the Balkans and rose through his uncle, who was a soldier and then emperor. Justinian wanted to make the empire great again. He sent armies to take back Italy and North Africa. He built churches and fortresses. But his most lasting project was quieter. He ordered a team of lawyers to fix the law.

The problem was a mess. Rome had made laws for a thousand years. Some contradicted others. Some were lost, and some lay buried in thousands of pages of old court opinions. A judge could not know which rule applied. Justinian’s lawyers, led by a scholar named Tribonian, read through it all. Between 529 and 534 they produced a set of books that we call the , or the Body of Civil Law.

The Code did three things. It threw out laws that no longer made sense. It sorted the rest by topic, so a judge could find the rule on, say, selling a house. And it added a short textbook for law students that began with a definition of justice: giving each person what is due to them. The books were written in Latin, the old language of Rome, even though most people in the empire now spoke Greek.

Centuries later, scholars in Italy rediscovered the Code and taught it in the first universities. Its ideas spread through France, Spain, Germany and their colonies. Today the legal systems of most of Europe and Latin America, and of the state of Louisiana, still trace back to Justinian’s books. A person reading a contract in Mexico City or Paris is using rules a Byzantine emperor had written down.

26.3

A Dome That Seemed to Float

Main ideaHagia Sophia showed the wealth and faith of Byzantium, and the split between its Greek church and Rome's Latin church still divides Christians today.

In January 532 a riot nearly ended Justinian’s reign. Crowds in Constantinople burned half the city, including the main church. The emperor packed to flee. His wife, the empress Theodora, told him she would rather die as a ruler than run. Justinian stayed, his soldiers crushed the riot, and he decided to rebuild the church bigger than anything on earth.

The new church was Hagia Sophia, which means Holy Wisdom in Greek. Two mathematicians designed it. Ten thousand workers finished it in under six years, in 537. Its great dome spans more than a hundred feet and rises about 180 feet above the floor. Forty windows circle the base of the dome, so sunlight pours in and the dome appears to hang in the air. Gold mosaics, tiny glass tiles, covered the walls. A writer of the time said visitors felt the building was not made by human hands.

Hagia Sophia was the center of the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Christianity of the Greek-speaking world. Its leader was the patriarch of Constantinople. Far to the west, the pope in Rome led the Latin-speaking church. The two churches prayed in different languages, argued about the wording of beliefs and about who was in charge. In 1054 their leaders formally split. That break is called the , and the Orthodox and Catholic churches remain separate to this day.

The Byzantines sent missionaries north. Two brothers, Cyril and Methodius, made an alphabet for the Slavic peoples in the 800s. Russia, Serbia, Bulgaria and Greece became Orthodox and still are. In 1453 the Ottoman Turks captured Constantinople and turned Hagia Sophia into a mosque. Today the building still stands, and Orthodox churches in Chicago neighborhoods still use domes and icons that look back to it.

Section 2

The Rise of Islam

26.4

A Merchant in Mecca

Main ideaIslam began in the trading city of Mecca in the early 600s, when Muhammad taught that there is one God and that all believers are equal before him.

Arabia in the year 600 was mostly desert. Its people lived in tribes, raising camels and sheep or trading along the caravan roads. Mecca was a trading town near the Red Sea coast. It also held a shrine, the Kaaba, that drew pilgrims from all over Arabia. They came to honor many gods and to buy and sell. Feuds between tribes were common, and a person without a strong family had little protection.

Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570. His parents died when he was young, and an uncle raised him. He became a merchant, known as honest. Around 610, when he was about forty, he began to report visions in which an angel gave him messages from God. He taught that there is only one God, the same God worshiped by Jews and Christians, and that people should care for the poor, the orphan and the widow. His followers memorized his messages, which were later gathered into the , the holy book of Islam.

The leaders of Mecca did not like this teaching. It insulted their gods and threatened their pilgrim trade. They harassed Muhammad’s followers. In 622 he and his followers left for the town of Yathrib, later called Medina. This journey is the , and Muslims count their calendar from that year. In Medina, Muhammad became a leader of the community and settled disputes among its tribes.

By the time Muhammad died in 632, most of Arabia had accepted Islam. The word Islam means submission to God, and a is one who submits. The faith asked five main duties of every believer: to declare that there is one God and Muhammad is his messenger, to pray five times a day, to give to the poor, to fast during the month of Ramadan, and, if able, to make the pilgrimage to Mecca once in life. Every duty was the same for a rich man and a poor one.

26.5

Caliphs and a Growing Empire

Main ideaWithin a century of Muhammad's death, Muslim rulers called caliphs governed lands from Spain to India, ruling many peoples who kept their own faiths.

When Muhammad died, his followers chose a leader called a , meaning successor. The first four caliphs ruled from Medina. Their armies moved fast. The Byzantine and Persian empires had worn each other out in long wars, and their people were heavily taxed. Arab armies took Syria, Egypt and Persia within twenty years. Many local people did not fight hard for the old rulers.

A family called the Umayyads took the caliphate in 661 and moved the capital to Damascus in Syria. Under them the empire reached Spain in the west and the Indus River in the east by about 750. Then a rival family, the Abbasids, overthrew them. The Abbasids built a new capital in 762 on the Tigris River: Baghdad. Within a century it was one of the largest cities in the world, a round city of palaces, markets and canals.

Ruling so many peoples took more than swords. Christians and Jews were called People of the Book, because they too had scriptures. They could keep their religion and their churches if they paid a special tax. Many did, and for centuries most people in Egypt and Syria remained Christian. Over time many converted, partly because Muslims paid less tax and could rise in government.

The empire was never fully united. Spain broke away under its own Umayyad ruler. Later, other rulers in Egypt and North Africa claimed the title too. Even so, from Cordoba in Spain to Samarkand in Central Asia, people shared Arabic as the language of learning and law, the same coins, and the same duty to travel to Mecca. That shared world is what let a king from Mali cross it in 1324.

26.6

The House of Wisdom

Main ideaScholars in Baghdad gathered the knowledge of Greece, Persia and India, and added algebra, new astronomy and medicine that Europe later learned from them.

In the early 800s the caliph al-Ma’mun paid scholars in Baghdad to collect books. Greek, Persian and Indian works were translated into Arabic. Historians call this effort the . It was part library, part translation office, part research center. Christian, Jewish and Muslim scholars worked there together. Paper, a Chinese invention, had recently reached the Muslim world, and it made books far cheaper than parchment.

One scholar, Muhammad al-Khwarizmi, wrote a book around 820 on solving problems with unknown numbers. Its title used the Arabic word al-jabr, and from that we get the word . He also explained the number system from India, which used ten symbols and a zero. We still call them Arabic numerals because Europe learned them from Arabic books. The word algorithm, a set of steps for solving a problem, comes from his name.

Astronomers built observatories and measured the positions of stars more carefully than the Greeks had. They improved the , a brass instrument that finds the time and one’s position from the stars. Muslims needed it to know the hour of prayer and the direction of Mecca, and sailors needed it at sea. Many star names we still use, like Aldebaran and Rigel, are Arabic.

A Persian physician, Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, wrote a huge medical textbook around 1025. Doctors in Europe used it for five hundred years. Hospitals in Baghdad and Cairo treated patients of any religion. When European scholars in the 1100s wanted Aristotle, Euclid or the latest medicine, they went to Spain to translate it from Arabic. Much of ancient Greek science survived because Baghdad had saved it.

Gold and Salt Across the Sahara

26.7

The Ship of the Desert

Main ideaThe camel turned the Sahara from a wall into a road, and along that road salt went south while gold went north.

The Sahara is the largest hot desert on earth, about the size of the United States. Crossing it from north to south meant weeks of sand, rock and heat with water only at scattered oases. Horses and donkeys died. Then, around the 300s CE, herders in North Africa began using the , brought from Arabia. A camel can carry several hundred pounds and go a week or more without drinking. It can close its nostrils against sand. Traders called it the ship of the desert.

A trading trip was a : dozens or hundreds of camels, guides who knew the wells, and armed guards. The main route ran from Sijilmasa in Morocco south to the Niger River, roughly a thousand miles, taking about two months. Caravans traveled by night in the hottest season and steered by the stars. A lost caravan was a dead one, and the desert is scattered with old bones to prove it.

The trade worked because each side had what the other lacked. In the middle of the desert, at a place called Taghaza, workers cut slabs of salt from the ground like stone. The lands south of the desert had almost no salt, and people and animals cannot live without it. South of the desert, in the forests along rivers, people dug gold from the earth. North Africa and Europe wanted that gold for coins. Salt went south, gold went north.

Traders also carried copper, cloth, horses and books south, and brought ivory, kola nuts and enslaved people north. Along with the goods came Islam. Merchants from North Africa were Muslims, and they settled in the trading towns south of the desert. Over centuries, kings and city people there adopted the faith, learned Arabic and built mosques. Village farmers often kept older beliefs much longer.

26.8

Ghana, the Land of Gold

Main ideaThe kingdom of Ghana grew rich by taxing the salt and gold that crossed its land, and we know about it mainly from Arab writers.

The first great kingdom on the southern edge of the Sahara was Ghana. It lay in what is now Mali and Mauritania, far northwest of the modern country named Ghana, which took the old name in 1957. Ghana’s people, the Soninke, controlled the strip of land between the salt mines to the north and the gold fields to the south. Every trader had to pass through, and every load paid a tax coming in and going out.

Almost everything we know about Ghana comes from writers in the Muslim world who never saw it. The best is al-Bakri, a scholar in Spain, who in 1068 wrote down what traders told him. He described the capital as two towns six miles apart: one for Muslim merchants with a dozen mosques, and one for the king, with his palace and his own religion’s shrines. The king sat in a pavilion with gold-decked horses and dogs in gold collars. Al-Bakri said the king could put a huge army into the field.

The king kept control of gold with a clever rule. All nuggets found in the kingdom belonged to him. People could keep only gold dust. This kept nuggets from flooding the market and made gold stay valuable. Al-Bakri also reported that a load of salt paid one gold coin entering and two leaving. This is a tax on trade, and it is the source of Ghana’s wealth.

Ghana had no written records of its own that survive, so historians are careful. Al-Bakri’s numbers came from travelers and may be stretched. But archaeologists have dug a large stone town at a site called Koumbi Saleh in Mauritania that matches his description of the merchant town. By the 1200s Ghana had weakened, hurt by attacks from the north and by new gold fields opening farther south, outside its control.

26.9

Mali, Mansa Musa and Timbuktu

Main ideaThe Empire of Mali replaced Ghana, and under Mansa Musa its wealth and its city of Timbuktu became famous across Africa and Europe.

Around 1235 a leader named Sundiata united the Mande people and defeated a rival king in battle. He founded the Empire of , which grew to include the old lands of Ghana and the new gold fields to the south. Storytellers called griots have passed down Sundiata’s story by memory for nearly eight hundred years. It is one of the great epics of Africa, and it is also a source historians use, carefully, alongside written records.

Mali’s most famous ruler, Mansa Musa, took the throne around 1312. means ruler or king. His pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 was the event you read about at the start of this chapter. On his way home he brought back scholars, architects and books. He had mosques built in Timbuktu and Gao. Fifty years later a mapmaker in Spain drew the Catalan Atlas and showed Mansa Musa sitting on a throne in the middle of Africa, holding a gold nugget. Europe now knew where the gold came from.

Timbuktu sat where the Niger River came closest to the desert. Salt caravans from the north and boats of grain and gold from the south met there. By the 1400s it had a university based at the Sankore mosque, and scholars wrote and copied books on law, religion, math and astronomy. Families in Timbuktu still keep thousands of these old manuscripts today. They prove that West Africa had a written learned tradition long before Europeans arrived.

The traveler Ibn Battuta visited Mali in 1352 and 1353. He praised the safety of its roads and the fairness of its judges. He also complained about customs he found strange and about the small gifts he received. His account is valuable because he was an outsider who saw things the empire’s own people would not bother to write down. By the 1400s Mali weakened, and the empire of Songhai took over Timbuktu.

Stone Cities of the South and East

26.10

Great Zimbabwe

Main ideaIn southern Africa, the builders of Great Zimbabwe raised huge stone walls and traded gold to the coast, and their ruins later exposed a racist lie.

On a high plateau in southern Africa stand the stone walls of . The word zimbabwe means houses of stone in the Shona language. The largest structure, the Great Enclosure, has curved walls up to about 36 feet high and as much as 16 feet thick, built from cut granite blocks laid without mortar. Inside stands a solid stone tower shaped like a cone. Between about 1100 and 1450, this was the capital of a kingdom of the Shona people.

The rulers of Great Zimbabwe raised cattle and controlled gold mines on the plateau. Gold went east, to the coast, and goods came back. Archaeologists digging inside the walls have found glass beads from India, pieces of Persian pottery and Chinese porcelain. A coin from the Swahili city of Kilwa turned up there too. A city with no writing left records anyway: the things it bought tell us who it traded with.

The Swahili Coast

Main ideaAlong Africa's east coast, city-states like Kilwa grew rich on Indian Ocean trade and blended African, Arab and Persian ways into Swahili culture.

The old trading world of the Indian Ocean would never be the same.

Life in Feudal Europe

27.1

Lords, Knights and Serfs

Main ideaAfter Rome fell, Europe was held together by feudalism: lords gave land to knights in exchange for military service, and serfs worked the land for both.

After the western Roman Empire broke up in the 400s, there was no emperor to build roads, pay soldiers or keep order. Vikings raided from the north, Magyars from the east, and Muslim fleets from the south. Kings were weak and far away. People needed protection close by. Out of that need grew a system historians call .

It worked like a ladder of promises. A king granted a large piece of land to a powerful noble, a . In return the lord swore loyalty and promised to bring soldiers when the king called. The lord then granted smaller pieces to , armored fighters on horseback, who swore the same to him. The land a knight or noble held was his fief. Everyone on the ladder owed something to someone above.

At the bottom were the farmers. Most were . A serf was not a slave and could not be sold, but he could not leave the lord’s land, and his children were serfs too. He farmed strips in the lord’s fields, gave the lord part of his crop and worked the lord’s own land several days a week. In return the lord let him farm and promised to protect him. The lord’s house, the church and the farmers’ huts together made up a , and most manors produced nearly everything they needed.

How do we know? The Domesday Book, a survey that King William of England ordered in 1086, lists thousands of manors, their lords, their plows, their mills and their serfs by number. Court records show serfs fined for missing work or for marrying without permission. Castles, at first wooden towers on earth mounds and later great stone fortresses, still dot the land where lords once ruled. Feudal Europe left a lot of paper and a lot of stone.

The Crusades

Main ideaFor two centuries European knights fought wars to take Jerusalem from Muslim rule; they failed in the end, but the wars changed trade and learning in Europe.

Jerusalem is holy to Jews, Christians and Muslims. Muslim rulers had held it since 638, and for centuries Christian pilgrims visited without much trouble. In the 1000s a new group, the Seljuk Turks, took over the region and defeated the Byzantine army. The Byzantine emperor asked the pope for help. In 1095 Pope Urban II gave a speech in France calling on knights to march east, free the holy city and win forgiveness for their sins. The crowd shouted that God willed it.

Tens of thousands went. The armies of the First captured Jerusalem in 1099 and massacred many of its Muslim and Jewish people. The crusaders set up small kingdoms along the coast. In 1187 the Muslim leader Saladin retook Jerusalem. He spared most of the city’s people, and even his enemies wrote of his fairness. More crusades followed for a century, but none won Jerusalem back for long. In 1204 one crusader army never reached the Holy Land at all; it attacked and looted Constantinople, a Christian city, instead.

The crusaders also attacked at home. On their way east in 1096, some bands murdered Jews in cities along the Rhine River. That pattern of violence against Jewish communities returned during the plague and for centuries after. Muslims, Jews and Byzantine Christians all wrote accounts of the crusades, and their view was very different from the knights’ songs. Historians read all of them.

The crusades failed at their goal, but they had effects no one planned. Italian cities like Venice and Genoa grew rich shipping soldiers and supplies and bringing back spices, silk and sugar. Europeans tasted goods from the East and wanted more. Scholars in contact with the Muslim world brought back Arabic books on math, medicine and Greek philosophy. Kings who taxed for the wars grew stronger, and many nobles who went east never came home.

The Black Death

Main ideaThe plague of 1347 to 1351 killed roughly a third or more of Europe's people, and the labor shortage it left behind helped break feudalism.

The began in Central Asia and traveled the trade routes the Mongols had opened. In 1347 ships from the Black Sea brought it to Sicily. By 1348 it was in Italy, France, Spain and England. By 1351 it had reached Russia and Scandinavia. Trade had connected the world, and the connection carried death as well as silk.

The disease is caused by a bacterium carried by fleas that live on rats. Fleas jumped from dying rats to people. A second form spread directly from person to person by coughing. No one in 1348 knew any of this. Scientists confirmed the bacterium in the 1890s, and in the 2000s they found its DNA in the teeth of plague victims dug from medieval cemeteries. That is how we know the killer’s name today.

The count is rough, because medieval records are patchy, but historians estimate that between a third and half of Europe’s people died within about four years. Some villages lost everyone. In Florence, the writer Giovanni Boccaccio described dead bodies piled in the streets and neighbors who abandoned each other. Records of English manors show fields left empty and rents unpaid. Some people turned to extreme religion, whipping themselves in the streets. Others blamed Jewish communities and killed thousands in Germany and elsewhere.

Then came a surprise. With so few workers left, a serf who survived could demand wages, or walk to a lord who would pay them. In 1351 the English parliament passed a law ordering pay frozen at the old rates. It did not work. When the government tried to raise taxes in 1381, English peasants rose in revolt and marched into London. The revolt was crushed, but serfdom kept fading. The Great Charter of 1215

Main ideaMagna Carta forced an English king to admit that even he was under the law, an idea that later shaped the United States Constitution.

King John of England was losing. He had lost his lands in France in war, and to pay for more war he squeezed his barons, the great lords, for money. By 1215 the barons had had enough. They raised an army and took London.

In June 1215, in a meadow beside the Thames River called Runnymede, John met the barons and put his seal on a long document in Latin. We call it , the Great Charter. Most of its sixty-some clauses were about the barons’ own complaints: inheritance fees, forests, fish traps in the river. But a few clauses said something bigger. The king could not take new taxes without the consent of his great council. And no free man could be jailed, stripped of property or exiled except by the judgment of his peers or the law of the land.

John never meant to keep it. Within months the pope declared the charter void and war began again. But John died in 1216, and his young son’s advisers reissued the charter to win support. The great council grew into Parliament, which held the power over taxes.

Five hundred years later, English colonists in America knew Magna Carta well. When they said no taxation without representation, they were pointing to it. When the United States Constitution promised that no one could lose life, liberty or property without due process of law, it echoed the clause sealed at Runnymede. Main ideaUnder Mongol rule the roads across Asia became safe enough for merchants and travelers like Marco Polo, and ideas, goods and disease all moved faster.

In 1271 a seventeen-year-old from Venice named Marco Polo set out with his father and uncle, who were merchants. His book described things Europeans found hard to believe: a city with canals and twelve thousand bridges, coal burned as fuel, paper money printed by the government, and a postal system faster than anything in Europe. Some readers called him a liar. Some historians still argue about which parts he saw himself and which he heard from others. And the peace did not last. The Mongol khanates fought each other, China threw out its Mongol rulers in 1368, and the roads grew dangerous again. Japan Under the Shoguns

Main ideaIn Japan a military ruler called the shogun held real power while the emperor reigned in name, and samurai warriors served their lords much as knights did in Europe.

Japan is a chain of mountainous islands off the coast of Asia. It borrowed writing, Buddhism and government ideas from China and Korea, but it was never conquered by them. For centuries an emperor in the city of Kyoto stood at the top of Japanese society, seen as descended from the sun goddess. But by the 1100s the emperor had little real power. Great families with private armies fought each other for control.

In 1185 a warrior named Minamoto Yoritomo won that war. In 1192 the emperor gave him the title , which means great general. The emperor kept his throne and his ceremonies, but the shogun ran the country from his own headquarters. For nearly seven hundred years, until 1868, shoguns ruled Japan in the emperor’s name. Under the shogun, lords called held land and kept armies of warriors called .

A samurai was much like a European knight. He fought on horseback with bow and sword, served a lord, and received land or rice in return. He followed a code of loyalty and honor that later got the name bushido, the way of the warrior. A samurai who failed his lord was expected to take his own life. Samurai families also produced poets, painters and Buddhist thinkers, and the tea ceremony grew up in their world.

The shoguns faced their greatest test from the Mongols. Kublai Khan sent fleets to invade Japan in 1274 and again in 1281, with tens of thousands of soldiers. Both times the samurai fought on the beaches, and both times huge storms wrecked the Mongol ships. The Japanese called those storms kamikaze, divine winds. Japan stayed independent, but the shogun could not reward his samurai with new land, because there was none to give, and his rule weakened.

The Maya

Main ideaIn the rain forests of Central America the Maya built stone cities, invented a full writing system and tracked the sky with a calendar more exact than Europe's.

In what is now Guatemala, Belize, southern Mexico and Honduras, the built cities in the jungle. Their greatest period ran from about 250 to 900 CE. Cities such as Tikal and Copan held tens of thousands of people, with stone pyramids taller than any building in Europe at the time, plazas, palaces and ball courts. Each city had its own king. They traded with each other and fought each other, like the city-states of Greece.

The Maya are the only people of the ancient Americas known to have had a full writing system. Their , picture-like signs, could spell any word in their language. They carved them on stone monuments and wrote them in folding books of bark paper. For a long time no one could read them. Scholars cracked the code mainly in the second half of the 1900s. Now we can read the names of Maya kings and the dates of their battles.

Maya astronomers watched the sky for generations and wrote down what they saw. They tracked the planet Venus so closely that their tables were off by only a day or two in a century. They used a symbol for zero and a place-value number system, centuries before Europe learned one. They kept several calendars at once, including one that counted every day from a starting point more than three thousand years before their time.

Around 900 the great cities of the south were abandoned. Historians still argue why. Evidence points to long droughts, recorded in lake mud and cave stones, along with overuse of the forest, war between cities and kings who lost their people’s trust. But the Maya did not vanish. Cities in the north, like Chichen Itza, thrived for centuries more. Today about six million Maya people live in Central America and speak Maya languages.

Think it through
Cracking a Code

For a century, scholars stared at Maya carvings and could not read them. Some said the glyphs were only pictures of gods. Then a young scholar noticed that some signs seemed to stand for sounds, not ideas. Working with Maya languages still spoken today, researchers matched signs to syllables. Suddenly the stones began to tell stories: this king was born, that city was conquered.

Why did it matter that Maya languages are still spoken? What would be lost if a writing system's language had died?

The Aztec and Their Island City

Main ideaThe Aztec built Tenochtitlan on a lake and ruled central Mexico through conquest and tribute, feeding a city of about two hundred thousand from floating farms.

According to their own history, the Mexica, whom we call the , were wanderers who arrived late in the Valley of Mexico. The good land was taken. In 1325 they settled on a swampy island in Lake Texcoco, where, their story said, they saw an eagle on a cactus. That image is on the flag of Mexico today. On that island they built , which by 1500 was one of the largest cities in the world, with about two hundred thousand people.

Feeding a city on a lake took engineering. The Aztec built , raised fields made by piling mud and plants in the shallow water and fencing them with willow trees. Farmers could take several harvests of corn, beans, squash and chilies from a chinampa every year. Stone causeways with bridges linked the island to the shore, and an aqueduct carried fresh water from springs. Canoes moved through canals, and a market in the district of Tlatelolco drew tens of thousands of people a day.

In 1428 the Aztec joined two neighboring cities in an alliance and began conquering the valley and far beyond. Conquered peoples paid : corn, cotton cloth, feathers, cacao beans, gold and captives. Records list every town and exactly what it owed. Aztec religion held that the gods needed human blood to keep the sun moving, and captives from war were sacrificed at the great temple. Neighboring peoples hated Aztec rule, and that hatred mattered later.

Spanish soldiers who saw Tenochtitlan in 1519 wrote that it looked like a dream, with towers rising from the water. Within two years they had destroyed it, with the help of smallpox and tens of thousands of native allies who wanted to end Aztec rule. Mexico City stands on the site today. Archaeologists digging under its streets have found the great temple, and the Aztec tribute lists survive because the Spanish copied them to learn what they could collect.

The Inca Road

Main ideaIn the Andes the Inca ruled the largest empire in the Americas with roads, storehouses and knotted strings, but no wheels, no horses and no writing.

The Andes Mountains run down the west side of South America, and their peaks stand more than twenty thousand feet high. Farming there means building , flat steps cut into the mountainsides and walled with stone. Andean peoples grew potatoes and corn on them and raised llamas and alpacas for wool and carrying loads. Around 1438 a ruler of a small mountain kingdom at Cusco, in Peru, began conquering his neighbors. His people were the .

In less than a century the Inca ruled from what is now Colombia to Chile, about 2,500 miles along the mountains, with perhaps ten million people. They held it together with roads. Two main highways, one along the coast and one through the mountains, plus side roads, added up to roughly 25,000 miles. Rope bridges swung across gorges. Runners in relays carried messages along the roads at a pace of a hundred miles or more a day. Storehouses along the way held food, cloth and weapons for armies and for villages in bad years.

The Inca had no writing. Instead officials kept records on a , a set of colored strings with knots. The position and type of knot recorded numbers: how many people in a village, how many llamas, how much corn in a storehouse. Every family owed the state labor, called mita, for building roads, terraces and cities. In return the state fed people in famine. Conquered peoples were sometimes moved far from home to break up resistance.

Inca builders cut stones so exactly that they fit without mortar, and the walls of Cusco still stand under Spanish churches built on top of them. Machu Picchu, a royal estate high on a ridge, was never found by the Spanish and survives nearly whole. The Inca did all this without iron tools, wheeled carts or horses. When Spanish soldiers arrived in 1532, a civil war and smallpox had already weakened the empire, and the Inca rulers fell within a few years.

27.12

The World in 1450

Main ideaIn 1450 the world's great societies were rich, connected within their own regions and unaware of each other across the oceans, and that was about to change.

Stand on the world in 1450 and look around. In China the Ming dynasty rules perhaps a hundred million people, the most on earth. Twenty years earlier its emperor sent Zheng He’s giant fleets to India and Africa, then stopped and let the ships rot. In India, Persia and the Ottoman lands, Muslim rulers govern rich cities. Along the Swahili coast and across the Sahara, African traders move gold. In Europe, small kingdoms quarrel, and a German craftsman is perfecting a press that prints with movable type.

Across the Atlantic, the Aztec are expanding in Mexico and the Inca in the Andes. Neither knows the other exists. Neither knows of Europe, and Europe does not know of them. In North America, the great mound city of Cahokia, near what is now St. Louis and Collinsville, Illinois, has already been abandoned for a century. Farming towns line the Mississippi and its branches. No one on either side of the ocean has any idea what is coming.

Compare the societies you have met. All built cities. All had religions that shaped law and daily life. Most had writing; the Inca did not. Europe and Asia had horses, iron, wheels and gunpowder; the Americas did not. Africa, Asia and Europe were linked by routes that carried goods, faiths and, in the 1340s, plague. The Americas were sealed off, so their people had never met the diseases of the other continents. That single fact would decide millions of lives.

Three events within a few years turned the page. In 1453 the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople, ending the Byzantine Empire and pushing Europeans to seek sea routes east. Around 1455 Gutenberg’s press printed a Bible, and cheap books spread faster than ever. In 1492 a Genoese sailor who had read Marco Polo sailed west from Spain, looking for Asia, and reached islands no European knew. The next unit begins there.
