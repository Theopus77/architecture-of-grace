The Ramayana

Epic I

The Ramayana is one of the best-loved stories in the world. It tells of Prince Rama, his wife Sita, his brother Lakshmana and their loyal helper Hanuman. Hindu tradition honors the sage Valmiki as its first poet.

In this unit you will follow the whole story, from the palace in Ayodhya to the forest, to Lanka and home again. You will see how the poem teaches dharma, doing what is right for your role. You will also see how readers ask hard questions and tell the story in many new ways.

By the end, you will be able to retell the main events in order. You will be able to explain dharma with examples. You will be able to compare two tellings of the Ramayana fairly.

The big question

What does the Ramayana teach about doing what is right, and why do people tell it in so many ways?

Big questionWhat does it mean to do what is right for your role?
The story

Sandals on the Throne

A brother carries a pair of sandals home and sets them on an empty throne.

Prince Bharata walks slowly back into the city of Ayodhya. His eyes are red from crying. In his hands he carries something small. It is a pair of wooden sandals.

The sandals belong to his older brother, Rama. Rama has gone to live in the forest for fourteen years. Everyone says Bharata should be king now. The people wait to see what he will do.

Bharata climbs the steps to the great hall. He does not sit on the throne. Instead, he kneels down. Carefully, he places Rama's sandals on the seat. The hall is silent.

"These sandals will rule," he tells the court. "I will only take care of the kingdom for my brother. When Rama comes home, the throne is his." Then he bows to the sandals.

The people are quiet. Then some begin to cry too. They have never seen a prince turn down a crown. Many Hindus still tell this story as a picture of loyalty and doing what is right.

Talk about itBharata could have been king. Why do you think he put the sandals on the throne instead?
Section 1

The First Poet and His Poem

9.1

Valmiki, the First Poet

Main ideaHindu tradition honors Valmiki as the first poet and the author of the Ramayana.

Hindu tradition calls the adi kavi, which means "the first poet." Tradition says he made the Ramayana in Sanskrit. The poem tells the life of Prince Rama.

The poem itself tells how Valmiki began. He saw a hunter shoot one of a pair of birds. The other bird cried out in grief. Valmiki felt deep sorrow, and his words came out in a new rhythm.

Tradition says that rhythm became the shloka, the verse form of the whole poem. So, the story goes, poetry was born from sorrow and care for another living thing.

From the source
Poetry Born From Grief
Valmiki sees a hunter kill one of two birds. Its mate cries out. Valmiki's sorrow pours out as a curse on the hunter, spoken in a new, even rhythm. He is surprised by his own words.
Retold from Valmiki's Ramayana, Bala Kanda (Book 1), canto 2 (paraphrased)

9.2

Seven Books

Main ideaValmiki's Ramayana has seven books, called kandas, and about 24,000 verses.

Valmiki’s Ramayana is divided into seven books. Each book is called a . Each kanda tells one part of Rama’s life, from his childhood to the end of his reign.

The poem has about 24,000 verses, by the traditional count. That is very long. It would take many days to chant it all. Many families read a small part at a time.

The names of the books tell where the story happens. Ayodhya Kanda happens in Rama’s city. Aranya Kanda happens in the forest. Sundara Kanda, the "beautiful book," follows Hanuman to Lanka.

9.3

How Old Is the Ramayana?

Main ideaTradition and scholars give different dates for the Ramayana.

Hindu tradition places Rama’s life in a very ancient age of the world, called the Treta Yuga. Many Hindus believe Rama lived long, long ago, far earlier than written history.

Scholars look at the poem’s language and its history. Most date Valmiki’s poem to between about 500 BCE and 100 CE. Many think the first and last books were added later.

Both views are held today. A student can name both without choosing between them. You might say, "Tradition says..." and then, "Scholars date..." Both are part of learning.

9.4

A King and Four Sons

Main ideaKing Dasharatha of Ayodhya has four sons, and Rama is the eldest.

The Ramayana begins in , a rich city in North India. Its king is Dasharatha. He is a good king, but for many years he has no children. He prays and waits.

At last, the poem says, his three queens have four sons. Queen Kausalya’s son is Rama. Queen Kaikeyi’s son is Bharata. Queen Sumitra has twins, Lakshmana and Shatrughna.

Rama is the eldest. He grows up brave, kind and wise. He marries Sita, a princess from the kingdom of Mithila. The king plans to make Rama the next ruler.

9.5

Kaikeyi's Two Wishes

Main ideaQueen Kaikeyi uses two old promises to change who will be king.

Long before, Queen Kaikeyi saved Dasharatha’s life in a battle. He promised her two wishes, called boons. She could ask for them any time. He never forgot that promise.

Now Rama is about to be crowned. Kaikeyi’s maid, Manthara, stirs up her fears. Kaikeyi goes to the king. She asks for her two boons at last. Manthara tells Kaikeyi her son will lose everything.

First, she wants her son Bharata to be king. Second, she wants Rama sent to the forest for fourteen years. The king is heartbroken. But he gave his word, and he cannot take it back.

9.6

Rama Chooses Exile

Main ideaRama accepts exile calmly so that his father's word is kept.

Rama hears the news. He does not argue. He does not get angry. The poem shows him accepting the exile calmly, so that his father’s promise is kept. He says he is ready to go.

Sita insists on coming with him. She says a wife’s place is at her husband’s side. Lakshmana insists on coming too, to serve and protect his brother. Neither one will stay behind.

The three leave Ayodhya in simple clothes made of bark. The people weep. Soon after, King Dasharatha dies of grief. Many readers see this as one of the saddest parts of the poem.

Bharata's Choice

Main ideaBharata refuses the throne and rules in Rama's name, with Rama's sandals on the throne.

Bharata rules for fourteen years as Rama’s stand-in. The poem says he lives simply, outside the city, waiting for his brother. Many Hindus honor Bharata as a model of a loyal brother.

Life in the Forest

9.8

Sages and Simple Living

Main ideaIn the forest, Rama, Sita and Lakshmana live simply and meet wise sages.

Rama, Sita and Lakshmana walk deep into the forests of India. They build huts of leaves and branches. They eat fruit and roots. They live very simply.

In the forest, they meet many sages. A sage is a wise person who lives a holy life. The sages welcome them and teach them. Some ask Rama to protect them from danger.

The poem describes rivers, flowers, birds and changing seasons. Many readers love these nature scenes. Some of the places named in the poem are still pilgrimage sites today.

9.9

Dharma: Right for Your Role

Main ideaIn the Ramayana, dharma means doing what is right for your role in life.

The Ramayana is full of a key word: . In this story, dharma means doing what is right for your role. A son, a brother, a wife and a king each have duties.

Rama keeps his father’s word. Bharata protects his brother’s throne. Lakshmana serves his brother. Sita stays true to Rama. Many Hindus see each one as a model of dharma.

Dharma is not always easy. Sometimes two duties pull against each other, like the king’s love for his son and his promise. The Ramayana shows people facing these hard choices.

9.10

Tulsidas Tells It Again

Main ideaIn the 1500s, the poet Tulsidas retold the Ramayana in a form of Hindi.

Valmiki’s poem is in Sanskrit. Most people in North India did not speak Sanskrit. In the 1500s, the poet retold the story in Awadhi, a form of Hindi.

His poem is called the Ramcharitmanas, "the lake of Rama’s deeds." Tulsidas was a devoted follower of Rama. His telling is full of love for Rama as God.

The Ramcharitmanas became one of the most loved books in North India. Families chant it at home and in temples. Many Hindus in Illinois know its verses by heart.

Lanka, Return and the Meaning of Dharma

The Ramayana
Big questionWhy do people keep retelling the Ramayana, and why do their tellings differ?
The story

Ten Nights of the Ramayana

A town in India puts on the whole Ramayana as a play that lasts ten nights.

In a small town in North India, the autumn air is cool. Every evening, people carry mats and blankets to an open field. Strings of lights hang above a wooden stage.

This is the Ramlila, a play of Rama's life. Each night the town acts out a new part. On the first night, Rama is born. On another night, he leaves for the forest. The crowd grows bigger every evening.

The actors are neighbors: a shopkeeper, a teacher, some boys from the high school. Children in the front row know the lines by heart. They whisper them along with the actors.

On the tenth night comes the festival of Dussehra. A giant figure of Ravana stands at the edge of the field. When the play reaches the battle, the figure is set alight. Fireworks burst overhead, and the crowd cheers.

An old man in the crowd has watched this play every year since he was a boy. "It is the same story," he says, smiling. "And it is new every time." Then he joins in the cheering.

Victory and Homecoming

10.4

The Battle

Main ideaRama defeats Ravana in a great battle and frees Sita.

Yuddha Kanda, the "book of battle," tells of a long war in Lanka. Many warriors fight on both sides. Lakshmana is badly hurt, and Hanuman flies to bring healing herbs from a mountain.

At last Rama faces Ravana himself. The fight is long and hard. Rama wins. Many Hindus remember this victory as good winning over evil. The long war is over at last.

After the battle, Rama makes Vibhishana the new king of Lanka. He does not take Lanka for himself. The poem also shows respect for Ravana’s funeral rites.

10.5

Coming Home

Main ideaRama returns to Ayodhya and is crowned king, and many Hindus remember this at Diwali.

The fourteen years are over. Rama, Sita and Lakshmana fly home to Ayodhya in a flying chariot. Hanuman goes ahead to tell Bharata the news. The chariot is called Pushpaka.

Bharata gives back the kingdom joyfully. Rama is crowned king at last, with Sita as queen. The people of Ayodhya celebrate. There is music and feasting in every street.

Many Hindus remember this homecoming at Diwali, the festival of lights, when they light lamps to welcome Rama and Sita. Others remember Lakshmi or Krishna at Diwali.

10.6

Rama Rajya

Main ideaRama Rajya, "the rule of Rama," is an ideal of fair and peaceful government.

The poem says Rama ruled with fairness for many years. People were safe, fields were full and no one was treated unjustly. This is called , "the rule of Rama."

Over time, Rama Rajya became an ideal, a picture of what good government could be. Mahatma Gandhi used the phrase for a fair India where the poorest people were cared for.

Others have used the phrase in different ways, and people still debate what it should mean. Many Hindus use it simply to mean a kind and just ruler. Its meaning is still discussed.

Hard Questions and Many Ramayanas

10.7

Hard Questions About Sita

Main ideaReaders ask hard questions about how Sita is treated, and they answer in different ways.

After the battle, Valmiki’s poem says Rama doubts Sita, because she lived in Ravana’s kingdom. Sita walks into a fire to prove she is pure, and the fire god, Agni, returns her unharmed.

In the last book, Uttara Kanda, Rama sends Sita away because people gossip about her. She raises their twin sons in Valmiki’s forest home. Many readers find these parts very painful.

Readers answer in different ways. Some explain Rama’s choices as a king’s duty. Some say these parts were added later. Many retellings, including women’s songs, tell the story from Sita’s side and question Rama.

10.8

Sita's Side of the Story

Main ideaMany tellings, including women's songs and a Bengali Ramayana by a woman poet, put Sita at the center.

For centuries, women in Indian villages have sung their own Ramayana songs. They sing them while working, at weddings and at festivals. Many of these songs focus on Sita’s feelings.

In the 1500s, the poet Chandrabati wrote a Ramayana in Bengali. She was one of the first women known to retell the story. Her telling puts Sita’s sorrow at the center.

Scholars say there are many Ramayanas, not just one. Each telling reflects its place, its people and its questions. This variety is part of why the story stays alive.

The Ramayana Across Asia

Main ideaThe Ramayana traveled to Southeast Asia, where it has its own tellings.

Traders, teachers and travelers carried the Ramayana beyond India. In Thailand it became the . In Cambodia it is the Reamker. In Indonesia, a Javanese poem tells it too.

These tellings keep the main story but change details. In the Ramakien, Hanuman has a bigger, more playful role. Dancers act out scenes in masks and golden costumes.

The great temple of Angkor Wat in Cambodia has stone carvings of the Ramayana battle. In Indonesia, many Muslims enjoy Ramayana puppet shows and dances as part of their culture.

Ramlila: The Story on Stage

Main ideaIn the Ramlila, towns act out the Ramayana, often over many nights before Dussehra.

The is a play of Rama’s life. Towns across North India perform it each autumn, often over about ten nights. Many performances follow Tulsidas’s Ramcharitmanas.

It usually ends at Dussehra, the festival of Rama’s victory. In many places, huge figures of Ravana are burned. Crowds cheer as good wins over evil. Fireworks light up the sky.

In 2005, UNESCO honored the Ramlila as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. Some Hindu communities in Illinois hold their own Ramlila plays.
