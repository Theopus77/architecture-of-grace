Geocentric and Heliocentric

A comparison of the two great models of the planetary system as they appear in the surviving documents. The sources cited are held by named libraries, museums, and academies. This page does not claim access to restricted government archives.

The two models

GeocentricHeliocentric
CenterEarth fixed (or near-center with equant)Sun fixed (or near-center)
Earth’s motionNoneDaily rotation and annual orbit
Planetary motionDeferents, epicycles, equantOrbits (later ellipses) around the Sun
RetrogradeProduced by epicycleApparent, from Earth’s faster inner orbit

Both models were mathematical tools fitted to observations. The geocentric system of Ptolemy could predict planetary positions accurately for more than a thousand years. The heliocentric system became superior once Kepler replaced circles with ellipses and Newton supplied a physical cause.

Primary documents

The comparison rests on texts and instruments that survive in public collections. No claim is made here about documents held in closed government vaults.

  • Ptolemy, Almagest (c. 150 CE) — the complete geocentric system of deferents, epicycles, and the equant. Greek manuscripts survive; the standard critical edition is Heiberg’s. Early printed editions and Arabic translations are held by major research libraries.
  • Copernicus, De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (1543) — the first full mathematical heliocentric system in print. The Nuremberg first edition and annotated copies (including those associated with the Wittenberg circle) are catalogued in European and American rare-book collections.
  • Kepler, Astronomia nova (1609) and Harmonices mundi (1619) — elliptical orbits and the harmonic law. Kepler’s manuscripts and correspondence are preserved chiefly in archives in Russia (Pulkovo / St. Petersburg holdings of earlier collections) and in European libraries.
  • Galileo, Sidereus nuncius (1610) and the later Dialogo (1632) — telescopic evidence and the defense of Copernicanism. The Vatican Library and the Archivio Segreto Vaticano hold trial documents; Galileo’s own papers are largely in the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze.
  • Antikythera fragments — National Archaeological Museum, Athens. The geared calculator embodies period relations used in geocentric astronomy; it does not decide between the two later models.

Institutions that hold the sources

  • National Archaeological Museum, Athens — Antikythera mechanism fragments.
  • Vatican Library and Archivio Apostolico Vaticano — Galileo trial records and related correspondence (publicly catalogued; many items digitized).
  • Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze — Galileo’s manuscripts.
  • British Library — early printed editions of Ptolemy, Copernicus, and Kepler; ancient Near Eastern tablets.
  • Bibliothèque nationale de France — Greek and Arabic astronomical manuscripts.
  • Bodleian Library, Oxford and Cambridge University Library — medieval and early-modern astronomical codices.
  • Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum and Museum of the History of Science, Oxford — instruments and teaching models.
  • International Astronomical Union and national academies (Royal Society, Académie des sciences, Leopoldina) — modern historical commissions and published critical editions, not secret holdings.

References here are to publicly described collections. Claims of hidden governmental vaults of suppressed cosmological documents are not supported by the published catalogues of these institutions.

The shift

The ancient Near Eastern layered cosmos (see the related lab) placed a fixed earth under a vault. Greek mathematical astronomy kept the earth central but replaced the vault with circles that could be calculated. Ptolemy’s system remained the working standard in the Islamic world and in Latin Europe for more than a millennium.

Copernicus offered a heliocentric alternative that simplified some motions but still used circles and epicycles. Kepler’s ellipses removed the need for most epicycles. Galileo’s telescopic discoveries (phases of Venus, moons of Jupiter) removed key objections but did not by themselves prove the earth’s motion. Newton’s Principia (1687) supplied the physical law that made the heliocentric (more precisely, barycentric) system the simpler and more predictive account.

The change was gradual, documented in printed books, correspondence, and institutional records that are available to researchers. It was not the sudden release of a single hidden document.
