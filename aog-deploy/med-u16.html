Ethics

Medicine is not only about what doctors can do. It is also about what they should do. In this unit you will meet people whose stories changed the rules: Henrietta Lacks, whose cells were taken without her knowledge; the men of the Tuskegee study, who were denied a known cure; and Dorothea Dix, who used careful evidence to demand better care for people with mental illness.

You will learn the four principles many hospitals use today: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. You will see how those principles guide consent, research, organ donation, scarce care and genetic privacy. Then you will turn to the mind: how mental health care has changed, how depression, anxiety and addiction are treated, and how respect for different minds is growing.

By the end, you will be able to explain key cases in medical ethics, weigh arguments on hard questions fairly, describe treatments that have evidence, and know where to find help for yourself or a friend, including 988 and your school counselor.

The big question

How should medicine respect people's rights and minds while trying to help them?

Bioethics
Big questionWhat do doctors and researchers owe the people they treat and study?
The story

The Cells That Kept Growing

In 1951 a young mother's cells were taken without her knowledge, and they changed medicine.

Baltimore, 1951. Henrietta Lacks was a 30-year-old mother of five. She grew up farming tobacco in Virginia and later moved to Maryland with her husband. She felt a pain that would not go away, so she went to Johns Hopkins Hospital. It was one of the few hospitals nearby that treated Black patients at the time.

Doctors found cancer on her cervix. During her treatment, a surgeon cut a small sample of the tumor. The sample went to a lab run by George Gey, a scientist who had spent years trying to grow human cells outside the body. Nobody asked Henrietta if her cells could be used this way. Nobody told her family either.

In most labs, human cells died after a few days. Henrietta's cells did something new. They divided again and again and did not stop. The lab named them HeLa, from the first letters of her names. Henrietta died in October 1951. She was 31 years old. Her cells lived on.

HeLa cells were shipped to labs around the world. Scientists used them to test the polio vaccine, study cancer and viruses, and learn how cells work. Companies sold them. For more than 20 years, her family did not know. When they found out, they had hard questions. Who decides what happens to a person's cells? Who should benefit? What should a patient be told?

Talk about itHenrietta Lacks's cells helped millions of people. Does that good outcome make it right that no one asked her? Explain your view.

Four Principles of Ethics

31.1

Autonomy and Beneficence

Main ideaGood care respects a patient's right to choose and also works for the patient's good.

Picture a 70-year-old man named Mr. Reyes. His doctor recommends heart surgery. Mr. Reyes listens, asks questions, and then says no. He would rather spend his remaining years at home with medicine alone. His doctor disagrees but accepts his choice. This scene shows two ideas that sit at the center of medical ethics.

In 1979 two American scholars, Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, published Principles of Biomedical Ethics. They named four principles that many hospitals now use. The first is . It means a capable adult has the right to make decisions about their own body and care, even choices a doctor would not make. Respecting autonomy means giving honest information and then listening.

The second principle is . It means acting for the patient’s good: recommending helpful treatment, relieving pain, and caring for the whole person. Sometimes these two principles pull in different directions. A doctor may believe surgery is best, but the patient decides. In the past, many doctors simply decided for patients. This habit is called , and modern ethics has moved away from it.

31.2

Do No Harm, Be Fair

Main ideaNon-maleficence means avoiding needless harm, and justice means sharing care and burdens fairly.

Every medicine has risks. An antibiotic can cure a bacterial infection, but it can also cause an allergic reaction. Chemotherapy can shrink a cancer, but it can also make a person very tired and sick. Doctors weigh these risks every day. The goal is not zero risk, which is impossible. The goal is that the likely good clearly outweighs the likely harm.

This is the principle of , often summed up as "first, do no harm." The phrase is linked with the Hippocratic tradition of ancient Greece, though the exact words do not appear in the oath itself. Non-maleficence tells doctors not to give treatments that are useless or more harmful than helpful, and not to cause pain without a good reason.

The fourth principle is . It means treating people fairly and sharing both the benefits and the burdens of medicine. Justice asks hard questions. Who gets a scarce organ? Should people with less money wait longer for care? Should one group carry the risks of research while another enjoys the results? You will see these questions again in this chapter.

31.3

Informed Consent

Main ideaInformed consent means a patient agrees to care after understanding its purpose, risks, benefits and choices.

Before a teenager gets her wisdom teeth removed, the dentist sits down with her and a parent. He explains what the surgery does, what could go wrong, how long healing takes, and what happens if they wait. He answers questions. Then the parent signs a form. That conversation, not just the signature, is the heart of .

Informed consent has several parts. The patient must get clear information about the purpose, the likely benefits, the risks and the other choices, including doing nothing. The patient must be able to understand it, which may require an or plain language. And the choice must be , meaning free of pressure or threats. A patient may also change their mind later.

Minors usually cannot give full legal consent, so a parent or guardian gives permission. Many doctors still ask the young person for their own agreement, called assent. In an emergency, when a person cannot speak and waiting would cause serious harm, doctors may act without consent. Outside these limits, treating someone without consent is a serious ethical and legal wrong.

When Research Went Wrong

31.4

The Nuremberg Code

Main ideaAfter Nazi doctors' crimes, the 1947 Nuremberg Code said voluntary consent is essential in research.

In 1946 and 1947, in the German city of Nuremberg, 23 defendants stood trial before American judges. Most were doctors. During World War II they had carried out experiments on prisoners in Nazi concentration camps. Prisoners were frozen, infected with diseases and harmed in other ways, without any choice. Many died. This trial is known as the Doctors’ Trial.

At the end of the trial in 1947, the judges set out ten principles for experiments on human beings. These became known as the . Its first principle says the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential. Other principles say research should aim at real good for society, avoid needless suffering, and let a person stop taking part at any time.

The code marked a turning point. It said a person in a study is a with rights, not an object. It did not end all harmful research, as the next lessons show. But it gave the world a clear standard. Later rules, such as the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki in 1964, built on its ideas.

The Tuskegee Study

Main ideaFor 40 years, a U.S. government study watched Black men with syphilis without telling them or treating them.

In 1932 the U.S. Public Health Service began a study in Macon County, Alabama, near the town of Tuskegee. About 600 Black men took part. Most were poor sharecroppers. About 400 had , a bacterial infection that can damage the heart, brain and other organs over many years. About 200 did not and served as a comparison group.

The men were told they were being treated for "bad blood." In truth, the study only watched what syphilis did to them over time. In the 1940s, became the standard cure for syphilis. The men were not offered it. Researchers even took steps to keep them from getting it elsewhere. Some men died of the disease, and some wives and children were infected.

In 1972 a Public Health Service worker, Peter Buxtun, gave the story to reporter Jean Heller of the Associated Press, and the study ended that year. The men and their families later won a legal settlement. In 1997 President Bill Clinton formally apologized. The study’s legacy includes new research rules and lasting of medicine among many Black Americans, a mistrust rooted in real events.

Henrietta Lacks and HeLa

Main ideaHeLa cells helped medicine enormously, but they were taken without consent, raising questions about fairness.

You met Henrietta Lacks in this chapter’s story. Her cells, called HeLa, became the first human cells to keep growing in a lab for a long time. Scientists called them an immortal . That means a group of cells that can divide again and again, so many labs can use copies of the same cells.

HeLa cells were used to test the polio vaccine in the 1950s. Later they helped research on cancer, viruses, genes and many medicines. Yet Henrietta was never asked. In 1951, it was common for doctors to use tissue left over from treatment without asking. Her family learned about the cells only in the 1970s. They received no money, while companies sold the cells.

Rebecca Skloot’s 2010 book, The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks, made the story widely known. In 2013 the U.S. National Institutes of Health reached an agreement with the Lacks family. Scientists who want to use HeLa data must now apply, and family members help review the requests. The story raises lasting questions about consent, privacy and who benefits from a person’s body.

The Belmont Report

Main ideaThe 1979 Belmont Report set three principles for U.S. research: respect for persons, beneficence and justice.

After the Tuskegee study became public, Congress passed the National Research Act in 1974. The law created a national commission to set clear rules for research with people. The commission met at the Belmont conference center in Maryland. Its short report, published in 1979, is called the .

The report names three principles. means treating people as able to make their own choices and protecting those who cannot, such as young children. Beneficence means maximizing possible benefits and reducing harm. Justice means sharing the burdens and benefits of research fairly, so that poor or powerless groups are not chosen just because they are easy to use.

The report turned each principle into practice: informed consent, a careful look at risks and benefits, and fair selection of subjects. Today, most U.S. research with people, including all government-funded studies, must be approved by an , or IRB. An IRB is a committee that checks a study’s plan before it starts. When your school or a hospital runs a survey study, an IRB may review it.

Hard Choices Today

31.8

Organ Donation

Main ideaTransplants save lives, and ethics guides how organs are given and shared.

In 1954 in Boston, surgeon Joseph Murray moved a kidney from one man into his identical twin brother. The brother lived for years afterward. It was the first long-term successful kidney . Murray later shared the Nobel Prize. Today surgeons transplant kidneys, livers, hearts, lungs and other organs.

Most organs come from a who has died. Some come from living donors, who can give one kidney or part of a liver. In the United States, people can join a donor registry, often when they get a driver’s license. Buying and selling organs is illegal under the National Organ Transplant Act of 1984, because payment could pressure poor people and make access unfair.

There are not enough organs for everyone who needs one. The U.S. government reports that more than 100,000 people are on the national waiting list. A national network matches organs using medical need, blood and tissue type, time waiting and distance. Ethics asks that these rules be fair and not depend on wealth or fame. Donation is a personal choice; families can talk it over with each other and a doctor.

Think it through

31.9

Sharing Scarce Care

Main ideaWhen there is not enough care for all, ethics asks for clear, fair rules decided ahead of time.

A bus crash sends 20 injured people to a small hospital at once. There are only a few surgeons. Staff quickly sort patients: who needs help right now, who can wait, and who has minor injuries. This sorting is called , from a French word meaning to sort. It began on battlefields and is now used in every emergency room.

Sometimes the shortage lasts longer. In the spring of 2020, some hospitals worried they would run out of breathing machines called for COVID-19 patients. Many states and hospitals wrote crisis plans. These plans tried to save the most lives while treating people equally, but they also stirred debate. Critics worried that some rules could treat older or disabled people unfairly.

Ethicists often say the rules for , meaning how scarce resources are shared, should be made in advance, in public and with input from the communities affected. They should use medical facts, not a person’s wealth, race or social worth. People still disagree about details, such as whether to consider age or who is a health worker. These remain open, contested questions.

Genetic Testing and Privacy

Main ideaGenetic tests can guide care, but genetic information needs careful privacy protection.

A woman’s mother and aunt both had breast cancer. Her doctor suggests a for changes in genes called BRCA1 and BRCA2. Certain changes in these genes raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer. A result can help her and her doctor plan screening. It may also matter to her sisters and children, who share some of her genes.

Genetic information is unusual because it is shared by families and cannot be changed. Many people send saliva to home DNA companies to learn about their ancestry. They may not think about who else could see the data. In some famous cases, police have used public DNA databases to find crime suspects through distant relatives. People disagree about whether that use is fair.

U.S. laws offer some protection. HIPAA, a 1996 law, protects health records held by doctors and insurers. The , or GINA, passed in 2008, bans health insurers and most employers from treating people unfairly based on their genes. GINA does not cover life insurance or long-term care insurance. A can help people decide whether to test and what a result means.

32.1

Dorothea Dix and the Asylums

Main ideaIn the 1800s, reformers like Dorothea Dix pushed for hospitals that treated mental illness with care, not punishment.

In 1841 Dorothea Dix volunteered to teach a Sunday class at a jail in East Cambridge, Massachusetts. She found people with mental illness held with criminals in an unheated room. She spent the next two years visiting jails and poorhouses. In 1843 she presented her findings, called a , to the Massachusetts legislature.

Dix belonged to a reform movement inspired partly by . This approach, developed by reformers such as Philippe Pinel in France and the Tuke family in England, held that people with mental illness should be treated with kindness, routine, fresh air and useful work. Dix spent decades asking states to build public hospitals based on these ideas.

Many such hospitals, then called , opened in the United States in the 1800s. At first, some offered real care. Over time, many became crowded and underfunded. Patients sometimes stayed for decades with little treatment. The story shows a pattern in history: a good reform can fall short when money, staff and attention run out.

Leaving the Big Hospitals

Main ideaIn the late 1900s, care moved from large hospitals to communities, with gains and real gaps.

By the 1950s, hundreds of thousands of Americans lived in state mental hospitals. Then several changes arrived. In the 1950s, the first effective medicines, such as chlorpromazine, helped many people with severe symptoms. Journalists and courts exposed poor conditions. Many people argued that patients should live in their own communities.

In 1963 President John F. Kennedy signed the Community Mental Health Act. It aimed to build local mental health centers so people could get care near home. Over the next decades, most large hospitals shrank or closed. This shift is called . Many people gained freedom, jobs and family life they could not have had in a hospital.

But far fewer community centers were built than planned, and funding often fell short. Some people who left hospitals did not get steady care. Many experts link these gaps to people with mental illness who are unhoused or in jails today. Historians and policy experts still debate how much each cause matters. Building strong remains unfinished work.

Talk Therapy and CBT

Main ideaCognitive behavioral therapy helps people notice and change thought patterns, and strong evidence supports it.

Ana tells herself, "If I don’t get an A, I’m a failure." Her therapist asks her to write down that thought and look for evidence. Has she ever gotten a B and still done well? Yes. Together they write a more balanced thought: "A B would be disappointing, but I can learn from it." Ana practices this each week.

This method is , or CBT. It was developed in the 1960s by psychiatrist Aaron Beck at the University of Pennsylvania, building on earlier work. CBT teaches that thoughts, feelings and actions affect one another. By learning to spot unhelpful thoughts and try new actions, people can change how they feel. Therapists often give short practice tasks between sessions.

Many controlled studies show that CBT helps with depression and anxiety disorders. It is often a first choice for young people. It is one kind of , or talk therapy. Other kinds focus on relationships, family, or skills for handling strong emotions. A good fit between the person and the matters a great deal.

One common group of antidepressants is called . The first widely used one in the United States, fluoxetine, was approved in 1987. Addiction and the Brain

Main ideaAddiction is a treatable brain condition, not a failure of character.

Picture a man who first took pain pills after back surgery. Over months, he needed more to feel the same effect. When he tried to stop, he felt sick and anxious. He kept using even as it hurt his job and family. His story is common. It shows how a substance can change the brain over time.

is a condition in which a person keeps using a substance or doing an activity despite harm. The National Institute on Drug Abuse describes it as a treatable brain disorder. Many drugs flood the brain’s reward system with a chemical messenger called . Over time the brain adapts, and cravings grow. Teen brains are still developing, which is one reason early use carries more risk.

Seeing addiction as a health condition changes how we help. Effective treatment includes counseling and, for opioid addiction, medicines such as buprenorphine and methadone, which have strong evidence. is possible, and many people reach it, sometimes after more than one try. Using respectful words, like "a person with a substance use disorder," makes it easier for people to seek help.

How Stigma Is Changing

Main ideaStigma keeps people from seeking help, and open talk from public figures is helping change it.

At the Tokyo Olympics in 2021, gymnast Simone Biles withdrew from several events. She explained that she needed to protect her mental and physical health. Some people criticized her. Many more praised her for being honest. Around the same time, other well-known athletes spoke openly about anxiety and depression.

is a set of negative beliefs and labels attached to a group. For mental health, stigma can sound like "just snap out of it" or jokes about people being "crazy." It can lead people to hide what they are going through. Many people with mental health conditions wait years before seeking help, and stigma is one of the reasons.

Stigma is changing through contact and honest stories. When people hear a friend, teacher or athlete describe a hard time and their recovery, fear drops. Schools add mental health lessons. Words matter too. , such as "a person with depression," puts the person before the condition. Some people prefer identity-first words; asking is a respectful habit.

Neurodiversity

Main ideaNeurodiversity describes the natural variety of human minds, including autistic people and people with ADHD or dyslexia.

In one classroom, Leo notices patterns in numbers that others miss and prefers a quiet corner. Priya thinks fast, has great ideas and does best when she can move. Sam reads more slowly but tells vivid stories out loud. Each of these students’ brains works a little differently. Each has real strengths and specific needs.

The word is usually credited to Australian sociologist Judy Singer in the late 1990s. It describes the natural variety in how human brains think, learn and sense the world. People whose brains work in less typical ways, such as autistic people or people with ADHD or dyslexia, are often called . The idea encourages respect rather than trying to make everyone the same.

The neurodiversity view does not deny that people may need support. Instead it focuses on , changes that help a person learn or work, such as extra time, quiet spaces, audiobooks or movement breaks. In U.S. public schools, laws such as the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act support plans that fit each student. Many neurodivergent people also value community with others who think like them.

Mindfulness From Buddhist Roots

Main ideaMindfulness began in Buddhist practice and has been studied as a support for stress, anxiety and depression.

A student sits at her desk before a test. She closes her eyes and notices her breath going in and out. When her mind jumps to worries, she gently brings it back to her breath. After two minutes, her shoulders are lower. She is practicing : paying attention to the present moment on purpose, without judging it.

Mindfulness has deep roots in Buddhism, which began in ancient India about 2,500 years ago. The Pali word sati, often translated as mindfulness, is part of Buddhist teaching on the path to ending suffering. In Buddhism, is a spiritual practice within a larger way of life and ethics.

In 1979, Jon Kabat-Zinn began a program at the University of Massachusetts Medical School called . It taught mindfulness in a nonreligious way to patients. A 2014 review in JAMA Internal Medicine found moderate evidence that meditation programs can ease anxiety and depression. They are a helpful tool for many people, not a replacement for treatment when someone needs it.

988 and School Supports

Main ideaHelp is close: 988 is available any time, and schools have trained people ready to listen.

Anyone can call or text 988, or chat online, any time of day. Trained counselors listen and help people find safety and support. You can also call for a friend.

Schools have supports too. A helps with stress, friendships and plans. School psychologists and social workers can help with learning, emotions and family needs. Many schools use a , a short written list of warning signs, calming steps, people to contact and places to go. In an emergency, call 911.

Helping a friend can be simple. Listen without judging. Take what they say seriously. Stay with them, and tell a trusted adult, even if they asked you to keep it secret. You are not expected to fix everything. Your job is to connect your friend to someone who can help. Reaching out for yourself is also a strong and healthy choice.
