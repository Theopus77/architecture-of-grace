Big questionHow can a message be hidden in plain sight, and how do people figure out what it says?
The story

Shapes on an Old Stone

A visitor in a busy New York churchyard spots a row of strange shapes carved on a gravestone.

It is a bright fall day in New York City. Tall office towers rise on every side. Cars and buses rush along Broadway. A girl we will call Rosa walks through a gate with her aunt. Suddenly the street noise feels far away. They are in Trinity Churchyard, one of the oldest burial grounds in the city.

Old gray gravestones lean in the grass. Most of them show names and dates. Rosa stops at one old stone. It belongs to a man named James Leeson. He died in 1794. The stone has a carving of a square and a compass. Rosa's aunt says that is a sign of the Freemasons, a very old club of friends.

Then Rosa sees something odd. There is a line of little shapes carved on the stone. Some look like boxes with open sides. Others look like corners or tiny boxes with dots inside. "Is that a secret language?" Rosa asks. Her aunt smiles. "It is a code. And we can learn to read it."

Rosa's aunt draws two tic-tac-toe grids and two big X shapes in a notebook. She writes letters of the alphabet inside the spaces. Rosa matches the first shape on the stone to a space in the grid. It is the letter R. Then E. Then M. Letter by letter, a message appears.

Rosa is a made-up visitor. But the stone is real. You can still see it in the churchyard today. In this chapter, you will learn the code that is carved on it. You will also learn how people break codes they have never seen before.

Section 1

The Pigpen Cipher

13.1

Letters in Little Pens

Main ideaThe pigpen cipher swaps each letter for the shape of the space it sits in on a grid or an X.

Picture a farm. Pigs live in small fenced areas called pens. The gets its name from that idea. Each letter of the alphabet lives in its own little pen. To write a letter in code, you draw the shape of its pen instead of the letter.

Here is one common way to set it up. Draw a tic-tac-toe grid and put A through I in its nine spaces. Draw a second grid with a dot in each space for J through R. Then draw a big X for S, T, U and V. A second X with dots holds W, X, Y and Z.

Now look at the letter A. It sits in the top left corner of the first grid. Its pen has a line on the right and a line on the bottom. So A is written as a shape like a backward L. Every letter gets a shape this way. There are other versions of the key, so the letters may sit in a different order.

Think it through
Draw the Key

Draw a tic-tac-toe grid. Write A, B, C in the top row, D, E, F in the middle row and G, H, I in the bottom row. Look at the middle space, where E lives. It has lines on all four sides. So in pigpen, E is written as a small closed box.

Using the same grid, what shape would you draw for the letter C? Describe which sides have lines.

13.2

An Old Idea, a Masonic Name

Main ideaPigpen is older than the Freemasons, but Freemasons used it so much that it is often called the Masonic cipher.

Many people say the Freemasons invented pigpen. The are a brotherhood that began with stonemasons, the workers who built with stone. Their lodges, or clubs, grew in Britain in the 1600s and 1700s. But the idea of hiding letters in a grid is older than their lodges.

In the 1530s, a German writer named Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa published a big book about hidden knowledge. Scholars agree it shows letters placed in a grid of boxes. Historians see this grid as an early ancestor of pigpen. That book came out long before the first grand lodge of Freemasons.

So the Freemasons did not invent the grid. They took it up and spread it. Masons used it widely from the 1700s. That is why pigpen is also called the Masonic cipher or the Freemasons’ cipher. A name can tell you who used a thing most, not who made it first.

13.3

Codes and Ciphers

Main ideaA code swaps whole words, a cipher swaps letters, and both turn plain words into hidden ones.

People often say "code" for any secret message. Codebreakers use two words. A swaps a whole word or idea for something else. For example, a team might agree that "pizza" means "meet at the gym." You need the list of meanings to understand it.

A cipher works on single letters. Each letter is changed into another letter or a symbol. Pigpen is a cipher, because every letter gets its own shape. You can hide any word with a cipher, even a word nobody planned for.

The message you start with is called . It is the plain words anyone can read. After you hide it, it is called ciphertext. Changing plaintext into ciphertext is called enciphering. Changing it back is called deciphering. Many people also say encode and decode.

13.5

Remember Death

Main ideaThe pigpen line on the Leeson stone reads REMEMBER DEATH, an old reminder that life is short and precious.

When you decode the shapes on the Leeson stone, they spell REMEMBER DEATH. You can check this yourself. Match each shape to its pen in the key. The letters line up one by one. Many writers and codebreakers have checked it the same way.

The words may sound gloomy at first. But they were a very common message in those days. People called this kind of reminder by a Latin name, . It means "remember that you will die." The idea was to live well, be kind and use your time wisely.

Old gravestones in Europe and America often carried this message. Some showed an hourglass, with sand running out. Leeson’s stone says the same thing in a quieter way. Only people who knew the cipher could read it at a glance.

13.6

Why Put Code on a Grave?

Main ideaA cipher on a gravestone can be a message for friends who share a secret, not a treasure clue.

A gravestone is public. Anyone can walk by and look. So why carve words that most people cannot read? We do not have a letter from Leeson or his friends that explains it. Historians can only look at the clues.

One clue is the square and compass. Masons in the 1700s knew the pigpen cipher well. A line in pigpen could be a quiet message to fellow Masons. It said, in effect, "This man was one of us." It was a way of belonging, like a team handshake.

Some people online claim the stone hides a treasure map. There is no evidence for that. The decoded words are a common saying about life and death. When a message is solved, it is wise to trust what the letters say. A good asks for evidence before believing an exciting story.

Swapping and Locking Letters

13.7

Substitution Ciphers

Main ideaIn a substitution cipher, every letter is swapped for another letter or symbol by the same rule.

To substitute means to put one thing in place of another. A puts a new letter or symbol in place of each letter. Pigpen is a substitution cipher. Each letter is always swapped for the same shape.

A very old example comes from ancient Rome. The Roman writer Suetonius wrote about the life of Julius Caesar. He said Caesar sent private letters in a cipher. Each letter was moved a few places down the alphabet. Today this is called a , or the Caesar cipher.

Here is how a shift of three works. A becomes D. B becomes E. C becomes F. So the word CAT becomes FDW. To read it, you shift each letter back three places. The rule is simple, and it stays the same for the whole message.

From the source
A Roman Writer on Caesar's Letters
When Caesar had something private to say, he wrote it in cipher. He changed the order of the letters so no word could be made out. To read it, you put D in place of A, and do the same with the other letters.
Suetonius, The Life of Julius Caesar (in The Lives of the Caesars), c. 121 CE (paraphrased)

13.8

The Key Opens the Lock

Main ideaA key is the rule or chart that locks a message and unlocks it again, so it must be kept safe.

Think of a locked diary. The lock keeps the pages private. The key opens it. A cipher works the same way. The is the rule or chart that hides the message and brings it back.

For a shift cipher, the key is a number, like three. For pigpen, the key is the drawing of the grids and X shapes. The person sending the message and the person getting it both need the same key. Anyone else should not have it.

Here is a problem. The pigpen key has been printed in many books. Today almost anyone can find it. So pigpen hides a message only from people who have not learned it. A cipher is only as strong as the secret of its key.

How Codebreakers Work

13.9

Counting Letters

Main ideaSome letters appear much more often than others, and counting them helps codebreakers crack a cipher.

More than 1,100 years ago, a scholar named al-Kindi lived in Baghdad. He wrote the oldest known guide to breaking ciphers. His big idea was to count. In any long piece of writing, some letters show up much more than others.

In English, the most common letter is E. Codebreakers measure it at about 12.7 out of every 100 letters. Next come T, A and O. Letters like Q and Z are rare. Counting how often each letter appears is called . Frequency means how often something happens.

Here is how it helps. Say a secret message uses the shape of a triangle more than any other symbol. That triangle probably stands for E. The codebreaker writes E above each triangle. One guess at a time, the message starts to open.

13.10

Clues From Short Words

Main ideaCodebreakers mix letter counts with clues from short words and patterns to test their guesses.

Counting letters is a great start, but it is not the whole job. Short messages can trick you. In REMEMBER DEATH, E is most common, which fits. But a short note about a zoo might have lots of Z and O. So codebreakers look for more clues.

Short words are strong clues. In English, a one-letter word is almost always A or I. A common three-letter word is THE. If a message has the same three symbols again and again, they may spell THE. Then you know T, H and E all at once.

Codebreakers also look for . A double symbol, like in the middle of LOOK or BALL, is often O, E, L, S or T. Each guess is tested against the whole message. If a guess makes real words, keep it. If not, try again. Mistakes are part of the work.

13.11

Make and Break a Code Together

Main ideaCodebreaking works best as teamwork, where each person spots different clues and checks the others' guesses.

Now it is your turn. Your class can split into two teams. Team One makes a message. They choose a short sentence, maybe ten to fifteen words. They pick a cipher, such as a shift cipher or their own symbol alphabet. They keep the key hidden.

Team Two gets only the ciphertext. First, they count each symbol and make a tally chart. Next, they look for one-letter words and repeating groups. One person can track guesses on the board. Another can read the message aloud as it changes.

Real codebreakers work in teams too. One person may be good at counting. Another may notice patterns or know many words. When the message is solved, both teams can talk. What made it hard? What gave it away? makes both sides stronger.

The Copiale Cipher and the Art of Memory

Codebreakers
Big questionHow do people and tools work together to unlock a secret, and how do people remember what they cannot write down?
The story

A Book Nobody Could Read

Three researchers in two countries stare at a handwritten book that nobody had read for almost 300 years.

Picture a book with a green and gold cover. Its pages are filled with neat handwriting. But the writing makes no sense. There are strange symbols mixed with ordinary letters. Many of the symbols are shapes you have never seen. Page after page, the lines go on.

Someone wrote this book long ago, in the 1700s. Then, for almost 300 years, nobody could read it. People looked at it and wondered. Was it a secret diary? A book of magic? A list of hidden treasure? Nobody knew.

In 2011, three researchers decided to try. Kevin Knight worked at the University of Southern California, in the United States. Beáta Megyesi and Christiane Schaefer worked at Uppsala University, in Sweden. They were far apart, but they shared their work and their ideas.

Knight used computers to count and compare the symbols. Megyesi and Schaefer knew a lot about languages and old writing. Slowly, the pieces began to fit. The book was written in German. And it told the secrets of a society that cared a great deal about eyes.

In this chapter, you will follow how they cracked the Copiale Cipher. You will also meet stonemasons in Scotland. They were tested on something surprising: their memory.

14.1

A Book Full of Symbols

Main ideaThe Copiale Cipher is a 105-page handwritten book from about 1730, written in a mix of symbols and letters.

The is a real book. It has 105 handwritten pages. Its cover is made of green and gold patterned paper. Experts at Stockholm University say it was written around 1730. That makes it almost 300 years old.

The book holds about 75,000 written characters. A character is any single mark, like a letter or a symbol. The writer used about 90 different symbols. Some are ordinary letters from the Roman alphabet. Others are made-up shapes.

Think about that number. English has only 26 letters. So why would a writer need about 90 symbols? That big number was one of the first clues. It told the researchers that this was not a simple swap of one letter for one symbol.

Section 2

Computers and People Together

14.4

What the Computer Did

Main ideaComputers helped by counting symbols fast and comparing the patterns to real languages.

Kevin Knight studies how computers work with human languages. For the Copiale, he first had the symbols typed into a computer. Each symbol got a name so the computer could track it. That took careful work by people.

Then the computer could do what it does best. It counted every symbol in seconds. It found which symbols came together often. It compared those patterns with patterns in many real languages. A person counting 75,000 characters by hand would need a very long time.

The team’s work pointed to German as the hidden language. But the computer did not understand German. It only matched patterns. People still had to test each guess and make sense of the words. A computer is a powerful , but it needs a thinking person to guide it.

The Word for Eye

Main ideaBeáta Megyesi noticed a symbol meaning "eye," and Christiane Schaefer linked it to a society called the Oculists.

As German words began to appear, Beáta Megyesi noticed something. One symbol seemed to stand for a whole word: "eye." In German that word is Auge. The word eye seemed to come up again and again.

Why would a book care so much about eyes? Christiane Schaefer found the link. She connected the book to a secret society called the . The name comes from a Latin word for eye. It is also the root of the English word ocular, which means about the eyes.

That one small word opened the whole book. Once the team knew the subject, other words made sense. In 2011 they shared how they did it in a paper for other scientists. Many news reporters around the world told the story.

14.6

Why Teams Crack Codes

Main ideaThe Copiale was cracked because people with different strengths shared what they knew.

Each person on the Copiale team brought something different. Knight knew how to make computers find patterns. Megyesi knew languages and how to study them with computers. Schaefer studies old languages and their history.

No one of them had every skill. Together, they did. This happens often in science and history. Big puzzles get solved when people share what they know. It is called , which means working together toward one goal.

Think about your own strengths. Maybe you notice tiny details. Maybe you remember words well. Maybe you stay calm when a puzzle is hard. Every one of those strengths helps a codebreaking team. Asking for help is also a skill.

Section 3

The Society of the Eye

14.7

Who Were the Oculists?

Main ideaThe Oculists were a secret society in German-speaking lands whose ceremonies used the eye as a symbol for seeing truth.

The decoded Copiale describes the ceremonies of the Oculists. A ceremony is a set of actions done in a special order for an important event. Many of these were for bringing in new members. The ceremonies were built around eyes and seeing.

Some summaries say the Oculists were a club of eye doctors. The researchers read it differently. They saw the eye as a symbol. To "see" meant to learn and understand the truth. People still talk this way. We say "I see" when we mean "I understand."

The Oculists seem to have been like the Freemasons in some ways. They met in secret, used symbols and had steps for joining. There were many clubs like this in Europe in the 1700s. The Copiale gives us a rare look inside one of them.

14.8

What the Secret Kept

Main ideaThe Copiale's secrets turned out to be ceremonies and lessons, not crimes or treasure.

Before it was cracked, people could imagine anything about the Copiale. A hidden plot? A treasure? A dark crime? Secret books make people wonder. But when the words were read, they described ceremonies and lessons for members.

Members of groups like the Oculists had their own reasons for secrecy. The ceremonies felt special because they were private. New members did not know what was coming. That made the moment of joining feel important. Secrecy also helped members trust one another.

Outsiders often saw it another way. In the 1700s, some critics feared that secret clubs hid plots against rulers or churches. Those fears were real feelings. But in this case, the evidence shows the secret protected a ceremony. Historians let the evidence decide.

Section 4

The Art of Memory

14.9

A Memory Test for Masons

Main ideaIn 1599, William Schaw's rules told Scottish masons to be tested on the art of memory.

William Schaw was the Master of Works for King James VI of Scotland. That means he was in charge of the king’s buildings. He also wrote rules for the lodges of stonemasons. These rules are called the . A statute is a written rule.

The first set came in December 1598. A second set followed in December 1599. One part spoke to the lodge at Kilwinning, a town in Scotland. It said the lodge leaders must test every skilled mason and every on "the art of memory." An apprentice is a person learning a trade.

The old Scots words say "airt of memorie." The rules even set a fine, a money penalty, for a mason who forgot. Why would builders be tested on memory? Masons had to keep a lot in their heads. They needed building skills. They also needed the rules and words of their lodge.

From the source
The Kilwinning Rule
The leaders of the lodge at Kilwinning must test the art of memory, and the knowledge of it, of every skilled mason and every apprentice, each by their level. A mason who fails is to pay a fine.
Second Schaw Statutes, signed by William Schaw, 28 December 1599 (modern English wording; see The Square magazine transcription, 2022) (paraphrased)

Who had to be tested, and what happened to a mason who failed? Why might a lodge care so much about memory?

14.10

Memory Palaces

Main ideaA memory palace is a way to remember things by placing them in a place you know well.

An old story comes from ancient Greece. The Roman writer Cicero told it. A poet named Simonides was at a big dinner. He stepped outside, and the roof of the hall fell in. Simonides could name every guest by remembering where each one had sat. It is told as a legend, and it gave people an idea.

The idea is called a . You picture a place you know well, like your home. Then you "put" each thing you need to remember in a spot along the way. To remember, you take a walk in your mind. You find each thing where you left it.

What did Schaw mean by the art of memory? Historian David Stevenson thinks it may mean memory methods like this one. They were popular in Schaw’s time. Other historians are not sure how far to take this idea. The rules do not explain the words, so the question stays open.

14.11

Codes That Help You Remember

Main ideaLater, some Masons kept pocket-size books in cipher to help them remember their ceremonies.

Codes and memory worked together for Masons. Masons were expected to learn their ceremonies by heart. That is a lot of words to remember. So some kept small books to help them study.

The Scottish Rite Masonic Museum and Library in Lexington, Massachusetts, keeps several of these books. One is a pocket-size notebook in . Another is a whole ceremony book in cipher from about 1920. Masons saw them as private study helps. Critics saw them as proof of hidden secrets.

You can use both tools too. Try a memory palace for spelling words or science facts. Or make a small study card in your own cipher. Everyone learns in their own way. Pictures, places, codes and practice can all help your brain hold on to ideas.
