Close Reading the Analects

The Analects

The Analects is a book of short sayings. Some are one sentence long. That makes them easy to quote and easy to misread. In this unit you slow down. You read famous passages from books 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 13 and 15 in James Legge's 1893 translation, word for word.

You will ask the questions close readers ask. Who is speaking, the Master or a disciple? Who is he speaking to? What English word did Legge choose, and what other words could fit? What do the nearby sayings add? How have Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Western readers taken the same line?

By the end you will be able to cite the Analects by book and chapter, explain key terms such as ren, li, xiao, de, shu and junzi, and write about a passage by describing what it says and how its readers have understood it.

The story

Still Listening at Sixty

A student reads Analects 2.4 aloud, and her grandfather finds his own age in it.

Mei has homework for her tenth-grade English class. She has to read one short passage aloud to someone at home and write down what they say. She picks Analects 2.4 from James Legge's 1893 translation, because it is short and it has numbers in it.

She reads it at the kitchen table. "At fifteen, I had my mind bent on learning. At thirty, I stood firm. At forty, I had no doubts. At fifty, I knew the decrees of Heaven." Her grandfather is peeling an orange. He stops when she reaches the line about sixty.

"At sixty, my ear was an obedient organ for the reception of truth," Mei reads. Her grandfather laughs. He says he is sixty-one, so he must be at the listening stage. He says his own teacher in Taipei made the class learn this passage by heart, and he still hears it differently every ten years.

Mei writes that down. Then she notices something. The passage is in the first person. It is Confucius describing his own life, not giving a rule for everyone. Her grandfather says some readers treat it as a map for every life, and others read it as one man's memory. Mei is not sure which reading fits better.

Talk about itIs Analects 2.4 a rule for every life, or one man's account of his own? What words in the passage help you decide?
Section 1

How the Analects Opens

23.1

The First Three Questions

Main ideaAnalects 1.1 opens the book with three questions about learning, friendship and staying calm when no one notices you.

The Analects begins with three questions from the Master. Legge’s 1893 translation gives them this way: "Is it not pleasant to learn with a constant perseverance and application? Is it not delightful to have friends coming from distant quarters? Is he not a man of complete virtue, who feels no discomposure though men may take no note of him?"

Each question expects the answer yes. That is a : a question asked to make a point, not to get information. The form invites the listener to agree rather than ordering them to. Many readers think this gentle form suits a teacher talking with students.

The three questions move outward. The first is about the learner alone. The second is about friends who share the learning. The third is about the wider world, which may ignore the learner. Traditional commentators such as Zhu Xi read the order as a path from private study to public life.

The third question uses the phrase that Legge renders "a man of complete virtue." The Chinese term is . It first meant the son of a ruler, a noble by birth. The Analects uses it for a person noble in character. Legge usually translates it as "the superior man."

23.2

Who Is Speaking?

Main ideaNot every saying in the Analects is from Confucius; some, like 1.2, come from his disciples.

Analects 1.1 begins "The Master said." Analects 1.2 does not. It begins with the words of Yu Zi, a disciple. Legge gives his name in the older spelling of his day. Close readers always check the opening line of a saying, because it tells you who is talking.

The Analects is a : a book gathered from many sources by later hands. Most scholars think disciples and their own students collected the sayings over several generations after Confucius died, around 479 BCE by the traditional date. Some books may be later than others. Scholars still debate the order and the dates.

Because of this, a can speak with real weight in the book. Yu Zi and Zeng Zi are even called "Zi," "master," a sign of respect. Some scholars read that title as a clue that their own followers helped shape the collection. So when you cite a line, name the speaker, not just "Confucius."

23.3

Counting Books and Chapters

Main ideaAnalects 2.4 means book 2, chapter 4, but some editions divide the chapters in slightly different places.

The Analects has 20 books. Each book holds short numbered chapters. A citation like 2.4 means book 2, chapter 4. Legge uses this book.chapter system, and most English translations follow it. That is why a reader can find the same passage in almost any edition.

The numbers are not perfect. Editors have sometimes split one saying into two chapters, or joined two into one. So book 10, for example, is numbered differently in some translations. A saying can be one number off. Careful writers name the translation they used, so a reader can find the same line.

The chapter numbers are not in the oldest texts. The early Chinese text was written as a stream of sayings. Later editors and commentators marked where each saying began. The numbering is a tool for readers. It is not part of what the disciples first wrote down.

23.4

Reading Legge Today

Main ideaLegge's 1893 English is old and sometimes wordy, but it is exact enough to quote and is free for anyone to use.

James Legge was a Scottish missionary and scholar who spent many years in Hong Kong. He worked with Chinese scholars, including Wang Tao, on his translations. His first volume of the Analects came out in 1861, and a revised edition in 1893. Later he became the first professor of Chinese at Oxford.

Legge’s English sounds old today. He writes "the superior man" for junzi and "propriety" for , the rites and good manners of the tradition. He puts his own added words in brackets or italics to show what is not in the Chinese. Modern translators often choose shorter words.

Because Legge’s translation is in the , anyone may quote it freely. That is one reason this course uses it. Readers also note that Legge’s Christian background sometimes shaped his word choices. Scholars compare him with later translators to see where his readings are his own.

The Root: Family Duty

23.5

Filial Piety as the Root

Main ideaIn Analects 1.2 the disciple Yu Zi calls filial piety and respect for elder brothers the root of goodness.

Analects 1.2 closes with a question from Yu Zi. Legge translates it: "Filial piety and fraternal submission!—are they not the root of all benevolent actions?" The saying uses a picture from farming. A plant grows from its root. Yu Zi suggests that goodness grows from how a person acts at home.

is the Chinese word Legge renders "filial piety." It means respect and care for one’s parents and ancestors. "Fraternal submission" is respect for older brothers. The word "benevolent" translates , the Analects’ word for full human goodness.

Readers have taken this saying in different ways. Many Confucian teachers read it as the order of learning: first home, then the wider world. Some modern critics worry that it puts family loyalty above fairness to strangers. Others answer that the root is where goodness starts, not where it ends.

23.6

More Than Feeding

Main ideaIn Analects 2.7 Confucius says caring for parents means reverence, not only food and shelter.

A disciple named Ziyou asks about filial piety in Analects 2.7. The Master answers with a sharp comparison. In Legge’s words: "The filial piety of now-a-days means the support of one’s parents. But dogs and horses likewise are able to do something in the way of support;—without reverence, what is there to distinguish the one support given from the other?"

The point turns on the word : deep respect shown in attitude and action. Food and a roof are needed. But the Master says they are not enough on their own. What makes care filial is the respect behind it. The comparison with animals is meant to make the listener stop and think.

Commentators read the dogs and horses line in two ways. One reading says people also feed their dogs and horses, so feeding parents is no special honor. Another says dogs and horses themselves serve people. Both readings reach the same end: support without reverence falls short of xiao.

23.7

Filial Piety in Living Families

Main ideaXiao is read and practiced in many ways today, from ancestor rites to daily care, and people disagree about its limits.

Many families in China, Taiwan, Korea, Vietnam and the Chinese diaspora still honor parents and ancestors in visible ways. At Qingming in spring, families visit graves and sweep them. Some families keep ancestor tablets at home and offer food or incense. Families in Illinois do these things too.

People connect these practices to xiao in different ways. For some it is a religious duty to ancestors. For others it is family memory and respect. Whether Confucian ancestor rites are religious is itself debated by scholars and by the families who keep them. This course names the views and does not settle it.

Modern readers also debate xiao’s limits. Some ask how it fits with a young person’s own choices. Some point out that tradition asked more of daughters-in-law than of sons. Others defend xiao as a strong bond across generations. Close reading helps: 2.7 asks for reverence, not blind obedience.

Section 3

Ruling by Virtue

23.8

The North Polar Star

Main ideaAnalects 2.1 compares a ruler who governs by virtue to the pole star, which stays still while all stars turn toward it.

Analects 2.1 is one sentence long. Legge translates: "He who exercises government by means of his virtue may be compared to the north polar star, which keeps its place and all the stars turn towards it." The image is taken from the night sky. The pole star seems to stay fixed while the others circle around it.

"Virtue" here translates . In early Chinese, de meant a kind of moral power or pull. A person of de draws others without force. So the ruler in 2.1 does not chase the people. The people turn to him, the way the stars seem to turn around the pole.

An compares two things to explain one of them. Analects 2.1 uses an analogy from astronomy to explain government. Readers ask where the analogy stops. Stars have no choice. People do. Some commentators say that is the point: good rule works like a natural pull, not a command.

23.9

Shame, Not Just Punishment

Main ideaAnalects 2.3 says laws and punishments make people avoid penalties, but virtue and ritual give them a sense of shame and make them good.

Analects 2.3 sets two kinds of government side by side. Legge: "If the people be led by laws, and uniformity sought to be given them by punishments, they will try to avoid the punishment, but have no sense of shame. If they be led by virtue, and uniformity sought to be given them by the rules of propriety, they will have the sense of shame, and moreover will become good."

The saying is built as a : two sentences with the same shape and different content. Laws are paired with punishments. Virtue is paired with propriety, or li. The results differ too. The first gives people a way to escape penalties. The second changes what they feel inside.

Later thinkers argued with this idea. The Legalist writers, such as Han Feizi, held that clear laws and firm rewards and punishments keep order. Confucian readers answered with 2.3. Many modern readers see a middle ground, with both laws and shared values. The Analects’ own position is clear: virtue and li work deeper.

23.10

A Life in Stages

Main ideaIn Analects 2.4 Confucius describes his own life in six stages, from setting his mind on learning at fifteen to following his heart at seventy.

Analects 2.4 is one of the best-known passages in the book. Legge’s version ends: "At sixty, my ear was an obedient organ for the reception of truth. At seventy, I could follow what my heart desired, without transgressing what was right." It is spoken in the first person, as the Master’s own story.

The stages move from effort to ease. At fifteen the Master sets his mind on learning. At thirty he stands firm. At fifty he knows the decrees of Heaven, , the high power that early Chinese thought linked to order and fate. At seventy, what he wants and what is right no longer pull apart.

Readers use the passage in different ways. In Chinese, the ages have become common names: "standing firm" for thirty, for instance. Some readers take the passage as a model for anyone’s life. Others read it as one person’s , a look back over his own growth. The text itself uses "I."

The story

One Word for a Whole Life

A disciple asks for a single word to live by, and the Master gives him one.

The scene in Analects 15.23 is very short. A disciple named Zigong, a clever speaker and a successful merchant, comes to the Master with a big request. He does not want a long lesson. He wants one word.

In James Legge's 1893 translation, Zigong asks: "Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?" It is a bold question. Zigong is known in the Analects for quick answers. Here he asks for the shortest answer of all.

The Master replies: "Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others." Legge prints the key word in capital letters. The Chinese word is shu. Legge chose "reciprocity," a word about give and take between people.

Readers have returned to this exchange for more than two thousand years. Some hear a simple rule. Some notice that it tells you what not to do rather than what to do. Others ask why one word needs a whole sentence to explain it. The scene is small, but the questions it raises are large.

24.1

To Love All Men

Main ideaIn Analects 12.22 the Master answers a question about ren with a short reply: it is to love others.

Analects 12.22 opens with a student named Fan Chi. Legge writes: "Fan Ch’ih asked about benevolence. The Master said, ’It is to love all men.’" Legge uses "benevolence" for ren. The Chinese word for "men" here, ren 人, means people in general, not only males.

is the central virtue of the Analects. It is often translated goodness, humaneness or benevolence. The Chinese character joins the sign for a person with the sign for two. Many teachers point out this shape to suggest that ren lives between people. Scholars debate how much weight that picture can bear.

The Master gives different answers about ren to different students. To Fan Chi he says love people. To others he says other things. Many readers think he fits each answer to the person asking. So no single line defines ren. A close reader gathers several answers and compares them.

24.2

Returning to Ritual

Main ideaIn Analects 12.1 the Master links ren to li: subduing oneself and returning to propriety.

Analects 12.1 is the first chapter of book 12, and it also answers a question about ren. This time the student is Yan Hui, the Master’s favorite. Legge’s reply reads: "To subdue one’s self and return to propriety, is perfect virtue." Here Legge writes "perfect virtue" for ren.

The saying links ren with li, ritual and proper conduct. "Subdue one’s self" means holding back one’s own selfish wants. "Return to propriety" means acting in the proper forms. Yan Hui then asks for details. The Master tells him to look, listen, speak and move only in line with li.

Readers argue about how inner goodness and outer ritual fit together. Some stress that li shapes the heart over time, like practice shapes a musician. Others stress that ritual without real feeling is empty, which the Analects also says in other places. Many readers see ren and li as two sides of one way of life.

Yan Hui's Bamboo Dish

Main ideaAnalects 6.9 praises Yan Hui for keeping his joy while living in poverty.

Yan Hui was poor. Analects 6.9 describes his life. Legge: "Admirable indeed was the virtue of Hui! With a single bamboo dish of rice, a single gourd dish of drink, and living in his mean narrow lane, while others could not have endured the distress, he did not allow his joy to be affected by it." The line about Hui’s virtue is repeated at the end.

The passage uses : it opens and closes with the same line of praise. This frame tells the reader what matters most. The details in the middle, a bamboo dish and a gourd cup, make the poverty concrete. "Mean" in Legge’s English means poor and humble, not unkind.

Readers ask what Yan Hui’s joy came from. Many Confucian readers, including the Song dynasty scholars, took it as the joy of learning and goodness. It is not joy in being poor. The Analects also records that Yan Hui died young, and that the Master grieved deeply for him.

Think it through

Why frame the passage with the same line twice? What do the small details in the middle add?

Section 2

Reciprocity

24.4

The Negative Golden Rule

Main ideaAnalects 15.23 states a rule of reciprocity in the negative: do not do to others what you do not want done to yourself.

In Analects 15.23 Zigong asks for one word to guide a whole life. Legge’s Master answers: "Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others." Many readers call this a version of the , a rule found in many traditions about treating others as you would be treated.

The form matters. The saying tells you what not to do. Scholars call this the negative form. Other traditions state the rule in a positive form: do to others what you would want. Readers argue about the difference. Some say the negative form is more modest. Others say the two forms end up close.

Legge himself, in his notes, argued that the negative form is weaker than the positive one. Many later scholars, Chinese and Western, disagree with him. They point out that the Analects also has positive statements of the same idea, such as 6.28. The debate shows how a translator’s views can travel with his translation.

From the source
One Word for Life
Tsze-kung asked, saying, "Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?" The Master said, "Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others."
Confucian Analects 15.23, trans. James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1, 2nd ed., 1893

24.5

The One Thread

Main ideaIn Analects 4.15 the disciple Zeng Zi explains the Master's one thread as loyalty and reciprocity.

Analects 4.15 is a puzzle scene. The Master tells Zeng Zi that his teaching has one thread running through it. Zeng Zi says yes, and the Master leaves. The other disciples ask what he meant. Zeng Zi answers. Legge: "The doctrine of our master is to be true to the principles of our nature and the benevolent exercise of them to others,—this and nothing more."

Legge’s long phrase stands for two short Chinese words. The first is , usually translated loyalty or doing one’s best. The second is shu, reciprocity. Legge’s wording is a reading, not just a translation. He explains the two words as he understands them. Modern translations are much shorter.

Notice who explains the thread. It is Zeng Zi, not the Master. Some scholars think Zeng Zi’s own followers helped shape this scene. Others take it as a faithful memory. Either way, readers link 4.15 with 15.23, where shu is named again. Reading the two together is a basic close-reading move.

Think it through
Two Passages, One Word

Analects 4.15: Zeng Zi names the Master's one thread as zhong and shu. Analects 15.23: the Master names shu, "reciprocity," as the one word for life.

What does 15.23 add to your understanding of 4.15? What does 4.15 add to 15.23?

24.6

How Translators Shape a Reading

Main ideaThe English words chosen for shu, ren and junzi change how readers hear the Analects.

Legge gives "reciprocity" for shu. Other translators have given "consideration," "like-hearted considerateness" or "understanding." Each choice points a reader somewhere. Reciprocity suggests a fair exchange. Consideration suggests imagining how another person feels. The Chinese character joins "like" or "as" with "heart."

Translation always involves choices. A is the feeling or extra meaning a word carries beyond its dictionary sense. "Superior man" carries a feeling of rank. "Exemplary person" carries a feeling of example. Readers who know only English meet the Analects through these connotations.

This is why scholars compare translations. They look for places where the English adds or loses meaning. Legge’s notes help, because he explains his choices. When you write about the Analects, name your translation and, where it matters, show one other version beside it.

Think it through

Legge (1893): "reciprocity." Other translators: "consideration," "like-hearted considerateness," "understanding." The Chinese character shu 恕 is built from 如, "as," over 心, "heart."

Names and the Junzi

24.7

Making Names Correct

Main ideaIn Analects 13.3 the Master says good government starts with making names correct, so that words match reality.

In Analects 13.3 the disciple Zilu asks what the Master would do first if he were asked to govern. The Master says he would make names correct. Zilu thinks this is a strange first step. The Master explains with a chain of results. Legge: "If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success."

This teaching is called the . To rectify means to set right. The idea is that words like "ruler," "father" or "minister" should match what the person actually does. When they do not, trust breaks down. The chain in 13.3 goes on to rites, music, punishments, and finally the people.

The passage uses a : each step leads to the next. Readers have applied it to many things. Some use it to discuss honesty in public speech. Some scholars link it to Analects 12.11, "let the ruler be ruler," where roles and names also meet.

Confucian Analects 13.3, trans. James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1, 2nd ed., 1893

24.8

The Junzi and the Small Man

Main ideaAnalects 4.16 contrasts the junzi, who thinks of what is right, with the small man, who thinks of gain.

Analects 4.16 is a pair in one line. Legge: "The mind of the superior man is conversant with righteousness; the mind of the mean man is conversant with gain." "Conversant with" means familiar with, or thinking about. The Chinese for "mean man" is , literally "small person."

The word for righteousness is : what is right or fitting to do. Many sayings in the Analects contrast the junzi and the xiaoren. The contrast is not about wealth or birth. It is about what a person’s mind turns to first. A poor person can be a junzi, as Yan Hui was.

Readers argue about how firm the line is. Some take it as two kinds of people. Others read it as two directions any person can face. The Analects never says profit is always wrong. Other passages say wealth gained rightly is fine. The point of 4.16 is what comes first.

Confucian Analects 4.16, trans. James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1, 2nd ed., 1893

24.9

Reading a Saying With Its Neighbors

Main ideaSayings in the Analects often sit in small groups, and reading nearby chapters can change what one line seems to mean.

Analects 4.15, the one thread, sits right before 4.16, the junzi and gain. Book 4 as a whole returns again and again to ren and the junzi. Many scholars think editors grouped sayings by theme. Reading one line alone, cut off from its neighbors, can miss that theme.

Here is a close-reading method. First, read the saying. Second, read two chapters before and after it. Third, note shared words, like ren, junzi or shu. Fourth, ask how the neighbors change or support your first reading. This is reading in : the words and setting around a passage.

Context can also mean history. Confucius lived in a time of warring states and failing rulers. Many readers think this is why 13.3 cares so much about titles that no longer fit. Other readers stress that the sayings speak beyond their time. Both kinds of context help a reader avoid a quote pulled loose.

Think it through

What shared idea connects these three neighbors? How does 4.17 change how you read 4.16?

24.10

Readers Across Asia

Main ideaKorean, Japanese and Vietnamese readers, as well as Chinese readers, have studied and debated these same sayings for centuries.

The Analects spread far beyond China. In Korea, Confucian learning shaped the Joseon dynasty’s schools and exams. In Japan, scholars such as Ito Jinsai read the Analects closely and challenged older commentaries. In Vietnam, royal exams tested the classics for centuries.

These readers did not simply copy Chinese views. Ito Jinsai, for example, argued for going back to the Analects itself rather than relying on Zhu Xi’s readings. Korean scholars debated human nature in long letters. Each place built its own of reading: a line of teachers and books handed down.

Today the Analects is read in schools, homes and universities across East Asia and around the world. Some readers study it as philosophy. Some keep it as a family and cultural heritage. Some question parts of it. Close readers can meet all of these voices and describe them without ranking them.
