Stories and True Books

Reading

Open a book. Is it a story? Or is it true? A frog in a red hat is a story. A photo of a real frog is true. Both are good. But you read them in different ways.

In a story, look for the characters, the setting and the events. Find the problem and the fix. Then retell it in order. In a true book, look for the topic, the headings, the labels and the captions. Find the facts.

By the end of this unit, you will be able to open any book and say what kind it is, tell what it is about, and find what you want to know inside it.

The big question

How do you read a story, and how do you read a true book?

The steps
Cover

Look at the cover. Read the title. Find the author and the illustrator.

Guess

Is it a story or a true book? Look for a talking animal or a photo of a real thing.

Who

In a story, find the characters. In a true book, find the topic.

Where

Find the setting: where and when the story happens.

Problem

Spot the problem. Watch for what the character wants.

Headings

In a true book, use the table of contents and headings to find the part you need.

Pictures

Read the pictures, the labels and the captions. They hold facts too.

Fix

Find the solution. See how the problem gets fixed.

Retell

Close the book. Tell it in order: first, next, then, last.

Ask

Ask who, what, where, when and why. Answer each one from the book.

Lesson

Say what the story teaches, or what fact you learned from the true book.

The story

The Slow One and the Fast One

A very old tale. A hare who is fast. A tortoise who is slow. Guess who wins.

Ms. Ortiz reads to her class in Chicago. It is a very old tale. A hare can run fast. A tortoise walks slow. The hare laughs at the tortoise. So the tortoise says, "Let us race." The hare says yes.

The race starts. The hare zooms off. He is far ahead. He looks back. The tortoise is a tiny dot. The hare says, "I have time." He lies down under a tree. He falls asleep.

The tortoise does not stop. Step. Step. Step. She walks past the sleeping hare. She walks to the line. The hare wakes up. He runs as fast as he can. Too late. The tortoise wins.

The class talks about it. Jae says, "The hare was fast, but he took a nap." Ruby says, "The tortoise never quit." Ms. Ortiz smiles. "Now you know what the story is about," she says.

Talk about itThe hare is faster. Why did the tortoise win the race?

The Parts of a Story

5.1

Who Is in the Story

Main ideaThe characters are the people or animals a story is about.

Think about the race. A hare. A tortoise. They are the ones in the story. They talk. They run. They do things. We call them the .

A character can be a person. A character can be an animal. A character can even be a talking cup! If it does things in the story, it is a character.

When you open a book, ask, "Who is this about?" Look at the pictures. Look at the names. Find the characters first. Then the story makes sense.

Who are the characters in this story?

5.2

Where and When It Happens

Main ideaThe setting is where and when a story happens.

The hare and the tortoise race on a road. It is a sunny day. There is a tree. The hare naps under it. The road, the day, the tree. That is the .

The setting is where a story happens. It is also when. A story can happen in a city. It can happen on the moon. It can happen at night or long ago.

The setting changes the story. Snow means cold. A dark forest means scary. Look at the pictures to find the setting. Ask, "Where are they? When is it?"

5.3

What Happens

Main ideaEvents are the things that happen in a story, one after another.

The race starts. The hare runs ahead. The hare takes a nap. The tortoise walks past. The tortoise wins. Each thing that happens is an .

Events come in order. First one thing. Then the next. Then the next. If you mix them up, the story breaks. The hare cannot nap before the race starts!

When you read, keep a list in your head. What happened first? What happened next? What happened last? Those are the events.

Think it through

What was the first event? What was the last one?

Check yourself

1. What is an event?

Why: An event is one thing that happens. The hare taking a nap is an event.

2. What happens after the hare takes a nap?

Why: While the hare sleeps, the tortoise keeps walking and passes him.

3. Why do the events need to stay in order?

Why: Events happen one after another. The hare cannot nap before the race starts.

5.4

What a Character Wants

Main ideaEvery character wants something, and that want moves the story.

The hare wants to win. The tortoise wants to win too. She also wants the hare to stop laughing at her. A is what a character is trying to get.

Ask, "What does this character want?" Then watch what they do. The tortoise wants to win. So she keeps walking. She never stops. Her want tells you why she does it.

Every story has a want. A dog wants a bone. A girl wants a friend. A boy wants to fly a kite. The want makes the story go.

5.5

How a Character Feels

Main ideaWords and pictures show how a character feels.

The hare laughs at the tortoise. He feels proud. He feels sure he will win. At the end, he loses. Now he feels surprised. Maybe a little sad. These are his .

A book does not always say the feeling. It shows it. A big smile means happy. Tears mean sad. Stomping feet mean mad. Look at the face in the picture. Look at what the character says.

Feelings can change. The hare felt proud at the start. He felt shocked at the end. Ask, "How does the character feel now?" Ask it again later.

5.6

The Problem and the Fix

Main ideaMost stories have a problem, and the story shows how it gets solved.

The tortoise has a . The hare is faster. How can she win? A problem is something that is wrong or hard for a character.

The story shows the fix. The tortoise keeps going. The hare naps. She passes him. The problem is solved. That is the . A solution is how the problem gets fixed.

Find the problem in every story. Then find the solution. Sam lost his mitten. The dog found it. Problem, then solution. Most stories work this way.

Think it through

5.7

Tell It in Order

Main ideaTo retell a story, say the events in order: first, next, then, last.

Close the book. Now tell the story. First, the hare laughs at the tortoise. Next, they race. Then the hare naps. Last, the tortoise wins. That is a .

Use order words. First. Next. Then. Last. They keep the events in line. Say who the characters are. Say where it happens. Say the problem and the fix.

The pictures can help you. Flip through them. Each picture shows an event. Point to a picture. Say what happens. Move to the next one.

5.8

Ask Good Questions

Main ideaWho, what, where, when and why questions help you understand a story.

Who raced? The hare and the tortoise. Where? On a road. When? On a sunny day. What happened? The tortoise won. Why? The hare took a nap. These are the .

Who asks about the characters. Where and when ask about the setting. What asks about the events. Why asks for the reason. Every story can answer all five.

Ask them before you read. Ask them while you read. Ask them after. If you can answer all five, you understand the story.

5.9

Two Tales, One Lesson

Main ideaTwo versions of a tale can differ, but each story teaches a lesson.

Ms. Ortiz reads a second book. This time the race is in a city. The hare rides a scooter. The tortoise walks. The hare stops for ice cream. The tortoise still wins.

Two books can tell the same tale. That is a . The setting changed. The hare’s nap became ice cream. But the characters, the problem and the ending stayed the same.

Both books teach the same . Keep going and do not quit. A lesson is what the story wants you to learn. Ask, "What did the character learn?" That is the lesson.

Section 1

Made Up or True

6.1

Some Books Are Made Up

Main ideaA story book is made up by the author, even if it feels real.

Pip the frog wears a hat. Pip talks. Pip hops to the pond to find his hat. None of this really happened. The author made it up. That makes it a story book.

A story book has characters. It has a problem. It has events in order. It can have talking animals. It can have magic. It does not have to be real.

Made up does not mean bad. Stories are fun. Stories teach lessons. But you do not use a story to learn facts. Pip cannot teach you what real frogs eat.

Think it through

What on this page tells you the book is made up?

Words to know
made up
not real; something the author thought up
Check yourself

1. What does made up mean?

Why: Made up means the author thought it up. Pip the frog is not real.

2. Which one shows a book is a story book?

Why: Real frogs do not talk or wear hats. A talking frog means the book is made up.

3. Can a story book teach you what real frogs eat?

Why: Stories are fun and teach lessons, but facts come from true books.
6.2

Some Books Are True

Main ideaA true book gives facts about real things.

A true book is about one thing. It might be frogs. It might be trucks. It might be the moon. It tells you facts about that thing. The pictures show real things.

You read a true book to learn. You can start at any page. You can read just the part you need. You do not have to read it in order like a story.

6.3

Headings Tell You What Is Next

Main ideaA heading is a big title on a page that tells what that part is about.

Ruby turns a page. Big dark words sit at the top: What Frogs Eat. Under them, smaller words tell about flies and bugs. The big words are a .

A heading is like a sign. It tells you what the next part is about. Frog Eggs. Where Frogs Live. What Frogs Eat. Each heading starts a new part.

Use the headings to find things. Want to know what frogs eat? Look for that heading. Skip the rest. Headings save you time.

Labels name parts. Captions tell about the whole picture. Read both. Sometimes the caption has a fact that is not in the big words.

Write it

Pick one book you know. Is it a story book or a true book? Say which one. Then give two things from the book that show you are right.

  • Start with your claim: This book is a story book, or This book is a true book.
  • Give proof. Does an animal talk? Are there photos of real things? Are there headings or captions?
  • Say why your proof shows it. Talking animals mean made up. Facts and photos mean true.
  • Think about the other side. Could someone say it is the other kind? Say why they are wrong.
