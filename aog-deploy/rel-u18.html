Hinduism: Dharma, Karma and the Gita

Traditions

About 1.2 billion people, roughly one in seven people on Earth, are Hindus. On any given morning, many of them light a lamp at a home shrine, offer a flower to an image of Ganesha or Krishna, or chant a verse older than any other scripture still in daily use. In the Chicago area, Hindu families worship at temples in Lemont, Aurora and beyond, shop for festival sweets on Devon Avenue, and take their children to weekend classes to learn the great stories.

Hinduism is hard to fit into the categories people often use for religion. It has no single founder, no single book and no central authority. It includes worshipers of Vishnu, of Shiva and of the Goddess, philosophers who speak of one ultimate reality, and poets who sing their love for a personal God. Its texts range from the ancient Vedas and Upanishads to the epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, and the Bhagavad Gita, which Swami Vivekananda quoted to an American audience in Chicago in 1893.

In this unit you will read those texts and the ideas they carry: Brahman and atman, samsara and karma, dharma and moksha. You will meet the many deities and the several paths Hindus describe, visit puja and festivals, and study the history and reform of caste. Then you will sit in Arjuna's chariot, follow Krishna's answer, and see how readers from Shankara to Gandhi have understood it. By the end you should be able to explain these ideas accurately, attribute them to the traditions that hold them, and compare readings fairly.

The big question

How do Hindu traditions answer the question of how to live rightly in the world while seeking freedom from it?

The story

Lamps on a West Ridge Windowsill

On one autumn night, a row of small clay lamps turns an ordinary Chicago apartment into a place of welcome.

It is a cold evening in late October, a few blocks from Devon Avenue on Chicago's North Side. The sari shops and sweet shops along the avenue have been busy all week. Inside a second-floor apartment, a sophomore named Priya is kneeling by the front window with a box of matches. Her grandmother hands her small clay lamps, one at a time. Each lamp is a shallow dish of oil with a cotton wick. Priya lights them and sets them in a row along the sill.

The lamps are diyas, and tonight is Diwali, the festival of lights. Her grandmother explains, as she does every year, that the lights welcome Lakshmi, the goddess of good fortune, into a clean and bright home. Then she tells the other story too: how Prince Rama came home to the city of Ayodhya after fourteen years in exile, and how the people lit lamps to guide him. Priya has heard both stories since she was small. She asks which one is the real reason for the lamps.

Her grandmother laughs. In her family's village in Gujarat, she says, people told it one way. Her friend from Tamil Nadu tells it another way. Her neighbor downstairs, a Jain family, lights lamps tonight for a different teacher altogether, and Sikh families in the suburbs celebrate their own festival on the same night. The lights mean all of these things at once, she says. That is not a problem to solve. That is how it works.

On the small shelf by the kitchen, there is a home shrine. It holds a brass image of Ganesha with his elephant head, a picture of Krishna playing a flute, and a small lamp that is lit every morning. Priya's father will say a short prayer there before the family eats sweets and the younger cousins run outside with sparklers. The apartment smells of ghee, incense and fried dough.

Priya's question is a good place to begin studying Hinduism. Many religions trace themselves to one founder or one book. Hinduism has neither. It is a large family of traditions that grew in South Asia over more than three thousand years. It holds many texts, many forms of worship and many ways of naming the divine. This chapter follows the threads that tie that family together.

A Family of Traditions

35.1

One Name, Many Paths

Main ideaHinduism is not one organization with one founder; it is a family of related traditions that grew in South Asia over thousands of years.

Ask a Hindu in Chicago, in Mumbai and in Bali to describe their religion, and you may hear three different answers. One may speak mostly of the god Vishnu. Another may center her life on the goddess Durga. A third may talk about meditation and the self, with little mention of any deity. Scholars therefore describe as a family of traditions, not a single church. It has no founder, no single sacred book and no central authority that speaks for everyone.

The word itself came from outside. Ancient Persians used the name of the Sindhu, the Indus River, for the people who lived beyond it. Over centuries, Hindu became a label for the people and traditions of India. Many Hindus prefer an older name from their own texts: , often translated as the eternal way or the eternal order. The name suggests a truth that was not invented at one moment but has always been there.

What holds the family together? Most Hindus share certain things. They honor the Vedas as sacred, even if few read them. They share ideas such as karma, rebirth and dharma. They worship in homes and temples through offerings called puja. And they tell the same great stories, such as the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. Within that shared frame, the variety is enormous, and adherents often see that variety as a strength.

Today there are about 1.2 billion Hindus, according to common estimates. Most live in India, where Hindus make up roughly four in five people, and in Nepal. Large communities also live in Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Indonesia’s island of Bali, Mauritius, the Caribbean, Britain and the United States. The Chicago area is home to many Hindu families, temples and cultural centers.

35.2

The Vedas: Heard and Remembered

Main ideaThe Vedas are the oldest Hindu scriptures, hymns and rituals in Sanskrit that were memorized and passed down by mouth for centuries before they were written.

Picture a boy in a traditional school in South India, sitting cross-legged in front of his teacher. The teacher chants a line in Sanskrit, and the boy repeats it exactly, with the same rise and fall of pitch. They do this for hours a day, for years. This is how the have been kept alive. For many centuries they were not written at all. They were memorized and chanted, with methods so careful that scholars think the wording changed very little.

The word Veda means knowledge. There are four Vedas: the Rig Veda, the Sama Veda, the Yajur Veda and the Atharva Veda. The oldest, the Rig Veda, holds more than a thousand hymns. Scholars date its earliest parts to roughly 1500 to 1200 BCE, though estimates vary. Many hymns praise deities of nature and ritual, such as Agni, the god of fire, and Indra, a god of storm and war. Priests chanted them at fire sacrifices.

Hindus divide their sacred texts into two kinds. The word means what is heard. It refers to the Vedas, which many Hindus believe ancient seers heard or perceived rather than composed. The word means what is remembered. It includes later texts written by human authors, such as the great epics, the law books and the Puranas. For many Hindus, shruti carries the highest authority, even though smriti texts are far more widely read.

Few Hindus today read the Vedas cover to cover. Yet Vedic verses are still chanted at weddings, at the thread ceremony that marks a boy’s entry into study in some families, and at funerals. One famous verse, the Gayatri mantra from the Rig Veda, is recited daily by many Hindus as a prayer for light and understanding.

35.3

The Upanishads: Brahman and Atman

Main ideaThe Upanishads teach that behind the changing world is one ultimate reality, Brahman, and that the deepest self, atman, is one with it.

The are later texts attached to the Vedas, many composed around 800 to 300 BCE. Their name suggests sitting down near a teacher. Many are dialogues, in which a student questions a teacher about the deepest things: What is real? What am I? What remains when everything changes? Because they come at the end of the Vedas, they are also called Vedanta, the end or goal of the Veda.

One famous scene appears in the Chandogya Upanishad. A father, Uddalaka, tells his son Shvetaketu to drop salt into a cup of water and come back the next morning. The salt has vanished from sight, but every sip tastes salty. So it is, the father says, with the subtle essence of the world. You cannot see it, yet it is in everything. Then he adds the line that later teachers made famous: you are that.

That essence is , the one ultimate reality behind all things. The self at the core of each person is called . The Upanishads teach that atman is not the body, the thoughts or the personality, which all change. Many passages hold that atman and Brahman are, at bottom, the same. To realize this truth is to be freed from ignorance.

Hindu thinkers have read these texts in different ways. The teacher Shankara, around the 8th century CE, taught Advaita, or non-dualism: atman and Brahman are one, and separateness is an illusion. Later teachers such as Ramanuja held that the soul is real and distinct but depends wholly on a personal God. Both schools claim the Upanishads as their source.

35.4

Samsara and Karma

Main ideaMany Hindus believe the soul is reborn again and again in samsara, and that karma, the result of one's actions, shapes each new life.

At a Hindu funeral, the body is usually cremated, and the family may later scatter the ashes in a river, ideally the Ganges. Mourners often say that the soul has moved on, not that it has ended. Many Hindus believe the atman sheds a body the way a person sheds worn-out clothes, then takes a new one. This round of birth, death and rebirth is called , which means wandering or flowing on.

What decides the next life? The answer is . The word means action, and it also means the result that action brings. In the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, a teacher says that as a person acts, so that person becomes: good actions make one good, harmful actions make one harmful. Karma is not seen as reward and punishment handed out by a judge. Many Hindus describe it more like a law of nature, the way a seed grows into its own kind of plant.

Karma includes thoughts and intentions, not only deeds. It can shape the circumstances of a future life, and many believe it also shapes character in this life. Hindus do not all agree on every detail. Some stress that a gracious God can soften or remove karma. Others stress personal effort. But most agree that each person’s choices matter, because they carry forward.

Samsara is not usually seen as a happy endless adventure. Even good rebirths end in death and more rebirth. For that reason, many Hindu texts describe samsara as something to be escaped. The next two lessons explain the ideas of right living and final release that answer this problem. Together, samsara, karma, dharma and moksha form a framework most Hindus share.

35.5

Dharma: Duty and Right Order

Main ideaDharma is the right way of living, the duty and moral order that fit a person's role, stage of life and community.

A young woman in Chicago must decide whether to take a job across the country or stay near her aging parents. A Hindu grandmother might ask her a question: what is your here? Dharma is one of the richest words in Hindu thought. It can mean duty, law, right conduct, religion and the order that holds the world together. In daily life, it usually means doing what is right for your role and situation.

Classical Hindu texts teach that dharma is not the same for everyone. A parent, a student, a ruler and a teacher each have different duties. Some texts also describe four stages of life, called ashramas: student, householder, forest dweller, who steps back from worldly life, and renunciant, who gives up possessions to seek release. Few Hindus today follow all four stages strictly, but the ideal of life as a journey with changing duties remains.

The same texts name four goals of life, the . Dharma is right living. Artha is prosperity and success. Kama is pleasure, love and beauty. Moksha is final release from rebirth. The first three are seen as good when they are pursued within dharma. Wealth and love are not condemned, but they are meant to serve a well-ordered life.

Hindus also speak of general duties that apply to everyone, such as truthfulness, self-control and , not harming living beings. Many Hindus are vegetarian partly because of ahimsa. When personal duty and general duty seem to clash, the question becomes hard. That is exactly the problem Arjuna faces in the Bhagavad Gita, as the next chapter shows.

35.6

Moksha: Release from the Wheel

Main ideaMoksha is the final goal in much Hindu thought: freedom from the cycle of rebirth, understood in different ways by different schools.

In the holy city of Varanasi, on the Ganges River, cremation fires burn day and night on stone steps called ghats. Many elderly Hindus travel there hoping to die in the city, because tradition holds that dying in Varanasi brings release. That release has a name: , freedom from samsara. Moksha means liberation. It is the fourth and highest of the four goals of life.

What is moksha like? Hindu schools answer differently. In the Advaita tradition, moksha is the realization that atman and Brahman were always one, like a drop of water returning to the sea. In devotional traditions, moksha is often pictured as eternal loving closeness to a personal God, such as Vishnu or Shiva, while the soul remains itself. Both agree that moksha ends rebirth and suffering.

How does one reach moksha? Again, Hindus describe several ways, which later lessons call paths or yogas. Some stress knowledge and insight. Some stress devotion and the grace of God. Some stress selfless action. Some stress meditation. Many Hindus combine them. Most also believe that moksha may take many lifetimes, which is why living well now still matters.

Not every Hindu thinks about moksha daily. Many focus on good karma, family duty, worship and a good rebirth. Scholars sometimes note that the tradition holds both goals side by side: living well within the world, and seeking release from it. Each family and teacher balances the two in its own way.

Vishnu and His Avatars

Main ideaVishnu, the preserver, is worshiped by many Hindus as the supreme God who descends to earth in forms called avatars, including Rama and Krishna.

In a Hindu temple in Aurora, west of Chicago, the central image shows a standing, dark-skinned figure crowned with gold. He is Venkateswara, a form of widely worshiped in South India. Devotees line up to see him, a moment of worship called , seeing and being seen by the deity. Worshipers of Vishnu are called Vaishnavas, and they form one of the largest groups of Hindus.

Hindu texts often describe three great functions of the divine: Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves and Shiva destroys so that renewal can come. Scholars call this grouping the Trimurti. In practice, few Hindus worship Brahma, and many see either Vishnu or Shiva as the supreme God who performs all three roles.

Vaishnavas believe that when goodness weakens and evil grows, Vishnu descends to earth to restore order. Each descent is an . Tradition often lists ten, including a fish, a boar and a man-lion. The two most beloved are Rama, the ideal king of the Ramayana, and Krishna, the divine teacher of the Bhagavad Gita and the playful cowherd of many stories.

Many Hindus see no contradiction in honoring several deities. A common teaching, drawn from a Rig Veda verse, holds that truth is one, though the wise call it by many names. Some Hindus understand the deities as many faces of one divine reality. Others worship one supreme God and see the other deities as powerful beings under that God.

Shiva, Devi and Ganesha

Main ideaMany Hindus worship Shiva or the Goddess, Devi, as supreme, and nearly all honor Ganesha as the remover of obstacles.

A famous bronze sculpture from South India shows dancing inside a ring of flames. One foot stands on a small figure representing ignorance. One hand holds a drum, the sound of creation. Another holds fire, the force of destruction. Shiva’s worshipers, called Shaivas, see him as the supreme God who creates, preserves and destroys in an endless cosmic dance. He is also pictured as a great yogi meditating on a mountain.

Other Hindus center their worship on the Goddess, often called or Shakti, meaning divine power. Her followers are called Shaktas. She takes many forms. Durga rides a lion and defeats a buffalo demon. Kali, dark and fierce, destroys evil and ego. Lakshmi brings good fortune, and Saraswati brings learning and music. For Shaktas, the Goddess is the supreme power behind the universe.

, with his elephant head, is honored by nearly all Hindus, whatever deity they worship most. In one well-known story, he is the son of Shiva and the goddess Parvati. He is called the remover of obstacles, so many Hindus pray to him before starting anything new: a trip, an exam, a wedding or a business. His image often stands near the doorway of homes and temples.

These traditions overlap. A family may keep images of Ganesha, Krishna and Durga on the same shelf. Some Hindus, called Smartas, worship several deities as equal forms of the one Brahman. The tradition makes room for devotion to one chosen deity while respecting others.

35.9

Four Paths to the Goal

Main ideaHindu teachers describe several paths, or yogas, toward moksha: knowledge, devotion, selfless action and meditation.

Four people might all be walking toward the same goal. One sits with a teacher, studying the Upanishads and asking who she truly is. One sings devotional songs to Krishna every evening. One runs a free clinic and tries to work without seeking praise. One wakes before dawn to sit still and quiet the mind. Hindu tradition would say all four are practicing , a word that means joining or discipline.

The path of knowledge is . It uses study, reflection and insight to see through ignorance and recognize the true self. It is closely tied to the Upanishads and to Advaita teachers such as Shankara. A student on this path might ask, again and again, who am I, and set aside every answer that names only the body or the mind.

The path of devotion is : love and surrender to a personal God. Bhakti movements spread across India from about the 6th century CE onward, carried by poet-saints who sang in everyday languages instead of Sanskrit. The 16th-century poet Mirabai, a devotee of Krishna, is still sung today. Many scholars describe bhakti as the most widely practiced path today. The path of action is : doing one’s duty without selfish attachment to results, a teaching at the heart of the Bhagavad Gita.

The path of meditation is often called raja yoga, the royal path. It trains the body, breath and mind to reach deep stillness. It draws on the Yoga Sutras attributed to Patanjali. Many Hindus see these paths as suited to different temperaments, and most mix them. The Gita itself teaches all of them.

The charioteer is Krishna, his friend and cousin. Krishna has promised not to fight in this war, only to drive Arjuna's chariot. Arjuna looks across. He sees Bhishma, the great-uncle who helped raise him. He sees Drona, the teacher who taught him to shoot. He sees cousins, uncles, the sons of friends. His mouth goes dry. 36.1

The Mahabharata in Outline

Main ideaThe Mahabharata is a vast Sanskrit epic about a family war; the Bhagavad Gita is one short part of it.

The is one of the longest poems ever composed. In its fullest versions it runs to about 100,000 couplets, many times longer than Homer’s Iliad and Odyssey together. Tradition names the sage Vyasa as its author. Scholars think it grew over many centuries, roughly from 400 BCE to 400 CE, as generations of storytellers added to it. It belongs to the smriti texts, the remembered tradition.

At its heart is a quarrel between cousins. The five Pandava brothers, sons of King Pandu, are rivals of the hundred Kaurava brothers, led by Duryodhana, son of the blind king Dhritarashtra. In a rigged game of dice, the Pandavas lose everything, including their kingdom. Their shared wife, Draupadi, is dragged before the court and humiliated. The Pandavas are sent into exile for thirteen years.

When they return, Duryodhana refuses to give back even five villages. Both sides gather allies, and the war at Kurukshetra lasts eighteen days. Nearly every warrior dies. The Pandavas win, but the victory is full of grief. The epic asks hard questions about dharma: what is right when every choice causes harm?

The sits in the sixth of the epic’s eighteen books, just before the fighting begins. It has 700 verses in eighteen chapters. Because so many Hindus treat it as scripture on its own, it is often printed and read by itself, apart from the larger epic.

Main ideaKrishna first answers Arjuna's grief by teaching that the true self, the atman, is eternal and is not destroyed when the body dies.

Krishna compares death to changing clothes. As a person throws away worn-out garments and puts on new ones, the embodied self casts off worn-out bodies and enters new ones. 36.6

Action Without Attachment

Main ideaThe Gita's most famous teaching is to act according to one's duty while letting go of attachment to the results.

Think of a doctor who gives every patient her best effort. Some recover and some do not. If she measured herself only by results, she might burn out, or cut corners to protect her record. The Gita offers another way. In chapter 2, Krishna tells Arjuna that he has a right to his action, but never to its fruits. Do not let the fruit of action be your motive, he says, and do not cling to doing nothing.

This is the heart of , the path of action. Krishna does not tell Arjuna to withdraw from the world. He tells him to act, and to act well, but without selfish desire for a particular outcome. The Gita calls this , desireless action. Krishna says that yoga is evenness of mind in success and failure, and also that yoga is skill in action.

Why does this matter? In Hindu thought, action done from selfish desire binds a person to karma and rebirth. Action done without attachment, as an offering to God, does not bind. So the Gita turns ordinary work into a spiritual path. A farmer, a parent, a soldier or a student can walk toward liberation through daily duties.

The Gita also warns against doing someone else’s duty. It is better, Krishna says, to do one’s own dharma imperfectly than another’s well. Readers debate how to apply this. Some see it as support for traditional roles. Others read it as a call to find and do one’s true work.

After the vision, the Gita’s tone grows warmer. Krishna says that those who worship him with love are dear to him, and that he quickly lifts them out of the ocean of death and rebirth.

This is , loving devotion. The Gita teaches that anyone can take this path, whatever their birth, gender or learning. Near the end, in chapter 18, Krishna gives what many devotees call his final word: let go of all other duties, take refuge in me alone, and I will free you from all wrongs; do not grieve.

The Gita does not reject the paths of knowledge and action. It weaves them together: know the eternal self, act without attachment, and offer everything to God with love. Different schools weigh the three differently. Devotional movements such as the Vaishnava traditions often see bhakti as the crown of the Gita.

36.8

The Person of Steady Wisdom

Main ideaThe Gita describes an ideal person whose mind stays calm and steady, free from craving, fear and anger, while still acting in the world.

Arjuna asks a practical question in chapter 2: how does a person of steady wisdom speak, sit and walk? Krishna’s answer paints a portrait of the , the one whose wisdom is steady. Such a person is not shaken by sorrow and does not crave pleasure. Fear and anger have fallen away. Success and failure, praise and blame, leave the mind level.

Krishna uses two images. Such a person draws the senses back from their objects, the way a tortoise draws in its limbs. And such a person is like the ocean: rivers pour into it from every side, yet it stays within its shores. Desires still arise, but they do not overflow the mind.

Krishna also explains how a mind falls apart. Dwelling on things creates attachment. Attachment breeds desire, and desire, when blocked, turns to anger. Anger clouds judgment, and confused judgment ruins a person. This chain of cause and effect is one of the Gita’s best-known psychological teachings.

Notice that the steady person is not someone who has left the world. Arjuna is still asked to fight, and householders are still asked to work. The Gita’s ideal is , inner calm, joined to action. Many modern readers, Hindu and not, value this part of the Gita for dealing with stress and pressure.

36.9

Gandhi and the Gita

Main ideaMohandas Gandhi called the Gita his guide and read its battlefield as the inner struggle of every person, drawing from it a teaching of selfless, nonviolent action.

As a young law student in London in the late 1880s, Mohandas Gandhi was invited by two English friends to read the Gita with them. They read Edwin Arnold’s translation, The Song Celestial. Gandhi later wrote that he was embarrassed to have not yet read it in Sanskrit or Gujarati. The book became central to his life. He called it his mother and turned to it whenever he faced doubt.

Gandhi did not read the Gita as approval of war. He read the battle at Kurukshetra as an , a story in which characters and events stand for deeper meanings. For him, the war was the struggle inside every human heart between good and evil impulses. The Pandavas and Kauravas were forces within the self. Krishna was the voice of truth guiding the person.

From the Gita’s teaching of action without attachment, Gandhi drew his idea of selfless service. He translated the Gita into Gujarati, his native language, with commentary he titled Anasakti Yoga, the yoga of non-attachment. Joined with , nonviolence, this reading shaped his method of , holding firmly to truth through nonviolent resistance.

Not all readers agree with Gandhi. Some Hindu thinkers of his time, such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, read the Gita as a call to energetic action in the world, including political struggle against British rule. Scholars note that the text itself is set on a real battlefield. The Gita’s openness to both readings is part of why it has mattered to so many people.

36.10

Many Readers, Many Readings

Main ideaFor more than a thousand years, teachers, translators and writers in India and the West have read the Gita in strikingly different ways.

Hindu philosophers have written on the Gita for centuries. The 8th-century teacher Shankara read it through Advaita: knowledge of the one Brahman is the key to liberation. The teacher Ramanuja, around the 11th and 12th centuries, read it as a text of devotion to a personal God. Each found his own school’s teaching in the same 700 verses.

The Gita reached the English-speaking world in 1785, when Charles Wilkins, working in India, published the first English translation. American writers soon took notice. Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau both read it. In Walden, published in 1854, Thoreau wrote about bathing his intellect each morning in the philosophy of the Gita. Edwin Arnold’s poetic translation followed in 1885.

In 1893, the Hindu monk spoke at the World’s Parliament of Religions in Chicago, opening with the words Sisters and Brothers of America. He drew on the Gita and the Upanishads to present Hindu ideas to American audiences. Later teachers brought the Gita to new groups. In 1966, A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada founded the International Society for Krishna Consciousness in New York, which spread a devotional reading of the Gita worldwide.

Today the Gita is read in schools, temples, business books and hospitals. Hindus recite it at funerals and in daily prayer. Its many readings show the point this course made in unit 13: every translation and commentary is also an , shaped by its reader’s time and tradition.

36.11

Yoga: Roots and Modern Forms

Main ideaYoga began as a set of Indian spiritual disciplines aimed at liberation; modern yoga, focused on postures and health, grew from those roots in the last 150 years.

Walk into a yoga class at a Chicago gym, and you will see mats, stretching and breathing exercises. The teacher may say namaste at the end. Most people there are not thinking about moksha. Yet the word yoga comes from Indian religious traditions, where it meant a discipline that joins a person to the divine or frees the self.

A key early text is the attributed to the sage Patanjali, compiled perhaps 1,600 to 2,000 years ago; scholars debate the date. It defines yoga as stilling the movements of the mind. It lays out eight limbs, or steps: ethical restraints such as nonviolence and truthfulness, personal disciplines, posture, breath control, withdrawing the senses, concentration, meditation and , complete absorption. Postures are only one limb, and in Patanjali they mostly mean a steady seat for meditation.

Later texts, from around the 11th to 15th centuries, developed more physical postures and breathing methods. In the late 1800s and 1900s, Indian teachers adapted yoga for modern audiences. Vivekananda’s book Raja Yoga (1896) presented meditation to Western readers. The teacher T. Krishnamacharya and his students, including B. K. S. Iyengar, developed the posture-based styles now practiced worldwide.

Modern yoga raises questions. Some Hindu groups ask that yoga’s Hindu roots be acknowledged, not erased. Some Christian parents have objected to yoga in schools, and courts have looked at whether particular school programs were religious. The United Nations declared June 21 the International Day of Yoga in 2014. The history shows a practice that is both ancient and constantly changing.
