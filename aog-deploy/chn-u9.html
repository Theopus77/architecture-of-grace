The Confucian Classics

Confucian Texts

For more than two thousand years, students across China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam learned from the same small library. It held the Five Classics and, later, the Four Books. Officials had to know them. Poets quoted them. Families argued over them at dinner.

In this unit you will open that library. You will learn what each of the Five Classics is and how Zhu Xi built the Four Books. You will look inside the Analects to see how it was put together. Then you will follow a great debate inside the Confucian tradition: Mencius and Xunzi on whether human nature is good.

By the end, you will be able to name the classics and their genres, read a citation like Mencius 2A.6, and explain both sides of the human nature debate. You will use the words of the texts themselves as your evidence.

The big question

What did the Confucian classics teach, and why did their readers argue about them?

The story

Four Hundred Thousand Characters

A boy in the year 1500 begins the long climb toward the imperial exam.

It is the year 1500, in a town south of the Yangzi River. Eight-year-old Wenming sits at a low desk before dawn. His teacher reads a line from the Analects aloud. Wenming repeats it, again and again, rocking gently to the rhythm, until he can say it without looking.

His family has saved for years to pay this teacher. If Wenming passes the imperial exams, he may become an official. That would bring honor and security to his parents, his grandparents' memory and his whole clan. If he fails, the savings may be gone.

The task is huge. Over the coming years he must memorize the Four Books and the Five Classics. Together they hold more than 400,000 characters. He must also learn the commentary of the scholar Zhu Xi, because the exam expects answers in Zhu Xi's reading.

Not everyone thinks this is the point of learning. Wenming's teacher tells him that Confucius studied to improve himself, not to impress others. Wenming nods, then goes back to his recitation. Outside, the rooster crows. He has only begun.

Section 1

The Five Classics

17.1

A Library of Five

Main ideaThe Five Classics are five ancient books of poetry, history, divination, ritual and chronicle that Han scholars made the core of learning.

A is a set of books that a community treats as its most important texts. In Han China, the canon was the . They were the Book of Songs, the Book of Documents, the Book of Changes, the Book of Rites and the Spring and Autumn Annals.

Each is a different , or kind of writing. The Songs is poetry. The Documents is speeches and records from early kings. The Changes is a divination book. The Rites describes ceremonies and conduct. The Spring and Autumn is a short year-by-year chronicle of the state of Lu.

Tradition held that Confucius edited or wrote some of these books. Modern scholars think most are older or later than Confucius, and grew over time. What is clear is that from the Han on, officials were expected to know them well.

17.2

The Book of Songs

Main ideaThe Book of Songs gathers 305 early poems, and Confucian readers used them to teach feeling, speech and virtue.

The (Shijing) holds 305 poems, most from about 1000 to 600 BCE. Some are folk songs about love, farming and soldiers far from home. Others are hymns sung at court and at ancestral temples.

The first poem, "Guan ju," pictures ospreys calling on a river island and a young man longing for a gentle lady. Han commentators read it as praise of a queen’s virtue. Many modern readers read it as a love song. The same poem, two readings.

Confucius valued the Songs highly. In Analects 17.9, he tells students the Songs can stir the mind, help them observe, help them get along with others, and teach them duties to father and ruler. In Analects 2.2, he sums up all 300 poems in one phrase: no twisted thoughts.

From the source
The Songs in One Phrase
In the Book of Poetry are three hundred pieces, but the design of them all may be embraced in one sentence — 'Having no depraved thoughts.'
Confucius, Analects 2.2, trans. James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1 (2nd ed., 1893)

17.3

Documents, Changes and Rites

Main ideaThe Book of Documents records early rulers, the Book of Changes is a divination text with commentaries, and the Book of Rites sets out ritual and its meaning.

The Book of Documents (Shangshu) gathers speeches and announcements credited to early kings and ministers, including the Zhou speeches on the Mandate of Heaven. As you learned, some chapters were later forgeries, which scholars sorted out over centuries.

The (Yijing, or I Ching) began as a divination manual. It uses 64 , figures of six broken or solid lines. People cast yarrow stalks or coins to choose one, then read its text. Later commentaries, the "Ten Wings," turned it into a book of philosophy about change, yin and yang.

The (Liji) explains ceremonies from mourning to archery, and why they matter. Two of its chapters, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean, would later become two of the Four Books.

Section 2

The Four Books

17.4

Zhu Xi's Four Books

Main ideaIn the 1100s, Zhu Xi grouped the Analects, Mencius, Great Learning and Doctrine of the Mean as the Four Books and wrote commentaries on them.

(1130–1200) was a scholar of the Song dynasty. He wanted a clear path into Confucian learning. So he grouped four texts as the : the Analects, the Mencius, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean.

Zhu Xi advised students to read them in a set order: first the Great Learning for the outline, then the Analects for the foundation, then the Mencius, and last the Doctrine of the Mean for its depth. He wrote detailed notes on all four and published them together in 1190.

His reading shaped centuries of study. In 1313, the Yuan government made his commentary the standard for the civil-service exams. It stayed the standard until the exams ended in 1905. Later thinkers, like Wang Yangming, challenged parts of his reading.

17.5

The Great Learning and the Mean

Main ideaThe Great Learning links personal growth to good government, and the Doctrine of the Mean describes balance and sincerity.

The is short. Its most famous passage traces a chain: to bring peace to the world, first govern the state well; to do that, first order the family; to do that, first cultivate yourself; and to do that, first set your heart right and make your thoughts sincere.

The (Zhongyong) is about balance, or zhong. It describes a state in which feelings are not yet stirred and then, when they arise, hit the right measure. It also stresses cheng, sincerity or being true, which it links to the Way of Heaven.

Both texts are paraphrased here, since the public-domain translations named for this course do not include them. Tradition credits the Mean to Confucius’s grandson Zisi. Many modern scholars date both texts to the late Warring States or early Han.

From the source
The Chain of the Great Learning
Those who wished to bring order to the world first ordered their states. Wishing to order their states, they first set their families in order. Wishing to set their families in order, they first cultivated themselves. Wishing to cultivate themselves, they first made their hearts right.
Great Learning, opening section, paraphrased; see Wing-tsit Chan, A Source Book in Chinese Philosophy (1963) (paraphrased)

17.6

The Mencius as a Book

Main ideaThe Mencius is a book of conversations between Mencius and rulers, students and rivals, arranged in seven books.

The is named for the thinker Mengzi, or Mencius (c. 372–289 BCE). It is much longer than the Analects. Many passages are full conversations, where Mencius argues with kings, students and rival thinkers.

The book has seven books, each divided into two parts. Scholars cite them as 1A, 1B, 2A and so on. So Mencius 2A.6 means book 2, part A, passage 6. Legge’s translation labels the parts "Part I" and "Part II" and uses titles from the book’s rulers and students.

The Mencius was not always in the canon. For centuries it was read as one philosopher among many. It rose in status during the Song dynasty, and Zhu Xi’s Four Books made it central. The next chapter looks closely at its ideas.

Inside the Analects

17.7

How the Analects Is Built

Main ideaThe Analects is a collection of short sayings and scenes in 20 books, gathered by students over time rather than written by Confucius.

Open the Analects anywhere and you find short entries. Many begin, "The Master said." Others show a student asking a question, or describe how Confucius dressed and ate. The book has 20 books and roughly 500 passages. Editions number some passages differently.

Confucius did not write it. The Chinese title, , means something like "selected sayings." His students, and their students, gathered the material. Most scholars agree it reached roughly its present form by the early Han.

Scholars debate its . Some argue that books 3 to 9 hold older material and that the last books were added later. Others think the evidence is too thin to be sure. Readers can still notice that some books feel different from others.

From the source

Is it not pleasant to learn with a constant perseverance and application? Is it not delightful to have friends coming from distant quarters?
Confucius, Analects 1.1, trans. James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1 (2nd ed., 1893)

17.8

Sayings, Dialogues and Scenes

Main ideaThe Analects mixes short sayings, question-and-answer dialogues and scenes, and each genre asks readers to read differently.

Some Analects passages are : one sentence to think about. "The Master said" introduces a thought, with no setting. Readers must imagine when, why and to whom it was said. That open space is one reason commentaries grew so long.

Other passages are dialogues. In Analects 12.1, the student Yan Hui asks about ren, humaneness. Confucius answers that it means mastering oneself and returning to ritual. Different students ask the same question and get different answers. Many readers think he fitted each answer to the student.

Book 10 is mostly . It describes how Confucius behaved at court, at home and at meals. He would not sit on a mat that was not straight. These details show ritual as part of daily life, not only temple ceremony.

17.9

Canons Beyond China

Main ideaKorean, Japanese and Vietnamese scholars also studied the Four Books and Five Classics, writing their own commentaries.

The classics did not stay inside China. In Korea, Silla and later Goryeo and Joseon rulers set up schools and exams on the classics. The Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) made Zhu Xi’s learning its official philosophy.

In Japan, scholars studied the classics for centuries. In the Tokugawa period (1603–1868), schools of Confucian thought flourished, and some scholars, such as Ito Jinsai, argued with Zhu Xi’s reading. Vietnam held exams on the classics from 1075 until 1919.

Because the classics were written in classical Chinese, educated people across East Asia could read the same texts even when they spoke different languages. Each country also built its own .

Mencius and Xunzi: Is Human Nature Good?

Debate
Big questionAre people born with the seeds of goodness, or do they become good only through shaping?
The story

Two Teachers, One Child

Two Confucian teachers watch the same crying child and draw opposite lessons.

Imagine a market square in the state of Qi, around 300 BCE. A small child has wandered from her mother and stands near an open well, crying. A stranger drops his basket, runs over and pulls her back from the edge. The crowd lets out its breath.

An older teacher watches. For him, the moment proves something. The stranger did not stop to think about reward or praise. He simply felt alarm and moved. "There," the teacher tells his students. "Every person has a heart that cannot bear to see others suffer. Goodness is already in us, like a sprout."

A younger scholar sees the same crowd differently. He notices two boys squabbling over the stranger's spilled fruit. "Look," he says. "Left alone, people grab. The stranger acted well because he was raised well. Goodness comes from teachers, rituals and practice, the way a craftsman straightens a warped board."

These two voices stand for real thinkers. The first sounds like Mencius. The second sounds like Xunzi. Both called themselves followers of Confucius. Yet they gave opposite answers to one of the oldest questions in philosophy.

Talk about itWhich part of the scene would you point to as evidence for each teacher's view?
Section 1

Mencius and the Four Sprouts

18.1

The Child at the Well

Main ideaMencius argued that everyone has a heart that cannot bear others' suffering, shown by instant alarm at a child about to fall into a well.

Mencius (c. 372–289 BCE) lived about a century after Confucius. Tradition says he studied with students of Confucius’s grandson. Like Confucius, he traveled to advise rulers. The book named for him records his arguments.

His most famous argument is in Mencius 2A.6. Suppose someone suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well. They will feel alarm and . Mencius says this is not to win the parents’ friendship or neighbors’ praise, or because they dislike the child’s cry. The feeling comes on its own.

From this, Mencius concludes that the heart of compassion is part of being human. Someone without it, he says, is not fully human. This is a , an imagined case used to test an idea. Readers still debate whether it proves what he claims.

From the source
A Mind That Cannot Bear Suffering
All men have a mind which cannot bear to see the sufferings of others.
Mencius 2A.6, trans. James Legge, The Works of Mencius, The Chinese Classics vol. 2 (2nd ed., 1895)

18.2

Four Sprouts of Virtue

Main ideaMencius taught that four inborn feelings are the sprouts of four virtues, which grow when they are cared for.

In the same passage, 2A.6, Mencius names four feelings. The feeling of compassion is the start of , humaneness. The feeling of shame is the start of , rightness. The feeling of modesty and yielding is the start of li, ritual propriety. The sense of right and wrong is the start of , wisdom.

The Chinese word Mencius uses, duan, can mean a beginning, a tip or a . Many translators choose "sprout" to show his picture. Virtue is not complete at birth. It is a small shoot that can grow into a tree, or be crushed.

Mencius says people have these four as they have four limbs. If they extend them, it is like a fire starting to burn or a spring starting to flow. If they neglect them, they cannot even serve their parents.

Mencius 2A.6, trans. James Legge, The Works of Mencius, The Chinese Classics vol. 2 (2nd ed., 1895)

18.3

Ox Mountain and the Farmer of Song

Main ideaMencius used stories of a stripped mountain and an impatient farmer to explain why goodness can be lost and how it grows.

If people are good by nature, why do so many act badly? Mencius answers with a picture in 6A.8. The trees on Ox Mountain were once lovely. But people cut them with axes, and cattle and sheep grazed the new shoots. Now the mountain is bare, and people think it never had trees.

Human beings are like this, Mencius says. The good heart is cut down day after day. But each night, and in the calm of early morning, a little goodness grows back. Bad conduct cuts it down again. Its was never bare.

In 2A.2, Mencius tells of a farmer of Song who worried his grain was growing slowly, so he pulled on every sprout. He came home tired and boasted he had helped them grow. His son ran to look; the plants had withered. Moral growth, Mencius suggests, cannot be forced, but it cannot be ignored either.

Section 2

Mencius on Rulers and People

18.4

The People Come First

Main ideaMencius taught that the people matter more than the ruler, and that a ruler who cares for them will win their hearts.

Mencius spoke boldly to kings. In 7B.14 he ranks what matters in a state: the people first, the altars of the land and grain next, and the ruler last. Few thinkers of his time said such a thing to a ruler’s face.

He urged . A king should lower taxes, not waste the people’s labor in farming seasons, and make sure the old do not go hungry. In 1A.7 he tells King Xuan of Qi that the king’s own pity for an ox being led to sacrifice shows he has the heart to rule well.

Mencius believed that a ruler who shows care wins the people’s hearts, and that such a ruler cannot be stopped. He connected this to the Mandate of Heaven: Heaven sees as the people see, a line he quotes from the Book of Documents.

From the source
The People and the Sovereign
The people are the most important element in a nation; the spirits of the land and grain are the next; the sovereign is the lightest.
Mencius 7B.14, trans. James Legge, The Works of Mencius, The Chinese Classics vol. 2 (2nd ed., 1895)

18.5

When a Ruler Loses the Mandate

Main ideaMencius argued that a cruel ruler loses the right to be called a ruler, a claim later readers used both to limit and to challenge power.

In Mencius 1B.8, King Xuan asks whether it was right for the founders of Shang and Zhou to overthrow the kings before them. Was that not a subject killing his ruler? Mencius answers that one who destroys humaneness and rightness is a mere , a ’fellow.’ He has heard of killing the fellow Zhou, the last Shang king, but not of killing a ruler.

The point is sharp. A ruler who is cruel to the people has already stopped being a true ruler. The Mandate of Heaven has passed from him. Some later readers called this a right of .

Rulers noticed. Tradition reports that the first Ming emperor, in the 1300s, was angered by such passages and had an edited Mencius prepared with some passages removed. Scholars also debate how far Mencius meant to go; he did not call on ordinary people to rise up.

18.6

Mencius Debates Gaozi

Main ideaIn Mencius 6A, Mencius argues with Gaozi, who said human nature is neither good nor bad, using images of willow wood and water.

Book 6A opens with a debate. The thinker Gaozi says human nature is like willow wood, and rightness is like cups and bowls. Making people good is like carving the wood. Mencius replies: do you follow the willow’s nature, or must you do violence to it? If violence, then you say goodness harms human nature.

Gaozi tries another image. Human nature is like water whirling in a pool. Open a channel east and it flows east; open one west and it flows west. So nature is neither good nor bad. Mencius answers in 6A.2 that water may not prefer east or west, but it always flows down.

Water can be splashed over your head or forced uphill, Mencius adds, but that is force, not its nature. So it is with people who do wrong. This debate shows how carefully early thinkers built arguments with analogies.

From the source
Water Flows Down
The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards.
Mencius 6A.2, trans. James Legge, The Works of Mencius, The Chinese Classics vol. 2 (2nd ed., 1895)

How does Mencius turn Gaozi's water image against him? Is the analogy convincing? Why?

Section 3

Xunzi: Goodness Is Made

18.7

Human Nature Is Bad

Main ideaXunzi argued that human nature is bad, meaning inborn desires lead to conflict, and that goodness comes from deliberate effort.

(c. 310–235 BCE) was the leading Confucian of the late Warring States. He taught at the Jixia Academy in Qi, and later lived in the state of Chu. Tradition says Han Feizi and Li Si studied with him.

Chapter 23 of the Xunzi is titled "Human Nature Is Bad." Xunzi says people are born loving profit, feeling envy and wanting to please their eyes and ears. If they follow these, fighting and disorder follow. Goodness, he says, is the result of (wei).

"Bad" here does not mean wicked from birth in every act. Many scholars translate the word e as "bad" or "crude." Xunzi means that raw desires, left unshaped, lead to conflict. His answer is teachers, ritual and law.

Human nature is bad; goodness is the result of conscious effort. People are born loving profit; if they follow this, strife arises and courtesy vanishes.
Xunzi ch. 23, 'Human Nature Is Bad,' paraphrased; see Burton Watson, Hsün Tzu: Basic Writings (1963) (paraphrased)

18.8

Straightening Warped Wood

Main ideaXunzi compared people to warped wood and dull metal that become straight and sharp through steaming, pressing and grinding.

Xunzi uses images from . A warped board must be steamed and clamped in a press frame before it is straight. Dull metal must be ground on a whetstone before it is sharp. In the same way, he says, people need teachers and ritual to become good.

His first chapter, "Encouraging Learning," opens with a famous line: learning must never stop. Blue dye comes from the indigo plant, yet it is bluer than the plant. Ice comes from water, yet it is colder. A student, shaped by learning, can surpass the teacher.

For Xunzi, is the great human invention. The ancient sages, he says, saw that desires led to conflict and created rites to set limits and share goods fairly. Ritual does not destroy desire; it guides it.

18.9

One Tradition, Two Answers

Main ideaMencius and Xunzi both followed Confucius, and later readers mostly favored Mencius while still learning from Xunzi.

Mencius and Xunzi agree on much. Both honor Confucius, value learning and ritual, and hope for humane rulers. Both think people can become sages. They on where goodness starts: inside, as a sprout, or outside, through training.

Zhu Xi and the Song Neo-Confucians sided with Mencius. The Mencius became one of the Four Books, and Xunzi was left out. Some blamed Xunzi for his Legalist students. Yet Xunzi’s views on ritual and learning shaped Confucian practice for centuries.

Modern readers revisit the debate. Some psychologists see sprouts of empathy in infants. Others stress how much children learn from their surroundings. Scholars caution against treating either ancient thinker as simply right; the itself is what the tradition keeps.
