The story

Tops and Bottoms

A hungry rabbit, a lazy bear, and one question that turns the whole story around.

Bear owned a big field but hated to work. Rabbit owned nothing but had six hungry kids. One spring Rabbit knocked on Bear's door. "Let me farm your field," he said. "I will do all the work. You just pick which half of each crop you want, the tops or the bottoms." Bear yawned. "Tops," he said, and went back to sleep.

Rabbit planted carrots. He watered, he weeded, he pulled. In the fall he brought Bear a wagon of leafy green tops. Bear got a wagon of leaves. Rabbit's kids got a winter of sweet orange carrots. Bear was angry. "Next year I want the bottoms!"

So Rabbit planted lettuce. In the fall Bear got a wagon of roots and dirt. Rabbit's family ate crisp lettuce all season. Bear roared. "Next year I take the tops AND the bottoms!" Rabbit only nodded, and planted corn.

When Mr. Okafor's class read this tale, he stopped and asked, "Where does the story turn?" Hands went up. Some said the corn. Some said the roar. Then Jada said, "It turns the first time Rabbit asks 'tops or bottoms?' That is when we know he has a plan." Mr. Okafor smiled. She had found the moment the whole story hangs on.

By the third harvest, Bear got a wagon of corn tassels and a wagon of roots. Rabbit's family got the ears of corn, the part in the middle. Bear finally decided to farm the field himself. That, the tale says, is how Bear learned to work.

Talk about itJada said the story turns on a question. Do you agree? Where would you say the turning point is, and why?
Section 1

Who Is in the Story

11.1

Traits Show in Actions

Main ideaA character trait is what a character is like, and you find it by watching what the character does and says.

Read this: "Marisol saw the new kid eating alone. She picked up her tray and walked over. ’Can I sit here?’ she asked." Nobody says Marisol is kind. But you know it. You saw what she did.

What a person is like is called a . Kind, brave, lazy, sneaky, and honest are traits. In a story, a writer rarely tells you a trait straight out. Instead the writer shows an . You read the action and name the trait yourself. That is your job as a reader.

Try it with Bear from the tale. He owns a field but will not plant it. He says "tops" and goes back to sleep. Those actions add up. Bear is lazy. Rabbit works all season and thinks ahead. Rabbit is clever and hard-working. Every trait you name should come from something the character actually did.

Think it through

Name one trait for Tomas and one for Lena. What action did you use for each one?

11.2

Why Characters Act

Main ideaA motive is the reason a character does something, and feelings often change what a character decides to do.

Why did Rabbit knock on Bear’s door? He was not being friendly. He had six hungry kids and no field. That reason is his . A motive is the reason behind an action. Ask "why did she do that?" and you are looking for a motive.

Feelings drive motives too. A character who is scared may hide or run. A character who is angry may shout something she regrets. Bear roared "tops AND bottoms" because he felt cheated. His anger made him greedy, and greed made him easy to trick again.

Writers give clues to feelings without naming them. "Her hands shook. She stared at the floor." Nobody said nervous, but you know. Clues like shaking hands, a tight voice, or a slammed door tell you the feeling. Then the feeling helps you guess the motive for what the character does next.

11.3

When a Character Changes

Main ideaMany characters are different at the end of a story than at the start, and the change usually comes from what happens to them.

At the start of the tale, Bear will not lift a paw. At the end, he farms his own field. That is a . Bear is not the same bear. Stories about a character who changes are everywhere, because change is what makes a story feel finished.

To track a change, take two snapshots. Snapshot one: what is the character like on page one? Snapshot two: what is he like on the last page? Then ask what happened in between. For Bear, three harvests of leaves and roots taught him a lesson he could not sleep through.

Not every character changes. Rabbit is clever at the start and clever at the end. He is the same. A character who stays the same is called . A character who changes is . When you read, watch for the character who is different at the end. That is usually the one the story is about.

Section 2

Where and What Happens

11.4

Setting Is Time and Place

Main ideaThe setting is where and when a story happens, and it shapes what the characters can and cannot do.

"Snow piled against the cabin door. The lamp was almost out of oil." Two sentences, and you already know a lot. It is winter. It is night, or nearly. There is no electricity. This is the : where and when the story happens.

Setting is not just a background. It changes what can happen. In a snowed-in cabin, a character cannot call for help or run to a store. In a Chicago apartment in July, the problem might be a broken fan and a hot night. Move the same character to a new setting and the story changes with her.

Writers give setting through : the weather, the light, the sounds, the year, the objects around. A story with a horse and a candle is probably set long ago. A story with a bus pass and a phone is set now. Collect the details, and the setting comes into focus.

Think it through

What is the setting? Which details told you? Is there anything you cannot tell yet?

Words to know
setting

Check yourself

11.5

The Shape of a Plot

Main ideaA plot moves from a problem, through events, to a climax, and then to a solution.

Rabbit has no field and hungry kids. That is the . He makes a deal, plants carrots, plants lettuce, plants corn. Those are the events. Bear roars "tops AND bottoms!" and Rabbit still finds a way. That is the , the most exciting moment. Bear gives up and farms for himself. That is the . You just read a whole plot in four sentences.

Almost every story follows this shape. First a problem shows up. Then events pile on and the problem gets harder. Then comes the climax, the turning point where things are most tense. After it, the story slides toward a solution and the ending. Picture a hill: up, up, the top, then down.

Knowing the shape helps you read. When you feel lost in a story, ask: "What is the problem? Has the climax happened yet?" If the character is still struggling, you are on the way up. If things are settling down, you are past the top. This works for a picture book and for a long novel.

11.6

Three Kinds of Trouble

Main ideaA conflict is the struggle in a story, and it can be against another character, against nature, or inside the character's own mind.

Rabbit against Bear. A girl against a blizzard. A boy against his own fear of the diving board. All three are stories, and all three have a struggle at the center. That struggle is the . No conflict, no story. If nothing pushes back, there is nothing to read about.

The first kind is character against character. Two people want different things, like Rabbit and Bear both wanting the crop. The second kind is character against nature: a storm, a flood, a long cold winter, a mountain. The character cannot argue with a blizzard. She can only find a way through.

The third kind happens inside. Character against means the fight is in the character’s own mind. Should I tell the truth? Can I make myself jump? These stories can feel the most real, because you have had that fight too. Many stories mix kinds. A boy lost in the woods may battle the cold and his own panic at the same time.

11.7

What Dialogue Shows

Main ideaDialogue is the words characters say, and it reveals who they are and what they want without the writer telling you.

"Give it back." "Make me." Two lines, four words, and you already know these two are fighting and neither one will back down. Words that characters say out loud are . Quotation marks show where the spoken words start and stop.

Dialogue does more than fill space. It shows traits. A character who says "Whatever, I don’t care" shows one thing. A character who says "Wait, let me help you carry that" shows another. It shows feelings, too. Short, sharp lines can mean anger. Long, rambling lines can mean nerves.

Dialogue can also move the plot. In the tale, Bear says one word, "tops," and that single word decides the whole first harvest. When you read, slow down at the quotation marks. Ask: what does this line tell me about the person saying it? What does it make happen next?

11.8

Summarize in a Few Sentences

Main ideaA summary retells only the main character, the problem, the big events and the ending, in your own words and in order.

A friend missed the movie and asks what happened. You do not repeat every line. You say who it was about, what went wrong, and how it ended. That short retelling is a . A good summary is short, in order, and in your own words.

Here is a summary of the chapter’s tale: "Rabbit has no field, so he farms Bear’s land and lets Bear pick tops or bottoms. Each year Rabbit plants a crop that gives Bear the useless half. After three harvests, Bear gives up and farms for himself." Three sentences. Character, problem, events, ending.

Watch out for two mistakes. One is retelling too much: every carrot, every wagon. The other is giving your : "It was funny." A summary is not a review. Leave out what you think and keep only what happened. A test: could someone who never read the story follow your summary? If yes, it works.

11.9

Prove It From the Page

Main ideaWhen you explain a story, back up your idea with evidence: the exact words or events from the text that show it.

"Bear is lazy." Okay, says your teacher, prove it. You flip to the tale and read: "Bear owned a big field but hated to work." Now it is not just your idea. It is on the page. Words or events from the text that support an idea are called .

Evidence turns a guess into an answer. Two students can disagree about a character. The one who can point to the page wins the argument. When you write about a story, use a pattern. Say your idea. Give the evidence. Then explain how the evidence proves the idea.

Try this frame: "I think ___. In the text it says ’___.’ This shows ___." For example: "I think Rabbit plans ahead. In the text it says he ’only nodded, and planted corn.’ This shows he already knew what Bear would ask for." Short, clear, and backed by the page. That is what teachers mean when they say your answer.

Old Stories, Many Lands

11.10

Fables and Their Lessons

Main ideaA fable is a very short story, usually with animal characters, that ends with a lesson about how to live.

A hare laughs at a tortoise for being slow. They race. The hare naps halfway, sure he will win. The tortoise keeps plodding and crosses the line first. You know this one. It is a : a short story, often with animals who talk, that teaches a lesson.

The lesson at the end of a fable is called the . In the race story, the moral is that steady effort beats careless speed. Many fables are said to come from Aesop. He was a storyteller in ancient Greece more than two thousand years ago. Nobody is sure what Aesop was really like. But the stories with his name traveled all over the world.

Fables work because the animals stand for people. The hare is every show-off who thinks the job is done. The tortoise is every kid who keeps going. When you read a fable, ask two questions. What does each animal stand for? What is the story telling me to do, or not do? Answer those and you have the moral.

11.11

Folktales and Myths Worldwide

Main ideaFolktales and myths are old stories passed down by many people, and each culture's stories carry that culture's ideas about how to live.

Nobody wrote the first version of Tops and Bottoms. People told it, and their children told it. It changed a little each time. A story passed down by telling is a . Rabbit tales like it were told by enslaved African Americans in the South. Clever-rabbit stories also appear in West Africa, in Native American nations, and in Asia. A small, weak animal who wins by wits shows up almost everywhere.

A is an old story that explains something big. How did the world begin? Why do seasons change? What are the gods like? In a Greek myth, a boy named Icarus flies on wings of wax and feathers. He flies too near the sun. The wax melts, and he falls. That story explained no weather, but it warned every listener about pride.

Reading stories from many cultures shows you two things. First, people everywhere want the same things: food, safety, fairness, a good laugh at a bully. Second, each culture tells it in its own way, with its own animals, foods and places. When you read a folktale, ask: what did the people who told this care about? The answer is usually the lesson.

Think it through

Theme and Point of View

Literature
Big questionHow does who tells a story change what it means?
The story

One Bike, Two Stories

Two brothers, one blue bike, one dented fender, and two versions of what happened on Saturday.

Here is how Andre, who is eleven, tells it. "The bike is mine. Grandpa gave it to me. Jalen took it without asking while I was at practice. When I got home the front fender was dented and he was acting like nothing happened. I yelled because he never asks. He just takes."

Here is how Jalen, who is nine, tells it. "Andre never even rides that bike. It sat in the garage for two weeks. I asked Mom and she said fine, as long as I stayed on our street. A car door opened and I swerved and hit the curb. I was going to tell him. He yelled before I could say a word."

Their mom heard both versions in the kitchen, one brother talking over the other. She held up a hand. "Both of you just told me a true story," she said. "And neither of you told me the whole one." Andre had not known Mom said yes. Jalen had not known the bike was from Grandpa, who died last year.

Ms. Reyes read both versions to her fourth-graders on Monday, without saying which brother was right. Then she asked, "What did Andre know that Jalen did not? What did Jalen know that Andre did not?" The class made two lists on the board. The lists did not match.

"That," said Ms. Reyes, "is point of view. Every storyteller stands in one spot and sees from there. Your job as a reader is to notice where they are standing." Then she asked the harder question: "What is this story really about? Not the bike. Underneath the bike." Somebody in the back said, "Asking." Somebody else said, "Listening first."

Talk about itWhat is the bike story really about, underneath the bike? Which brother's version did you believe more at first, and why?
Section 1

What a Story Means

12.1

Theme Is Not Topic

Main ideaThe topic is what a story is about on the surface; the theme is the big idea or lesson underneath it.

Ask a kid what the bike story is about. "A bike," she says. True, but that is the : the subject on the surface. Now ask what the story is really about. "Asking before you take." "Listening before you yell." Those are the : the big idea the story carries underneath.

Here is a quick test. A topic is usually one or two words: friendship, a dog, a race, a bike. A theme is a whole idea you can say in a sentence: "True friends stay when things get hard." "Slow and steady wins." If you can say it as a sentence about life, not just about this story, it is a theme.

Themes are not written on the page. Nobody in the bike story says "the theme is listening." You build the theme from what happens and what the characters learn. That is why two readers can find slightly different themes in one story. Both can be right, as long as each one can point to the events that support it.

12.2

What the Character Learns

Main ideaThe fastest way to a theme is to ask what the main character learned, and how that lesson could apply to anyone.

At the start, Andre yells first and asks later. At the end, he finds out Mom said yes and that he had the story wrong. What did Andre learn? Get the facts before you blow up. Turn that into a sentence about life and you have a theme: "Ask before you judge."

This works for almost any story. Find the main character. Find what she believes or does at the beginning. Find how she is different at the end. The gap between those two is the . The lesson, stated so it fits anyone, is the theme. Sometimes a character does not learn, and the reader is meant to. The hare in the race never says he was wrong. You still know.

One story can hold more than one theme. Jalen’s side of the bike story teaches something too: even when you have permission, tell the person it belongs to. Look for the theme by following the character who changed the most. Then check it against the events. If the events it, keep it.

Making an Inference

Main ideaAn inference is a smart guess that joins a clue in the text with what you already know.

"Andre had not known Mom said yes." The story never says "Andre felt bad about yelling." But you know he did. How? A clue in the text, plus what you know about people. That is an : a smart guess built from a clue and your own knowledge.

Here is the formula: clue from the text + what I already know = inference. Clue: a kid comes in soaked, dripping on the floor. What I know: rain soaks people. Inference: it is raining outside. You do this all day without noticing. Reading just asks you to do it on purpose.

An inference is not a wild guess. It has to start from a clue on the page. "Maybe Andre is secretly a robot" is not an inference, because nothing in the story points there. "Andre misses Grandpa" is an inference, because the bike was Grandpa’s and Grandpa died last year. The clue is what makes it fair.

The Drum Inside the Poem

The first time the class read the poem, it was just words. The second time, they heard hoofbeats.

Mr. Bell put four lines on the board. "Whenever the moon and stars are set, / Whenever the wind is high, / All night long in the dark and wet, / A man goes riding by." He asked Kenji to read them. Kenji read them the way he read everything: fast, flat, done. "Okay," said Mr. Bell. "Now we read it again. This time, listen for the drum."

"What drum?" said Kenji. Mr. Bell did not answer. He tapped the desk. Tap, TAP, tap, TAP. Then he read the first line, slow, letting his voice land hard on some words and light on others. "Whenever the MOON and STARS are SET." A few kids started tapping too. By the third line, the whole room was doing it.

"That," said Mr. Bell, "is a horse." The poem is about a rider who gallops past in the storm every night, and the poet built the sound of hooves right into the words. Robert Louis Stevenson wrote it more than a hundred years ago for a book of poems for children. He was a sick boy who spent a lot of nights awake, listening to the wind.

Kenji read the lines a third time, tapping as he went. This time he slowed down at "dark and wet" and sped up at "riding by." It sounded like a story. It sounded a little scary. "I didn't get that the first time," he said. "Nobody does," said Mr. Bell. "A poem is not finished until you hear it."

Then Mr. Bell handed out a one-page play with two parts, a rider and a child at a window. "Same night, same storm," he said. "But this time you are going to say the words out loud and be the people. That is the other thing we do with words on a page: we stand up and become them."

13.9

Reading a Script

Main ideaA play is written as a script with a cast list, lines of dialogue, stage directions and scenes, and each part has a job.

A play does not look like a story. It looks like this. CAST: RIDER, CHILD. SCENE 1: A window, night. Wind. CHILD: (pressing her face to the glass) Who is out there? RIDER: (from far off) Only me, and the storm. The words a character says come after the character’s name. Everything else is instructions.

The list of characters at the top is the . The words after a name are that character’s lines, the dialogue. The words in parentheses, like (pressing her face to the glass), are . They tell actors what to do, not what to say. You never read them out loud.

A play is cut into . A new scene usually means a new place or a new time. "SCENE 2: The same window, morning." When you read a play alone, picture the stage. When you read it with others, each person takes a part. The story only exists when someone speaks the lines. That is the strange, great thing about a play.

13.10

Reader's Theater

Main ideaIn reader's theater you read a script aloud with a group, using your voice to become the character, with no costumes or memorizing needed.

Mr. Bell’s class did not build a set or sew costumes. They pulled chairs into a half circle, took parts, and read the storm play out loud. That is reader&#x27;s theater. You keep the script in your hands. Your voice does the acting. It is the easiest way to perform a play, and one of the best ways to get better at reading.

Your voice has tools. Volume: loud for the rider shouting over the wind, soft for the child at the window. Speed: fast when scared, slow when sleepy. : the feeling in your voice, angry or kind or teasing. Read your line to yourself first, decide what the character feels, then say it that way.

Two habits make it work. Read your part more than once before the group starts. And listen to the others, so you come in on time. Kids who do reader’s theater often read faster and smoother afterward, because they have practiced the same lines until the words feel like their own. Reading out loud with feeling is practice that does not feel like practice.

Think it through
One Line, Four Ways

The line is: "Only me, and the storm." Say it as a tired old rider. Say it as a ghost. Say it as someone trying not to scare a small child. Say it as someone trying very hard to scare a small child.

What did you change each time: volume, speed or tone? Which version fits the poem best?

13.11

Poem, Story, Play

Main ideaA poem, a story and a play can all tell about the same thing, but each form gives the reader something different.

A rider gallops through a storm at night. Stevenson made it a poem: four short lines, a drumbeat, one picture. Mr. Bell’s play made it two voices, a child and a rider, talking across the wind. A story could make it a whole chapter: who the rider is, where he is going, why the child cannot sleep. Same night, three .

Each form has strengths. A poem is short and musical, so it gives you a feeling fast. A story gives you time: characters, setting, a plot with a problem and a solution. A play gives you voices and puts you in the room. When you forms, ask: what can this one do that the others cannot?

Try it yourself. Take any small moment, like the first snow of the year in Illinois. As a poem it might be six lines about the hush. As a story it might be a kid who wakes up, sees white, and races to find her boots. As a play it might be two kids at a bus stop arguing about whether school will close. All three are true. Each one shows a different side.

Think it through
First Snow, Three Ways

Version one, a poem: six short lines about the hush when snow falls on a Chicago street at night. Version two, a story: a girl wakes up, sees white out the window, and tears the house apart looking for her boots. Version three, a play: two kids at a bus stop arguing about whether school will close.

Which version would you rather read? Which would you rather write? What does each form do that the others cannot?

Write it

Pick one character from this unit: Bear, Rabbit, Andre or Jalen. Make a claim about what that character learned, or failed to learn. Support it with at least two pieces of evidence from the story and explain how each one proves your claim.

  • Claim: say in one sentence what the character learned or did not learn.
  • Evidence: quote or describe two exact moments from the story, not your feelings about it.
  • Reasoning: after each piece of evidence, write 'This shows...' and connect it to your claim.
  • The other side: name one thing a reader might say against your claim, and answer it.
  • Finish with the theme: what could anyone learn from this character's story?
