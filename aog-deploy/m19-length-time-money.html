Everything starts at zero

Lined up at the 1, this pencil looks five inches long. It is not five inches long.

Pick the unit, start at zero, count, write it down. Those four steps work on a ruler, on a clock face and on a handful of coins.

Two things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

The minute hand pointing at the 4 means four minutes.

It means twenty. Each number on the clock face is worth five minutes to the minute hand, so the 4 is 4 × 5 = 20. The same hand, the same number, two completely different jobs.

Minute hand: count by fives.

⚠️ People often think…

The nickel is bigger than the dime, so it is worth more.

The dime is smaller and worth twice as much. Size tells you nothing about value — and neither does how many coins you are holding. Three coins can be 3 cents or 75 cents.

Count the value, never the coins.

Helpful Hints

🕔 Reading a clock to five minutes — the three steps

Find the SHORT hand → the hour is the number it has already passed (between 7 and 8 means 7) → find the LONG hand → count by fives from the 12 to the number it points at (on the 7: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35) → write hour, colon, minutes: 7:35. Then ask: morning or afternoon? a.m. or p.m.

Short hand, hour passed. Long hand, count by fives.

📏 The whole topic in one idea

Measuring is counting units, starting at zero. A ruler counts inches or centimeters from 0. A clock counts minutes from the 12, five for each number. Coins count cents — 1, 5, 10, 25 — and you count the biggest first. Every time, pick the unit, start at zero, count, and write the number with its unit.

⚠️ Traps the test loves

  • Starting the ruler at 1 instead of 0. A 4-inch pencil measured from the 1 mark looks like 5 inches.
  • Reading the hour as the NEXT number when the hour hand is close to it. At 2:40 the hour hand is almost on 3, but the hour is still 2.
  • Reading the minute hand as the number it points at: on the 4 is 20 minutes, not 4.
  • Mixing up quarter past (15 minutes) with a quarter coin (25 cents).
  • Counting coins instead of their value. A dime, a nickel and a penny are 3 coins but 16 cents.
  • Thinking a bigger coin is worth more. The nickel is bigger than the dime, but the dime is worth twice as much.

🎯 How the test will ask

  • A picture of a ruler with an object on it — “how long is it?” Check that it starts at 0.
  • Two lengths — “how much longer?” Subtract the short one from the long one.
  • “Which unit would you use?” Small things: inches or centimeters. Big things: feet or meters.
  • A clock face — “what time is it?” Short hand for the hour it passed, long hand counted by fives.
  • “a.m. or p.m.?” Before noon is a.m. After noon is p.m.
  • A handful of coins — “how much?” Biggest first, then count on.
  • A story with money or lengths — decide: put together, or take away? Then work.

✅ Before the test, can you…

  • Measure an object with a ruler, starting at 0?
  • Say which unit fits: inches, feet, centimeters or meters?
  • Find how much longer one thing is than another?
  • Read a clock to the hour, the half hour and five minutes?
  • Say whether a time is a.m. or p.m.?
  • Name each coin and its value, and count a mixed handful?
  • Make 25 cents three different ways?
