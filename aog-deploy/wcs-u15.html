Comparing Systems

Comparison

People often say a leader is far left or a party is center-right. But what do those words really measure? This unit gives you tools to compare political systems with care. You will start with the one-line spectrum from 1789, then add a second axis, test the horseshoe idea, and see how labels shift from the United States to Europe, Latin America and Asia.

Next you will follow countries that moved toward democracy: Portugal and Spain in the 1970s, South Korea and Taiwan in the 1980s and 1990s, Eastern Europe in 1989, Chile in 1990 and South Africa in 1994. You will also learn how political scientists describe democracies weakening, and why they watch courts, the press and the rule of law under governments of the left and the right.

By the end you will be able to place ideas on a model and explain its limits, compare paths to democracy with evidence, and name warning signs fairly. You will practice using the same test for every side, which is one of the strongest habits a citizen can build.

The big question

How can we compare political systems fairly, and what helps democracy grow or weaken?

A class places twenty leaders on a chart and finds that three of them refuse to sit still.

Ms. Okafor tapes a large grid to the whiteboard. One line runs left to right. Another runs from top to bottom. Section 1

The One-Line Model

29.1

Seats in a French Hall

Main ideaThe words left and right come from where members sat in France's National Assembly in 1789, and they became a one-line map of politics.

In the summer of 1789, France’s new met to decide how much power the king should keep. Members who wanted to keep a strong king and old privileges tended to sit on the right side of the hall. Members who wanted big changes, and more power for ordinary people, tended to sit on the left.

Over time those seats became labels. The came to mean people who push for change toward greater equality. The came to mean people who value order, tradition and gradual change. Placing everyone on a single , a line from one end to the other, became the most common way to describe politics.

The one-line model is useful because it is simple. A newspaper can say a party is center-left, and readers get a rough picture. The Italian thinker Norberto Bobbio argued that the line still means something real: in his view, the left and right differ mainly in how they feel about equality. Critics answer that one line squeezes many different questions into a single number.

From the source
Bobbio on Left and Right
The Italian philosopher Norberto Bobbio argued that the left and right have not stopped meaning something. The steady difference, he wrote, is attitude toward equality: the left sees most inequalities as made by society and fixable, while the right sees many as natural or impossible to remove.
Norberto Bobbio, Left and Right: The Significance of a Political Distinction, 1994 (paraphrased)

29.2

What a Line Leaves Out

Main ideaA single line mixes up two different questions: how much the state should run the economy and how much it should control personal life.

Picture two people. One wants low taxes, few business rules and almost no limits on speech or personal choices. The other wants the government to own factories and also to control newspapers and travel. On a single line, both might be called extreme. But they disagree about nearly everything.

The problem is that politics asks at least two questions. The question asks how much the state should own, tax and plan. The social or personal question asks how much the state should direct what people say, believe and do. A wants little state power on both. An wants a strong state that limits personal freedom, whatever its economics.

A one-line model hides these differences. The Soviet Union under Stalin and Chile under Pinochet had opposite economic plans. Stalin’s state owned nearly everything, while Pinochet’s government sold state companies and opened markets. Yet both ruled without free elections and jailed critics. One line cannot show both what they shared and where they differed.

Adding a Second Axis

29.3

A Grid With Two Axes

Main ideaTwo-axis models place ideas on a grid, with economics on one line and freedom versus control on the other.

Around 1970 an American activist named David Nolan drew a chart to show that one line was not enough. His chart, later called the , used two measures: economic freedom and personal freedom. Many later quizzes and textbooks built similar grids, often called two- models.

A common version puts economics on the horizontal axis. Left means more state planning and sharing of wealth. Right means more private property and free markets. The vertical axis runs from authoritarian at the top to libertarian at the bottom. That makes four , or boxes, plus a center.

The grid shows things a line hides. A leader who is economically left but authoritarian sits in the upper left. One who is economically right but authoritarian sits in the upper right. Critics note that the grid has limits too. Real positions on foreign policy, religion or nationalism may not fit either axis, and who places whom can reflect the placer’s own views.

29.4

The Horseshoe Debate

Main ideaThe horseshoe idea says the far left and far right resemble each other, and critics argue it hides deep differences.

Bend a straight line into a U shape, and the two ends come close together. That is the picture behind . The idea, often linked to the French writer Jean-Pierre Faye, holds that the far left and far right have more in common with each other than with the center.

Supporters point to the twentieth century. Stalin’s Soviet Union and Hitler’s Germany both used one-party rule, secret police, camps and a , the practice of treating a leader as nearly superhuman. Both crushed independent newspapers. To supporters, how a government rules matters more than the goals it claims.

Critics say the horseshoe goes too far. They note that the far left and far right had opposite goals: one aimed at a classless society, the other at a nation ranked by race or blood. Some political scientists also argue that the model can unfairly lump peaceful critics of the center with violent extremists. The debate remains open, and careful students can test it against real cases.

One Word, Many Meanings

Main ideaPolitical labels change meaning from country to country, so a word like liberal must be read in its local setting.

In the United States, a usually means someone who favors a larger role for government in helping people. In much of Europe, a liberal party often stands for free markets and lower taxes, closer to what Americans call conservative. In Australia, the main center-right party is called the Liberal Party. The same word can point in different directions.

This is the problem of : the setting that gives words their meaning. In Japan, the Liberal Democratic Party has been the main conservative party since it formed in 1955. In Latin America, Juan Perón’s movement in Argentina, called , has had left-wing and right-wing branches under the same name.

China shows another puzzle. Its ruling Communist Party uses Marxist language, yet since the reforms that began in 1978 it has allowed private companies and large markets. Some observers call it left because of its name and one-party state. Others stress its markets. Careful readers ask what a party actually does, not only what it is called.

29.6

Singapore: Hard to Place

Main ideaSingapore mixes free markets and low taxes with large public housing and firm state control, so it does not fit neatly on a line.

Walk through a Singapore neighborhood and you will see tall apartment blocks built by the government’s Housing and Development Board. Most Singapore residents live in these flats, which many families own on long leases. That sounds like a strong state role in daily life.

Yet Singapore is also known for open trade, low income taxes and welcoming global companies. The People’s Action Party, led for decades by Lee Kuan Yew, has won every national election since 1959. Elections are held regularly, but critics point to strict limits on protest and a press that rarely challenges the government. Many observers describe the system as a .

Supporters credit this mix with turning a small port into one of the richest places in Asia, with low crime and clean government. Critics argue that prosperity came with too little room for dissent. On a two-axis grid, Singapore might sit right of center on economics and above the center on authority. Its own leaders often rejected Western labels altogether.

29.7

Sweden: A Welfare State

Main ideaSweden pairs high taxes and broad public services with private companies and open trade, and people debate what that mix proves.

A Swedish parent can take paid leave for more than a year after a child is born, shared between both parents. University is free for Swedish students, and health care is mostly public. These are features of a , a system where the government provides a wide safety net paid for by taxes.

Sweden’s Social Democratic Party led the government for most of the twentieth century and built much of this system. Taxes are high by world standards. Yet most Swedish companies are privately owned, and famous firms like Volvo, IKEA and Ericsson began as private businesses. Sweden also trades heavily with the world. Many call this mix .

People read Sweden in different ways. Some on the left see proof that a large safety net can work with a strong economy. Some on the right note that Sweden cut some taxes and opened schools and services to private providers from the 1990s on, and credit those market reforms. Both sides use Sweden as evidence, which shows how case studies can be argued.

29.8

Chile: From Allende to Pinochet

Main ideaChile swung from an elected socialist government to a military dictatorship with free-market policies, a history that challenges any simple model.

In 1970 Chileans elected Salvador Allende, a Marxist who promised a peaceful road to . His government took over copper mines and many large farms and businesses. Supporters saw a fairer country taking shape. Critics pointed to shortages, soaring prices and deep division between Chileans.

On September 11, 1973, the armed forces overthrew Allende in a , the seizure of power by force. Allende died during the attack on the presidential palace. General Augusto Pinochet then ruled as a dictator until 1990. Thousands of people were killed or disappeared, and many more were jailed or tortured, as Chile’s own later truth commissions documented.

Pinochet’s government brought in economists trained at the University of Chicago, often called the Chicago Boys. They sold state companies, cut trade barriers and set up private pension accounts. Supporters credit these policies with later growth. Critics say growth came with high inequality and cannot be separated from the repression. Chile shows free-market economics and harsh authoritarian rule in one government.

29.10

Models, Not Labels for People

Main ideaPolitical models help us compare ideas, but they should never be used to sort or stereotype real people.

Imagine a student takes an online political quiz and learns she is center-left. The next day a classmate calls her a communist. That jump is the danger of models. A model is a , a picture that leaves out detail so we can see a pattern. It is not the full truth about a person.

Real people hold mixed views. A farmer might support trade protection, which some call left, and also favor strong local churches, which some call right. Surveys by researchers such as the Pew Research Center have found that many Americans hold views that mix positions from both sides. A single dot on a grid cannot show that.

Using a model well means a few habits. Place ideas and policies, not whole people. Say which axis you mean. Admit that placements can be argued. Avoid a , a fixed belief that everyone in a group thinks the same way. Models are tools for thinking; people deserve to be heard in their own words.

Paths to Democracy and Away From It

Democracy
Big questionWhat helps a country become a democracy, and what warning signs show that a democracy is weakening?
The story

June in Seoul, 1987

Students and office workers fill the streets of Seoul, and a general makes a promise no one expected.

In June 1987 the streets of Seoul, South Korea, fill with students. They are angry about a government that chooses the president through a small, controlled electoral college. They want to vote for their president directly. Tear gas hangs in the air. A Yonsei University student, Lee Han-yeol, is struck by a tear gas canister during a protest and later dies. His death brings even more people into the streets.

Soon the marchers are not only students. Office workers in shirts and ties join during lunch breaks. Shop owners wave from doorways. Religious leaders speak out. The protests spread to cities across the country. Many South Koreans later called this the June Democracy Movement.

The ruling party has chosen Roh Tae-woo, a former army general and close ally of President Chun Doo-hwan, as its candidate. Many expect the government to send in troops. Instead, on June 29, Roh announces that the government will accept a direct presidential election and restore key freedoms. People in the streets cheer and cry.

In December 1987 South Koreans vote directly for their president. Roh himself wins, partly because the opposition vote splits between two candidates. Still, the rules have changed. Within a decade, power passes peacefully to former opposition leaders. A military-backed government has taken the first step toward handing power to voters.

Waves of Democracy

30.1

Huntington's Three Waves

Main ideaPolitical scientist Samuel Huntington described democracy spreading in waves, each followed by a partial pullback.

In 1991 the Harvard political scientist Samuel Huntington published The Third Wave. He noticed that countries did not become democracies one at a time at random. They moved in groups, like waves rolling onto a beach. Each wave was followed by a , when some new democracies fell back into dictatorship.

Huntington’s first long wave ran from the 1820s to the 1920s, as voting rights widened in the United States and Europe. A reverse wave followed with fascism and military rule in the 1920s and 1930s. A second wave came after World War II. The began in Portugal in 1974 and swept through southern Europe, Latin America, East Asia and, after 1989, Eastern Europe.

Huntington offered several causes: economic growth, changes in the Catholic Church, pressure from the United States and Europe, and , the way one country’s change inspires its neighbors. Critics note that waves are easier to see looking back, and that each country’s story has its own local causes. The model is a starting point, not a law.

Huntington described a wave of democratization as a group of transitions from non-democratic to democratic rule that happen within a certain period and clearly outnumber moves in the other direction. He counted three such waves in modern history.

Main ideaIn the 1970s Portugal and Spain left long dictatorships behind, one through a military revolt and one through negotiated reform.

On April 25, 1974, army officers in Portugal overthrew a dictatorship that had lasted nearly half a century. Crowds put red carnations in the soldiers’ rifles, and the event became known as the . After two unsettled years, Portugal adopted a democratic constitution in 1976.

Supporters of Spain’s path praise its compromises. Critics argue that a 1977 amnesty law meant crimes of the dictatorship were never tried in court.

30.3

South Korea's June Movement

Main ideaMass protests in 1987 pushed South Korea's military-backed government to accept direct presidential elections.

After 1961, South Korea was ruled for most years by military-backed leaders. The economy grew fast, turning a poor country into an industrial one. But elections were limited and critics were jailed. In 1980 the government crushed an uprising in the city of Gwangju, killing many civilians.

In 1987, President Chun Doo-hwan’s government refused to change the indirect way presidents were chosen. The followed: weeks of protests joined by students, workers and middle-class families. On June 29, the ruling party’s candidate, Roh Tae-woo, announced that the government would accept a , in which citizens vote for the president themselves.

A growing had joined students in the streets. South Korea later saw power pass peacefully to former opposition leaders such as Kim Young-sam and Kim Dae-jung.

Taiwan's Step-by-Step Change

Main ideaTaiwan moved from martial law to full democracy gradually, ending martial law in 1987 and holding its first direct presidential election in 1996.

From 1949 Taiwan was governed by the Kuomintang, or Nationalist Party, which had retreated there after losing China’s civil war. Taiwan lived under , rule with military powers that limited parties, the press and public protest. Critics of the government could be arrested.

Change came in steps. In 1986 activists formed the Democratic Progressive Party, even though new parties were not yet legal. President Chiang Ching-kuo did not crush it, and in 1987 his government lifted martial law. His successor, Lee Teng-hui, the first Taiwan-born president, continued reforms. In 1996 Taiwan held its first , and Lee won.

In 2000 the opposition candidate Chen Shui-bian won the presidency, and the Nationalists handed over power peacefully. Political scientists call this a , and many see a first one as a key test. Taiwan’s path shows reform can come partly from inside a ruling party, pushed by pressure from outside it.

The End of Apartheid

Main ideaSouth Africa ended apartheid through protest, pressure and negotiation, holding its first election open to all races in 1994.

Under , South Africa’s system of racial separation from 1948, Black South Africans could not vote in national elections and were forced into separate areas, schools and jobs. People resisted for decades, through strikes, boycotts, church leaders and movements like the African National Congress (ANC). Nelson Mandela spent 27 years in prison.

By the late 1980s, protests at home, economic from abroad and a costly economy pressed the government. In 1990 President F. W. de Klerk lifted the ban on the ANC and released Mandela. Years of hard followed, with violence threatening to derail the talks. Mandela and de Klerk shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1993.

On April 27, 1994, millions of South Africans lined up, many voting for the first time in their lives. The ANC won, and Mandela became president. A Truth and Reconciliation Commission later heard testimony about abuses. Some praised it for helping the country heal. Others felt it gave too little justice to victims.

Eastern Europe, 1989

Main ideaIn 1989 communist governments across Eastern Europe fell, mostly without war, opening the way to elections and new constitutions.

In Poland, the independent trade union , led by electrician Lech Wałęsa, grew from shipyard strikes in 1980 into a movement of millions. The government banned it, but by 1989 it had to negotiate. In partly free elections in June 1989, Solidarity candidates won nearly every seat they were allowed to contest.

Change raced across the region. Hungary opened its border with Austria. On November 9, 1989, the Berlin Wall opened, and crowds crossed freely. In Czechoslovakia, peaceful mass protests brought the , and the writer Václav Havel became president. In Romania, by contrast, the change was violent, and the dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu was executed.

Many of these countries later joined the European Union and NATO. Their paths since have differed. Some built lasting courts and free press. Others have faced disputes over whether governments have weakened checks on power. Scholars still debate why 1989 happened so fast; many point to Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev’s decision not to send troops.

Chile Votes No

Main ideaChile returned to democracy after voters rejected Pinochet in a 1988 vote, and an elected president took office in 1990.

Pinochet’s 1980 constitution called for a , a yes-or-no vote, on whether he would stay in power for eight more years. It was held on October 5, 1988. Many feared the vote would be rigged, but opposition parties, from the center to the left, joined forces behind the No campaign.

The No campaign used television spots full of music, humor and a rainbow logo, promising that joy was coming. Volunteers trained poll watchers to guard the count. The No side won about 56 percent of the vote. Pinochet accepted the result, partly because some members of his own government and the armed forces refused to overturn it.

In 1989 Chileans elected Patricio Aylwin, a Christian Democrat, and he took office in 1990. The transition kept limits that Pinochet had built in: he stayed head of the army until 1998, and some seats in the Senate were appointed. Chileans debated these limits for decades. The case shows a , where old rulers keep some power as they leave.

Away From Democracy

30.8

What Backsliding Means

Main ideaPolitical scientists use democratic backsliding for the slow weakening of democracy by elected leaders, rather than a sudden coup.

In the past, democracies often ended with tanks in the streets. Political scientists today describe a quieter path they call : an elected government slowly weakens the rules that let voters remove it. Each step may look legal. Together they tilt the field.

Harvard scholars Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt describe this in How Democracies Die (2018). They argue that democracies depend on written rules and also on , unwritten habits such as accepting defeat and treating rivals as legitimate. When leaders break those habits, the written rules alone may not protect democracy.

The result can be what some scholars call : elections still happen, but the ruling side controls courts, media and election rules so tightly that the opposition can rarely win. Scholars debate where to draw the line, and governments accused of backsliding usually reject the label. Careful students weigh the evidence case by case.

Levitsky and Ziblatt argue that many democracies now end not at the hands of generals but through elected leaders who slowly weaken the institutions meant to check them. They say two unwritten norms matter most: mutual toleration of rivals and restraint in using power.

Warning Lights

Main ideaIndependent courts, a free press and the rule of law act as warning lights; when they dim, democracy may be weakening.

Think of a car’s dashboard. A single warning light does not mean the engine has failed, but it tells the driver to look closer. Political scientists watch several lights in a democracy. The first is the : the idea that everyone, including leaders, must obey the same laws.

A second light is . Judges must be free to rule against the government. Warning signs include packing courts with loyal judges, ignoring rulings or removing judges early. A third light is a . Signs of trouble include jailing reporters, forcing critical outlets to close or letting allies buy up media.

Other lights include fair election rules and the right to protest. Groups such as Freedom House and V-Dem, a research project based in Sweden, rate countries on measures like these every year. Their ratings are widely used but also debated, since experts must make judgment calls. No single light proves a democracy is failing; patterns across many lights matter most.

Backsliding on Left and Right

Main ideaScholars describe democratic backsliding under governments of both the left and the right, and those governments dispute the charge.

In Venezuela, Hugo Chávez won the presidency in 1998 promising to use oil wealth to help the poor, and many Venezuelans credit him with new social programs. Over time his government and that of his successor, Nicolás Maduro, gained control of the courts and the election authority, and critical TV stations lost their licenses. Many political scientists describe Venezuela as having moved from democracy to authoritarian rule.

In Hungary, Viktor Orbán’s right-leaning Fidesz party won a two-thirds majority in 2010 and wrote a new constitution. Supporters say he defended national sovereignty and traditional values. Critics, including European Union bodies, have said his government weakened courts, reshaped media ownership and redrew election rules in its favor. Hungary still holds competitive elections.

The lesson is that backsliding is not owned by one side. Scholars watch , the ways branches of government limit each other, no matter who is in power. Leaders accused of backsliding usually say they are carrying out the voters’ will against . Careful citizens ask the same questions of governments they like and governments they do not.
