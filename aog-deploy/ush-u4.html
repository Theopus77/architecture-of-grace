The Early Republic

1789–1844

In 1789 the Constitution was only words on paper. No one knew for sure whether it would work. Over the next 50 years, Americans found out. The first presidents set examples that leaders still follow. Two political parties formed and fought hard, yet power passed peacefully from one to the other. The young nation stood up to powerful countries in Europe and fought a second war with Britain.

The nation also grew in size and changed shape. The Louisiana Purchase doubled its land. Explorers mapped the West. Roads, canals, steamboats and railroads connected farms to cities. Factories began to spin cloth in New England, and Illinois joined the Union as a state. But growth came at a high cost to Native nations, who lost huge amounts of land.

Growth also spread slavery. The cotton gin made cotton king in the South, and hundreds of thousands of enslaved people were forced into new cotton lands. By 1820 an argument over Missouri showed that slavery was splitting the nation into sections. As you read, ask who gained from the nation's growth, who paid for it, and how Americans began to divide.

The story

Mount Vernon: Two Stories on One Hill

George Washington's beloved home was also a place where hundreds of people lived in slavery.

Mount Vernon sits on a green hill above the Potomac River in Virginia. It was George Washington's home. He called it the place he loved most. During the Revolution and his years as president, he wrote letters home about crops, fences and animals. Mount Vernon was a huge plantation made up of five farms. Washington grew wheat and corn, ran a gristmill and caught fish in the river.

Washington did not do most of the work himself. Enslaved people did. When Washington died in 1799, 317 enslaved people lived at Mount Vernon. They plowed fields, cooked meals, spun thread, built barrels and shod horses. Many families were split between different farms on the property. Parents worked from sunrise to sunset and then walked to see their children at night.

One of them was a young woman named Ona Judge. She worked as a personal servant to Martha Washington. She traveled with the Washingtons to Philadelphia, the capital in the 1790s. Pennsylvania had a law that was slowly ending slavery. Ona Judge saw free Black people living and working in the city. In 1796 she learned that she would be given away as a wedding gift to Martha's granddaughter. One evening she slipped out of the President's House and escaped by ship to New Hampshire.

Washington tried for years to get Ona Judge back. She refused to return. She married, raised a family and lived the rest of her life in freedom. The next year, Hercules, Washington's highly skilled cook, also escaped. Both of them chose freedom over life in the household of the most famous man in America.

Washington's feelings about slavery changed over his life. In his will, he ordered that the people he owned be freed after Martha's death. Martha freed them early, in 1801. But more than half of the enslaved people at Mount Vernon belonged to the estate of Martha's first husband. They were not freed. They were passed down to her grandchildren.

Today visitors to Mount Vernon learn about both stories on that hill. One is the story of the man who led a new nation built on liberty. The other is the story of the people whose work made his home run, and who were denied that liberty.

8.1

The First President Takes Office

Main ideaAs the first president, George Washington knew that almost everything he did would become a model for later leaders.

On April 30, 1789, George Washington stood on a balcony of Federal Hall in New York City. New York was the nation’s first capital. A crowd filled the street below. Washington placed his hand on a Bible and took the oath of office. The crowd cheered. The United States had its first president under the new Constitution.

The Constitution described the job of president in just a few paragraphs. It did not explain many everyday details. Washington knew that his choices would set a . A precedent is an action or decision that later people follow as an example. He wrote that he walked on untrodden ground, meaning a path no one had walked before.

Even small questions mattered. What should people call the president? Some senators, including Vice President John Adams, wanted a grand title that sounded like a king’s. Others thought that was dangerous in a , a nation where citizens choose their leaders. In the end, the simple title "Mr. President" was used. Washington also chose to serve only two terms. That precedent lasted for almost 150 years.

8.2

Cabinet, Courts and Rights

Main ideaCongress created departments to help the president and a system of federal courts to settle legal questions.

The president could not run the government alone. Congress created three departments in 1789: State, Treasury and War. It also created the job of attorney general, the government’s chief lawyer. Washington picked strong leaders. Thomas Jefferson ran the State Department, which handled relations with other countries. Alexander Hamilton ran the Treasury, which handled money. Henry Knox led the War Department, and Edmund Randolph became attorney general.

Washington met with these leaders to hear their advice. This group became known as the . The Constitution does not use that word. The cabinet grew out of Washington’s habit of asking for advice. Every president since has had one.

The Constitution created a Supreme Court but gave few details. The of 1789 filled them in. It set the Supreme Court at six justices and created lower federal courts around the country. John Jay became the first chief justice. That same year, Congress also sent a list of amendments to the states. In 1791 these ten amendments were approved as the Bill of Rights.

8.3

Counting the People

Main ideaThe first census in 1790 counted about 3.9 million people, including about 700,000 enslaved people.

The Constitution says the government must count the people every ten years. This count is called the . The number of seats each state gets in the House of Representatives depends on it. In 1790 officials called marshals went door to door. They rode on horseback to farms, towns and frontier cabins.

The first census counted about 3.9 million people. Most lived on farms near the Atlantic coast. Cities were small by today’s standards. New York City was the largest, with about 33,000 people. Philadelphia was second. Virginia had more people than any other state.

The census also showed how deeply slavery was built into the nation. About 700,000 people were enslaved, most of them in the South. About 60,000 free African Americans were counted, too. Because of the , each enslaved person counted as three-fifths of a person for deciding House seats. Most Native Americans were not counted at all.

Hamilton's Plan and Its Critics

8.4

Paying Off the War Debt

Main ideaHamilton wanted the national government to pay all the Revolutionary War debts so the new nation would be trusted with money.

The Revolution was expensive. The national government and the states had borrowed huge sums to pay for it. They owed money to other countries and to their own citizens. Alexander Hamilton believed the nation had to pay its . If it did not, no one would ever lend it money again.

Hamilton’s plan had two big parts. First, the national government would pay back its war debts in full. Second, it would take over, or , the debts of the states. Many people objected. Some states, such as Virginia, had already paid off much of their debt. Why should they help pay for states that had not? Critics also pointed out that had bought old war bonds cheaply from soldiers and farmers. Now the speculators would earn big profits.

James Madison and Thomas Jefferson worked out a deal with Hamilton in 1790. Southern members of Congress would support Hamilton’s debt plan. In return, the nation’s permanent capital would be built in the South, on the Potomac River. Philadelphia would serve as the capital for ten years while the new city was built. In 1800 the government moved to Washington, D.C.

A Bank for the Nation

Main ideaThe fight over a national bank raised a lasting question: how much power does the Constitution give the national government?

Hamilton also wanted a national . It would keep the government’s money safe, make loans and print paper money people could trust. In 1791 Congress created the Bank of the United States. It was based in Philadelphia and was allowed to operate for 20 years.

Jefferson and Madison said the bank was unconstitutional. The Constitution does not mention a bank. Jefferson believed in , meaning the government could do only what the Constitution clearly says. Hamilton believed in . He pointed to the clause that lets Congress make all laws that are "necessary and proper" for carrying out its powers. Collecting taxes and borrowing money were listed powers, so a bank could help do them.

Washington agreed with Hamilton and signed the bill. Hamilton also asked for a , a tax on goods from other countries. Tariffs raised money and helped American makers compete. The debate over strict and loose construction did not end in the 1790s. Americans still argue about it today.

8.6

The Whiskey Rebellion

Main ideaWhen farmers rose up against a tax on whiskey, Washington sent a large army to show that the new government could enforce its laws.

To help pay the debts, Congress passed a tax on whiskey in 1791. This was an , a tax on a product made inside the country. The tax hit western farmers hard. Roads over the mountains were rough. It was easier to turn corn and rye into whiskey and carry it to market in barrels. Some farmers even used whiskey in place of money.

In western Pennsylvania, angry farmers refused to pay. Some attacked tax collectors. In 1794 the protest grew into an armed uprising. Several thousand rebels gathered near Pittsburgh. People called it the Whiskey .

Washington remembered Shays’s Rebellion, when the old government under the Articles of Confederation could not act. This time he called up about 13,000 militia soldiers from several states and rode out with them. The rebels scattered before any battle. A few were arrested, and Washington later pardoned the two men found guilty. The message was clear. The new national government could enforce its laws.

8.7

Two Parties Form

Main ideaDisagreements between Hamilton and Jefferson led to the nation's first two political parties.

The writers of the Constitution did not plan for political parties. Many of them feared that parties would split the nation. But by the mid-1790s, two groups had formed. A is a group of people who share ideas about government and work to elect leaders.

The followed Hamilton. They wanted a strong national government. They supported the bank and the growth of trade, cities and manufacturing. Many merchants and business owners in the Northeast joined them. In foreign affairs, they leaned toward Britain, America’s biggest trading partner.

The followed Jefferson and Madison. They wanted a smaller national government and more power for the states. They believed the nation’s future lay with small farmers. Many Southerners and Westerners joined them. They leaned toward France, which had helped win the Revolution. Each party started newspapers that attacked the other, sometimes in very harsh words.

8.8

Staying Out of Europe's Wars

Main ideaWashington kept the young nation neutral in a war between Britain and France, and treaties with Britain and Spain eased tensions.

In 1789 the French Revolution began. The French people overthrew their king. At first many Americans cheered. But the revolution turned violent, and in 1793 France and Britain went to war. Democratic-Republicans wanted to help France. Federalists wanted to stay close to Britain.

Washington believed the young nation was too weak for war. In 1793 he declared that the United States would stay , meaning it would not take sides. A French diplomat named Edmond Genêt tried to get Americans to fight for France anyway. Washington asked France to call him home. Jefferson disagreed with the president’s policies and left the cabinet at the end of 1793.

Britain still caused trouble. British ships seized American ships trading with France. British soldiers also stayed in forts on U.S. land near the Great Lakes. Washington sent John Jay to London. In Jay&#x27;s Treaty (1794), Britain agreed to leave the forts. But it did not stop seizing ships, and many Americans were furious. A year later, Pinckney&#x27;s Treaty with Spain went over much better. It let Americans ship goods down the Mississippi River and through the port of New Orleans.

8.9

War in the Northwest Territory

Main ideaAfter years of fighting, Native nations north of the Ohio River were forced to give up most of what is now Ohio in the Treaty of Greenville.

Settlers were pouring into the Northwest Territory, the land north of the Ohio River. This was the homeland of Native nations such as the Miami, Shawnee, Delaware and Potawatomi. They had not agreed to give it up. Led by the Miami leader Little Turtle and the Shawnee leader Blue Jacket, they formed a , an alliance of nations.

The confederacy won major battles. In 1791 its warriors crushed an American army led by General Arthur St. Clair. Hundreds of U.S. soldiers died. It was one of the worst defeats the U.S. Army ever suffered. Washington then sent General Anthony Wayne with a larger, better-trained army.

In 1794 Wayne’s army won the Battle of Fallen Timbers, near today’s Toledo, Ohio. The Native nations had hoped British soldiers at a nearby fort would help them, but the British closed the gates. In 1795 leaders of the nations signed the . They gave up most of present-day Ohio. The treaty also handed over small pieces of land for American forts. One was a square of land six miles wide where the Chicago River meets Lake Michigan.

Washington Says Goodbye

Main ideaIn his Farewell Address, Washington warned Americans about political parties, regional divisions and permanent alliances.

By 1796 Washington was tired. He had served two terms as president. Many people wanted him to stay, but he decided to go home to Mount Vernon. By stepping down, he showed that power in a republic passes peacefully from one leader to the next.

Before he left, Washington wrote a long letter to the American people. It was printed in newspapers in September 1796. It became known as his . Hamilton helped him write it. Washington used it to share lessons he hoped the country would remember.

First, he warned against the spirit of party. He feared that angry political parties could tear the nation apart. Second, he warned against , or putting the interests of one region above the whole country. Third, he said the nation should avoid permanent with foreign countries. For many years afterward, American leaders followed this advice about alliances.

Big questionHow did the young United States grow and test its strength, at home and against more powerful nations?
The story

Into the Unknown West

A small band of explorers set out from Illinois to cross a continent they had never seen.

In May 1804, a group of about three dozen men pushed their boats into the Missouri River. They had spent the winter at Camp Dubois, a camp on the Illinois side of the Mississippi, near St. Louis. Their leaders were Meriwether Lewis and William Clark. President Thomas Jefferson had sent them west. They called themselves the Corps of Discovery.

Jefferson wanted to know what lay in the huge land the nation had just bought. He hoped they would find a water route to the Pacific Ocean. He asked them to map rivers, study plants and animals, and meet the Native nations along the way. The men rowed, poled and pulled their boats upstream against the strong current, mile after mile.

They spent their first winter among the Mandan and Hidatsa peoples, in what is now North Dakota. There they met Sacagawea, a young Shoshone woman, and her French Canadian husband. Both joined the expedition. In February 1805, Sacagawea gave birth to a baby boy. She carried him all the way to the Pacific and back. Another member of the Corps was York, a Black man enslaved by William Clark. He hunted, scouted and did the same hard work as everyone else.

In the Rocky Mountains, the Corps needed horses badly. When they met a band of Shoshone, Sacagawea realized that its chief was her own brother. The Shoshone traded horses to the explorers. Later, crossing the snowy Bitterroot Mountains, the men nearly starved. The Nez Perce people fed them and helped them build canoes.

The story

Smoke on the Mississippi

When the first steamboat came down the great river, it changed how the West lived, worked and traded.

For thousands of years, the Mississippi River was a highway for the peoples who lived along it. Native nations traveled it in canoes. Later, American farmers built flatboats, big wooden boxes that floated downstream with the current. They loaded them with corn, flour, pork and whiskey and drifted all the way to New Orleans. The trip could take weeks.

Going back upstream was the hard part. Crews pushed long, narrow keelboats against the current with poles or pulled them with ropes from the shore. They might travel only a few miles a day. Many flatboat crews simply sold their boats for lumber in New Orleans and walked home on a long forest trail called the Natchez Trace.

In 1807 Robert Fulton showed that a steam engine could power a boat up the Hudson River in New York. In 1811 a steamboat named the New Orleans set out from Pittsburgh to try the western rivers. That winter, a series of powerful earthquakes struck near New Madrid, in what is now Missouri. The ground shook, riverbanks fell in, and parts of the Mississippi ran wild. The little steamboat made it through and reached New Orleans in January 1812.

Within a few decades, hundreds of steamboats churned up and down the Mississippi, Ohio and Missouri Rivers. Their tall smokestacks puffed black smoke. Their paddle wheels slapped the water. Towns along the rivers, such as St. Louis, Louisville and Cairo, Illinois, grew into busy ports. Farmers could now ship crops downstream and get goods like tools, cloth and coffee back upstream quickly.

Steamboats were exciting but dangerous. Boilers sometimes exploded, and snags could rip open a hull. The boats also carried darker cargo. Enslaved people worked as deckhands and firemen, feeding wood into the fires. And many enslaved people were shipped down the river to be sold in the cotton country of the Deep South.

The steamboat tied the West to the rest of the nation and to the world. Crops, goods and people now moved both ways on the rivers. It was one part of a transportation revolution that would change where Americans lived and how they earned a living.

Section 2

Roads, Rivers and Rails

10.3

The National Road and the Steamboat

Main ideaNew roads and steamboats made travel faster and cheaper, tying the West more closely to the East.

In 1800 most roads were dirt paths. They turned to mud in the rain and were full of stumps and holes. It could cost more to haul goods 30 miles over land than to ship them across the Atlantic. Americans called for better , meaning roads and canals built to help trade.

In 1811 workers began building the , paid for by the national government. It started in Cumberland, Maryland, and reached Wheeling on the Ohio River by 1818. Later it was pushed west across Ohio and Indiana and ended at Vandalia, Illinois. Wagons, stagecoaches, herds of animals and families heading west crowded onto it.

Rivers were even better highways, once boats could go upstream. Robert Fulton’s steamboat traveled up the Hudson River in 1807. The trip from New York City to Albany took about 32 hours, far faster than sailboats. Soon carried people and goods up and down the Mississippi, Ohio and Missouri Rivers. Shipping costs dropped, and river towns boomed.

10.4

The Erie Canal

Main ideaThe Erie Canal linked the Great Lakes to New York City, cutting shipping costs and making New York the nation's greatest port.

New York’s governor, DeWitt Clinton, had a bold plan. He wanted to dig a , a human-made waterway, across New York State. It would connect the Hudson River at Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo. Critics laughed and called it "Clinton’s Ditch." Work began in 1817.

Thousands of workers, including many Irish immigrants, dug the canal mostly by hand with shovels and horse-drawn tools. They built , which raise and lower boats like water elevators, to climb hills. The Erie Canal opened in 1825. It was 363 miles long. Mules on a towpath beside the water pulled the boats.

The canal was a huge success. Shipping costs from the Great Lakes to New York City fell sharply. Crops from Ohio and other western states flowed east, and manufactured goods flowed west. New York City became the nation’s busiest port. Towns along the canal, such as Rochester and Buffalo, grew fast. Other states rushed to build canals of their own, including Illinois.

10.5

The First Railroads

Main ideaRailroads began in the 1830s and spread quickly, because trains could go almost anywhere and run all year.

Canals had limits. They froze in winter, and they could only be built where there was enough water. had neither problem. In 1827 businessmen in Baltimore started the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, one of the first in the country. Its first short section of track opened in 1830.

Early trains were small and bumpy. In 1830 Peter Cooper built a tiny steam called the Tom Thumb. It raced a horse-drawn railcar. The engine was pulling ahead until a belt slipped, and the horse won. But people could see that steam engines would soon beat horses.

Railroads spread fast. Sparks from engines set fires, and crashes were common. Still, trains were faster than canal boats and could run in any season. By 1840 the United States had nearly 3,000 miles of track. By 1860 it had about 30,000 miles, more than any other country. In the 1850s, Chicago would become the railroad center of the nation.

Section 3

Cotton and Slavery Spread

10.6

The Cotton Gin

Main ideaThe cotton gin made growing cotton very profitable, which spread slavery across the Deep South.

In the 1790s, slavery seemed to be growing weaker in some places. Northern states were ending it, and tobacco had worn out much Southern soil. Then came a small machine with huge effects. In 1793 Eli Whitney built the . It used a turning drum with wire teeth to pull cotton fibers away from the sticky seeds.

Before the gin, one worker could clean only a pound or so of cotton a day by hand. With a gin, far more cotton could be cleaned. Factories in Britain and New England wanted all the cotton they could get. Growing cotton suddenly became very profitable. People called it .

Planters rushed to new lands in Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana and later Texas. Much of this land had been taken from Native nations. Cotton needed many workers to plant, weed and pick it. Planters used enslaved people to do this work. The gin saved labor in cleaning cotton, but it led to far more slavery overall. The number of enslaved people in the United States grew from about 700,000 in 1790 to nearly 4 million by 1860.

10.7

The Slave Trade Inside the Nation

Main ideaAfter bringing enslaved people from Africa was banned in 1808, a huge trade in enslaved people grew inside the United States.

The Constitution allowed Congress to ban bringing enslaved people from other countries starting in 1808. Congress did so. But slavery itself was still legal in the South. As cotton spread, planters in the Deep South wanted more workers. Many enslaved people lived in older states such as Virginia and Maryland.

A large grew up. Domestic means inside the country. Traders bought enslaved people in the Upper South and sold them in the Deep South. Historians estimate that about one million people were moved this way between 1790 and 1860. Many were forced to walk hundreds of miles, chained together in lines called . Others were shipped by sea or down the Mississippi River.

The trade tore families apart. Husbands, wives, parents and children could be sold to different owners and never see each other again. At , people were sold to whoever paid the most. For enslaved families, the fear of being sold away was one of the worst parts of slavery. After freedom came, many spent years searching for lost relatives.

10.8

Surviving and Resisting Slavery

Main ideaEnslaved people kept their families, faith and culture alive and resisted slavery in both quiet and open ways.

Enslaved people worked from sunrise to sunset under the threat of punishment. Yet they built strong communities. They formed families, even though the law did not protect their marriages. They told stories, sang songs and passed down traditions. Many became Christians and met, sometimes in secret, to worship. Their religious songs, called , often carried messages of hope and freedom.

Enslaved people also practiced , meaning actions taken against slavery. Some worked slowly on purpose, broke tools or pretended to be sick. Some secretly learned to read. Many ran away, hiding in swamps and woods or heading north. A few planned revolts. In 1800 a blacksmith named Gabriel planned an uprising near Richmond, Virginia, but it was discovered before it began.

In 1831 Nat Turner, an enslaved preacher in Virginia, led the largest revolt of the era. His group killed about 55 white people before militia stopped them. In the panic that followed, white mobs and militias killed many Black people, including some who had nothing to do with the revolt. Turner was caught and executed. Southern states then passed harsher laws, including laws against teaching enslaved people to read.

One Nation, Many Sections

10.9

An Era of Good Feelings

Main ideaAfter the War of 1812, national pride grew, and leaders worked to build a stronger, more connected economy.

In 1816 James Monroe, a Democratic-Republican, was elected president. The Federalist Party was fading, so for a few years there was really only one party. A Boston newspaper called this time an . Monroe was re-elected in 1820 with almost no opposition.

Nationalism was strong. Congress created the Second Bank of the United States in 1816. It also passed a , a tax on imports meant to help American factories compete. Henry Clay of Kentucky called his plan for a bank, a tariff and internal improvements the . He hoped it would tie the regions together, with each one helping the others.

The Supreme Court, led by John Marshall, also strengthened the national government. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), it ruled that a state could not tax the national bank. In 1819 Spain agreed to give Florida to the United States. But the good feelings were thinner than they looked. Sharp arguments were coming over tariffs, money and, above all, slavery.

10.10

Illinois Becomes a State

Main ideaIllinois became the 21st state in 1818 as a free state, though slavery and forced servitude still existed there.

Illinois was part of the old Northwest Territory. It became its own territory in 1809. At that time most American settlers lived in the south, near the Mississippi and Ohio Rivers. Many had come from Kentucky, Tennessee and Virginia. On December 3, 1818, Illinois became the 21st state. Its first capital was Kaskaskia. The capital moved to Vandalia in 1820 and to Springfield in 1839.

Nathaniel Pope, the territory’s delegate to Congress, made one change with huge effects. He got the state’s northern border moved about 41 miles north. That put the land at the mouth of the Chicago River, and a stretch of the Lake Michigan shore, inside Illinois. Chicago was only a tiny settlement then. It became a town in 1833 and a city in 1837.

The Northwest Ordinance had banned slavery in the territory. Illinois’s first also banned bringing in new slavery. But the rules had gaps. Some people who were already enslaved stayed that way. Others were held as , forced to work for years under contracts that were close to slavery. In 1824 Illinois voters decided whether to call a convention that might allow slavery. Governor Edward Coles led the fight against it, and the voters said no.

10.11

The Missouri Compromise

Main ideaThe Missouri Compromise of 1820 kept a balance between free and slave states, but it showed how slavery was dividing the nation.

In 1819 the United States had 11 free states and 11 slave states. That balance gave each side equal votes in the Senate. Then Missouri, part of the Louisiana Purchase, asked to join as a slave state. That would tip the balance. A New York congressman proposed slowly ending slavery in Missouri. Southern members were furious. The debate grew bitter.

Henry Clay helped work out a deal in 1820. It was called the . A compromise is an agreement where each side gives up something. Missouri would join as a slave state. Maine, which had been part of Massachusetts, would join as a free state. The balance in the Senate would stay even.

The compromise also drew a line across the rest of the Louisiana Purchase at 36°30′ north latitude. Slavery would be banned north of the line, except in Missouri. For the time being, the crisis was over. But the fight showed the growing power of , the loyalty to one’s own region. Many leaders feared that slavery could one day split the nation.

The Monroe Doctrine

Main ideaIn 1823 President Monroe warned European nations not to set up new colonies or interfere in the Americas.

In the early 1800s, many colonies in Latin America won independence from Spain and Portugal. Mexico, Argentina, Chile, Colombia and others became new nations. Americans cheered. But some leaders worried that European powers might try to take these lands back.

Secretary of State John Quincy Adams helped President Monroe shape an answer. In December 1823, Monroe gave a message to Congress. It later became known as the . A doctrine is a statement of official policy. Monroe said the Americas were closed to new European colonies. The United States would stay out of Europe’s affairs. In return, Europe should stay out of the Western Hemisphere.

At the time, the United States did not have a strong navy to back up these words. Britain’s navy, which also wanted to keep other European powers out, did much of that job. But the Monroe Doctrine became a lasting part of American . Later presidents used it to claim a special role in Latin America, which many Latin Americans came to resent.
