The Gemara and the Two Talmuds

The Talmud

The Mishnah was short. The book that grew around it is huge. In this unit you will meet the rabbis who made it. Some lived by a lake in Galilee. Some lived by a river in Babylon, in the land that is now Iraq. For about three hundred years they asked questions about the Mishnah, and their discussion became the Gemara.

Then you will open the book itself. You will learn what a daf is, why every tractate starts on page 2, and who all the people are whose words crowd the edges of the page. You will meet Rashi, who lived in France, and his grandsons, who loved to find contradictions. You will meet a printer in Venice and a family of printers in Vilna.

By the end you will be able to read a citation like Berakhot 2a and find the page, in a book or on a free website. You will know why there are two Talmuds and which one people usually mean. And you will know why, on any morning, people in Chicago, Denver and Jerusalem can all be reading the same page.

The big question

How did the Mishnah grow into the Talmud, and how does one page hold so many voices?

Babylon and the Land of Israel

The Gemara
Big questionHow did rabbis in two faraway lands turn the short Mishnah into the long Talmud?
The story

A Question Crosses the Desert

A question asked by a lake in Tiberias finds its answer in Babylon, three generations later.

The sun is warm on the Sea of Galilee. In the town of Tiberias, around the year 250, a student reads a line of the Mishnah out loud. It is a rule about a lost coat. He stops. "But what if the coat has no mark on it?" he asks. His teacher, Rabbi Yochanan, nods. "Good question," he says. "I am not sure. Let us keep it."

The question does not stay in Tiberias. A traveler named Rav Dimi is packing for a long trip. He is going east to Babylon, where many Jews live. He carries no books. He carries teachings in his memory. He has learned this question by heart, along with dozens of others. Then he walks with a caravan for many weeks.

In Babylon, the rabbis meet in a big hall near a river. Rav Dimi tells them what the teachers in the Land of Israel said. Some agree. Some do not. One rabbi finds a verse from the Torah that helps. Another finds an old teaching that seems to say the opposite. They argue all afternoon. They do not finish. The question is passed to their students.

Years go by. Then more years. Rabbis who were babies when the question was first asked are now old men with white beards. In a school called Pumbedita, a teacher finally gives an answer that most of the others accept. He explains his reason step by step. His answer is remembered. Much later it is written down right beside the question.

This is how the Gemara was made. Not by one person in one place, but by many rabbis in two lands, over about three hundred years. A question in one town, an answer in another, and every step in between kept on the page. When you open the Talmud today, you can still hear them talking.

Section 1

Two Lands, One Mishnah

9.1

After the Mishnah

Main ideaThe Gemara is the rabbis' long discussion of the Mishnah, and Mishnah plus Gemara equals Talmud.

Around the year 200, Rabbi Judah the Prince finished the Mishnah. It was short and clear. But short rules make people ask questions. What does this word mean? What if two rules seem to disagree? Why did the Mishnah say it this way? Rabbis in the Land of Israel and in Babylon began to study it line by line.

The rabbis’ discussion of the Mishnah is called the . The word comes from Aramaic and means something like "completion" or "study." The rabbis of the Gemara are called . One of them is an , which means "one who speaks" or "one who explains." They lived from about 200 to about 500 CE.

Put the two parts together and you get the . Talmud means "learning." A page of Talmud shows a line of Mishnah, then the Gemara that talks about it. The Gemara does not just give an answer. It shows the question, the proof, the objection and the reply, so a reader can follow the whole path.

Think it through
One Line, Then a Question

Mishnah: If you find a lost coat, announce it so the owner can claim it. Gemara: How long must the finder keep announcing it? What if the coat has no special mark? What if the owner has already given up hope of finding it? The rabbis take each question in turn.

9.2

The Land of Israel

Main ideaIn the Land of Israel, rabbis in Galilee towns like Tiberias studied the Mishnah under Roman rule.

The Mishnah was finished in the Land of Israel, in the north, in a hilly region called . Rome ruled the land. Many rabbis lived in the towns of Sepphoris, Caesarea and , a town on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. Tiberias became the most important center for study after about 250.

The best-known teacher there was Rabbi Yochanan. Historians think he died around 279 CE. His study partner was Resh Lakish. The Talmud says Resh Lakish had once been a strong man who lived by his sword, and Rabbi Yochanan talked him into a life of study. The two argued about almost every line.

Rabbi Yochanan valued his partner’s objections. The Talmud tells that after Resh Lakish died, other students only agreed with the teacher, and this made him sad. A good study partner pushes back. The rabbis of the Land of Israel wrote their Gemara in their own kind of Aramaic, with plenty of Hebrew mixed in.

From the source
A Partner Who Pushed Back
The Gemara tells that after Resh Lakish died, a new student was sent to study with Rabbi Yochanan. The student kept agreeing with him. Rabbi Yochanan said that Resh Lakish used to raise twenty-four objections to each thing he said, and answering them made the teaching clear. Agreement, he said, did not help him learn.
Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 84a, retold in plain words from the William Davidson Talmud (Sefaria, 2017) (paraphrased)

9.3

Babylon, Today's Iraq

Main ideaA large Jewish community had lived in Babylon since the exile, and it became the Talmud's other home.

Far to the east of the Land of Israel lay , between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Today that land is Iraq. Jews had lived there for a very long time. In 586 BCE the Babylonians destroyed the First Temple and carried many people from Judah into exile. Some went home later. Many stayed.

By the time of the Mishnah, Babylonia was a large and settled Jewish community. Farmers grew dates and grain. Traders sailed the rivers. The rulers were Persian kings, called the Sasanians from about 224 CE. The Persians let the Jewish community run many of its own affairs.

The community had a leader called the , the "head of the exile." Jewish tradition says his family came from King David. The Exilarch dealt with the Persian court and helped support the schools. In these schools the rabbis of Babylon studied the Mishnah that had come from the west.

Section 2

The Great Schools

9.4

Sura and Rav

Main ideaRav brought the Mishnah to Babylon and founded the academy at Sura, where Talmud study began there.

Tradition says a teacher called Rav had studied with Judah the Prince in the Land of Israel. Around 219 CE he traveled east to Babylonia. He brought the Mishnah with him, in his memory. He settled in a town called and started a school. A school like this is called an .

Another great teacher, Shmuel, led the school at Nehardea. Tradition remembers him as a doctor who also knew the stars. Rav and Shmuel disagreed often. The Gemara records both opinions. A later rule says: in matters of ritual, follow Rav; in matters of money, follow Shmuel.

Sura lasted for hundreds of years. Rav Ashi taught there much later, around 400. The academies of Babylonia did not look like a school today. Students sat in rows by rank. The head of the academy sat in front. A speaker repeated his words loudly so everyone could hear. Then the arguing began.

From the source
When Two Teachers Disagree
The Gemara states a rule for the many disagreements between Rav and Shmuel. When the question is about ritual, such as what is permitted or forbidden, the law follows Rav. When the question is about money, such as who owes what, the law follows Shmuel.
Babylonian Talmud, Bekhorot 49b, retold in plain words from the William Davidson Talmud (Sefaria, 2017) (paraphrased)

9.5

Pumbedita, Abaye and Rava

Main ideaAt Pumbedita, Abaye and Rava argued so well that later readers called all Talmud study "the debates of Abaye and Rava."

In 259 CE the town of Nehardea was destroyed in a war. Its scholars moved. A new academy grew up at , on the Euphrates river. For centuries Sura and Pumbedita were the two great schools of Babylonia. Students called them simply "the two academies."

The most famous pair from Pumbedita were Abaye and Rava. They lived in the 300s CE. They had studied together as young men. Then they argued for the rest of their lives. The Gemara records their debates on hundreds of pages. In most of them, later readers follow Rava’s view. But Abaye’s side is always kept.

Twice a year, in the months of Adar and Elul, the academies held a . Farmers and traders left their work and came to study for a whole month. They reviewed one tractate together. Then they went home. The Talmud was never only for rabbis. It was for anyone who came.

Behind them sit farmers who have left their date palms, and traders who have tied up their boats. A speaker repeats each line so the back rows can hear. 9.6

Travelers With Good Memories

Main ideaTravelers carried teachings between the Land of Israel and Babylon, so the two centers kept answering each other.

There were no phones and no printing presses. So how did the rabbis of Tiberias know what was said in Sura? People walked. Some rabbis made the long journey again and again. They learned teachings by heart in one land and repeated them in the other. The Gemara often says, "When Rav Dimi came, he said..."

Rav Dimi and a scholar named Ulla were two of these travelers. Their trips took weeks each way. Because of them, an answer given in Babylon could be tested in Galilee, and a ruling from Galilee could be argued over in Pumbedita. The two Gemaras grew apart, but they never stopped talking.

Both centers spoke , the everyday language of the Middle East then. But each had its own kind. Babylonian Aramaic and the Aramaic of Galilee sounded a little different, like English in Chicago and English in London. The Mishnah stayed in Hebrew, and the Gemara around it was in Aramaic.

Section 3

Two Talmuds

9.7

The Jerusalem Talmud

Main ideaThe Jerusalem Talmud, finished around 400 CE, is the Gemara of the Land of Israel, though it was made mostly in Tiberias.

The rabbis of the Land of Israel finished their Gemara first, around the year 400. It is called the Jerusalem Talmud, or in Hebrew the . The name is a little surprising. It was not made in Jerusalem. Most of the work was done in Tiberias, in Galilee. The name honors the holy city.

The Yerushalmi is shorter than the Babylonian Talmud. Its arguments are often brief. Historians think the rabbis of the Land of Israel had a hard time under the Roman rulers, who by then were Christian, and the work stopped earlier than it did in Babylon.

The Yerushalmi has Gemara on 39 of the Mishnah. A tractate is one book of the Mishnah on one subject. Most of the Yerushalmi’s tractates are in the first four orders. It is the main source for the laws of farming in the Land of Israel, which the Babylonian rabbis had less reason to discuss.

Think it through
A Name and a Place

A book called the Jerusalem Talmud was written mostly in Tiberias, about 70 miles north of Jerusalem. Readers have given the name two explanations. One: Jerusalem stood for the whole Land of Israel. Two: the name honors the city where the Temple had stood. Either way, the name tells you where the book comes from, not which town.

9.8

The Babylonian Talmud

Main ideaThe Babylonian Talmud, finished around 500 CE, is longer than the Yerushalmi and is the Talmud most people study.

The rabbis of Babylonia kept going for another hundred years. Their Gemara is the Babylonian Talmud, or in Hebrew the . Tradition says two teachers at Sura, Rav Ashi and Ravina, gathered and arranged it. Rav Ashi died in 427. The Gemara itself calls them "the end of instruction."

After them came teachers called the , the "explainers." Between about 500 and 600 they added short notes and smoothed the text. Historians still debate how much of the Bavli was shaped in this time. The book was finished slowly, by many hands, not on one day.

The Bavli has Gemara on 37 tractates. In the standard printing it fills 2,711 leaves, each with two sides. It is long, and its arguments run deep. When people today say "the Talmud" with no other word, they almost always mean the Bavli. It became the main book of Jewish law and study for the next 1,500 years.

From the source
The End of Instruction
The Gemara says that Rabbi Judah the Prince and Rabbi Nathan were the end of the Mishnah, and that Rav Ashi and Ravina were the end of instruction. Rashi explains this to mean that after Rav Ashi and Ravina, no one added new rulings to the Talmud in the same way; later teachers explained what was already there.
Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 86a, with Rashi's comment, retold in plain words from the William Davidson Talmud (Sefaria, 2017) (paraphrased)

9.9

Why Two, and Which One?

Main ideaBoth Talmuds discuss the same Mishnah, but the Bavli became the main one because the Babylonian community and its later teachers spread it.

Why are there two Talmuds? Because there were two centers, each with its own rabbis, questions and language. Both start from the same Mishnah. Often they reach the same answer by different roads. Sometimes they disagree. Readers who compare them can see how one question looked from two places.

The Bavli became the main Talmud for a simple reason. The Jewish community of Babylonia stayed strong for centuries. After the Talmud, its academies were led by teachers called the . From about 600 to 1000 they answered questions sent from Jewish communities everywhere, and their answers followed the Bavli.

So the Bavli became the base of , Jewish law as the rabbis develop it. The Yerushalmi was studied less, but never forgotten. Today Orthodox, Conservative and Reform scholars all study both, and university scholars compare them to see how the text grew. Both are open to anyone who wants to read.

A Page of Talmud

The Page
Big questionHow can people all over the world open the same book to the same page on the same day?
The story

Same Page, Two Cities

A grandmother in Skokie and a grandson in Denver open the same page on the same morning, and call each other about it.

It is early in Skokie, just north of Chicago. Miriam pours her tea and opens a thick book to the second page. The book is Berakhot, the first tractate of the Talmud. She reads the first line in Hebrew, then checks the English on her tablet. "From when do people say the evening Shema?" she reads. She writes the question on a sticky note.

A thousand miles away in Denver, her grandson Eli is eating cereal. He opens a website called Sefaria on his laptop and finds the same page: Berakhot 2a. He is eleven. The Hebrew is hard, so he reads the English. Today is the first day of a new cycle. Thousands of people, all over the world, are reading this exact page today.

At nine o'clock, Miriam's phone rings. "Grandma, why does it start with a question about bedtime?" Eli asks. Miriam laughs. "That is what I wanted to ask you," she says. They look at the page together, each on their own screen. In the middle is the Mishnah, and then the Gemara. Along the inside edge is a note from Rashi, who lived in France almost a thousand years ago.

"Rashi says the priests in this line are the ones who had to wait until dark," Miriam reads. "So the Mishnah is saying: night begins when the stars come out." Eli finds the same note in English. "So the first question in the whole Talmud is what time it is," he says. "Sort of," says Miriam. "It is when the day ends and the night begins."

They will do this again tomorrow, with page 2b. And the day after, with 3a. If they keep going, they will finish the whole Talmud in about seven and a half years. Eli will be eighteen. Miriam says she will be there for it. For now, they say goodbye. The page waits, the same page, in two cities.

Section 1

Sides of a Leaf

10.1

One Leaf, Two Sides

Main ideaA daf is one leaf with two sides, a and b, and every tractate starts at daf 2.

Open a printed Talmud and you see a leaf, not a page. The rabbis call it a . Each daf has two sides. The front is called side a, and the back is side b. A side is called an , which means "column." So "Berakhot 2a" means tractate Berakhot, leaf 2, front side.

Why does every tractate start at daf 2? Because in the early printings the title page was daf 1. It had no Talmud on it. The first leaf with Gemara was leaf 2. Printers kept that habit for 500 years. Today every edition, in every country, still begins at 2a.

This is very useful. A teacher in Chicago can say "look at Berakhot 2a," and a student in Jerusalem, Buenos Aires or Denver will find the same words in the same spot. The page numbers are the same in every standard edition, so everyone can point to the same place.

Think it through
Reading a Citation

Here are three citations: Berakhot 2a. Shabbat 31a. Bava Metzia 59b. Each one has two parts. The first word is the name of the tractate, the book. The number is the leaf. The letter says which side: a for the front, b for the back. Berakhot means "blessings." Shabbat means "the Sabbath." Bava Metzia means "the middle gate."

For each citation, say the tractate, the leaf and the side. Which one is on the back of a leaf?

10.2

The Middle Column

Main ideaThe Mishnah and Gemara sit in the middle column of the page, with no vowels and no punctuation.

Look at the middle of the page. That is the main text. It begins with a short piece of Mishnah, in Hebrew. The word "Mishnah" is often printed in big letters before it. Then comes the Gemara, in Aramaic, with the word "Gemara" in big letters before it. The two follow each other down the .

The letters in the middle column are large and square. But there are no vowel marks, no periods and no question marks. Readers have to know where a sentence ends. Many words are cut short. An is a shortened word. The Talmud is full of them, so a beginner needs help.

That is why people study out loud, and usually with a partner. One reads, the other checks. When the middle column is hard, the reader looks to the sides of the page, where the helpers wait. The first line of the whole Talmud, in Berakhot, is a question about when to say the evening Shema prayer.

From the source
The First Line of the Talmud
The first Mishnah of tractate Berakhot asks: from what time may a person say the Shema in the evening? It answers: from the time the priests go in to eat their portion, and it gives other views about how late the Shema may be said. The Gemara then asks why the Mishnah begins with the evening and not the morning.
Mishnah Berakhot 1:1 and Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 2a, retold in plain words from the William Davidson Talmud (Sefaria, 2017) (paraphrased)

The Mishnah gives a rule. The Gemara asks why the rule is written this way. What does that tell you about how the Gemara reads?

10.3

Words All Around

Main ideaRashi's explanation sits on the inside edge of the page and the Tosafot on the outside, so the page holds a conversation across centuries.

Around the middle column run other blocks of text in smaller letters. On the inside edge, nearest the fold of the book, is the explanation by . He lived in France from 1040 to 1105. He explains hard words and fills in missing steps. Beginners read Rashi first when they get stuck.

On the outside edge are the . The word means "additions." They were written by Rashi’s grandsons and their students in the 1100s and 1200s. They ask hard questions. Often they say: but on another page the Talmud seems to say the opposite! Then they try to work it out.

The small letters on the sides are printed in a special font called Rashi script. Along the very edges are even smaller notes that point to other pages and to later law books. The page is like a table where a rabbi from the year 300, a teacher from 1100 and a printer from 1880 all sit and talk.

Section 2

The Helpers on the Page

10.4

Rashi, the Teacher From Troyes

Main ideaRashi wrote a clear, step-by-step commentary on almost the whole Babylonian Talmud, and readers have leaned on it ever since.

Rashi is a short name made from the first letters of Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki. He was born in 1040 in , a town in northern France, and died there in 1105. As a young man he studied in schools along the Rhine river in Germany. Then he came home and taught.

Rashi wrote a , an explanation that follows a text line by line. He did it for the Torah and for almost all of the Babylonian Talmud. His style is plain. He tells you what a word means. He tells you who is speaking. He fills in a missing step so the argument makes sense.

Jewish readers of every kind, Orthodox, Conservative and Reform, still study with Rashi. So do university scholars. His Torah commentary was one of the first Hebrew books ever printed, in 1475. When printers made the Talmud page, they gave Rashi the inside edge, the place closest to the text.

From the source
Rashi Explains a Hard Word
The first Mishnah says the evening Shema may be said from the time the priests go in to eat their portion. Which priests? Rashi explains: priests who had become impure, then washed, and had to wait until the stars came out before they could eat. So, Rashi says, the Mishnah means the Shema may be said from nightfall.
Rashi on Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 2a, retold in plain words from the William Davidson Talmud (Sefaria, 2017) (paraphrased)

The Mishnah did not say "nightfall." How did Rashi get there? What did he add?

10.5

Tosafot, the Questioners

Main ideaThe Tosafot compare one page of Talmud with another and ask why they seem to disagree.

Rashi had daughters, and their sons became great teachers. One grandson was called . Another was the Rashbam. They and their students, in France and Germany in the 1100s and 1200s, wrote the Tosafot. They loved Rashi. They also argued with him.

The Tosafot ask a special kind of question. They remember the whole Talmud at once. So they say: here the Gemara says one thing, but on a page in another tractate it says the opposite. That is a . Then they find a difference between the two cases that explains both.

Reading the Tosafot is like watching a chess player think many moves ahead. Their notes are harder than Rashi’s, so students usually learn Rashi first. But the Tosafot show what Talmud study became: not only reading a page, but holding the whole book in your head and asking it to agree with itself.

Think it through
A Tosafot-Style Question at School

The school rule book says: no running in the halls. The gym rule book says: run three laps at the start of class. A Tosafot-style reader asks: do the rules contradict each other? Then answers: no, the hall rule is about safety in a crowded space, and the gym rule is about exercise in an open one. Each rule fits its own place.

Find two rules at school or at home that seem to disagree. Can you find the difference between the cases that makes both rules make sense?

10.6

Printers Who Fixed the Page

Main ideaA printer in Venice set the page's shape in the 1520s, and the Vilna printing of the 1880s made it the page everyone uses.

For a thousand years the Talmud was copied by hand. Each copy looked different. Then came the . In Venice, Italy, between 1520 and 1523, a printer named Daniel Bomberg printed the whole Babylonian Talmud. Bomberg was a Christian. His Jewish editors helped him plan the page.

Bomberg’s page put the Mishnah and Gemara in the middle, Rashi on the inside and the Tosafot on the outside. He numbered the leaves. Printers who came after him copied his page numbers, so that Berakhot 2a stayed Berakhot 2a everywhere.

The most famous printing came from , today the city of Vilnius in Lithuania. The Romm family press printed it between 1880 and 1886. The press was run by a widow, Deborah Romm, with her brothers-in-law. The Vilna edition added more notes around the edges. It became the standard page, copied by nearly every edition since.

Section 3

One Page a Day

10.7

Daf Yomi Begins

Main ideaDaf Yomi, started in 1923, has people everywhere read one daf a day and finish the whole Talmud in about seven and a half years.

In 1923 a young rabbi from Poland, Meir Shapiro of Lublin, stood up at a big meeting of Jewish leaders in Vienna. He had an idea. What if Jews everywhere read the same daf on the same day? One page a day, every day, until the whole Babylonian Talmud was done. He called it , "the daily page."

The plan was simple. Day one is Berakhot 2a. Day two is Berakhot 2b. The Bavli has 2,711 leaves, so one takes 2,711 days, about seven years and five months. When the last page is read, the next day starts again at Berakhot 2a.

Rabbi Shapiro said a traveler could get off a train in any city, walk into a study hall and find people on the same page. It worked. The first cycle began in September 1923. Since then the cycle has run without a break, through wars and hard years, with people joining and leaving along the way.

10.8

A Stadium Full of Readers

Main ideaWhen a cycle ends, readers gather for a siyum, and in 2020 tens of thousands filled a stadium to celebrate.

Finishing a book of Talmud is a happy day. Jewish tradition marks it with a , a celebration of finishing. People read the last lines, say a special prayer, and share a meal. When a whole Daf Yomi cycle ends, the celebration is called the Siyum HaShas, the finishing of the whole Talmud.

On January 1, 2020, the thirteenth cycle ended. About 90,000 people filled MetLife Stadium in New Jersey for a Siyum HaShas. Days later, thousands of women gathered in Jerusalem for a siyum of their own, since more women now study the daily page too. Groups met in many cities, including Chicago.

The next cycle began on January 5, 2020, with Berakhot 2a. Since then the readers have moved together through tractate after tractate. Some study in a hall with a teacher. Some listen to a podcast on the train. Some read with a grandchild on the phone. The page is the same for all of them.

Think it through

Then a prayer. Then a song, and people in the stands dancing in their coats. Some of them have read a page a day for seven and a half years.

10.9

Finding Berakhot Page 2

Main ideaAnyone can find Berakhot 2a, in a book or online, and read it best with a partner.

Here is how to find the first page. The Mishnah has six orders. The first order is Zera&#x27;im, "Seeds," about farming and blessings. Its first tractate is Berakhot, "Blessings." Find the volume marked Berakhot. Open it past the title page to daf 2. Side a is on your left when the book is open the Hebrew way.

You can also find it online. A free website called Sefaria shows the Hebrew and Aramaic beside an English translation. It lets you click on Rashi and the Tosafot. Type "Berakhot 2a" and the page appears. Many people in Chicago and around the world read the daily page this way.

The best way to read is with a , a study partner. One reads a line. The other asks what it means. When you are stuck, you look at Rashi together. The Talmud says students learn from their teachers, more from their friends, and most from the questions of their own students.
