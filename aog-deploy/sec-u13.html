The Klan and the People Who Fought It

Hate Orders

Twice in American history, a secret order called the Ku Klux Klan grew into a powerful force. The first Klan rode at night in the South after the Civil War. It used terror to keep freedpeople from voting, learning and owning land. The second Klan rose in 1915 and spread across the whole country, including Illinois. It sold memberships like a lodge and targeted Black Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants.

This unit puts the people who fought back at the center. You will meet survivors who testified to Congress, like the teacher and preacher Elias Hill. You will meet prosecutors, reporters, a Chicago league that printed Klan members' names, a young woman whose last words convicted a Klan leader, and Ida B. Wells, who fought lynching with facts from her home in Chicago.

By the end, you will be able to explain how secrecy helped a hate group escape the law. You will be able to compare sources that disagree and sort documented facts from disputed stories. You will also weigh a hard question: when does exposing names protect people, and when does keeping names private protect them instead?

How did secrecy make the Klan powerful, and what finally broke its hold?

The story

A Long Road to Washington

In 1871, a farmer who lived through a night raid carries his story to Congress.

This scene is a composite. It is built from the kinds of accounts many witnesses gave Congress in 1871. It is spring in upstate South Carolina. A Black farmer stands at the edge of his small cornfield. He is paying for the land, bit by bit. He voted in the last election. Everyone in the county knows it.

A few weeks ago, riders came in the night. Cloth hoods covered their faces. They pulled him from his cabin and whipped him in front of his children. They warned him never to vote again. Before they rode off, he heard one of them laugh. He knew that laugh. It belonged to a man who farms a few miles down the road.

Now word has come that a committee of Congress wants to hear what is happening in the South. He can go and tell his story under oath. His wife is afraid. If the riders learn that he spoke, they may come back. He thinks about it for two nights. Then he packs his one good coat.

In Washington, the room is large and full of strangers. Lawmakers sit at a long table. A clerk writes down every word. The farmer tells what he saw, in order. He names the man he recognized. It is the first time anyone in power has asked him what happened to him.

Across the South, hundreds of people made choices like his. Some spoke when the committee came to their own counties. Others traveled far. Their sworn words filled thousands of printed pages. Those pages are the main reason we know the truth about the first Ku Klux Klan today.

Section 1

Freedom and Backlash

25.1

After Emancipation

Main ideaAfter the Civil War, new amendments made freedpeople citizens and voters, and many white Southerners were furious.

The Civil War ended in 1865, and about four million enslaved people became free. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified that December, ended slavery in the United States. An is a formal change added to the Constitution. Freedpeople moved quickly to build their own lives. They found lost family members, founded churches, opened schools and looked for land and fair pay.

The years from 1865 to 1877 are called . During this time the nation rebuilt the South and argued over what freedom would mean. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) made formerly enslaved people citizens. It promised every person equal protection of the laws. The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) said no citizen could be denied the vote because of race.

Each now had rights that had been denied for generations. Black men voted in large numbers. Hundreds served in state legislatures, and some went to Congress. Many white Southerners accepted the new order, even if unhappily. Others were furious that Black citizens now shared power. That fury soon took the form of a secret order.

25.2

Six Men in Pulaski

Main ideaThe Ku Klux began in Pulaski, Tennessee, as a club of six Confederate veterans, though its exact founding date is disputed.

Pulaski is a small town in southern Tennessee. There, six young men formed a club they called the Ku Klux. Each was a Confederate , someone who had served as a soldier in the war. The traditional founding date is December 24, 1865. The Tennessee Encyclopedia instead places it in May or early June 1866. Historians have not settled which date is right.

The name is usually traced to the Greek word kuklos, meaning circle. The founders borrowed from the lodges of their day. They used grand titles, costumes and an , a ceremony for bringing in a new member. At first they rode around town in disguise, playing at mystery. It looked like one more club among many.

Later, the Klan’s defenders described this beginning as a harmless joke that got out of hand. Be careful with that story. It came from people who wanted to excuse what followed. A is evidence made at the time, like a letter or sworn testimony. The sworn testimony gathered in 1871 shows organized political terror, not a joke.

25.3

From Club to Terror Network

Main ideaBy 1868 the Klan had spread across the South as a loose paramilitary network using terror for political goals.

In April 1867, Klan leaders met in Nashville. They adopted a rulebook called the . It set out ranks with grand titles, from local groups called dens up to a Grand Wizard at the top. Historians widely believe the first Grand Wizard was Nathan Bedford Forrest, a former Confederate general. No membership list survives, so the details of his role are still debated.

The Klan spread across the South in 1867 and 1868. Its dens were only loosely connected. Each local group often acted on its own. What joined them was a shared goal. They meant to stop Black citizens from voting, owning land, going to school and holding office. They also meant to drive out white Republicans who worked with them.

Historians call the Klan a force. It acted like an army without being an official one. Its members used : violence and threats against ordinary people to reach a political goal. Attacks often rose before elections, when votes were at stake. The Klan was no longer a club. It had become a weapon in a fight over who would rule the South.

Section 2

Night Riders

25.4

Masks and Night Raids

Main ideaKlansmen attacked at night in disguise, using violence as intimidation to frighten whole communities.

Klansmen rode at night. They wore robes, hoods or painted masks. They came to a family’s cabin in groups. They dragged people outside, whipped them and sometimes killed them. They burned homes, churches and schools. People called these attacks .

A did two jobs. It hid who the attackers were, so victims could not easily name them in court. It also spread fear. Yet disguises often failed. Survivors frequently knew the riders by their voices, their horses or their size. Many later named these men to Congress. The riders were not strangers. They were local men, and that made the danger closer.

Historians use the word for threats meant to make people too afraid to act. Night riding was intimidation by design. A family whipped for voting was a warning to every family nearby. The goal was not only to hurt one person. It was to make a whole community stay home on election day.

25.5

Who Was Targeted

Main ideaThe Klan attacked the people who stood for the new South: Black voters, officeholders, teachers, and white Republicans.

The Klan’s targets had one thing in common. They stood for the new order in the South. Black voters were attacked. So was any Black , a person elected or appointed to a government job. Black ministers, teachers and families who bought land or asked for fair pay were targeted too. Teachers from the North who taught in freedpeople’s schools were threatened.

White Southerners who joined the Party were targeted as well. The Klan called them traitors. In the South at this time, a Republican usually meant someone who supported Reconstruction and Black voting rights. The parties have changed a great deal since then. So the word meant something different in 1870 than it does today.

Schools drew special anger. Education let freedpeople read contracts, newspapers and ballots. Many Klan attacks burned schoolhouses or drove teachers away. Yet freedpeople kept rebuilding. Families pooled their money to pay teachers and put up new buildings. Their , the act of continuing despite danger, is part of this story too.

25.6

Secrecy and the Courts

Main ideaSecrecy made Klan terror work because it let members escape local courts, so accountability became the heart of the fight.

Secrecy did more than hide faces. Klan members swore to protect one another. When a case reached a local court, a member might sit on the , the group of citizens who decide a verdict. Other members could supply an , a claim that the accused was somewhere else at the time. Some local sheriffs were members or friends of members.

Witnesses faced a terrible choice. Naming an attacker could bring another raid. Many survivors spoke anyway. Others fled their homes, sometimes hiding in the woods for many nights. Local courts so rarely punished the riders that many Black Southerners came to believe only the federal government could protect them.

Here is the key idea of this chapter. A secret order becomes dangerous when secrecy lets its members escape the law. Many lodges of the time kept harmless secrets, like handshakes and passwords. The Klan used the same forms to hide crimes. That is why , making people answer for what they do, became the heart of the fight against it.

Section 3

Witnesses Speak

25.7

Congress Investigates

Main ideaIn 1871 a Joint Select Committee of Congress took sworn testimony from hundreds of Southerners and printed it in 13 volumes.

In April 1871, Congress created a , a special group with members from both the House and the Senate. Its job was to study the condition of affairs in the former Confederate states. In plain words, that meant the violence. Its members heard witnesses in Washington. Some of them also traveled to towns across the South.

Witnesses gave , statements made under oath. Clerks wrote down their words. Hundreds of people testified: Black and white, farmers and preachers, officials and Klan suspects. Survivors described raids, whippings and killings. Many named the men they recognized, even though speaking up was dangerous.

The committee sent in its report on February 19, 1872. It filled 13 printed , from the Government Printing Office. The first volume holds the report itself. Most of the rest is testimony. Today libraries share these volumes free online. They are one of the richest records of ordinary Americans describing political violence in their own words.

25.8

Elias Hill of York County

Main ideaElias Hill, a disabled Black preacher and teacher, survived a Klan attack, testified, and led his neighbors to a new life in Liberia.

Elias Hill lived in York County, South Carolina. A childhood illness left him unable to walk or use his arms. He grew up to become a Baptist preacher and a schoolteacher. People in his community trusted him to teach their children to read. They trusted him to lead them in worship.

On the night of May 6, 1871, disguised Klansmen came to his cabin. They dragged him outside and beat him. The attack was aimed at a respected Black leader. When members of the congressional committee came to York County that year, Hill chose to , or give evidence under oath. He described the attack in careful detail.

Hill was a and a leader, not someone to pity. After the attack, he decided his community could not be safe at home. In the autumn of 1871 he led a group of neighbors who chose to , or move to another country to live. They sailed to Arthington, Liberia, in West Africa. Hill died there in March 1872. His testimony still speaks for him.

25.9

Two Reports, One Record

Main ideaThe committee split: the majority found an organized conspiracy, and the minority said the danger was overstated.

The committee did not agree. The Republican members formed the majority. Their concluded that the Klan was a real, organized , a secret plan by a group to do something unlawful. It found that the order used violence to overturn elections and deny citizens their rights. The majority supported strong federal action to stop it.

The Democratic members wrote a . They argued that the danger had been overstated. They blamed the Reconstruction state governments for much of the disorder. Both reports were printed together in the first volume. The same testimony stood behind both conclusions.

This gives you a real historian’s task. When two groups read the same evidence and reach opposite conclusions, go back to the evidence. Ask who had reasons to see it one way. Each side was , strongly loyal to its own party. Historians today, reading the hundreds of sworn accounts, widely agree with the majority on the main point: the Klan was organized terror.

The Law Strikes Back

25.10

The Enforcement Acts

Main ideaCongress passed three Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, and part of the Ku Klux Klan Act still protects rights today.

Congress passed three between May 1870 and April 1871. They were meant to enforce the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. They made it a crime to use force or threats to keep citizens from voting. A federal crime is one against the laws of the whole nation. Federal courts, not only local ones, could now hear these cases.

The third law, signed on April 20, 1871, is called the Ku Klux Klan Act. It made it a federal crime to , or secretly plan together, to deny people their rights. It also let the president suspend where the violence was out of control. Habeas corpus is a person’s right to be brought before a judge and not held in jail without cause.

One part of that law still works today. Section 1 lets people sue officials who violate their constitutional rights. It is now known as 42 U.S.C. section 1983. In 1961 the Supreme Court relied on it in Monroe v. Pape, a case about Chicago police who searched a family’s home without a warrant. A law written against night riders now protects people across the country.

25.11

Grant and South Carolina

Main ideaPresident Grant suspended habeas corpus in nine South Carolina counties in 1871, leading to arrests and federal trials.

Upstate South Carolina saw some of the worst Klan violence. On October 17, 1871, President Ulysses S. Grant suspended habeas corpus in nine counties there, including York, Spartanburg and Chester. Federal troops and , officers who make arrests for the federal courts, moved in. Suspects could now be held for trial without the usual delays.

Hundreds of men were arrested, and many others fled the state. Grant’s attorney general, Amos T. Akerman, pushed hard for , the work of bringing criminal charges in court. Federal trials of accused Klansmen were held in South Carolina in 1871 and 1872. Historian Lou Falkner Williams tells their story in a 1996 book.

The trials had real limits. Courts were slow, and only a small share of the accused were ever punished. Still, the arrests broke the Klan’s power in much of the South. Survivors had asked for protection, and for a time the federal government answered. It showed that the law could drag a secret order into the open.

25.12

The Order Fades, the Terror Stays

Main ideaThe first Klan broke up by about 1872, but open groups like the White League carried on its goals and Reconstruction ended in 1877.

By about 1872, the Klan as an organized order had largely broken up. Arrests, trials and federal troops did much of that work. But the Klan’s goals did not disappear. , the belief that white people should rule over others, stayed strong in Southern politics.

New groups took the Klan’s place. The , founded in Louisiana in 1874, did not hide. It operated openly, as an armed force backing the Democratic Party there. In South Carolina, armed men called Red Shirts rode openly during the 1876 campaign. Violence and fraud helped these groups take back state governments.

In 1877 the federal government stopped protecting the last Reconstruction governments, and Reconstruction ended. Over the next decades, Southern states found ways to most Black men. Remember the witnesses, though. Their testimony became part of the national record. Nearly a century later, the civil rights movement drew on the same amendments and laws they had fought for.

The Second Klan and Those Who Exposed It

Second Klan
Big questionHow did the 1920s Klan grow so large, and how did reporters, courts and ordinary people break its hold?
The story

Names in the Morning Paper

In 1922, a Chicago weekly called Tolerance prints the names of people it says belong to the Klan.

It is 1922 in Chicago. The Ku Klux Klan has come north, and it has come to the city. By one count it will soon have about 50,000 members in the Chicago area, more than any other American city. Members meet in rented halls and in fields at the edge of town. Most keep their membership secret from neighbors and bosses.

A new group, the American Unity League, has decided to fight that secrecy. Most of its members are Catholic. The Klan attacks Catholics, Jews, Black Americans and immigrants. The League's weapon is a weekly paper called Tolerance. Week after week, it prints the names of people it says are Klan members. It adds their addresses and their jobs.

26.4

The World's Exposé

Main ideaThe New York World's 1921 exposé revealed the Klan's money and violence, won a Pulitzer Prize, and also helped the Klan grow.

On September 6, 1921, the New York World began a series about the Klan. It ran for 21 days in a row. Editor Herbert Bayard Swope oversaw the project. Reporter Rowland Thomas did much of the digging. Other newspapers across the country also printed the series, so readers far beyond New York saw it.

An is a report that reveals hidden wrongdoing. The World printed the Klan’s own membership forms. It showed how money from fees moved up to the leaders. It listed violent attacks linked to Klan members, including floggings and tar-and-featherings. The paper won the 1922 for Public Service.

Then came a surprise. After the series, Klan membership grew. Klan leaders themselves said the exposé had helped them. For some readers, the stories worked like free advertising. This is a real puzzle for anyone fighting hate. Attention can expose a group, and it can also spread its message. Growth was an of the World’s work.

The Hearing in Congress

Main ideaIn 1921 a House committee questioned Simmons, who called the Klan a patriotic fraternal order, but Congress passed no law.

The exposé pushed Congress to act. On October 11, 1921, the House Committee on Rules opened on the Klan. A hearing is a meeting where lawmakers question witnesses to gather facts. Reporters from the World and critics of the Klan gave evidence about what they had found.

William J. Simmons testified too. He presented the Klan as a patriotic, law-abiding , a brotherhood like many other lodges. He denied that the Klan as an organization had ordered the violence in the newspapers. His performance was calm and confident. Many listeners found him convincing.

Congress passed no law against the Klan after the hearings. The printed record, 184 pages from the Government Printing Office, survives today. It lets you set Simmons’s beside the evidence the World gathered. Comparing a group’s own story with outside evidence is one of the most important skills a historian has.

26.7

The Klan War in Williamson County

Main ideaIn southern Illinois, Klan-backed liquor raids set off a deadly local war in 1924 and 1925.

Williamson County lies in the coal country of southern Illinois. In the 1920s, Prohibition made alcohol illegal, but a , someone who makes or sells alcohol against the law, could still find buyers. Beginning in the winter of 1923 to 1924, the Klan backed a series of raids on bootleggers and saloons. A raider named S. Glenn Young led them.

The raids set off a local war. On one side were the Klan and its raiders. Critics called them a force, people taking the law into their own hands. On the other were the sheriff’s allies and the Shelton gang of bootleggers. Gunfights broke out in the towns of Herrin and Marion. On January 24, 1925, a shootout in a Herrin cigar store killed four men: Young, Deputy Ora Thomas, and two of Young’s companions.

Was it about liquor or about people? The Klan said it was enforcing the law. Critics point out that many of those raided were Italian immigrants and Catholics, groups the Klan attacked everywhere. Historians see more than one at work. Within a few years the local Klan had lost its power. Keep this separate from the Herrin Massacre of 1922, a deadly mine-strike clash that was not a Klan event.

Main ideaScandal, exposure and organized opposition drove the Klan's membership down sharply in the late 1920s.

Main ideaIn NAACP v. Alabama (1958), the Supreme Court protected the NAACP's membership list from a state that wanted it.

4. (Unit 12) Why are the 1787 printed papers both valuable and biased?

Why: The papers seem genuine, but the government printed them as propaganda against the order.

5. (Unit 12) Which story lacks corroboration from the official record?

Why: The 1787 books credit the house search, not papers found on Lanz.
