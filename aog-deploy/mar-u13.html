Film and the New League

Claim

Many people first meet martial arts on a screen. A 1943 Japanese film retold judo's early rivals, a 1984 American film sent children to karate schools, and Thai and Indonesian films carried muay boran and pencak silat around the world. Films invent schools, names and costumes. Real schools then have to answer students who arrive asking for them.

In the same decades, a new institution grew up: mixed martial arts. It drew on older mixed contests in Greece, Brazil and Japan. In the United States it began in 1993 as a pay-per-view show with few rules. State athletic commissions, first built to license boxing, later wrote its rule book and licensed its fighters.

In this unit you will separate film names from school names and trace a league from its first event to its fifty-state approval. By the end, you can name the films, companies, laws and commissions involved. You can also explain the difference between an invented name, a documented new style and a regulated league.

The big question

When films and promoters create new names for fighting arts, what records show who really built a style or a league?

Invented Style

Film
Big questionWhen a film makes a style famous, who decides what that style's name means in a real school?
The story

A Name From the Screen

A new student asks a teacher for an art that exists mostly in movies.

In the middle of the 1980s, parents across the United States walked into small karate schools with a new request. Their children had seen a movie, and they wanted to learn what the hero learned. Many of them named a ninja, a fictional dojo, or a teacher who existed only on screen.

The teachers in those rooms usually belonged to real lines. Some traced their training to Okinawan teachers, some to Japanese university clubs, and some to Korean federations. Their certificates named places and people. The movies named characters, villains and invented schools with dramatic names.

Studios chose names that sounded good in a trailer. Schools, by contrast, chose names tied to a teacher, a town or an association. When a hit film reached theaters, those two ways of naming ran into each other at the front desk of a strip-mall gym.

Historians study that meeting. They ask where a style's name first appears, in a record or in a script, and what happened to real schools after the film came out. This chapter follows that question from wartime Japan to Bangkok, Jakarta and suburban America.

Talk about itIf a film invents a school's name and a real gym then adopts it, is the style invented, real, or both?

25.1

Kurosawa's Judo Film

Main ideaAkira Kurosawa's first film, Sanshiro Sugata (1943), turned the early rivalry between judo and older jujutsu schools into a screen drama.

Akira Kurosawa directed his first feature film, , in Japan in 1943. It was based on a 1942 novel by Tsuneo Tomita. Tomita’s father had trained at the Kodokan, the judo school that Kanō Jigorō founded in Tokyo in 1882. Kurosawa went on to direct Seven Samurai in 1954.

The film is set in the 1880s, when judo competed with older schools for students and police contracts. The hero was loosely modeled on Saigō Shirō, an early Kodokan student. The story shrinks years of real rivalry into a few dramatic contests between invented characters.

Japan was at war when the film opened, and government censors reviewed every movie. Wartime officials favored stories that praised discipline and Japanese tradition. The film is now read as evidence of two eras at once: the 1880s it shows and the 1940s that made it.

25.2

The Ninja on Stage and Screen

Main ideaHistorical shinobi were spies and scouts, while the black-clad ninja of film grew largely from theater and later popular fiction.

Records from Japan’s Sengoku period, the age of warring states from the late 1400s to about 1600, mention . They were hired scouts, spies and raiders. Many came from the Iga and Kōga areas, in today’s Mie and Shiga prefectures. Warlords of the period hired them to gather news about rival armies.

The black suit most people picture came later. Many historians link it to theater, where stagehands dressed in black so the audience would treat them as unseen. Novels, comics and films of the twentieth century then turned the ninja into a superhero with a uniform.

The 1967 James Bond film You Only Live Twice showed a ninja training camp to world audiences. In 1981 Enter the Ninja, starring Japanese-born actor Sho Kosugi, helped start an American ninja boom. The comic Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles, created by Kevin Eastman and Peter Laird in 1984, carried the word to children.

25.4

A Title Keeps Its Word

Main ideaThe 2010 remake of The Karate Kid kept the word karate in its title even though its story is set in Beijing and shows Chinese martial arts.

In 2010 Columbia Pictures released a new The Karate Kid. Jaden Smith played an American boy who moves to Beijing. Jackie Chan played Mr. Han, a maintenance man who teaches him Chinese martial arts. The film was a new version of the 1984 story, set in a different country. Its fights were planned with Chinese martial arts in mind.

Inside the film, characters call the art kung fu, and karate is a Japanese and Okinawan art. The title kept the famous 1984 word anyway. In China the film was released under a Chinese title that means roughly kung fu kid, and several other countries also used a kung fu title.

The case shows a at work. A brand is a name a company owns and sells because audiences already know it. Studios kept the name for its market value, while the story inside the film pointed to a different tradition and a different country. Audiences in each country saw the name they were most likely to buy.

Main ideaThe 2003 Thai film Ong-Bak sold muay boran to world audiences and sent new visitors looking for the older Thai art.

The film advertised , the older Thai fighting styles that came before modern ring Muay Thai. Its marketing stressed that stunts were done without wires or computer effects. Main ideaKajukenbo, founded in Hawaii in 1947, shows how a new style can have a real, recorded beginning instead of an invented legend.

Not every new style comes from a film. began in 1947 at the Palama Settlement in Honolulu, Hawaii. Five teachers from different backgrounds worked together to build it. The Palama Settlement was a community center serving working families of many backgrounds.

The name combines parts of the arts its founders brought: karate, judo and jujutsu, Kenpo, and Chinese boxing. Hawaii’s plantation history had brought together Japanese, Chinese, Filipino, Korean and Native Hawaiian families. The style’s mixed name reflects that mixed neighborhood.

Kajukenbo schools keep records of the founders and their students. That kind of , a documented chain of teachers, is what historians look for. A style can be new and still be real when its founding place, date and people are on record. The Kajukenbo name is still used by schools in Hawaii and on the mainland.

Tae Bo and the Trademark

Main ideaIn the 1990s Tae Bo turned martial arts names into a trademarked fitness program sold on video.

Billy Blanks, an American karate competitor, created in the 1990s. The name blends taekwondo and boxing. It was a workout set to music, not a combat art or a competitive sport. Blanks had competed in karate tournaments in the 1970s and 1980s. He had also worked in films as a performer.

Blanks sold Tae Bo on home videos and through television ads, and it became hugely popular by the late 1990s. Blanks registered Tae Bo as a . A trademark is a name protected by law, so others may not sell products under it without permission.

Gyms soon offered classes with generic names like cardio kickboxing. Those classes borrowed the look of martial arts for fitness. Historians see Tae Bo as a case where a martial arts name became a legal product, owned by one person, rather than a lineage.

MMA as a League

MMA
Big questionHow did mixed martial arts go from a no-rules spectacle to a sport licensed by governments?
The story

The Stamp on the Bout Sheet

A government stamp turns a fight card into a licensed event.

Before a professional mixed martial arts event in the United States, a state official checks a stack of paper. Each fighter needs a license, a medical clearance and a signed bout agreement. The official stamps the sheet that lists who meets whom, in which weight class, for how many rounds.

That stamp belongs to an *athletic commission*, a government office that regulates combat sports. The same kind of office had licensed boxing and pro wrestling for decades. In the early 1990s, most commissions had no rules at all for a sport called mixed martial arts.

The new sport was not an ancient art rediscovered in a lost temple. It was a business built by promoters, cable television and athletes trained in wrestling, boxing, judo, Brazilian jiu-jitsu and Muay Thai. Its rules were written in the late 1990s and 2000s by commissions and companies.

This chapter follows that paper trail. It starts with older mixed contests in Greece, Brazil and Japan. Then it follows one American company, its critics and the commissions that turned a spectacle into a league.

26.1

Pankration at Olympia

Main ideaAncient Greeks held pankration, a contest joining boxing and wrestling, at the Olympic Games from 648 BCE.

Ancient Greek writers record that entered the Olympic Games at Olympia in 648 BCE. The name means all strength or all power. It combined elements of Greek boxing and Greek wrestling in one event. Athletes trained for it in the gymnasium, a Greek building for exercise and education.

Pankration still had rules and officials. Ancient sources say biting and gouging the eyes were banned, and referees carried rods to punish fouls. Athletes competed without weight classes, so heavier men held an advantage. Winners were honored with an olive wreath, as in other Olympic events.

Modern MMA promoters sometimes point to pankration as an ancestor. Historians note a long gap between the two. The ancient games ended in late antiquity, and no living line of pankration teachers connects Olympia to modern cages. The Roman emperor Theodosius I is often linked to the end of the games near 393 CE.

Vale Tudo in Brazil

Main ideaIn twentieth-century Brazil, open-rule contests called vale tudo and the Gracie family's challenges shaped Brazilian jiu-jitsu.

In 1914 the Japanese judo teacher Mitsuyo Maeda arrived in Brazil after years of touring the Americas and Europe. He settled in Belém, in northern Brazil. Carlos Gracie, a son of a local family, studied with him. Maeda had trained at the Kodokan in Tokyo under Kanō Jigorō’s system.

Carlos and his brothers, including Hélio Gracie, opened a school in Rio de Janeiro in the 1920s. They developed what became . The family publicized it through open challenges to fighters from other arts. Later generations of the family spread the art across Brazil and abroad.

Brazilians called contests with very few rules , Portuguese for anything goes. These events appeared in circuses and on Brazilian television over the century. They were public shows before any government wrote a rule book for them. Promoters sold tickets to crowds who came to see styles tested against each other.

UFC 1 in Denver

Main ideaThe first Ultimate Fighting Championship was held in Denver on November 12, 1993, as a pay-per-view tournament between styles.

The held its first event at McNichols Sports Arena in Denver, Colorado, on November 12, 1993. It was sold on , meaning cable viewers paid to watch one event. Semaphore Entertainment Group produced it with Art Davie and Rorion Gracie.

The event was an eight-man tournament. Its promoters matched fighters from different styles, including boxing, karate, sumo and savate. It was held in an eight-sided fenced ring that the company called the Octagon. Its bouts ended only by a stoppage, a knockout, or a fighter’s corner giving up.

Royce Gracie, Rorion’s younger brother, won the tournament. The event advertised Brazilian jiu-jitsu to American audiences. It had few rules, no weight classes and no time limits for its bouts. Rorion had moved from Brazil to California and taught jiu-jitsu there.

26.5

Critics and Cable

Main ideaIn the mid-1990s critics led by Senator John McCain pushed states and cable companies to reject no-holds-barred events.

Early UFC events were sold with slogans claiming there were no rules. Senator John McCain of Arizona, a boxing fan, watched a tape and campaigned against the events. He famously compared them to human cockfighting and wrote to state governors urging bans.

Many states refused to allow the events, and several major cable companies dropped them from pay-per-view by the late 1990s. Without cable, the company lost most of its audience. Events moved to smaller venues in places that would permit them. Promoters kept the events alive with smaller home video and live audiences.

Under that pressure, the promotion added rules. It introduced weight classes, rounds, gloves and a longer list of . Historians see this as the point where a spectacle began turning into a sport that a commission could license. The company also began seeking approval from state athletic commissions.

26.6

The Unified Rules

Main ideaNew Jersey regulators wrote the Unified Rules of Mixed Martial Arts in 2000 and 2001, the same year Zuffa bought the UFC.

In 2000 the New Jersey State Athletic Control Board met with promoters, referees and doctors to write rules for mixed martial arts. The board adopted the in 2001. They set weight classes, round lengths, required gloves and a list of fouls.

In January 2001 a company called , owned by casino executives Frank and Lorenzo Fertitta with Dana White as president, bought the UFC. Zuffa worked to win approval from state commissions. Nevada, home of Las Vegas, soon allowed sanctioned MMA events.

The Unified Rules gave every commission one shared text to adopt. A fighter licensed in one state could expect the same fouls, rounds and weight limits in another. That shared rule book is what separates a league from a string of one-night shows. Promoters could also plan a national calendar of events around it.

Section 3

Commissions and Classes

26.7

Where Commissions Came From

Main ideaState athletic commissions were first created to regulate professional boxing, and MMA later joined the sports they license.

New York passed the in 1920, legalizing professional boxing under state control. It created the New York State Athletic Commission to license boxers, referees and promoters. The law was named for State Senator James J. Walker, later mayor of New York City.

Other states followed with their own commissions. Nevada created its State Athletic Commission in 1941. Las Vegas later became a center of big boxing events, which made Nevada’s commission one of the most watched in the country. Walker’s law replaced an earlier ban on public prize fights in the state.

A commission’s main tools are licenses, medical exams, inspectors and suspensions. It can refuse to license a fighter who fails a medical test. When MMA sought approval, it entered this older system that boxing had built over eighty years. Doctors appointed by the commission examine fighters before and after bouts.

26.8

Weight Classes on Paper

Main ideaThe Unified Rules divide fighters into weight classes, so a bout sheet always names a class and a limit.

Early UFC tournaments matched fighters of any size. The Unified Rules replaced that with , groups of fighters within set limits of body weight. Boxing had used weight classes since the late 1800s, and MMA borrowed the idea. Under the Unified Rules, each class has its own upper weight limit in pounds.

Fighters step on a scale at an official , usually the day before a bout. A commission inspector records each weight on paper. A fighter who comes in over the limit can be fined or have the bout changed. Commissions set these rules to reduce dangerous weight cutting before fights.

Weight classes changed who could compete. Smaller athletes, including many wrestlers and judo players from lighter divisions, could build careers. The classes also made each division a separate ranking, much like boxing’s championship belts. Women’s divisions were added later with their own limits.

26.9

The Last State to Say Yes

Main ideaState by state, commissions licensed MMA, and New York became the last state to legalize professional MMA in 2016.

In 2009 the , a national group of state and tribal regulators, adopted the Unified Rules. Most states already licensed MMA by then. The shared rule book made the sport look the same from one state to the next. Tribal regulators oversee events held on the lands of Native American nations.

New York held out. A 1997 state law had banned professional MMA events. Lawmakers debated the ban for years, and in 2016 New York passed a law to legalize and regulate the sport. The state had allowed amateur events under rules, but professional bouts stayed banned. Supporters argued for jobs and tax money; opponents argued about safety.

UFC 205 was held at Madison Square Garden in New York City in November 2016. The event followed nearly twenty years of the ban. With New York’s vote, professional MMA could be licensed in all fifty states. That event drew a large paying crowd at the Garden.

Section 4

A League Built From Other Rooms

26.10

Borrowed Rooms

Main ideaMMA athletes train by borrowing from older institutions: school wrestling rooms, boxing gyms, judo clubs, Brazilian jiu-jitsu academies and Muay Thai camps.

Many successful MMA fighters came from American high school and college . That pipeline ran through bodies like the NCAA and state high school associations, which in Illinois means the IHSA. Their first training rooms were school gyms, not MMA gyms.

Other fighters came from boxing gyms, judo clubs, Brazilian jiu-jitsu academies and Muay Thai camps in Thailand. Each of those rooms kept its own ranks, rules and teachers. Many MMA athletes held ranks or records in one of them before they turned pro.

In the 2000s a new kind of grew up to combine these sources in one building. Its coaches often held separate credentials from separate arts. Historians describe MMA as a league that borrows rooms, not as a single old art with one founder. Wrestling and Brazilian jiu-jitsu coaches often worked beside boxing and Muay Thai coaches there.

26.11

Women Enter the Octagon

Main ideaThe UFC held its first women's bout in February 2013, and its first women's champion came from Olympic judo.

Women fought in smaller MMA promotions before the UFC admitted them. Strikeforce, a California promotion, featured women’s bouts in the late 2000s. UFC leaders had said publicly that women would not fight in the company. Female fighters had built records in Japan, the United States and other countries.

That changed in February 2013 at UFC 157 in Anaheim, California. Ronda Rousey defended her bantamweight title against Liz Carmouche, a U.S. Marine veteran. It was the first women’s bout in UFC history. Rousey won the bout in the first round. Carmouche was the first openly gay fighter in the UFC.

Rousey had won a bronze medal in at the 2008 Beijing Olympics, the first Olympic judo medal for an American woman. Her path ran from a judo club to the Olympics to MMA. Her career shows the league borrowing an older room and its record.

26.12

Television Builds the League

Main ideaThe reality series The Ultimate Fighter, first aired in 2005, brought MMA from pay-per-view to regular cable television.

Zuffa lost money in its first years with the UFC. In 2005 it launched , a reality series on the cable channel Spike TV. Young fighters lived in one house, trained on teams and competed for a UFC contract. The UFC paid to produce the first season.

The first season’s finale in April 2005 aired free on cable. A close bout between Forrest Griffin and Stephan Bonnar drew a large audience. Many business histories of the sport treat that night as the start of its growth. Dana White awarded contracts to both fighters after the bout.

The show made MMA a with seasons, rankings and stars that viewers followed week to week. In 2016 the Fertittas and White’s group sold the UFC to a group led by the talent agency WME-IMG. The deal was reported at about four billion dollars. By then the UFC held events on several continents.
