What the Tanakh Is

Orientation

Before you can read a big book, it helps to know what it is. This unit is about the Tanakh, the Hebrew Bible. You will learn its three parts and its twenty-four books. You will see how it is written on a scroll, in Hebrew, from right to left. And you will find out how Jewish communities read it, one piece each week, all year long.

Then you will follow the story of the Torah, its first five books, from the first day of the world to the edge of the land. It is one long journey: a family leaves a city called Ur, moves to Egypt, walks out again with Moses, stops at a mountain and wanders for forty years. Jewish tradition reads the five books as one story held together by a covenant.

By the end of this unit you will be able to name the three parts of the Tanakh and the five books of the Torah in Hebrew and English. You will be able to tell the Torah's story in order, stop by stop. And you will know what a scroll, a scribe, a parashah and a covenant are.

The story

A Scroll That Takes a Year

Every Saturday, one more piece of a very long scroll is read out loud.

It is Saturday morning in a synagogue on the North Side of Chicago. A synagogue is a Jewish house of prayer and study. At the front of the room is a tall cabinet with a curtain. The curtain slides open. Inside stand several scrolls, each one wrapped in a cloth cover.

A woman lifts one scroll out. It is heavy. She carries it to a table and unrolls it a little. The scroll is not paper. It is parchment, made from animal skin. Every letter on it was written by hand with a quill. There are no pictures and no page numbers.

A boy named Eli watches from the third row. He asks his aunt, "Why don't they just read the whole thing?" His aunt smiles. "It is too long," she whispers. "We read one piece each week. It takes a whole year to reach the end."

The reader touches the parchment with a small silver pointer shaped like a hand. She does not touch the letters with her finger. Then she begins to chant, slowly, in Hebrew. Eli cannot understand the words yet. But he can hear that this is not ordinary reading.

After the reading, the scroll is rolled up, dressed again and carried back to the cabinet. Eli's aunt tells him that next Saturday the reading will start right where it stopped. "And when we finish the last line," she says, "we go straight back to the first one."

Three Parts, One Name

7.1

What the Name Tanakh Means

Main ideaTanakh is a name built from the first letters of its three parts: Torah, Nevi'im and Ketuvim.

The Hebrew Bible has a short name: . The name is a bit like a code. It is made from three first letters: T, N and K. Each letter stands for one part of the book. Put the three parts together and you have the whole Tanakh.

The T is for . Torah means "teaching." The N is for Nevi&#x27;im. That word means "prophets." A prophet is a person who brings a message from God, as the tradition tells it. The K is for . That word means "writings." It holds songs, sayings and stories.

Jewish tradition puts the three parts in this order: Torah first, then Nevi’im, then Ketuvim. The Torah comes first because Jewish tradition treats it as the heart of the whole book. In this course we will read all three parts, in that order.

Think it through
Build the Name

Torah starts with T. Nevi'im starts with N. Ketuvim starts with K. In Hebrew the three letters are tav, nun and kaf. Say the three letters together with some vowels in between, and you get Ta-Na-Kh. Now imagine a school with three parts: Art, Math and Science. What short name could you build from those first letters?

Make up a three-part name for something you know, using its first letters, and explain what each letter stands for.

7.2

Twenty-Four Books

Main ideaJewish tradition counts 24 books in the Tanakh: five in the Torah, eight in Nevi'im and eleven in Ketuvim.

Jewish tradition counts twenty-four books in the Tanakh. The Torah has five books. Nevi’im has eight. Ketuvim has eleven. Five plus eight plus eleven makes twenty-four. Some of these books are long. Some are only a page or two.

The five books of the Torah are Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. Those are the English names. Each book also has a Hebrew name. You will learn the Hebrew names in the next chapter. Together the five books tell one long story.

Nevi’im holds books named for prophets, like Isaiah and Jeremiah. It also holds history books, like Joshua and Kings. Ketuvim holds Psalms, which are songs, and Proverbs, which are wise sayings. It also holds short books like Ruth and Esther. The very last book is Chronicles.

7.3

What Each Part Holds

Main ideaThe Torah tells the story from creation to Moses, Nevi'im tells of the land and the prophets, and Ketuvim holds songs, sayings and scrolls.

The three parts of the Tanakh hold different kinds of writing. The Torah is mostly story and law. It begins with the creation of the world. It ends with the death of Moses, just before the people enter the land. Jewish tradition calls the whole thing "the teaching."

Nevi&#x27;im, the prophets, comes in two halves. The first half is history. It tells how the people entered the land, chose kings and, later, lost the land. The second half holds the words of prophets. A speaks a message that, as the tradition tells it, comes from God. The message is often hard to hear.

Ketuvim, the writings, is the most mixed part. It has songs, called Psalms. It has wise sayings, called Proverbs. It has a poem about love and a poem about grief. It has stories about a queen and about a man in a lions’ den. It ends with a history that looks back over everything.

Two are in Ketuvim, which holds songs, sayings and short stories. Use the hints: Genesis is the first book of all. Deuteronomy holds Moses's last words. Joshua takes over from Moses. Isaiah is a prophet. Psalms are songs. Esther is a queen.

7.4

The Language of the Tanakh

Main ideaThe Tanakh was written mostly in Hebrew, with a few parts in a related language called Aramaic.

Almost all of the Tanakh was written in . Hebrew is an old language. It is still spoken today in Israel. Many Jewish children in Chicago learn some Hebrew so they can read the Tanakh and the prayers. Hebrew has its own alphabet with twenty-two letters.

A few parts of the Tanakh are in . Aramaic is a cousin of Hebrew. Long ago, many people in the Middle East spoke it. Some chapters in the book of Daniel are in Aramaic. So are some parts of the book of Ezra. Scholars can tell the two languages apart.

Because the Tanakh is in Hebrew, people who read English use a . A translation says the same thing in a new language. This course uses a translation made in 1917. Translators try hard to be faithful. But every translation makes some choices, and no translation is the Hebrew itself.

7.5

Reading From Right to Left

Main ideaHebrew is read from right to left, and a Torah scroll is written without vowel marks.

English goes from left to right across the page. Hebrew goes the other way. You start at the right edge and read toward the left. A Hebrew book opens from what looks like the back to an English reader. When a Torah scroll is unrolled, the reading moves from the right roller to the left one.

Here is something surprising. A Torah scroll is written with almost no . Vowels are the sounds like a, e, i, o and u. In Hebrew, most vowels are small dots and dashes placed under or above the letters. A scroll leaves them out. The reader has to know the words already.

Children’s Hebrew books do have the vowel dots. So do most printed Hebrew Bibles. Those dots were added by careful scholars long after the Tanakh was written. Reading from a scroll without them takes a lot of practice. That is why the reader in a synagogue studies the passage all week before Saturday.

Think it through
Words Without Vowels

It is easier if you already know what the sentence is about. Readers of a Torah scroll face this on every line, and in Hebrew. That is why they practice the reading all week.

Section 3

The Scroll in the Ark

7.6

A Scribe and a Quill

Main ideaA Torah scroll is written by hand on parchment by a trained scribe, and it must have no mistakes.

A Torah scroll is not printed by a machine. It is written by hand. The person who writes it is called a . In Hebrew, a scribe is a sofer. A sofer trains for years. The work is slow and careful. One scroll can take a year or more to finish.

The scroll is made of . Parchment is animal skin that has been cleaned and stretched until it is smooth. The sofer writes with a quill, which is a feather cut to a point, and with special black ink. Sheets of parchment are sewn together and attached to two wooden rollers.

Jewish tradition says every letter must be correct. If a sofer makes a mistake, it must be fixed before the scroll can be used. A scroll with a broken letter is set aside until it is repaired. This care is one reason the words of the Tanakh have stayed so steady across hundreds of years.

7.7

The Pointer and the Ark

Main ideaIn a synagogue the Torah scroll rests in a cabinet called the ark, and the reader follows the words with a pointer called a yad.

In a synagogue, the Torah scrolls live in a special cabinet at the front of the room. It is called the . In Hebrew it is the aron kodesh, the holy cabinet. A curtain or a door covers it. When the ark is opened, people in many synagogues stand up.

Each scroll is dressed. It wears a cloth cover, and sometimes a silver crown or a breastplate. When it is time to read, the scroll is carried to a table and undressed. Then it is unrolled to the right place. The reader does not touch the letters with a finger. Fingers can smudge the ink.

Instead, the reader uses a . Yad means "hand" in Hebrew. A yad is a thin pointer, often made of silver, with a tiny hand at the tip. The reader moves the yad along the line while chanting. After the reading, the scroll is rolled up, dressed again and carried back to the ark.

Section 4

A Year of Reading

7.8

The Weekly Portion

Main ideaThe Torah is divided into weekly portions, called parashot, so the whole scroll is read aloud over one year.

The Torah is long. To read it all, Jewish communities split it into pieces. Each piece is called a . That word means "portion" or "section." One parashah is read aloud each week, on Shabbat morning. The plural is parashot.

Most years there are fifty-four portions on the list. Some years have fewer weeks, so two short portions are read together. Each parashah has a name. The name comes from one of its first words. The first parashah is called Bereshit, "In the beginning," because Genesis starts with that word.

Each week many Jewish families and schools talk about that week’s parashah. A child might hear the story of Noah one week and the story of Abraham a few weeks later. Because the cycle is the same each year, the stories come around again and again. Children grow up with them.

7.9

Simchat Torah: Ending and Starting

Main ideaOn the holiday of Simchat Torah, Jewish communities finish the last words of the Torah and immediately begin again at the first.

The yearly cycle ends and begins on one holiday. It is called . The name means "rejoicing in the Torah." It comes in the fall, right after the holiday of Sukkot. On that day the last portion of Deuteronomy is read aloud. Then, without a pause, the first portion of Genesis is read.

Simchat Torah is a joyful day. In many synagogues, people take all the scrolls out of the ark. They carry them around the room while singing. They dance with them. Children often walk in the parade holding flags. Everyone celebrates that a whole year of reading is done.

Why start again right away? Jewish tradition teaches that the study of Torah never ends. The last word of Deuteronomy leads back to the first word of Genesis. A person who has read the Torah many times can still find something new. So the scroll is rolled all the way back, and the reading begins again.

7.10

Same Books, Different Order

Main ideaChristians call these books the Old Testament and arrange them in a different order; this course follows the Tanakh's order and the JPS translation of 1917.

The books of the Tanakh are also holy books for Christians. Christians call them the . The words inside are the same books. But the order is different, and the count is different. Christians usually list thirty-nine books, because they split some books that Jewish tradition counts as one. Samuel, for example, becomes First Samuel and Second Samuel.

The order matters. The Tanakh ends with Chronicles, which looks back and ends with a call to return home to Jerusalem. The Christian Old Testament ends with the prophet Malachi. Each tradition arranges the books in the way that fits how it reads them. This course keeps the Tanakh’s order: Torah, Nevi’im, Ketuvim.

This course reads an English translation called the . JPS stands for the Jewish Publication Society. In 1917 a group of Jewish scholars in Philadelphia finished it. It was the first time a group of American Jewish scholars worked together to translate the whole Tanakh into English. Its English sounds old, with words like "thou" and "unto," but it is careful and free for anyone to use.

The story

A Map With Footprints

A class draws the whole Torah as one long walk, and marks every stop with a footprint.

Ms. Ortiz unrolls a long strip of paper across the floor of her fourth-grade classroom in Chicago. On it she has drawn a map. A river curls across the right side. A big sea sits in the middle. A long green river runs down the left. "This," she says, "is where the Torah's story happens."

She hands out paper footprints. "Every time the story moves, we add a footprint." Jamal gets the first one. He reads a card: "Abraham's family leaves a city called Ur." He sticks his footprint near the river on the right. Then Priya reads the next card: "They walk to a land called Canaan." Her footprint goes near the sea.

The footprints keep coming. Down to Egypt, when Joseph's brothers come looking for food. Out of Egypt with Moses. Across the sea. To a mountain called Sinai. Then a long line of footprints going around and around in the desert. "Why so many?" asks Jamal. "Forty years," says Ms. Ortiz. "That is a lot of walking."

The last footprint goes on a hill just east of the long green river. Priya reads the card: "Moses looks across the Jordan River at the land, but he does not cross." The room is quiet for a moment. "The Torah ends right here," says Ms. Ortiz. "Five books, one long walk, and the story stops at the edge."

Jamal looks at the whole strip. Ur to Canaan. Canaan to Egypt. Egypt to Sinai. Sinai to the river. "It is one story," he says. Ms. Ortiz nods. "Five books," she says, "but Jewish tradition reads them as one. Let's find out what happens at each footprint."

Jewish tradition reads the five books as one long story. It begins with the world being made. It ends with Moses looking at the land from a mountain. In this chapter we walk through the story book by book. Keep the map from the opening story in your mind as you go.

8.2

Bereshit: Beginnings and a Family

Main ideaGenesis tells of creation, the flood, and one family, from Abraham and Sarah to Joseph in Egypt.

Genesis, or Bereshit, opens with the world being made in six days and a rest on the seventh. Then come the first people in a garden, the first brothers, and Noah’s flood. After the flood comes a tower and many languages. These early stories are about the whole world, not one people.

Then the story narrows to one family. The Torah says God told a man named Abram to leave his home and go to a land he did not know. He and his wife Sarai went. Later their names became Abraham and Sarah. The Torah says God made a with Abraham, a bond with promises on both sides. Their son was Isaac. Isaac’s son was Jacob, who was also called Israel.

Jacob had twelve sons. One of them, Joseph, was sold by his brothers and taken to Egypt. Years later Joseph became a great leader there. When food ran out in Canaan, his brothers came to Egypt, and Joseph forgave them. The whole family moved to Egypt. That is where Genesis ends, with the family far from home.

Section 2

Out of Egypt

8.3

Shemot: Slaves and a Baby in a Basket

Main ideaExodus begins with the family grown into a people, made slaves in Egypt, and a baby named Moses saved from the river.

Exodus, or Shemot, begins many years after Joseph. The family has grown. Now it is a whole people, the children of Israel. A new , the king of Egypt, is afraid of them. He makes them slaves. They build cities for him. He even orders that their baby boys be thrown into the river.

One mother hides her baby in a basket among the reeds of the river. His big sister, Miriam, watches. Pharaoh’s daughter finds the basket and decides to raise the boy. She names him . He grows up in the palace. But when he sees a slave being beaten, he runs away to the desert.

In the desert, the Torah says, Moses sees a bush that burns but does not burn up. From the bush a voice tells him to go back to Egypt and lead the people out. Moses says he is not a good speaker. His brother Aaron goes with him to help. Together they stand before Pharaoh and say, "Let my people go."

Think it through
Two Brave Women

Look at the two women in the story. One is a Hebrew mother who hides her baby in a basket by the river, even though the king has ordered the baby boys killed. The other is Pharaoh's own daughter, who finds the basket, knows the baby is a Hebrew child, and keeps him anyway. Neither woman has an army. Neither one gives a speech. Each one just does one brave thing.

What does each woman risk? Why do you think the Torah tells us about them before it tells us about Moses's big deeds?

Words to know
Pharaoh

Moses

Miriam
the sister of Moses, who watched over his basket

1. Why does the new Pharaoh make the children of Israel slaves?

Why: Exodus says the new Pharaoh feared the people because they had grown so many, so he made them slaves.

2. Who finds the baby in the basket?

Why: Pharaoh's daughter finds the basket and raises the boy as her own. Miriam is the sister who watches.

3. What does the Torah say Moses sees in the desert?

Why: Exodus tells of the burning bush. The ladder is Jacob's dream, and the rainbow and dove belong to Noah's story.
8.4

The Night of Passover and the Sea

Main ideaThe Torah says ten plagues struck Egypt, the people left in a hurry on the night of Passover, and the sea opened before them.

Pharaoh says no. The Torah says ten then come upon Egypt, one after another. The river turns to blood. Frogs fill the land. There are bugs, sick animals, sores, hail, locusts and darkness. The last plague is the worst: the firstborn of Egypt die. Only then does Pharaoh let the people go.

The people leave so fast that their bread has no time to rise. Jewish families remember this every spring on . They eat flat bread called matzah. They tell the story at a special meal called a Seder. The youngest child asks questions, and the family answers with the story of the going out from Egypt.

Pharaoh changes his mind and chases them with chariots. The people are trapped at the edge of the sea. The Torah says Moses lifted his staff, the sea opened, and the people walked through on dry ground. On the other side Miriam took a drum and led the women in a song and dance. The people were free.

8.5

Sinai and the Mishkan

Main ideaAt Mount Sinai the Torah says God gave the people the Ten Sayings and the teaching, and then they built a tent called the Mishkan to carry with them.

Three months after leaving Egypt, the people camp at a mountain called . The Torah describes thunder, lightning, a thick cloud and the sound of a horn. Moses goes up the mountain. The Torah says God spoke ten sayings to the whole people. Jewish tradition calls them the ; many English readers call them the Ten Commandments.

The Ten Sayings include: there is one God; do not make idols; rest on the seventh day; honor your father and mother; do not murder; do not steal; do not lie about your neighbor. Then, the Torah says, God gave Moses many more laws. Jewish tradition says the whole Torah was given at Sinai. Jewish families remember this day on the holiday of Shavuot.

The second half of Exodus is about building. The people make a tent that can be taken down and carried. It is called the , or tabernacle. The Torah gives every detail: the boards, the curtains, the gold lamp, the table. Inside is a chest holding the tablets of the Ten Sayings. The Mishkan travels with the people wherever they go.

Section 3

In the Wilderness

8.6

Vayikra: Holy Living

Main ideaLeviticus is mostly laws about offerings, priests and holy living, including the rule to love your neighbor as yourself.

Leviticus, or Vayikra, is the middle book of the Torah. It has almost no story. Instead it is full of rules. Many are about , gifts of animals, grain or oil brought to the Mishkan. Other rules are for the , the men from Aaron’s family who served there.

Some of the rules are about food, about being clean, and about days of the year. Leviticus describes , the Day of Atonement. On that day the people ask forgiveness for their wrongs. Jewish communities still keep Yom Kippur today as the most solemn day of the year.

One part of Leviticus is called the holiness laws. It says the people should be holy. Then it lists what that means: leave part of your harvest for the poor and the stranger; pay workers on time; do not spread rumors; be fair in court. In the middle of the list is a famous line. The Torah says to love your neighbor as yourself. Jewish tradition treats that line as a key to the whole book.

From the source
A Rule for Neighbors
Leviticus chapter 19 tells the people not to take revenge or hold a grudge against their own people, and then says to love your neighbor as yourself. The verse ends by saying who is giving the command: I am the LORD.
Leviticus 19:18, Jewish Publication Society translation (1917), retold in plain words (paraphrased)

The rule about loving a neighbor comes right after a rule about grudges. Why might the Torah put those two ideas together?

Why: Leviticus has almost no story. It is mostly laws about offerings, priests and living a holy life.

2. Which day does Leviticus describe as a day of asking forgiveness?

Why: Leviticus describes Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, still the most solemn day of the Jewish year.

8.7

Bamidbar: Forty Years of Walking

Main ideaNumbers tells of counting the people, sending spies, and forty years in the wilderness before a new generation reaches the edge of the land.

Numbers, or Bamidbar, gets its English name from a count. At the start of the book the people are counted, tribe by tribe. Its Hebrew name means "In the wilderness," and that is where the whole book happens. The people leave Sinai and head toward the land the Torah says was promised to Abraham.

Moses sends twelve ahead to look at the land. They come back with huge grapes. But ten of the spies say the people there are giants and the cities are too strong. Only two, Joshua and Caleb, say the people can go in. The people are afraid and cry all night. The Torah says that because of this, they will wander for forty years.

The book tells of hard times in the desert. The people complain about food and water. There are arguments about who should lead. But there are also surprises. A man named Balaam is hired to curse the people, and his donkey talks to him. Five sisters ask for their father’s land, and the law is changed for them. By the end of the book, a new generation is camped by the Jordan River.

Think it through
Two Reports, One Land

Twelve spies see the same land. Ten come back and say: the fruit is wonderful, but the people are giants and we looked like grasshoppers next to them. We cannot do this. Two come back and say: the land is very good, and we can go in. Same grapes, same cities, same walls. The crowd listens to the ten, not the two.

Why might people who saw the same thing give such different reports? Which report would you have believed, and why?

Words to know
wilderness

Joshua
one of the two hopeful spies; later he took over from Moses as leader
Check yourself

1. Why is the book called Numbers in English?

Why: Numbers begins with a count of the people, tribe by tribe. The Hebrew name, Bamidbar, means "In the wilderness."

2. Which two spies said the people could enter the land?

Why: Numbers 13–14 says only Joshua and Caleb gave a hopeful report. The other ten spies were afraid.

3. How long does the Torah say the people wandered in the wilderness?

Why: Numbers says the people wandered for forty years, until a new generation was ready to enter the land.
Section 4

Last Words and a New Leader

8.8

Devarim: Moses Speaks

Main ideaDeuteronomy is mostly speeches by Moses, who retells the journey and asks the people to keep the covenant and choose life.

Deuteronomy, or Devarim, is different from the other books. It is mostly . The people are camped by the Jordan River. Moses is old. He knows he will not go into the land with them. So he stands up and speaks to the new generation, who were children or not yet born when they left Egypt.

In his speeches Moses retells the journey. He reminds them of Sinai. He says the Ten Sayings again, with a few small changes in the words. He repeats many laws. That is why the English name is Deuteronomy, which means "second law." He tells them to teach these words to their children and to talk about them at home and on the road.

Moses also tells the people they have a choice. If they keep the covenant, the Torah says, they will have blessings. If they break it, there will be trouble. Near the end, Moses says he has set before them life and death, blessing and curse, and he asks them to choose life. Jewish tradition reads this as a call that is still open to every generation.

From the source
Choose Life
Near the end of his last speech, Moses tells the people that he has put before them life and death, blessing and curse. He asks them to choose life, so that they and their children may live.
Deuteronomy 30:19, Jewish Publication Society translation (1917), retold in plain words (paraphrased)

Moses is speaking to people who will live in the land without him. Why do you think he ends with a choice instead of an order?

2. What does the name Deuteronomy mean?

3. Who is Moses speaking to in Deuteronomy?

8.9

Mount Nebo and Joshua

Main ideaThe Torah ends with Moses seeing the land from Mount Nebo, dying there, and Joshua taking over as leader.

The last chapter of the Torah is short. Moses climbs a mountain called , on the east side of the Jordan River. From the top, the Torah says, he can see the whole land. He sees the hills and the valleys, all the way to the far sea. But he does not go in. The Torah says he died there, at the age of 120.

The people cry for Moses for thirty days. The Torah says no one knows where his grave is. Then it looks back. It says no prophet like Moses has risen again in Israel. Jewish tradition calls him Moshe Rabbenu, "Moses our teacher." The name says what he means to the tradition: not a king, but a teacher.

Before he died, Moses laid his hands on Joshua. Joshua was one of the two hopeful spies. Now he is the new leader. He will take the people across the river. That story is in the book of Joshua, the first book of Nevi’im. The Torah stops at the edge of the land, and the next part of the Tanakh picks up right there.

From the source
The View From the Mountain
The Torah says Moses went up from the plains of Moab to the top of Mount Nebo, and the LORD showed him all the land. He was told that this was the land promised to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and that he could see it with his eyes but would not cross into it. Then Moses died there in the land of Moab.
Deuteronomy 34:1–5, Jewish Publication Society translation (1917), retold in plain words (paraphrased)

Moses leads the people for forty years and then sees the land only from far away. How do you think a reader should feel at this ending? What might Jewish tradition find hopeful in it?

8.10

One Long Journey

Main ideaRead together, the five books tell one journey, from Ur to Canaan to Egypt to Sinai to the Jordan, held together by a covenant.

Step back and look at the whole map. The story starts with the whole world. Then it follows one family from a city called Ur to the land of Canaan. It follows them down to Egypt, where they grow into a people and are made slaves. It follows them out of Egypt, across the sea, to Sinai, and through the wilderness to the Jordan.

What holds the story together? Jewish tradition answers: the covenant. In Genesis, the Torah says God makes a covenant with Abraham and promises him a land and a big family. At Sinai, the Torah says the whole people enter a covenant and take on its laws. In Deuteronomy, Moses asks the new generation to keep it. The promise of the land is always just ahead.

Scholars and Jewish readers both notice something about the ending. The Torah stops before the promise is finished. The people are at the edge, not inside. Some readers say this keeps the story open, so every generation can feel it is still on the . That is one reason the reading begins again each year on Simchat Torah.

Think it through
Footprints in Order

Here are the big stops in the Torah's journey, mixed up: crossing the sea; Abraham's family leaves Ur; Moses sees the land from Mount Nebo; Joseph's family moves to Egypt; the Ten Sayings at Sinai; forty years in the wilderness; the family settles in Canaan. Genesis is first, then Exodus, then Numbers and Deuteronomy.

Put the seven stops in the order the Torah tells them, and name the book where each one happens.
