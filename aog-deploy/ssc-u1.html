Me, My Family, My School

Civics and History

You have a name. You have a birthday. You like some things and not others. You can do some things and are still learning others. All of that is you. No one else is just like you.

You live with people who take care of you. That is your family. Families come in many shapes. Some are big. Some are small. In every one, people help each other and tell stories.

You also go to school. There you meet kids who are like you and not like you. There are rules there. There are rules at home too. This unit is about who you are, who you live with and why rules help us live together.

The big question

Who am I, who are my people, and how do rules help us live together?

Big questionWhat makes me who I am, and who are the people I live with?
The story

Grandpa Tells It Again

Four generations sit at one table. Grandpa has a story. Everyone has heard it before.

It is Sunday in Chicago. Ana's family sits down to eat. There is a lot of them. Ana. Her big brother Mateo. Her mom and dad. Her grandma and grandpa. And Great-Grandma Rosa, who is ninety years old. Seven people. One long table.

Ana passes the rice. Mateo pours the water. Grandma cuts the bread. Dad holds Great-Grandma's arm when she sits down. Everyone has a job. Everyone helps.

Then Grandpa clears his throat. Mateo grins. He knows what is coming. "When I was six," Grandpa says, "I walked to school in the snow. I had one pair of boots. They were my brother's old boots." Ana has heard this story ten times. She still likes it.

Great-Grandma Rosa laughs. "I bought those boots," she says. "They cost two dollars." The whole table laughs. Ana looks around. This is her family. These are her people.

Talk about itWho sits at your table? What story does your family tell again and again?

Families Come in Many Shapes

Main ideaA family is the people who love you and take care of you. Families are not all the same size.

A is the people who take care of you. Ana lives with seven people. That is a big family. Her friend Leo lives with his mom. That is two people. Both are families.

Some kids live with a mom and a dad. Some live with one parent. Some live with a grandma. Some live with two moms or two dads. Some kids have a step-parent. Some are adopted. All of these are families.

A family can be big or small. It can live in one home or two homes. What makes a family is not the size. It is the care. Family members love each other and help each other.

Rules at School and at Home

Civics
Big questionWhy do we have rules, and who gets to make them?
The story

Three Rules for Room Twelve

It is the first day of school. The class has no rules yet. So they make their own.

It is the first day of school. Twenty kids sit on the rug in Room Twelve. Ms. Park sits in a small chair. "This is our room," she says. "We need some rules. But I will not make them. You will."

The kids talk. Leo says, "No hitting." Mia says, "Be quiet when someone is talking." Sam says, "No running in the hall. I fell last year." Ana says, "Say sorry if you are mean." Ms. Park writes every idea on the board. There are twelve ideas.

"Twelve is too many," Ms. Park says. "Let's pick three." The kids look at the list. Some ideas are almost the same. They put those together. Then each kid gets three sticky dots. They stick a dot next to the rules they like best.

The three rules with the most dots win. Be safe. Be kind. Listen. Ms. Park writes them big on a chart. Every kid signs the chart. Now the rules belong to them.

Talk about itWhy did Ms. Park let the kids make the rules instead of making them herself?

Why We Have Rules

2.1

Rules Keep Us Safe

Main ideaMany rules keep people from getting hurt.

Sam fell in the hall last year. He was running. He hit his head. It hurt. Now Sam knows why there is a . A rule tells you what to do or not do. No running in the hall.

Many rules keep us . Walk, do not run. Keep your hands to yourself. Look both ways before you cross. Sit down on the bus. Each rule stops someone from getting hurt.

Think of a rule at your school. Ask yourself: who could get hurt without it? If you can name someone, that rule is a safety rule.

2.2

Rules Are Fair

Main ideaRules make things fair so everyone gets a turn.

There is one swing. Five kids want it. Without a rule, the biggest kid gets it every time. That is not . Fair means everyone gets the same chance.

So there is a rule. Count to twenty. Then give the next kid a turn. Now all five kids swing. The rule made it fair.

Rules help people get along too. Raise your hand to talk. Then everyone gets heard. Clean up your own mess. Then no one does all the work. Fair rules stop fights before they start.

Think it through

Look at the plate. There are ten cookies. There are five kids. Mia says, "Two each." Everyone takes two. The plate is empty. No one cries. Now imagine there was no rule. Sam grabs six. Ana gets none.

Which way is fair? How do you know?

Why: Fair means everyone gets the same chance, like every kid getting a turn on the swing.

2. What does the swing rule do?

Why: Count to twenty, then give the next kid a turn. All five kids get to swing.

3. Why do we raise our hands to talk?

Why: If everyone talks at once, no one is heard. Raising hands makes it fair.

When a Rule Is Broken

Main ideaWhen you break a rule, you can make it right.

Everyone breaks a rule sometimes. What matters is what you do next. Tell the truth. Say sorry. Fix it if you can. Then try again.

Write it

Pick one rule from your class or your home. Say what the rule is. Tell who could get hurt or feel sad without it. Tell why the rule is a good rule.

  • Claim: name the rule and say it is a good rule.
  • Evidence: tell what happened in the reading when a rule was broken, like Sam falling or Leo grabbing the crayon.
  • Reasoning: say if your rule is about being safe, being fair or getting along.
  • The other side: say why someone might not like the rule, and why it still helps.
