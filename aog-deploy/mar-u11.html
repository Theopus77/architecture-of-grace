Illinois Storefronts

Local

Walk down a shopping strip in almost any Illinois town and you may pass a dojo, a dojang and a boxing gym. Across the road, a public high school has its own wrestling room. Each room has a different owner, a different source of money and a different set of rules.

The names on those doors came from far away. Dojo is Japanese, dojang is Korean, kwoon is Cantonese and gym is Greek. Many of these arts reached Illinois only when laws let their teachers come. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the quotas of 1924, the camps of 1942 and the new immigration law of 1965 all shaped who arrived in Chicago, and when.

In this unit, you will compare who owns, pays for and governs four kinds of rooms. You will read laws, reports and street records. You will explain how immigration history is written on the signs of Chicago neighborhoods.

Four Rooms in One County

Illinois
Big questionWhy do a school wrestling room, a karate storefront, a college judo club and a boxing gym answer to different rules and different people?
The story

Four Doors on One Strip

On one Illinois shopping strip, four doors use four different words for a place to practice.

Picture a row of shops along a busy road in an Illinois suburb. There is a pizza place, a dry cleaner and a tax office. Between them are three doors with painted signs. One says Karate Dojo. The next says Taekwondo Dojang. The third says Boxing Gym.

Across the road stands the public high school. Behind its gym is a padded room with a red mat. The sign on that door says Wrestling Room. A few miles away, a state university runs a judo club in a campus recreation building.

Each word on each door comes from a different place and time. Dojo is Japanese. Dojang is Korean. Gym comes from ancient Greek. The school word, room, belongs to a public building paid for by local taxes.

The four rooms also answer to different people. A school board and the state high school association oversee the wrestling room. A private owner runs each storefront. A university and a national judo body shape the club. The state of Illinois licenses professional boxers.

Talk about itIf you walked past these four doors, what could the words on the signs tell you before you went inside?
Section 1

Words on the Doors

21.1

Dojo and Dojang

Main ideaDojo and dojang are Japanese and Korean readings of the same two Chinese characters, meaning a place of the way.

The Japanese word is written with two Chinese characters. The first means way or path. The second means place. Together they mean a place of the way. In Japanese Buddhism, the word first named a place for religious practice.

Later, schools of Japanese swordsmanship and other arts used dojo for their training halls. Judo and karate schools kept the word. When karate spread to the United States after World War II, American teachers painted dojo on their signs.

Korean uses the same two characters. In Korean they are read . Taekwondo schools in Korea and abroad use this word for their rooms. So a karate dojo and a taekwondo dojang share one old written word, spoken in two languages.

21.2

Kwoon and Gym

Main ideaKwoon comes from Cantonese and gym comes from ancient Greek, so the names of practice rooms carry the history of the people who built them.

Many Chinese martial arts schools in the United States use the word . It is the Cantonese way of saying a Chinese word for hall or house. In standard Chinese the same word is said guan. Early Chinese immigrants to America came mostly from Guangdong province, where Cantonese is spoken.

The English word is short for gymnasium. It comes from the ancient Greek gymnasion. Greek gymnasion comes from gymnos, which means naked. Greek men exercised and wrestled without clothes in these public grounds.

So a sign that says kwoon points to southern China and Cantonese-speaking migrants. A sign that says gym points back to Greek cities more than two thousand years ago. Each name is a small record of where a practice came from.

The School Wrestling Room

21.3

A Room Owned by the Public

Main ideaA high school wrestling room is public property, run by an elected school board and a state school sports association.

A public high school is paid for mostly by local property taxes and state money. Voters in the district elect a . The board hires the staff, sets the budget and decides which sports the school offers. The wrestling room is part of that public building.

Illinois public high schools belong to the Illinois High School Association, the IHSA. The IHSA is a private, non-profit group run by its member schools. It sets the dates each sport season may begin and end. It also runs the state tournaments in wrestling and other sports.

Because the room is public, students do not pay rent to use it. A school may charge a small athletic fee, but the mats, the coach and the bus come from the school budget. That is a big difference from a storefront school, which must earn its own rent.

21.4

Laws That Opened the Door

Main ideaFederal laws passed from 1972 onward require public schools to open their sports to girls and to students with disabilities.

A public school team is open to students enrolled in that school. For much of the 1900s, though, many schools offered few sports for girls. In 1972 Congress passed of the Education Amendments. It bans sex discrimination in schools that receive federal money, including their sports.

In 1973 Congress passed the Rehabilitation Act. Its bans discrimination against people with disabilities in programs that receive federal money. The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 widened those protections across public life.

In 2013 the U.S. Department of Education sent a letter to schools about sports. It said schools must give students with disabilities a fair chance to try out and take part. It said schools should make reasonable changes when they can. A student who is deaf, for example, may need a visual start signal.

Karate in a Storefront

21.5

Veterans Bring Karate Home

Main ideaAmerican karate schools began after World War II, when servicemen who had studied in Asia and the Pacific came home and opened schools.

During and after World War II, millions of Americans served overseas. The United States governed Okinawa from 1945 until 1972. Many American servicemen stationed on Okinawa and in Japan took karate and judo lessons from local teachers.

Robert Trias served in the U.S. Navy in the Pacific during the war. There he studied a Chinese fighting art. In 1946 he opened a karate school in Phoenix, Arizona. Histories of American karate often call it the first karate school on the U.S. mainland.

Ed Parker was born in Honolulu, Hawaii. He studied kenpo there, an art taught in Hawaii by Chinese and Japanese immigrant teachers. In 1956 he opened a school in Pasadena, California. Returning and teachers like these made karate a word Americans could read on a sign.

21.6

A Tournament in Long Beach

Main ideaOpen tournaments like the Long Beach International Karate Championships, first held in 1964, brought many styles into one American hall.

In 1964 Ed Parker organized the first Long Beach International Karate Championships in Long Beach, California. It was an . Competitors from many different styles could enter, not only students of one school.

At that first event, a young teacher named Bruce Lee gave a demonstration. He was then teaching Chinese martial arts in Oakland and Seattle. Film of his demonstration helped lead to his casting in the television series The Green Hornet, which began in 1966.

Open tournaments mixed Japanese, Okinawan, Korean, Chinese and Hawaiian styles on one floor. Promoters, not a national government, wrote the rules for each event. That is one reason American karate grew with many separate associations instead of a single governing body.

21.7

A School Run as a Business

Main ideaA storefront martial arts school is usually a private business that sets its own fees, ranks and rules.

After World War II, American suburbs grew quickly. New shopping strips offered cheap for small businesses. Karate and taekwondo teachers rented these spaces. By the late 1960s and 1970s, storefront schools were common in many American towns.

A storefront school is usually a . The owner pays rent, insurance and taxes. Students pay monthly fees and often a fee to test for a new rank. The school chooses its own style, its own rank system and its own rules for the room.

Illinois does not issue a state license to teach karate or taekwondo the way it licenses professional boxers. A school may join a private association or a national body for its art. Families usually judge a school by its teacher’s history and its reputation.

Campus Judo and the Boxing Gym

21.8

Judo on Campus

Main ideaIn most American colleges, judo is a club sport run by students, with national judo bodies rather than the NCAA setting its contests.

Judo came to American colleges through Japanese American teachers. Yosh Uchida, a Japanese American, coached judo at San Jose State in California from the 1940s. His teams won many national collegiate titles. San Jose State became the best-known college judo program in the country.

The NCAA, which runs most varsity college sports, does not hold a judo championship. So at most universities judo is a . Students elect officers, collect dues and book mat time in a recreation building. A coach may be a volunteer.

USA Judo is the for judo in the United States. It is the group that the U.S. Olympic committee recognizes for the sport. It sets rules for American contests and helps choose Olympic teams. College clubs often enter events run under its rules.

The Boxing Gym and Its Licenses

Main ideaA boxing gym trains amateurs under USA Boxing rules, while the state of Illinois licenses boxers who fight for pay.

Many boxing gyms began in city parks and neighborhood clubs. The Chicago Park District was formed in 1934, when 22 separate park districts joined into one. Park field houses have long held boxing programs for young people alongside other sports.

An boxer does not fight for pay. In the United States, amateur boxing is run by USA Boxing, the national governing body for Olympic-style boxing. Amateur boxers register with it, and its rules set bout lengths, gloves and headgear for each age group.

A boxer fights for pay. In Illinois, the Department of Financial and Professional Regulation licenses professional boxers, referees, judges and promoters. So the same gym may train amateurs under a private national body and professionals under a state license.

Newcomers and Their Arts in Chicago

Illinois
Big questionHow did immigration laws decide which martial arts reached Chicago, and when?
The story

A Lion on Wentworth Avenue

Drums sound in Chicago's Chinatown, and a street tells the story of a century of immigration laws.

It is Lunar New Year in Chicago's Chinatown. Crowds line Wentworth Avenue near Cermak Road. Drums, gongs and cymbals start to play. A lion costume made of cloth, fur and paper dances down the street, with two performers inside.

The lion passes a large building with a green and red tiled roof. It was built in the late 1920s for the On Leong Merchants Association. Chinese families had moved to this part of the city around 1912. Before that, Chicago's Chinatown stood in the Loop, near Clark Street.

Many lion dance teams in the United States are linked to kung fu schools or community associations. The drum rhythms and the lion's steps are taught inside those halls. The teams perform at New Year, at store openings and at weddings.

Yet for sixty years a federal law kept most new Chinese immigrants out of the country. Other laws later shut out Japanese and Korean newcomers too. The story of martial arts in Chicago is also a story of who was allowed to come, and when.

Section 1

Chicago's Chinatown

22.1

Exclusion and Repeal

Main ideaThe Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred most Chinese workers from entering the United States until Congress repealed it in 1943.

Chinese workers helped build the first transcontinental railroad, finished in 1869. Afterward, some moved east to cities like Chicago. Most came from Guangdong province in southern China. They opened laundries, shops and restaurants.

In 1882 Congress passed the . It barred Chinese laborers from coming to the United States. It also kept Chinese immigrants from becoming citizens. It was the first major federal law to ban immigration by one nationality.

Congress repealed exclusion in 1943, during World War II, when China was an American ally. The , called the Magnuson Act, set a quota of only 105 Chinese immigrants a year. Chinese communities in Chicago stayed small for decades because of these limits.

Chinatown Moves South

Main ideaAround 1912 Chicago's Chinatown moved from the Loop to Cermak and Wentworth, where the On Leong building still stands.

Chicago’s first Chinatown grew in the 1880s near Clark and Van Buren Streets, in the Loop. Rents there rose, and landlords pushed some tenants out. Around 1912, many Chinese businesses moved south to the area around Cermak Road and Wentworth Avenue.

The On Leong Merchants Association helped lead the move. A is a group of business owners who help each other. Such groups also settled disputes, helped newcomers find work and kept community records at a time when the law gave Chinese immigrants little protection.

In the late 1920s, the association finished a large building on Wentworth Avenue. Its tiled roof and towers made it a of Chinatown. Today the building is called the Pui Tak Center and is used for classes and community programs.

Lion Dance at New Year

Main ideaLion dance is a southern Chinese performance tradition, often kept by kung fu schools and associations in Chinese communities abroad.

The is a performance tradition from China. The southern style most often seen in American Chinatowns comes from Guangdong. Two performers move inside one lion costume, while a band plays drum, gong and cymbals.

In southern China and in Chinese communities abroad, lion dance teams have often trained in kung fu schools. The same hall, or kwoon, taught both. Teams perform at Lunar New Year, the first day of the Chinese lunar calendar, and at openings and celebrations.

In Chicago, lion dancers appear each year at the Chinatown Lunar New Year parade. For many families, the dance is a public way to mark the new year and welcome good fortune. The tradition links a Chicago street to villages in Guangdong.

Japanese Americans Come to Chicago

22.4

Removal in 1942

Main ideaIn 1942 the U.S. government forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast into camps.

By 1940, most Japanese Americans lived on the West Coast and in Hawaii. Many families belonged to Buddhist temples, churches and clubs. In Japanese American communities, judo and kendo clubs often met in these halls.

On February 19, 1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed . It let the Army remove people from military zones. The Army forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast. About two-thirds of them were American citizens.

Most were held in ten camps run by the War Relocation Authority. The camps stood in remote places in the West and in Arkansas. Records and photographs show that people in the camps organized schools, sports and clubs, including judo and sumo, behind barbed wire.

Resettlement in Chicago

Main ideaFrom 1943, the government let Japanese Americans leave the camps for cities away from the West Coast, and Chicago became a major new home.

In 1943 the War Relocation Authority began letting some people leave the camps. They could not go back to the West Coast yet. The agency opened offices in Midwestern and Eastern cities, including Chicago, to help them find jobs and housing. This was called .

Chicago had jobs in factories and offices during the war. Estimates say about 20,000 Japanese Americans lived in Chicago by the late 1940s. Many first settled on the Near North Side, around Clark and Division Streets. Later, many moved north to Lake View and Uptown.

Newcomers built new institutions. The Midwest Buddhist Temple was founded in Chicago during the 1940s. Churches, and sports leagues followed. As on the West Coast, such halls gave families places to meet, and some offered classes in Japanese arts.

An Apology in 1988

Main ideaIn 1988 the Civil Liberties Act gave a formal apology and $20,000 to each surviving person who had been incarcerated.

After the war, many Japanese Americans pushed for the government to admit its wrong. In 1980 Congress created a commission to study the removal. Its 1983 report, Personal Justice Denied, found that the removal was not justified by military need.

The report named the causes as race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership. Those words became well known. They shaped how Congress and schools came to describe the camps.

In 1988 President Ronald Reagan signed the . It gave a formal from the nation. It also paid $20,000 to each surviving person who had been held. Chicago’s Japanese American community took part in the long campaign for this law.

22.7

The Quotas of 1924

Main ideaThe Immigration Act of 1924 set national quotas and barred almost all immigration from Asia.

In 1924 Congress passed a new immigration law, often called the Johnson-Reed Act. It set . Each country got a yearly number based on how many Americans had roots there. The system favored Northern and Western Europe.

The law also barred immigrants who could not become citizens. Earlier laws and court rulings had kept most Asian immigrants from citizenship. So the 1924 law closed the door to almost all new immigration from Japan, Korea and the rest of Asia.

For Chicago, this meant Asian communities grew slowly for forty years. Most growth came from children born in the United States, who were citizens by birth. Teachers of Asian martial arts could rarely move to America during those years.

22.8

The Immigration Act of 1965

Main ideaThe Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended national origins quotas, and immigration from Asia grew quickly.

On October 3, 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the . He signed it at the Statue of Liberty in New York Harbor. The law is often called the Hart-Celler Act, after two lawmakers who led it.

The act ended the national origins quotas of 1924. In their place, it gave preference to , meaning relatives of people already here, and to workers with needed skills. The new system took full effect in 1968.

Immigration from Asia grew fast after the change. Newcomers from Korea, China, the Philippines, India and other countries settled in Chicago. Some of them were trained martial arts teachers. They opened schools that taught arts from their home countries.

Korean Teachers in America

Main ideaKorean teachers like Jhoon Rhee opened taekwondo schools in the United States, and Korean immigration after 1965 spread them further.

Jhoon Rhee came from Korea to the United States in 1956 to study at a college in Texas. He taught Korean martial arts to fellow students there. In the early 1960s he opened a school in Washington, D.C. Many members of Congress later trained with him.

After the 1965 law, Korean immigration to the United States rose sharply. Many families settled in Chicago. A Korean business district grew along Lawrence Avenue in Albany Park, on the city’s North Side. Churches, groceries and opened along the street.

Many taekwondo schools in the Chicago area were founded by Korean immigrant teachers. Their ranks were often certified through the Kukkiwon in Seoul. Their students came from every background, so a Korean word for room became common on Illinois signs.

Reading a Street as a Record

Main ideaThe signs on a city street can be read as evidence of when and why different communities arrived.

Historians use many kinds of . Business directories, building permits, church records and census tables all show who lived where. Old photographs of signs can show when a new language appeared on a street.

In Chicago, the dates line up with laws. Chinese businesses clustered in Chinatown during the years of exclusion. Japanese American groups appeared on the Near North Side after 1943. Korean signs spread along Lawrence Avenue after 1965.

A historian reading a street asks careful questions. When did this sign first appear? Who owned the building? What law or event made the move possible? The answers turn a row of doors into a about a city.
