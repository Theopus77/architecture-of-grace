Prophets and Poets Close Up

Prophets and Writings Close Up

This unit reads two kinds of biblical poetry close up. The first is the poetry of the prophets: Amos at a northern shrine, Hosea in a broken marriage, Isaiah before an Assyrian army, Jeremiah at the temple gate, Ezekiel in a valley of bones. These books speak to kings and crowds about the poor, about trust, and about what comes after disaster. Their words have traveled far: a line of Amos was quoted from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial in 1963.

The second is the poetry of the wisdom books, where the questions turn inward. Job, ruined for no reason he can see, demands a hearing from God and gets a storm. Ecclesiastes tests every path to meaning and calls it all hevel, breath. Proverbs, beside them, says that wisdom usually works. These books disagree with each other in public, inside the same Bible.

By the end of the unit you will be able to name the forms the prophets use, place each prophet in his crisis, explain how Jewish and Christian readers read the Servant Songs and the new covenant differently, describe the architecture of Job, and say what Ecclesiastes means by hevel. You will also be able to show how modern writers, from Martin Luther King Jr. to Elie Wiesel, have picked these texts up and used them.

The story

One Verse, Two Crowds

A line first spoken at a northern shrine around 760 BCE was read aloud again in Washington, D.C., in 1963.

Picture the shrine at Bethel, in the northern kingdom of Israel, in about 760 BCE. The book of Amos sets its scene here. Worshippers bring animals and grain. Music plays. Trade is good, and the king, Jeroboam II, has made the country wealthy. Then a stranger from the south begins to speak. He is a herdsman from Tekoa, in Judah. He says that the feasts and songs mean nothing while the poor are sold for the price of a pair of sandals.

The book records the reaction. Amaziah, the priest at Bethel, tells Amos to go home to Judah and earn his bread there. Amos answers that he never trained as a prophet; he was a herdsman and a tender of sycamore figs, and God took him from the flock. Then he repeats his message. Near the middle of the book is the line readers remember most: let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream (Amos 5:24).

Now move ahead about 2,700 years. On August 28, 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. stands on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial before some 250,000 people. In the speech known as "I Have a Dream," he says the movement will not be satisfied until, in the prophet's words, justice rolls down like waters and righteousness like a mighty stream. He does not name Amos. He does not need to. Many in the crowd knew the verse from church.

King was a Baptist minister who had studied the prophets in seminary. He quoted Amos again that same year in his "Letter from Birmingham Jail," calling Amos an extremist for justice. In 1966 he brought his campaign to Chicago, marching for open housing on the city's West and Southwest sides. The words of a herdsman from Tekoa had traveled a long way.

This chapter reads the prophets close up. They are not fortune tellers. In the Hebrew Bible a prophet is a messenger who delivers a word to a people and its leaders, often a hard word about how the poor are treated. Amos, Hosea, Micah, Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel each speak into a real crisis. They also use forms of speech you can learn to spot. And their words have been read, and read differently, by Jews and Christians ever since.

The Prophet as Critic

29.1

What a Prophet Is

Main ideaIn the Hebrew Bible a prophet is a messenger who delivers God's word to a people and its rulers, often as a challenge.

The English word comes from a Greek word meaning "one who speaks for" another. The Hebrew word is navi. In the Hebrew Bible a prophet is not mainly a predictor of the future. A prophet is a messenger who says, "Thus says the LORD," and then delivers a message to a king, a priest or a whole nation. The message often points at the present: how the poor are treated, whom the people trust, what the leaders are doing.

The books of the prophets sit in the second part of the Jewish Bible, the Nevi&#x27;im. Jewish tradition counts the "Former Prophets" (Joshua through Kings) and the "Latter Prophets" (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve). Christian Bibles group the same books differently, placing the prophets at the end of the Old Testament. The three long books are often called the Major Prophets and the twelve short ones the Minor Prophets. "Minor" means shorter, not less important.

Most of the writing prophets spoke between about 760 BCE and 500 BCE. That is the age when the Assyrian and then the Babylonian empires rose and crushed the small kingdoms of Israel and Judah. Many scholars read the prophets as voices from inside those crises. The books are mostly poetry, gathered and edited over time, and they name their speakers, their kings and sometimes their years. That lets readers place them on a map and a timeline.

29.2

Amos and the Poor

Main ideaAmos says worship is worthless while the poor are cheated, and calls for justice to flow like a river.

The book of Amos opens by dating itself to the reigns of Uzziah of Judah and Jeroboam II of Israel, about 760 BCE. Israel was prosperous. Amos, a herdsman from the southern town of Tekoa, traveled north to Bethel and spoke against that prosperity. His charge is specific. The rich, he says, sell the righteous for silver and the needy for a pair of sandals (Amos 2:6). They lie on ivory beds, drink wine by the bowlful, and take bribes in the courts.

Amos does not say that worship is wrong. He says that worship without is an insult. In chapter 5 the text has God say that he hates the people’s feasts and will not accept their offerings or listen to their songs. Then comes the demand: let justice roll down like waters and righteousness like a strong, steady stream. Justice here means fair courts and fair dealing, especially for people with no power to fight back.

The book also uses a form scholars call the oracle against the nations. Chapters 1 and 2 pronounce judgment on Israel’s neighbors one by one: Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab. A listener at Bethel might have cheered. Then Amos turns the same words on Judah and, last and longest, on Israel itself. The rhetoric traps the audience. Jewish and Christian readers alike have long seen in Amos a model of the prophet as social critic.

29.3

Hosea's Marriage

Main ideaHosea turns his own painful marriage into a picture of the bond between God and Israel.

Hosea spoke in the northern kingdom in the years before it fell to Assyria in 722 BCE. His book begins with a shocking command. The text says God told Hosea to marry a woman named Gomer, who would be unfaithful, and to give their children symbolic names. One child is called Lo-ammi, "not my people." The marriage becomes a living sign. Israel, the book says, has left God for other gods the way a spouse leaves a marriage.

Readers have handled this image with care. Some Jewish and Christian interpreters have read the marriage as a real event; others read it as a parable, a story told to make a point. Modern readers also note that the image treats the nation as an unfaithful wife and God as the wronged husband, and they ask what that picture assumes about women. Studying the text means noticing both the power of the image and its cost.

The book does not end in anger. In chapter 11 the picture changes. God is a parent who taught Ephraim (another name for Israel) to walk and lifted the child to his cheek. The text has God ask, "How can I give you up?" In chapter 6 comes a line that Jewish and Christian readers both treasure: God desires steadfast love, , rather than sacrifice. The Gospel of Matthew reports Jesus quoting it twice (Matthew 9:13; 12:7).

29.4

Micah's Short Answer

Main ideaMicah 6:8 sums up what the prophets ask: do justice, love kindness, walk humbly.

Micah came from Moresheth, a small town in Judah, and spoke in the late 700s BCE, around the same time as Isaiah of Jerusalem. Like Amos, he attacks leaders who seize fields and houses and judges who take bribes. He is remembered for two things. He announced that Jerusalem would be plowed like a field (Micah 3:12), a line the book of Jeremiah later quotes. And he gave the shortest summary of prophetic religion in the Bible.

Chapter 6 is built as a . God calls the mountains as witnesses and asks the people what he has done to wear them out. The people answer with a question: what should we bring? Burnt offerings? Thousands of rams? Rivers of oil? Even a firstborn child? The questions climb higher and higher. Then the answer comes, plain and short. God has already told you what is good: do justice, love kindness, and walk humbly with your God (Micah 6:8).

The three verbs matter. "Do" justice is action in the courts and the market. "Love" kindness uses hesed, the loyal love Hosea spoke of. "Walk humbly" describes a way of living, not a single act. Jewish tradition cites Micah 6:8 as a summary of the commandments; the Talmud reports a rabbi who said Micah reduced the whole Torah to these three. Many Christian churches print the verse on their walls.

Isaiah, First and Second

29.5

Isaiah and the Assyrians

Main ideaIsaiah of Jerusalem spoke during the Assyrian crisis and told kings to trust God rather than alliances.

The book of Isaiah has 66 chapters, and most scholars think they come from more than one time. Chapters 1 through 39 are largely tied to a prophet in Jerusalem in the late 700s BCE, often called First Isaiah or Isaiah of Jerusalem. Chapter 6 tells his call: in the year King Uzziah died, he saw the LORD on a throne, heard the seraphim cry "Holy, holy, holy," and answered, "Here am I; send me."

His years were the age of . In 722 BCE the Assyrians destroyed Samaria and ended the northern kingdom. In 701 BCE King Sennacherib marched on Judah and besieged Jerusalem. Assyrian records and Isaiah 36 and 37 both describe the campaign, though they tell it differently: Sennacherib’s inscription boasts that he shut Hezekiah up like a bird in a cage, while Isaiah says the city was spared. When two ancient sources differ, historians compare them rather than choosing one blindly.

Isaiah’s message to kings was political. In chapter 7 he tells King Ahaz not to panic before a coalition of Israel and Syria. In chapters 30 and 31 he warns Hezekiah not to lean on Egypt’s horses and chariots. Trust, he says, belongs to God, not to alliances. He also shares Amos’s concern for the poor: chapter 1 says the people should seek justice, defend the orphan and plead for the widow, and that God is weary of their sacrifices without it.

Isaiah is also the prophet of hope. Chapter 2 pictures a day when nations beat their swords into plowshares and study war no more, a line carved on a wall across from the United Nations in New York. Chapter 11 pictures a shoot from the stump of Jesse, David’s father, ruling with justice while the wolf lives with the lamb. Jews and Christians have both read these pictures as promises, and have differed on when and in whom they are fulfilled.

29.6

Comfort from Babylon

Main ideaIsaiah 40-55 speaks to exiles in Babylon, announcing comfort, return, and a God who rules all nations.

Isaiah 40 opens with a new tone: "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people." The setting has changed. Jerusalem has already fallen; the people are in Babylon; and Cyrus, the Persian king who conquered Babylon in 539 BCE, is named as God’s anointed (Isaiah 45:1). For these reasons most scholars since the 1800s have read chapters 40 through 55 as the work of an unnamed prophet of the , called Second Isaiah for convenience. Traditional Jewish and Christian readers have often held that the whole book comes from the eighth-century prophet, who foresaw these events. Both views are held today, and this course describes them rather than deciding between them.

The poetry of these chapters is some of the most admired in the Bible. Grass withers and flowers fade, but God’s word stands. Those who wait on the LORD renew their strength and rise on wings like eagles (Isaiah 40:31). The prophet mocks idols made by carpenters and insists that there is one God who made the ends of the earth and who calls the nations to account. Many scholars see here the clearest statement of in the Hebrew Bible.

Second Isaiah also describes a road home. God will make a highway through the desert, lift the valleys and lower the hills, and lead the people back to Zion (Isaiah 40:3-5). In Jewish liturgy, chapters from this part of Isaiah are read as the "consolation" readings in the weeks after Tisha B’Av, the fast that mourns the temple. Christians hear the same chapters in Advent, and Handel set several of them in the oratorio Messiah (1741).

29.7

The Servant Songs

Main ideaFour poems in Second Isaiah describe a suffering servant, whom Jews and Christians have identified differently.

Within Isaiah 40 through 55 are four poems about a figure called "the servant of the LORD." Scholars since Duhm (1892) have called them the : Isaiah 42:1-4, 49:1-6, 50:4-9, and 52:13 through 53:12. The servant is chosen, brings justice to the nations, is despised and beaten, suffers for others, and is finally vindicated. The fourth song is the longest. It says the servant was wounded for our transgressions and that by his stripes we are healed.

Who is the servant? The text itself gives more than one clue. In Isaiah 49:3 the servant is called Israel by name. Jewish readers, from the medieval commentators Rashi and Ibn Ezra to most Jewish readers today, have read the servant as the people of Israel, suffering in exile among the nations and later restored. On this reading the "we" who speak in chapter 53 are the nations, amazed at Israel’s survival.

Christian readers, from the New Testament on, have read the fourth song as a description of Jesus. Acts 8 tells of an Ethiopian official reading Isaiah 53 in his chariot, and Philip explaining it as about Jesus. Christian worship has used the passage on Good Friday for centuries, and Handel’s Messiah sets it to music. Some ancient Jewish readings also connected the servant with a messiah, and some Christian scholars accept the collective reading. The two traditions read the same words and reach different conclusions.

A public-school class does not decide who the servant is. It learns to see what each reading rests on: the name Israel in 49:3, the singular "he" of chapter 53, the speakers, the history of exile. Reading closely means holding both readings in view and being able to explain each one fairly.

29.8

Jeremiah's Call

Main ideaJeremiah was called young, protested, and spent forty years delivering a message his people did not want.

The book of Jeremiah dates his call to the thirteenth year of King Josiah, about 627 BCE. The text says the word of the LORD came to him: before you were formed in the womb I knew you, and I appointed you a prophet to the nations. Jeremiah objected that he could not speak, for he was only a youth. The answer was that God would put words in his mouth, to pluck up and to break down, to build and to plant (Jeremiah 1:4-10).

His career ran through the last forty years of the kingdom of Judah. He watched Josiah’s reforms, Josiah’s death in 609 BCE, the rise of Babylon, the first deportation in 597 BCE, and the destruction of Jerusalem and its temple in 587 or 586 BCE. His message was that Babylon was God’s instrument and that resistance would fail. Kings and officials called that treason. He was beaten, put in stocks, and lowered into a muddy cistern to die, until an Ethiopian court official named Ebed-melech pulled him out (Jeremiah 38).

Jeremiah is the most personal of the prophets. Scattered through chapters 11 to 20 are poems scholars call his "confessions," in which he complains to God about his task. He says the word burns in his bones like fire and he cannot hold it in. He even curses the day he was born. The book also names his scribe, , who wrote down his words on a scroll that King Jehoiakim cut up and burned piece by piece; Jeremiah dictated it again (Jeremiah 36).

29.9

The Temple Sermon

Main ideaJeremiah told worshippers the temple would not save them, and was nearly executed for saying it.

Jeremiah 7 records a sermon at the gate of the in Jerusalem, and chapter 26 tells what happened afterward. The people, Jeremiah says, keep repeating "the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD," as if the building itself were a guarantee. He tells them these are deceptive words. If they do not deal justly, if they oppress the stranger, the orphan and the widow, the temple will not protect them.

Then he points to history. Shiloh, in the north, had once held the ark of the covenant, and Shiloh lay in ruins. God would do to this house, Jeremiah says, what he did to Shiloh. He also accuses the people of turning the temple into a den of robbers, a phrase the Gospels later have Jesus repeat in the temple courts (Mark 11:17). Chapter 26 reports that priests and prophets seized Jeremiah and demanded his death.

Jeremiah’s life was saved by a precedent. Some elders reminded the crowd that Micah of Moresheth had said, a century before, that Zion would be plowed like a field, and King Hezekiah had not killed him but had listened. Jeremiah was released. The scene shows how the prophets read one another and how a community argued over what to do with an unwelcome message.

29.10

A New Covenant

Main ideaJeremiah promises a covenant written on the heart, a passage Jews and Christians have read in different ways.

Jeremiah’s book is mostly warning, but chapters 30 to 33 are often called the Book of Consolation. Here the prophet buys a field in his hometown while Jerusalem is under siege, as a sign that houses and fields will be bought again (Jeremiah 32). And here he speaks of a . A covenant is a binding agreement; the Hebrew Bible tells of God’s covenant with Israel at Sinai, written on tablets of stone.

In Jeremiah 31:31-34 the text has God promise a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah. It will not be like the one made at the exodus, which the people broke. This time God will put his law within them and write it on their hearts. Everyone will know God, from the least to the greatest, and sins will be forgiven. The passage stays inside Israel’s story: it is a renewal of the bond with the same people and the same Torah.

Christians gave this phrase a central place. The Gospel of Luke and Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians both have Jesus speak of a new covenant in his blood at the Last Supper (Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25). The Letter to the Hebrews quotes Jeremiah 31 in full. The Christian name for its scriptures, the New Testament, means new covenant. Jewish readers have read the same passage as a promise of restoration after exile and of a Torah so deeply learned that it needs no teacher. Both readings start from the same verses.

29.12

How Prophets Speak

Main ideaProphetic books use recognizable forms: the oracle, the woe, the lawsuit and the vision report.

Prophetic books can feel like a jumble of speeches. They become easier to read once you can name the forms. The basic unit is the oracle, a message delivered as God’s word. It often opens with "Thus says the LORD" and closes with "says the LORD." Oracles of judgment state a charge and announce a consequence. Oracles of salvation announce rescue and often begin "Do not fear." Both kinds sit side by side in Isaiah and Jeremiah.

The is a cry of grief turned into an accusation. "Woe to those who join house to house," says Isaiah 5:8, against landowners who swallow up their neighbors’ fields. Amos 6:1 says woe to those who are at ease in Zion. The word imitates the wail at a funeral, so a woe treats the guilty as already dead. The lawsuit, seen in Micah 6 and Hosea 4, uses the language of a courtroom: a summons, witnesses, charges, and a verdict.

The vision report tells what the prophet saw and how it was explained. Amos 7 through 9 has a set of them: locusts, fire, a plumb line held against a wall, a basket of summer fruit. Zechariah and Ezekiel are full of them. Add the sign-act, in which the prophet does something in public (Jeremiah’s yoke, Ezekiel’s packed bag), and you have most of the toolbox. Naming the form helps you ask the right question: who is charged, what is announced, and to whom.

Main ideaCivil rights leaders read Amos, Micah and Isaiah as a language for justice, and their words shaped the movement.

King placed himself in a line of prophets who spoke unwelcome words to comfortable people.

Four months later, at the March on Washington, he quoted Amos 5:24 again, and the speech ended with the words of Isaiah 40: every valley exalted, every hill made low, the glory of the LORD revealed. King’s audience included Black churchgoers who had sung these texts for generations and white listeners who knew them from their own churches. The prophets gave the movement a shared vocabulary. Rabbi Abraham Joshua Heschel, who marched beside King at Selma in 1965, had written a major study called The Prophets (1962); he said that marching there felt like praying with his legs.

Chicago felt this directly. In 1966 King moved into an apartment on the city’s West Side to lead the Chicago Freedom Movement for open housing. He was struck by a rock during a march in Marquette Park. The Reverend Jesse Jackson, who had worked with King, led Operation Breadbasket in Chicago in those years. Black churches on the South and West Sides preached the prophets through it all. To study Amos in an Illinois classroom is to study a text that has been at work in the state’s own history.

The prophets have been read in other movements too, from abolition to labor to the environment, and by people who disagree with one another about politics. The texts do not belong to one side. What they offer is a form: a voice that names the powerful, defends the weak, and calls for a change that flows like water.

They sit with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights without a word.

Then Job opens his mouth, and the folktale becomes something else. In chapter 3 he curses the day he was born. For the next twenty-eight chapters, in dense poetry, Job and his friends argue. The friends say that God is just, so Job must have sinned. Job says he has not. He does not ask for his wealth back. He asks for a hearing. If only he could find God, he says, he would lay out his case and hear the answer.

In chapter 38 he gets one, and it is not what anyone expected. Out of a storm a voice asks Job where he was when the earth's foundations were laid. The voice describes the sea, the stars, the mountain goat, the wild donkey, the ostrich, the war horse, the hawk, and two monsters, Behemoth and Leviathan. It never mentions Job's suffering. It never explains the wager in chapter 1. Job answers that he has heard with his ears but now his eyes have seen, and he falls silent.

Then the folktale returns. God tells the friends they have not spoken rightly, as Job has. Job's fortune is doubled, and he has ten more children. Readers have argued for two thousand years about what this book means. 30.1

The Shape of the Book

Main ideaJob is a prose folktale wrapped around a long poem, and its meaning depends on how the two parts fit.

Job belongs to the third part of the Hebrew Bible, the or Writings, among the books scholars call : Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes. Wisdom books do not tell Israel’s national story. They ask how life works and how to live well, and they speak in the voice of teachers and thinkers rather than prophets or lawgivers. Job’s hero is not even an Israelite. He lives in Uz, a land the text places somewhere to the east.

The book has a clear architecture. Chapters 1 and 2 and the end of chapter 42 are prose: a simple story with a test, a loss and a restoration. Chapters 3 through 41 are poetry: three rounds of speeches between Job and his friends, a hymn about where wisdom is found (chapter 28), Job’s final oath of innocence (chapter 31), the speeches of a younger man, Elihu (chapters 32-37), and two speeches from the whirlwind (chapters 38-41).

The prose and the poetry do not quite match. In the prose Job is patient; in the poetry he is furious. The prose explains his suffering as a test; the poetry never mentions it. Many scholars think an old folktale was taken up by a poet who put the argument in the middle, and that the Elihu speeches may have been added later still. Traditional readers treat the book as one. Either way, reading Job means reading the frame and the poem together and noticing where they pull against each other.

No one knows who wrote Job or exactly when. The Talmud records several opinions, including one that Job never lived and the book is a parable. Many modern scholars date the poem to sometime between the 600s and 300s BCE, partly because its Hebrew is unusual and hard. Ezekiel 14:14 names Job with Noah and Daniel as a famously righteous man, so the figure was known early. Beyond that, honest readers say "c." and leave it open.

The Friends and Their Theology

Main ideaJob's friends defend a rule that the good prosper and the wicked suffer, and the book shows that rule breaking down.

Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar come to comfort Job and end up prosecuting him. Their argument rests on one idea, which scholars call : God rewards the righteous and punishes the wicked, in this life, in ways you can see. Proverbs says something like it often. If that rule holds, then Job’s suffering proves his guilt. Eliphaz says it gently at first: who that was innocent ever perished? Bildad says Job’s children died for their sins. Zophar says God is punishing Job less than he deserves.

Job refuses the logic. He does not deny that God is powerful; he denies that he has done anything to earn this. He points out what everyone can see: the wicked grow old, grow rich, and die in peace (chapter 21). He accuses his friends of lying for God’s sake, of defending God with falsehoods (13:7). In chapter 31 he swears a long oath listing the wrongs he has never done, from adultery to neglecting the poor to trusting in gold, and signs it, demanding an answer.

The friends’ theology collapses in two ways. Within the poem, they run out of things to say; the third round of speeches is short and broken, and Zophar never speaks a third time. Then in the epilogue, the text has God say to Eliphaz that his anger burns against the three friends because they have not spoken rightly about God, as Job has (42:7). The book that let the friends talk for twenty-five chapters ends by rejecting their case. Jewish and Christian readers have both taken this as a warning against explaining another person’s pain too neatly.

The Editor's Last Word

Main ideaEcclesiastes ends with a second voice that sums up Qohelet and adds a call to fear God and keep the commandments.

Ecclesiastes 12:9-14 changes voice. For eleven chapters Qohelet has spoken in the first person. Now someone speaks about him: Qohelet was wise, taught the people, weighed and arranged many proverbs, and sought fitting words. This is an , a closing note. Many scholars think an editor added it to frame the book for readers who found its doubts unsettling. Traditional readers see one author summing up.

The epilogue gives advice and then a conclusion. Of making many books there is no end, and much study wearies the body (12:12), a line teachers have quoted for two thousand years. Then: the end of the matter, all having been heard, is to fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of everyone, and God will bring every deed into judgment (12:13-14). The word "judgment" is one Qohelet doubted; the epilogue affirms it.

The epilogue may be one reason the book stayed in the Bible. The Talmud reports that some sages wanted to hide Ecclesiastes away because its words seemed to contradict one another, and that it was kept because it begins and ends with words of Torah. Christian readers have often read the ending as the key to the whole book. Other readers, ancient and modern, take Qohelet’s own voice as the point and the epilogue as a softening. Either way, the book you hold has two voices, and knowing where one ends and the other begins is part of reading it.

Ecclesiastes 12:13, King James Version, 1611

Confidence and Doubt

30.8

Proverbs Beside Job

Main ideaProverbs teaches that wisdom generally leads to a good life; Job and Ecclesiastes test that teaching against hard cases.

The book of Proverbs is confident. Its thirty-one chapters gather short sayings that teach a pattern: the diligent grow rich and the lazy go hungry; the righteous are delivered and the wicked fall; a soft answer turns away wrath. Trust in the LORD with all your heart and do not lean on your own understanding, says 3:5. Proverbs assumes the world has an order, that wisdom can learn it, and that living by it usually works.

Job and Ecclesiastes do not deny the pattern; they show where it fails. Job is a man who did everything Proverbs says and lost everything anyway. Qohelet has seen the righteous perish in their righteousness and the wicked live long in their evil (Ecclesiastes 7:15). Scholars sometimes call this a : Proverbs states the rule, and the other two books press on the exceptions. All three sit together in the same canon, and neither side is removed.

A good reader keeps all three in view. Proverbs is right that lying, laziness and rage usually bring trouble. Job is right that suffering does not prove guilt. Ecclesiastes is right that death comes to wise and foolish alike. Jewish tradition has long read the books together; Christian Bibles print them side by side. Holding a general rule and its exceptions at the same time is itself a kind of wisdom, and the Bible’s wisdom books model it by disagreeing in public.

30.9

The Problem of Evil

Main ideaWhy a good and powerful God allows suffering is a question many traditions ask, and each answers in its own way.

Job raises a question that philosophers later named the . If God is all-good and all-powerful, why is there suffering that seems undeserved? Attempts to answer it are called . The philosopher Leibniz coined that word in 1710 from the Greek for "God" and "justice." The book of Job is not a theodicy. It lets several answers speak and rebukes the neatest one. But it set the terms for a very long conversation.

Traditions answer differently, and this course describes rather than judges. Many Jewish thinkers have held that suffering can be a test or a discipline, or that some suffering is beyond human understanding. Others say the real question is what to do next. The rabbis also debated whether the righteous are rewarded in this world or the next. Many Christian thinkers, following Augustine (354-430), have said evil comes from the misuse of free will and that God brings good out of it. Others stress the suffering of Jesus as God sharing human pain. Islamic tradition tells of Ayyub, the Qur’an’s Job (Qur’an 21:83-84; 38:41-44), as a model of patience. It teaches that life is a trial whose full accounting comes on the Day of Judgment.

Traditions that do not center a creator ask the question differently. Buddhist teaching begins with dukkha, the unsatisfactory nature of ordinary life. It asks how to end craving rather than who is to blame. Hindu traditions often speak of karma, the results of past action across lifetimes. Many people without a religion say suffering has natural causes and needs no cosmic explanation. Job’s friends, Job himself, and the voice from the storm each have descendants in this conversation. A reader in a public school should be able to state each position fairly, in its own terms.

30.10

Job on the Stage

Main ideaArchibald MacLeish's J.B. (1958) put Job in a modern circus tent and asked the old question after two world wars.

Job has been retold by painters, poets and playwrights. The English poet William Blake made a set of engravings of Job in the 1820s. In 1958 the American poet Archibald MacLeish opened a play on Broadway called , which won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama in 1959. Its Job is a wealthy American businessman with five children. The heavenly scene becomes two broken-down circus vendors, Mr. Zuss and Nickles, who put on masks to play God and Satan.

MacLeish wrote after the Depression, the Second World War and the atomic bomb, and his J.B. loses his children to a car crash, a soldier’s death, a random murder and a bombing. The friends arrive as a psychiatrist, a Marxist and a clergyman, each with a modern theodicy, and J.B. rejects them all. Nickles keeps repeating a rhyme that sets God’s goodness and God’s power against each other, which is the problem of evil in six lines.

The ending changes the book. MacLeish’s J.B. does not get a whirlwind. He gets his wife, Sarah, back, and the play closes on the two of them choosing to love each other in a world that offers no explanation. Critics argued about whether that was a faithful reading of Job or a replacement for it. That argument is itself part of the book’s life: every retelling decides what the whirlwind meant, and the play’s popularity shows how many people wanted to ask Job’s question out loud in the 1950s.

30.11

Job After Auschwitz

Main ideaElie Wiesel's The Trial of God (1979) puts God on trial in Job's spirit, arguing and still refusing to let go.

Elie Wiesel (1928-2016) survived Auschwitz and Buchenwald as a teenager, wrote the memoir Night, and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1986. In 1979 he published a play called . It is set in 1649 in a Ukrainian village after a massacre of its Jews, on the festival of Purim, when Jewish custom allows plays and jokes. Three traveling actors agree to stage a mock trial with God as the defendant. Berish, the innkeeper who lost his daughters, prosecutes.

Wiesel wrote that the idea came from something he saw as a boy in Auschwitz: three learned men who put God on trial, found him guilty, and then went to pray the evening service. That pairing, accusation and prayer together, is Job’s pairing. Job says God has wronged him and still insists on speaking to God, not about him. In Wiesel’s play the only person willing to defend God turns out to be a stranger who is not what he seems. The play ends without a verdict, as the massacre resumes.

Jewish readers after the Holocaust returned to Job often, and disagreed. Some found in the book permission to argue with heaven; the tradition already had Abraham bargaining over Sodom and Moses pleading at Sinai. Some found the whirlwind’s silence about Job’s suffering unbearable. Christian and secular readers took up the same questions in their own terms. What Wiesel adds is the setting: Job’s ash heap in a century when the losses were counted in millions.

This lesson closes where the chapter began. A man on an ash heap asked for a hearing. Writers in 1958 and 1979 staged one. Readers in Chicago and everywhere else keep the case open each time they read chapter 3 aloud. The wisdom books do not close the question of suffering. They keep it in the Bible, in Job’s own words, so that no one has to ask it alone.
