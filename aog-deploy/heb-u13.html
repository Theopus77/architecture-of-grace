Law, Covenant and the Prophetic Voice

Torah and Nevi'im Close Up

Two kinds of writing sit at the heart of the Tanakh, and this unit reads both up close. The first is law: the ten sayings and the case laws of Exodus 19–24. The Torah says they were given at a mountain in the wilderness and sealed as a covenant between God and Israel. The second is prophecy: the speeches of Amos, Hosea, Micah and Isaiah. They told a people keeping the rituals that God wanted justice, loyal love and return. The prophets quote the law, and the law already sounds like the prophets. Both protect the stranger, the widow and the orphan.

We read these texts as a public school studies them: as Jewish tradition reads them, as historians and literary scholars read them, and in comparison with the world around them. Hammurabi's laws sit beside the goring ox of Exodus. Hittite treaties sit beside the Sinai scene. Rashi, Ibn Ezra, the Talmud and Maimonides sit beside modern scholars. Martin Luther King Jr. and Abraham Joshua Heschel sit beside Amos. We describe what each says and how each has been read, and we do not decide whether any of it is true.

By the end you will be able to do several things. You will explain why the same ten sayings are numbered three different ways. You will compare an ancient Babylonian law with one in Exodus and say what changed. You will state how the rabbis read "eye for eye." You will read Amos 5, Micah 6 and Isaiah 58 closely enough to say who is speaking to whom and what is demanded. You will explain why Jonah is read on Yom Kippur afternoon. And you will learn how these texts moved from the synagogue's weekly reading into the streets of Selma and Chicago.

The big question

How do the Torah's covenant laws and the prophets' demands for justice belong to one conversation, and how have their readers carried it on?

Exodus 19–24
Big questionWhat does it mean that the Torah presents its laws as the terms of a covenant, and how have readers handled the hardest of those laws?
The story

Two Oxen, Two Laws

A cracked black stone in Paris and a scroll read in every synagogue describe the very same accident.

Picture an ox that has gored before. Its owner knows it is dangerous. The neighbors have warned him. He does nothing, and one morning the ox breaks loose and kills a person in the street. Who is responsible, and what does the owner owe? That case is old. It appears in two texts written in two languages, hundreds of miles and perhaps several centuries apart.

The first is carved on a tall black stone, more than seven feet high. It now stands in the Louvre museum in Paris. It was made for Hammurabi, king of Babylon, around 1750 BCE. French excavators found it in 1901 at Susa, in what is now Iran. A later conqueror had carried it off as a prize. Its laws about the goring ox are clear. If the owner was warned and did not restrain the animal, he must pay silver to the dead person's family.

The second is in the book of Exodus, in a section scholars call the *Covenant Code*, Exodus 21 to 23. There the case runs almost the same way, with the same ox, the same warning, and the same negligent owner. But the outcome is different. The ox is stoned and its meat may not be eaten. The owner may face death, or a payment set by the court. If the victim was a slave, the owner pays thirty shekels.

For the Torah, this is not just a piece of legal advice. It sits inside a larger scene. In Exodus 19 the people arrive at a mountain in the wilderness. In Exodus 20 they hear ten sayings. In Exodus 24 they answer, and the covenant is sealed with blood and a meal. The laws in between are presented as the terms of a bond between God and Israel. Jewish tradition reads all of it as Torah given at Sinai; historians read it beside the older laws of Babylon and ask what is shared and what is new.

This chapter puts the two ox laws side by side. Then it works outward. It looks at the treaty form that the Sinai scene resembles. It reads the Ten Sayings and the three ways of counting them. It takes up the famous line "eye for eye." It reads the laws that protect the stranger, the widow and the orphan. It ends with Shavuot, the spring festival that Jewish tradition connects to the day the Torah was given.

Sinai as a Treaty Scene

25.1

Arriving at the Mountain

Main ideaExodus 19 opens the Sinai scene with an offer: if Israel keeps the covenant, it will be a treasured people and a kingdom of priests.

Exodus 19 begins with a date. In the third month after leaving Egypt, the Torah says, the people camp in the wilderness in front of a mountain. The book calls it ; in Deuteronomy the same mountain is usually called Horeb. Moses goes up, and the text reports God’s message to the people below. It is short, and it is built like an offer with a condition.

The offer runs like this. God reminds the people what has already happened: "Ye have seen what I did unto the Egyptians." Then comes the condition, "if ye will hearken unto My voice indeed, and keep My ." Then the promise: they will be God’s own treasure among all peoples, "a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:4–6, JPS 1917). A covenant, brit in Hebrew, is a binding bond between two parties with duties on each side.

The people answer as one: "All that the LORD hath spoken we will do" (Exodus 19:8). Only after that answer does the text describe the thunder, the cloud and the ten sayings. Rashi, the eleventh-century French commentator, reads "kingdom of priests" as meaning princes or nobles, a people set apart for service. Modern literary readers notice the order of the scene: memory of a rescue, then a condition, then a promise, then consent. That order will matter in the next lesson.

25.2

The Shape of an Ancient Treaty

Main ideaMany scholars see in Exodus 19–24 the pattern of ancient Near Eastern treaties between a great king and a lesser one.

In the 1950s the American scholar George Mendenhall noticed something about the Sinai chapters. Treaties from the Hittite empire, in what is now Turkey, dating from about 1400 to 1200 BCE, follow a set pattern. A great king makes a pact with a smaller king who becomes his , his loyal dependent. The treaty opens by naming the great king. It recalls what he has done for the vassal. It lists the vassal’s duties. It calls witnesses, orders the text to be kept and read aloud, and ends with blessings for loyalty and curses for betrayal.

Set Exodus 19–24 beside that list and the fit is striking. "I am the LORD thy God" names the greater party. "Who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" recalls the favor already done. The ten sayings and the laws that follow are the duties. In Exodus 24 the text is written down, read aloud, and the people answer. Deuteronomy, which retells the covenant, adds long blessings and curses in chapters 27 and 28. Scholars call this the form, from a French word for an overlord.

Not everyone agrees on the details. Some scholars think the closest parallels are later Assyrian treaties from the 600s BCE, which would point to a later date for parts of Deuteronomy. Others caution that a shared form does not tell you where a text came from. What most readers accept is the main point: the Torah presents law not as a king’s decree but as the terms of a relationship. Jewish tradition had long said something similar in its own words. The midrash pictures God offering the Torah to other nations first, and Israel accepting it, a story of consent rather than force.

25.3

Thunder, Boundaries and Fear

Main ideaThe Torah frames the giving of the sayings with thunder, fire and a boundary, and the people ask Moses to speak for them.

Before the sayings, the Torah sets the stage with care. The people wash their clothes and prepare for three days. A boundary is drawn around the mountain, and no one may cross it. On the third day, the text reports thunder and lightning, a thick cloud, a loud horn blast, smoke and fire. The mountain shakes. "And all the people that were in the camp trembled" (Exodus 19:16). The scene is built to convey the weight of what follows.

After the sayings, the people react. In the JPS 1917 numbering, Exodus 20:15 says the people "saw the thunderings" and stood far off. They ask Moses to speak with them instead, "but let not God speak with us, lest we die" (Exodus 20:16). Moses agrees. From here on, most of the Torah’s laws come through Moses rather than directly to the crowd. The rabbis noticed this. One tradition in the Talmud (Makkot 24a) says the people heard the first two sayings directly and the rest through Moses, because those two are phrased in the first person.

Readers also notice the odd phrase "saw the thunderings." Rashi takes it literally as a miracle: they saw what is normally only heard. Ibn Ezra, a twelfth-century Spanish commentator, reads it as ordinary language, since Hebrew often uses one sense-word for another. Two commentators, one verse, two methods: one presses the strange wording, the other explains it as idiom. Both are honored in Jewish study, and comparing them is part of how the Torah is read.

The Ten Sayings Close Up

25.4

Aseret HaDibrot: Ten Sayings

Main ideaThe Hebrew Bible calls them the ten words or sayings, and gives them twice, in Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5, with small differences.

English speakers say "the Ten Commandments," but the Torah’s own phrase is aseret hadevarim, "the ten words" (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 4:13). Rabbinic Hebrew calls them , the ten sayings or utterances. The difference matters. The first saying in Jewish counting, "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage" (Exodus 20:2, JPS 1917), commands nothing. It is a statement of who is speaking and what has already been done. Calling it a "word" fits it better than calling it a command.

The sayings appear twice. Exodus 20 gives them at Sinai. Deuteronomy 5 has Moses repeat them forty years later. The two versions are nearly identical, but not quite. The Shabbat saying in Exodus begins "Remember" () and gives creation as the reason: God rested on the seventh day. In Deuteronomy it begins "Observe" () and gives Egypt as the reason: you were slaves, so let your servants rest too. The Talmud (Shevuot 20b) says "Remember" and "Observe" were spoken in a single utterance, a way of saying both are equally the word of God.

Jewish practice keeps both. The Friday night hymn Lekha Dodi opens with the line "Observe and Remember in a single utterance." In synagogue, when the ten sayings are read from the Torah, many congregations stand. But the rabbis also resisted giving the ten a special status above the rest of the Torah. The Talmud (Berakhot 12a) reports that the daily recitation of the ten sayings was stopped so that no one could claim that only these ten were given at Sinai.

25.5

Three Ways to Count to Ten

Main ideaJews, Catholics and Lutherans, and Protestants and Orthodox Christians divide the same verses into ten in three different ways.

Everyone agrees the text of Exodus 20:2–14 (JPS numbering) contains ten sayings, because Exodus 34:28 says so. But the verses do not come numbered. Where one saying ends and the next begins is a matter of tradition, and three main systems exist. Each covers exactly the same words; they simply cut them at different places.

In the Jewish count, the first saying is "I am the LORD thy God." The second joins "Thou shalt have no other gods before Me" with the ban on graven images. The rest follow in order, and the tenth is the ban on coveting. The Catholic and Lutheran count goes back to Augustine (354–430 CE). It treats "I am the LORD" as an introduction. It joins "no other gods" and "no images" as the first. Then it splits coveting into two: the ninth covers a neighbor’s house or wife, the tenth the rest. Most Protestants and Orthodox Christians use a third count, which matches the ancient Jewish writers Philo and Josephus. There "no other gods" is the first, "no images" the second, and coveting a single tenth.

This is why a Catholic student and a Baptist student may both say "the fifth commandment" and mean different things: for one it is "Honor thy father and thy mother," for the other "Thou shalt not murder." No system changes the content. What differs is how each tradition hears the opening. Jewish tradition puts the relationship first: before any command, the speaker names who is speaking and what has been done.

25.6

Parents, Neighbors and Rest

Main ideaThe second group of sayings deals with people: parents, life, marriage, property, truth and desire, and Shabbat rest extends to servants and animals.

Jewish tradition often pictures the sayings on two tablets, five and five. The first five in the Jewish count concern the relationship with God, and they include honoring parents. The second five concern the neighbor: murder, adultery, theft, false witness and coveting. The Mekhilta, an early rabbinic commentary on Exodus, pairs them across the tablets. It matches "I am the LORD" with "Thou shalt not murder," since a human being is made in the divine image. The pairing is a reading, not a fact about the stone. But it shows how the rabbis heard the two halves as one.

The Hebrew of the sixth saying is . The King James Version (1611) translates it "Thou shalt not kill," and JPS 1917 does the same. Many later translations, including NJPS (1985), prefer "You shall not murder," because the Hebrew verb is generally used for unlawful killing and not for war or the death penalty, both of which the Torah elsewhere permits. The choice of word carries a reading with it. A class can compare the two translations and ask what each one lets the verse say.

The Shabbat saying is the longest of the ten. It names everyone who rests: you, your children, your servants, your animals and the stranger within your gates (Exodus 20:10). No other ancient law code known to scholars orders a weekly rest for servants and animals. Jewish families keep Shabbat from Friday sunset to Saturday night with candles, a meal, prayer and a break from work. For observant Jews it is the most frequent of all the commandments, arriving every seven days. The Torah presents it here as a reminder of creation; in Deuteronomy, as a reminder of freedom.

The Covenant Code Beside Hammurabi

25.7

A King's Stone

Main ideaHammurabi's laws, carved around 1750 BCE, are the best-known of several Mesopotamian law collections that let readers compare the Torah's laws with their neighbors.

In December 1901 a French team digging at Susa, in southwestern Iran, uncovered a pillar of black stone taller than a person. At the top, a carving shows a king standing before a seated god. Below run column after column of cuneiform writing: a prologue, some 282 laws, and an epilogue with blessings and curses. The king is of Babylon, who ruled about 1792 to 1750 BCE. An Elamite conqueror had hauled the stone to Susa centuries later as loot. It now stands in the Louvre, and it is the most complete ancient law collection known.

Hammurabi’s is not the oldest. Laws of Ur-Nammu from about 2100 BCE, written in Sumerian, and laws from the city of Eshnunna, from about 1770 BCE, survive in part. Later Middle Assyrian and Hittite laws also exist. Scholars debate whether these were codes that courts actually consulted or monuments meant to show the king as a just ruler. Either way, they show the kinds of cases that ancient Near Eastern societies argued about: injury, theft, marriage, slaves, wages, loans and animals.

The University of Chicago has a long connection to this world. Its Oriental Institute, now the Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures, spent ninety years on one project. From 1921 to 2011 it produced the Chicago Assyrian Dictionary. That is the standard dictionary of Akkadian, the language in which Hammurabi’s laws were written. Much of what any reader can say about those laws in English passes through work done in Hyde Park.

Why does this matter for reading Exodus? Because Exodus 21–23, the section scholars call the Covenant Code, treats many of the same cases in a similar style: "if a man does X, then Y." Nobody claims the Torah copied Hammurabi. The likeliest view is that both draw on a shared legal culture of the region, and the interesting question is where the Torah goes its own way. Jewish tradition, reading the same laws as Torah given at Sinai, asks a different question: what each law teaches and how it is to be practiced.

25.8

The Goring Ox, Twice

Main ideaExodus and Hammurabi treat the goring ox almost identically until the outcome, where the Torah stones the animal and treats the death as more than a debt.

Exodus 21:28–32 sets out the case in steps. If an ox gores a man or woman to death, the ox is stoned and its flesh is not eaten. The owner is clear. But suppose the ox was known to gore, the owner was warned, and he did not keep it in. Then the ox is stoned and the owner too is liable to death. A ransom may be set for him instead, and then he pays what is laid on him. If the victim is a slave, the owner pays thirty shekels of silver to the slave’s master, and the ox is stoned. The next verses handle an ox that gores another ox.

Hammurabi’s laws 250–252 cover the same ground. If an ox gores a man on the street and kills him, there is no claim. But suppose the ox was known to gore, the owner was told by the authorities, and he did not blunt its horns or tie it up. Then if the ox kills a free man, the owner pays half a mina of silver. If it kills a slave, he pays a third of a mina. The structure is the same. First an accident with no fault. Then an owner who ignored a warning. Then a lower payment for a slave.

The differences are the point. Hammurabi never touches the ox; the whole matter is money. Exodus stones the ox in every case where a human dies, and forbids eating the meat, which suggests the animal has done something more than cause a loss. And for the negligent owner, Exodus raises the stakes to his own life, with ransom as an option, while Hammurabi sets a fixed sum. The Mishnah and Talmud (tractate Bava Kamma) later build a whole system on these verses: a , an ox with no record, whose owner pays half damages, and a mu&#x27;ad, a warned ox, whose owner pays in full.

25.9

Eye for Eye

Main ideaExodus 21:24 states "eye for eye," Hammurabi applies it literally between equals, and rabbinic tradition reads the Torah's words as monetary compensation.

The most quoted line in the Covenant Code is Exodus 21:23–25: "life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot." Scholars call this , Latin for the law of retaliation in kind. It appears again in Leviticus 24:19–20 and Deuteronomy 19:21. In Exodus it comes at the end of a case about two men fighting and hurting a pregnant woman, and it reads like a general principle attached to a specific case.

Hammurabi has the same principle in laws 196–200: if a free man destroys the eye of another free man, his eye is destroyed; if he breaks a bone, his bone is broken; a tooth for a tooth. But Hammurabi’s version depends on rank. If the victim is a commoner, the offender pays one mina of silver; if a slave, half the slave’s value. Talion applies only between equals. The Torah’s version names no ranks, and the verse right after it frees a slave whose master destroys his eye or tooth (Exodus 21:26–27).

Rabbinic tradition reads "eye for eye" as payment, not mutilation. The Mishnah (Bava Kamma 8:1) lists five kinds of compensation an injurer owes: the injury, the pain, the medical costs, lost work and the shame. The Talmud (Bava Kamma 83b–84a) argues at length that the verse must mean money. Two eyes are never exactly equal, and a blind man could not be blinded in return. Was the verse ever applied literally in ancient Israel? Historians cannot settle that. Both the plain wording and the rabbinic reading are part of the Torah’s history, and a student should be able to state both.

Main ideaThe Covenant Code protects the stranger, widow and orphan and gives a reason from Israel's own story: you were strangers in Egypt.

For historians, the chapter shows the treaty pattern coming to its end. There is a written text, a public reading, the parties’ consent, and a ceremony that binds them. Blood rites sealing a pact are known from elsewhere in the ancient world. A shared meal is a common way to close an agreement. A literary reader notices something else. The same people who trembled at the thunder in Exodus 19 now eat and drink in God’s presence. That movement from fear to fellowship frames the laws in between.

The story

Noise at Bethel

A cattle herder from the south stands in the royal shrine of the north and tells the worshippers that God is not listening.

Picture the shrine at Bethel around 760 BCE. It is the royal sanctuary of the northern kingdom of Israel, and the kingdom is rich. Under King Jeroboam II the borders have expanded and trade is good. Pilgrims crowd the courtyard with animals for sacrifice. Musicians play. Priests move through the smoke. Everything looks like a religion working exactly as it should.

Into this walks a man named Amos. He is not from here. He is from Tekoa, a village in the hills of Judah, the southern kingdom, and by his own account he is a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore figs, not a prophet by trade. He begins to speak, and what he says is unwelcome. God, he says, hates their festivals and takes no pleasure in their offerings. "Take thou away from Me the noise of thy songs" (Amos 5:23, JPS 1917). Then the line that will be carved on monuments and shouted in marches for the next twenty-seven centuries: "But let justice well up as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream."

The priest of Bethel, Amaziah, does what officials do. He sends word to the king and orders Amos to go home and prophesy in Judah, where he came from. Amos answers that he did not choose this. He was taking care of animals when the LORD took him, and he must say what he was sent to say. Then he adds a message for Amaziah himself, and it is not gentle.

Amos is the earliest of the prophets whose words fill a book of their own, and the pattern he sets runs through the rest: Hosea in the same northern kingdom a few years later, Micah and Isaiah in Judah in the decades that follow. They speak to a people who are keeping the rituals and breaking the covenant, and they say the two cannot be separated. Sacrifice without justice is noise. Fasting without feeding the hungry is theater.

This chapter reads their sharpest passages closely: Amos 5, Hosea's strange marriage, Micah 6, and Isaiah 1, 58 and 40. It follows the prophets into the synagogue, where they are read every Shabbat as the *haftarah*. And it follows them into the twentieth century. Abraham Joshua Heschel and Martin Luther King Jr. carried their words into the streets of Selma and Chicago.

Amos: Justice Like Water

26.1

A Herdsman from Tekoa

Main ideaAmos, a southerner who denied being a professional prophet, preached in the northern kingdom around 760 BCE and was told to go home.

The book of Amos opens with a date and an address. Amos was "among the herdmen of Tekoa," and he spoke "concerning Israel" in the days of King Uzziah of Judah and King Jeroboam of Israel, "two years before the earthquake" (Amos 1:1). Scholars place this around 760 to 750 BCE. Tekoa lies south of Jerusalem in Judah. Israel, the , was a separate state with its own king and its own shrines, the chief one at Bethel. Amos crossed a border to deliver his message.

Amos 7:10–17 tells what happened. , priest of Bethel, reported to King Jeroboam that Amos was conspiring against him. He told Amos to flee to Judah and earn his bread there. Amos replied that he was no prophet and no prophet’s son but a herdsman and a dresser of sycamore trees. The LORD took him from following the flock and said, "Go, prophesy unto My people Israel" (7:15). The exchange is one of the few places where a prophetic book shows the prophet being answered back.

Readers have drawn different things from this. Historians note that Amaziah treats Amos as a professional prophet, one who earns a living by prophesying, and Amos rejects the label. There were such prophets, and the books sometimes attack them. Rabbinic tradition lists Amos among the twelve minor prophets. "Minor" means short, not unimportant. His book is read as the haftarah on several Shabbatot. Literary readers notice that Amos speaks in the language of the countryside: locusts, plumb lines, baskets of summer fruit, a lion roaring.

Main ideaIn Amos 5:21–24 God rejects Israel's festivals and songs and demands justice and righteousness instead.

The two Hebrew words matter. , justice, is what happens in a court: a fair judgment, especially for the poor. , righteousness, is doing right by others; in later Hebrew it came to mean charity. The prophets pair the words often. Jewish commentators reject that reading, and most modern scholars regard it as a misreading of a prophet who was addressing his own people. Near the end of the book comes a verse that has startled readers for a long time. "Are ye not as the children of the Ethiopians unto Me, O children of Israel? saith the LORD. 26.6

What Does the LORD Require?

Main ideaMicah 6:6–8 answers the question of what to bring to God with three verbs: do justly, love mercy, walk humbly.

came from Moresheth, a town in the Judean lowlands, and prophesied in the late 700s BCE, in the same years as Isaiah in Jerusalem. Micah 6 is staged as a lawsuit. God calls the mountains as witnesses and asks the people what wrong has been done to them, recalling the Exodus and the wilderness. Then a speaker, standing for the people, asks what could possibly be enough: burnt offerings, calves, thousands of rams, ten thousand rivers of oil, even a firstborn child (6:6–7). The list climbs to horror and stops.

The answer is short. "It hath been told thee, O man, what is good, and what the LORD doth require of thee: only to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God" (Micah 6:8, JPS 1917). Three verbs, and the word "only." "Mercy" here is again chesed. "Walk humbly" translates a rare Hebrew word, hatznea, which some render "modestly" or "carefully." The verse does not name a single ritual. It also does not forbid one.

Jewish tradition gave the verse a special place. The Talmud (Makkot 23b–24a) tells that Moses received 613 commandments, David reduced them to eleven, Isaiah to six, and Micah to three, quoting this verse. Rabbi Simlai’s teaching is not that the other commandments are canceled; it is that Micah states the principle beneath them. The verse is read as the haftarah for the Torah portion Balak, and it appears on synagogue walls and in the mission statements of Jewish agencies. King quoted it too. It is one of the shortest summaries of the prophetic demand, and one of the most quoted.

Isaiah: Worship, Justice, Comfort

26.7

Worship Without Justice

Main ideaIsaiah 1 says God is weary of sacrifices and prayers from hands full of blood, and calls the people to seek justice and defend the orphan and widow.

son of Amoz prophesied in Jerusalem from about 740 to 700 BCE, through the years when Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom and besieged Judah. His book is the longest of the prophets, sixty-six chapters, and it opens with a chapter that works as an overture. Heaven and earth are called to hear. Israel is a child who has rebelled, a body covered in wounds, a country burned by invaders, with Jerusalem left "as a booth in a vineyard" (1:8).

Then comes the attack on worship, in words close to Amos and Hosea. God is full of burnt offerings and has no delight in the blood of bulls. New moons and Sabbaths, the sacred gatherings, are called a burden. "When ye spread forth your hands, I will hide Mine eyes from you; yea, when ye make many prayers, I will not hear; your hands are full of blood" (1:15, JPS 1917). The remedy follows at once: wash, cease to do evil, "learn to do well; seek justice, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow" (1:17).

In the synagogue, Isaiah 1 is read as the haftarah on the Shabbat before Tisha B’Av, the summer fast that mourns the destruction of both Temples. That Shabbat is named , "the Sabbath of the vision," from the chapter’s first word. The choice places the prophet’s warning right before the day of mourning, as if to say that the destruction the prophets warned of has a cause. Then, the week after the fast, the reading turns to comfort, as the next lessons show.

26.8

The Fast God Chooses

Main ideaIsaiah 58, read on Yom Kippur morning, says the fast God wants is to free the oppressed, feed the hungry and shelter the poor.

Isaiah 58 comes from the later part of the book, which most scholars date after the Babylonian exile. It addresses a community that fasts and complains that God has not noticed. The answer is sharp. On the day of your fast you pursue your business and press your workers. You fast to quarrel and to strike with the fist. Is that the fast God has chosen, a day for bowing the head like a bulrush? Then the chapter defines the fast God wants. Loose the bonds of wickedness. Let the oppressed go free. Share bread with the hungry. Bring the homeless poor into your house. Clothe the naked. Do not hide from your own kin (58:6–7).

Then the promise: your light will break forth like the dawn, and when you call, the LORD will answer, "Here I am" (58:8–9). The chapter ends with the Sabbath: call it a delight, and you will delight in the LORD (58:13–14). The chapter is not against fasting or Sabbath. It insists that the fast and the Sabbath include the hungry and the worker.

The rabbis chose this chapter as the haftarah for the morning of , the Day of Atonement. It is the one fast day in the Torah itself (Leviticus 16:29; 23:27) and the holiest day of the Jewish year. Congregations have fasted since the previous sunset. Now they hear a prophet ask whether their fast is the kind God wants. Many synagogues pair the day with food drives for local pantries. The practice grew in the twentieth century and is common in Chicago-area congregations. The passage is a case of a tradition reading its own harshest critic aloud on its most solemn day.

26.9

Comfort Ye, Comfort Ye

Main ideaIsaiah 40 opens a new voice of consolation for the exiles, and Jewish tradition reads it on the Shabbat after Tisha B'Av as the first of seven weeks of comfort.

Something changes at Isaiah 40. The Assyrians are gone; the enemy is Babylon; Jerusalem is in ruins and the people are in exile. A voice says, "Comfort ye, comfort ye My people, saith your God" (40:1, JPS 1917). Jerusalem’s term of service is over. A highway is to be made in the desert, and the glory of the LORD will be revealed. Chapters 40 to 55 speak of a return from Babylon and even name the Persian king Cyrus (44:28; 45:1), who in 539 BCE conquered Babylon and allowed exiles to go home.

Because of this, most scholars since the 1700s have held that chapters 40–66 were written later. They credit one or more prophets in the exile and after, sometimes called Second and Third Isaiah, whose words were joined to the book of Isaiah of Jerusalem. Traditional Jewish and Christian readers held that Isaiah foresaw the exile, and many still do. Interestingly, the medieval commentator Ibn Ezra hints at a later author in his note on Isaiah 40:1, though he says it guardedly. The question of authorship is a live one. A class can hold both positions in view.

In the synagogue, Isaiah 40:1–26 is read on the Shabbat after Tisha B’Av, called , "the Sabbath of Comfort," from its first word. It begins the , seven weeks of readings from Isaiah 40 onward that lead up to Rosh Hashanah. The calendar tells a story: three weeks of warning before the fast, then seven weeks of comfort after it. The same book that provided the harshest reading of the year provides the gentlest. Christians know the chapter too; its opening is the first words sung in Handel’s Messiah (1741).

Return: Teshuvah, Jonah and the Prophets Today

26.10

Teshuvah: The Prophetic Demand

Main ideaThe prophets' constant call is to return, teshuvah, and rabbinic tradition built the Days of Awe around that idea.

The prophets do not only accuse. Nearly every one of them ends an oracle with a door left open. Hosea’s last chapter begins, "Return, O Israel, unto the LORD thy God" (14:2 in JPS numbering; 14:1 in most Christian Bibles), and promises that God will heal their backsliding and love them freely. Amos says, "Seek ye Me, and live" (5:4). Isaiah 55:7 calls the wicked to forsake his way and return, "for He will abundantly pardon." The Hebrew verb is , to turn or return, and the noun is .

Rabbinic tradition made teshuvah the center of the ten days from Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur, the . The Shabbat between them is called after the first word of its haftarah, Hosea 14. Maimonides, the twelfth-century philosopher and legal authority, defined teshuvah in his Laws of Repentance (2:2). The sinner abandons the sin, removes it from his thoughts, resolves in his heart never to do it again, and regrets the past. He adds a test. Full teshuvah is proven when the same temptation returns and the person passes it by.

Two things stand out. First, the prophets address the nation, and the rabbis address each person; teshuvah moves from the public square to the individual heart without leaving the square. Second, teshuvah is not only sorrow. Maimonides also requires that wrongs against other people be put right with the person wronged; Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, the Mishnah says (Yoma 8:9), but not for sins against a fellow human until that person is appeased. The prophetic demand for justice and the personal work of repentance meet in that rule.

Think it through
Maimonides on Complete Return

A person who faces the same chance to do the wrong and could do it, but turns away because of the teshuvah, not from fear or weakness. And what is teshuvah? That the sinner leave the sin, put it out of his thoughts, resolve never to do it again, and regret the past.

Maimonides makes the test of repentance a future event, not a feeling. Why might he set the bar there? Compare it with Hosea 14:2, which asks the nation to return.

26.11

Jonah on Yom Kippur Afternoon

Main ideaThe book of Jonah, read on Yom Kippur afternoon, shows a foreign city that repents and a prophet who must be taught mercy, and ends with a question.

Jonah is the shortest story among the prophets and the strangest. God sends the prophet to , capital of Assyria, the empire that destroyed the northern kingdom, to announce its doom. Jonah runs the other way, to the sea, and is thrown overboard and swallowed by a great fish. Returned to land, he walks into Nineveh and delivers a message of five Hebrew words: in forty days the city will be overturned. The whole city, from the king down to the cattle, fasts and puts on sackcloth. God relents. And Jonah is furious.

The last chapter is a dialogue. Jonah sits outside the city, and God makes a plant grow to shade him, then sends a worm to kill it. Jonah grieves for the plant. God asks: you cared about a plant you did not grow, that came up in a night and died in a night; should I not care about Nineveh, a great city with more than a hundred and twenty thousand people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and much cattle? (4:10–11, paraphrased from JPS 1917). The book ends there, on a question with no answer written.

The rabbis chose Jonah as the haftarah for the afternoon of Yom Kippur, the last reading before the fast ends. The Mishnah (Ta’anit 2:1) had already made the point: on a public fast day, the elder says to the people that of Nineveh it is not written that God saw their sackcloth and fasting, but that God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way. Nineveh, the enemy, becomes the model of teshuvah. And the prophet, who wanted judgment, is the one still learning at the end. Readers argue about what Jonah is: a satire on prophets, a lesson on mercy to enemies, a story about the limits of prophecy. The book seems built to leave the argument open.

26.12

The Prophets Marching

Main ideaThe prophets live on in the daily prayer book and in modern Jewish social action, and Heschel and King carried their words into the civil rights movement.

The prophets are not only read once a year. The , the Jewish prayer book, is threaded with their lines. Isaiah 6:3, "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts," is the heart of the Kedushah in every morning service. Hosea’s betrothal verses are said with the tefillin. Micah 7:18–20, "Who is a God like unto Thee, that pardoneth the iniquity," is recited at on Rosh Hashanah beside a stream. Every Shabbat brings a haftarah. A Jew who prays regularly hears the prophets more often than almost any other part of the Tanakh outside the Torah.

In the twentieth century the prophets became a language of action. was a rabbi and scholar who escaped Nazi Europe and taught at the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York. He published The Prophets in 1962, arguing that the prophets’ anger was God’s own concern for the poor. In March 1965 he marched beside Martin Luther King Jr. from Selma toward Montgomery. He later wrote that he felt his legs were praying. King, a Baptist minister, quoted Amos 5:24 in the "I Have a Dream" speech in 1963 and again in the Letter from Birmingham Jail. He drew on Isaiah and Micah throughout his sermons.

Chicago has its own chapter of this story. In 1966 King moved into an apartment on the West Side to lead the Chicago Freedom Movement for open housing, and rabbis and Jewish congregations marched with him. Jewish social-justice organizations in the city today, from congregational committees to community-wide agencies, cite Amos, Isaiah and Micah in their statements of purpose. Not every Jewish reader agrees on what the prophets require in politics; Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and secular Jews draw different conclusions from the same verses. What they share is the text, and the belief that reading it is not finished until something is done.
