Traditions

When you feel sick, who helps you? Maybe a nurse, a doctor or a pharmacist. Around the world, people have asked the same question for thousands of years. They have built different ways of healing.

In this unit you will meet healers from the West, like Hippocrates, Florence Nightingale, Louis Pasteur and Clara Barton. You will also learn about Traditional Chinese Medicine from China and Ayurveda from India. Each tradition has its own ideas about the body.

You will see how science tests what works and what is safe. By the end, you will be able to compare traditions with respect. You will also know why it helps to tell your doctor about everything you use.

Doctors of the West

Western Medicine
Big questionHow did people in the West learn what really makes us sick and what helps us get well?
The story

The Lady with the Lamp

In a crowded army hospital, one nurse walks the halls at night and starts counting.

It is 1854. The place is Scutari, across the water from the city now called Istanbul. A huge army hospital sits on a hill. Inside, British soldiers from the Crimean War lie in long rows. The halls are dark and cold.

A nurse named Florence Nightingale carries a small lamp. She walks the long halls late at night. She stops at each bed. She speaks softly to the men. The soldiers start to call her the Lady with the Lamp.

Florence sees more than sick men. She sees dirty floors and dirty sheets. She smells bad air. She sees that the water is not clean. Many soldiers are not dying from their wounds. They are dying from sickness that spreads inside the hospital.

So Florence does something new. She counts. She writes down who gets sick and who dies. She and her nurses scrub, wash and bring in clean food. Later she uses her numbers to show leaders that clean hospitals save lives.

Hippocrates Looks Closely

Main ideaHippocrates of ancient Greece taught that illness has natural causes that doctors can watch and study.

About 2,400 years ago, a doctor named Hippocrates lived on the Greek island of Kos. Many people then believed that angry gods sent sickness. A sick person might pray at a temple and hope to be healed.

Hippocrates and his students had a different idea. They taught that illness has . Food, water, weather and the way a person lives can all play a part. That means a doctor can look for the cause.

So Hippocrates watched his patients closely. He wrote down their fever, breathing and sleep day by day. This careful watching is called . Doctors still start the same way today. They look, listen and ask questions.

11.2

The Four Humors

Main ideaFor about 2,000 years, doctors believed in four humors, until science replaced that idea.

Greek doctors tried to explain why people got sick. They taught that the body holds four fluids, called the four . They were blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Health meant the four were in balance.

Doctors in Europe and the Middle East believed this for about 2,000 years. To fix a fever, a doctor might take blood out of a sick person. This was called bleeding. We now know it often made people weaker.

Then scientists looked more closely. They used microscopes and careful tests. They found germs and learned how organs work. The humor idea was replaced. This is how works. When new evidence comes, ideas can change.

A Promise to Patients

Main ideaThe Hippocratic Oath is an old promise that doctors will help the sick and not harm them.

When new doctors finish school, many stand up and make a promise out loud. The oldest famous promise for doctors is the . It is named for Hippocrates. His followers probably wrote it.

An is a serious promise. In the old oath, a doctor promises to help the sick as well as he can. He promises not to hurt them on purpose. He also promises to keep private what patients tell him.

Today many medical schools use newer promises. The words have changed over time. But the main ideas stay the same. Help the patient. Do no harm. Keep the patient’s secrets safe.

Nightingale Cleans Up

Main ideaFlorence Nightingale showed that clean, airy hospitals help sick people get well.

Florence Nightingale was born in 1820 to a rich English family. She wanted to be a nurse. Her family did not like the idea. In those days nursing was not a respected job. Florence did it anyway.

In 1854 she led a group of nurses to the army hospital at Scutari. She saw dirt, bad air and crowded beds. Her team scrubbed floors, washed sheets and cooked healthy food. They opened windows for fresh air. Good , or keeping places clean, became her goal.

Back home, Florence wrote a famous book called Notes on Nursing in 1859. In 1860 she started a school to train nurses in London. She helped make a skilled and respected job.

11.5

Counting Saves Lives

Main ideaNightingale used numbers and charts to prove that clean hospitals save lives.

Florence Nightingale loved math. At Scutari she kept careful records. She wrote down how many soldiers died each month. She also wrote down why they died.

Her numbers showed something surprising. Far more soldiers died from sicknesses like cholera and typhus than from battle wounds. Many of these sicknesses spread through dirty water and crowded rooms. Cleaning up could prevent them.

Long tables of numbers are hard to read quickly. So Florence drew colorful charts. Each wedge showed deaths in one month. The charts made the problem easy to see. This kind of number evidence is called . Her data helped change army hospitals.

11.6

Pasteur Finds Germs

Main ideaLouis Pasteur showed that tiny living germs cause spoiling and many diseases.

In France in the 1860s, a scientist named Louis Pasteur studied why milk and wine go bad. He looked through a microscope. He saw tiny living things too small to see with your eyes. We call them , or microbes.

Pasteur did a famous test. He boiled broth in flasks with long curved necks. Dust and germs from the air got stuck in the curve. The broth stayed fresh. When germs got in, the broth spoiled. Germs came from other germs, not from nothing.

Pasteur found that gentle heating kills many harmful germs in wine. Later, milk was heated the same way. This is called , after him. He also helped make vaccines. His work led to , the idea that germs cause many diseases.

Think it through

Two flasks hold boiled broth. Flask A has a long neck that curves down like a swan's neck. Air gets in, but dust gets stuck in the curve. Flask B has a short, straight neck. Dust falls right in. After a few days, the broth in Flask B is cloudy. Flask A stays clear.

Why did the broth in Flask B spoil, but not the broth in Flask A?

11.9

Testing What Works

Main ideaScientists test medicines on groups of people to find out if they are safe and if they work.

Suppose a company makes a new medicine for headaches. How do we know it works? People might feel better anyway. So scientists run a fair test called a .

In a trial, volunteers are split into two groups by chance. One group gets the new medicine. The other group gets a , a pill with no medicine in it. Often, nobody in the groups knows which one they got. Then scientists compare the two groups.

Trials start small, with a few people, to check safety. Then they grow bigger. Only after many tests can a medicine be sold. In the U.S., the Food and Drug Administration, or FDA, checks the results. This is science testing what works.

Main ideaAcupuncture uses very thin needles, and studies show it may help some kinds of pain for some people.

How Scientists Test

Main ideaScientists test traditional practices with fair tests, and results show some help and some do not.

Scientists test traditional practices the same way they test new medicines. They use fair tests, like clinical trials. One group tries the practice. Another group does not. Then they compare.

Some results are clear. Tai chi can help older adults with balance. Yoga helps with stretching and feeling calm. Acupuncture may help some kinds of pain. Tu Youyou’s work with sweet wormwood led to a malaria medicine used around the world.

Other results are unclear, or show no help. Some remedies may even cause harm. That is why scientists keep testing. Good science asks: does it help, for whom, and is it safe? The answer is not always the same for every practice.

One Team for Your Health

Main ideaTell your doctor about everything you use, so all your helpers can work together safely.

Remember Wei’s grandmother. She sees an acupuncturist and a regular doctor. She tells both of them what the other does. This helps keep her safe. Her helpers act as one .

Some herbs can change how other medicines work. A doctor or can check whether things are safe to take together. They can only help if they know what you use.

You can help your own health, too. Tell a parent or trusted adult when you feel sick. Help your family make a list of any medicines, herbs or supplements. Bring the list to checkups. Asking questions is a smart and brave thing to do.
