Judaism and Christianity in History

Traditions

Walk down a street in Chicago and you may pass a synagogue with Hebrew letters over the door, then a church with a cross on its roof. Both communities read some of the same ancient books. Both look back to Abraham. Yet they tell the story that follows in different ways, and each has a long history of its own.

In this unit you will study Judaism and Christianity the way a historian does. You will read their texts, watch their practices and follow them through exile, empire, reform and life in America. You will describe what adherents believe and do, in their own words, without deciding who is right.

By the end, you will be able to put key events in order, explain terms like covenant, Talmud, creed and Reformation, and compare how the two traditions answer the same questions. You will also find their stories in Chicago today.

The big question

How did Judaism and Christianity grow from shared roots into two traditions with their own texts, practices and histories?

From Abraham to the Rabbis

Judaism
Big questionHow did the Jewish people keep their tradition alive after losing their Temple, their land and, many times, their safety?
The story

A Scroll Leaves a Burning City

In 70 CE, as Roman soldiers surround Jerusalem, one teacher finds a way to save something that cannot burn.

It is the year 70 CE. Roman legions have surrounded Jerusalem for months. Food is running out inside the walls. The great Temple still stands on its hill, but everyone can see the smoke of the siege camps. Inside the city, Jewish groups argue about whether to fight to the end or to seek peace.

Jewish tradition tells a story about a teacher named Yohanan ben Zakkai. According to the Talmud, his students carried him out of the city in a coffin, as if he had died. Outside the walls, he met the Roman commander. He asked for one small thing: a town called Yavneh and its scholars. The request was granted.

Soon after, the Romans burned the Temple. For about a thousand years, Jews had brought offerings to a temple in Jerusalem. Now there was none. Many people wondered how the tradition could go on without its center. Historians debate how much of the coffin story happened as told. But they agree that Yavneh became a center of learning after the war.

At Yavneh and in the schools that followed, rabbis studied the Torah and taught how to live by it. Prayer, study and good deeds took the place of offerings. The scroll could be carried anywhere. This chapter follows the Jewish people from Abraham to the rabbis, and on to Chicago today.

Section 1

The Story the Bible Tells

17.1

Abraham and the Covenant

Main ideaJews, Christians and Muslims all look back to Abraham, and in the Torah God makes a covenant with him and his family.

The Hebrew Bible opens with the creation of the world. Then, in the book of Genesis, the story narrows to one man. Abram lives in Haran with his family. God tells him to leave his home and go to a new land. Abram goes. Later his name is changed to Abraham, which the text explains as father of many nations.

In Jewish tradition, this journey begins a . A covenant is a binding agreement. The Torah says God promises Abraham land, many descendants and a blessing. In return, Abraham and his family will be faithful to God. Jews today often call Abraham our father Abraham. They see the covenant as passing down through Isaac and Jacob.

Christians and Muslims also honor Abraham. Christians read the same stories in their Old Testament. The Qur’an calls Abraham, Ibrahim, a model of faith in one God. For this reason, Judaism, Christianity and Islam are often called the traditions. Each tells the family story in its own way.

17.2

Moses, the Exodus and Sinai

Main ideaThe Torah tells how Moses led the Israelites out of slavery in Egypt and how they received God's teaching at Mount Sinai.

Every spring, Jewish families sit down to a meal called the seder. On the table are flat bread called matzah and bitter herbs. Children ask four questions. The answers retell the book of Exodus. In that story, the Israelites are enslaved in Egypt, and God frees them through a leader named Moses.

After leaving Egypt, the Torah says, the people camp at Mount Sinai. There God gives them the Ten Commandments and the rest of the teaching. The word means teaching or instruction. It names the first five books of the Hebrew Bible: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy.

At the center of Jewish prayer is a line from Deuteronomy called the Shema. It says that God is one. Many Jews say it each morning and each night. For adherents, the Exodus shows a God who frees people from slavery. That is why the seder asks each person to feel as if they had left Egypt themselves.

17.3

Reading the Story as Historians

Main ideaHistorians compare the Bible with archaeology and other records; they describe what people believed without deciding if it is true.

In 1993, archaeologists at Tel Dan in northern Israel found a broken stone with writing on it. It dates to about the 800s BCE. One line mentions the house of David. Many scholars see it as the oldest mention of King David’s family outside the Bible. Finds like this help historians place the Bible’s stories in time.

The Bible says David made Jerusalem his capital. It says his son Solomon built the first there, often dated c. 950 BCE. After Solomon, the kingdom split into Israel in the north and Judah in the south. Assyria conquered Israel in 722 BCE. Judah lasted longer.

Historians ask different questions from believers. For the early stories of Abraham and Moses, there is little evidence outside the Bible, and scholars disagree about them. A historian can say what the Israelites believed and practiced. A historian cannot say whether God spoke at Sinai. That question belongs to each reader’s own tradition.

Exile, Return and Loss

17.4

Exile in Babylon

Main ideaIn 586 BCE Babylon destroyed the first Temple and sent many Jews into exile, where they learned to keep their faith far from home.

In 586 BCE, the army of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon burned Jerusalem. The first Temple was destroyed. Many leaders and families were marched to Babylon, in what is now Iraq. This time is called the , a forced life away from home.

A psalm remembers the sadness of that time. The exiles sit by the rivers of Babylon and weep for Zion, another name for Jerusalem. Their captors ask them to sing. They ask how they can sing the Lord’s song in a strange land. Jews still recite lines from this psalm, and some say them before the blessings after meals on weekdays.

Yet the exile also became a time of new strength. Without the Temple, people gathered to remember, pray and study. Many scholars think much of the Hebrew Bible was collected and edited in this period. Communities learned that faith could travel. That lesson would matter again and again.

17.5

The Second Temple Rises and Falls

Main ideaJews rebuilt the Temple after the exile, but Rome destroyed this second Temple in 70 CE.

In 539 BCE, Cyrus of Persia conquered Babylon. The Bible says he let the exiles go home. Some returned and rebuilt a smaller Temple, finished c. 516 BCE. This began the period. Not everyone went back. A large Jewish community stayed in Babylon for many centuries.

Later, Greek kings ruled the land. In the 160s BCE, a family called the Maccabees led a revolt. They took back and rededicated the Temple. The holiday of Hanukkah remembers this. Later still, Rome took control in 63 BCE. King Herod, who ruled under Rome, rebuilt the Temple on a grand scale.

In 66 CE, Jews in Judea revolted against Rome. In 70 CE, the Romans burned Jerusalem and destroyed the Temple. Only part of an outer retaining wall stands today. It is called the Western Wall. Jews from around the world come there to pray and tuck written prayers between its stones.

17.6

The Rabbis and Their Books

Main ideaAfter 70 CE, rabbis made study, prayer and good deeds the center of Jewish life, and wrote the Mishnah and the Talmud.

Open a page of the Talmud and you will see a block of text in the middle. Around it, in smaller letters, are notes by teachers from later centuries. The page looks like a conversation across time. That is exactly what it is. Students often study it in pairs, arguing out loud.

A is a Jewish teacher of the Torah. In Jewish tradition, God gave Moses a written Torah and also an oral teaching that explains it. Around 200 CE, Rabbi Judah the Prince collected the oral teaching into a book called the . It covers prayer, farming, holidays, marriage, courts and more.

Later rabbis discussed the Mishnah for centuries. Their debates became the . The Babylonian Talmud, completed c. 500 to 600 CE, is the one studied most. It often records the losing side of an argument as well as the winning one. Many Jews see study itself as a form of worship.

A Week and a Life

17.7

Shabbat and the Synagogue

Main ideaShabbat is a weekly day of rest, and the synagogue is where Jews gather to pray, read Torah and study.

On Friday just before sunset, candles are lit in many Jewish homes. A blessing is said over wine and two braided loaves called challah. , the Sabbath, has begun. It lasts until dark on Saturday night. The Torah commands a day of rest, and many Jews set aside work, phones and errands.

On Shabbat morning, many go to a , a Jewish house of prayer, study and gathering. At the front is the ark, a cabinet holding Torah scrolls. The scroll is handwritten in Hebrew on parchment. Each week a portion is read aloud, so the whole Torah is read in a yearly cycle.

Some Jews also keep kosher, eating by rules found in the Torah. For example, pork and shellfish are not eaten, and meat and milk are kept apart. Practice varies widely. Some Jews keep every rule. Others keep a few customs that matter to their family.

17.8

Holidays and the Life Cycle

Main ideaJewish holidays follow a lunar calendar, and rites mark birth, coming of age, marriage and death.

In the fall, a ram’s horn called a shofar is blown in the synagogue. This is , the Jewish New Year. Ten days later comes Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement. Many adults fast and pray to ask forgiveness. The Jewish calendar follows the moon but adds a month in some years, so holidays stay in their seasons.

Other holidays retell stories. Passover remembers the Exodus. Sukkot recalls the years in the desert, and families eat in a hut with a leafy roof. Hanukkah recalls the Maccabees. Shavuot remembers the giving of the Torah at Sinai.

Life has its rites too. Baby boys are often circumcised at eight days old, as the Torah says Abraham did. At 13, a boy becomes a , a son of the commandment. In many communities a girl becomes a bat mitzvah at 12 or 13. Weddings take place under a canopy. After a death, mourners sit shiva, a week of visits and comfort.

A People Around the World

17.9

Communities and Movements

Main ideaJews live in many lands with different customs, and today most belong to Orthodox, Conservative, Reform or other movements, or to none.

After 70 CE, Jewish life spread far from Jerusalem. This spread is called the . Ashkenazi Jews lived in central and eastern Europe. Sephardic Jews trace their roots to Spain and Portugal. Mizrahi Jews lived across the Middle East and North Africa. Each group kept the Torah, with its own songs, foods and customs.

In 1654, a small group of Jews arrived in New Amsterdam, later New York. Millions more came to America from Europe in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Many settled in Chicago, first on the West Side. Today Jewish communities in Chicago and its suburbs number in the hundreds of thousands.

Jews today practice in different ways. Jews hold that the Torah and its laws should be kept as tradition has taught them. Reform Judaism began in Germany in the early 1800s and allows more change. Conservative Judaism grew in America as a middle path. Many Jews are secular, feeling Jewish by family and culture.

17.10

Antisemitism and the Holocaust

Main ideaJews have faced hatred called antisemitism for centuries; in the Holocaust, Nazi Germany murdered about six million Jews.

is hatred of or prejudice against Jews. It has taken many forms. In medieval Europe, Jews were blamed for plagues and expelled from England in 1290 and Spain in 1492. In the Russian Empire, violent attacks called pogroms drove many families to leave for America.

In 1933, the Nazi Party took power in Germany. It passed laws that stripped Jews of their rights. In November 1938, mobs burned synagogues across Germany in the attack known as Kristallnacht. During World War II, the Nazis and their allies built camps to murder Jews. About six million Jews were killed. This genocide is called the , or Shoah in Hebrew.

Survivors worked to make sure the world remembers. Many settled in the Chicago area. In 2009, the Illinois Holocaust Museum opened in Skokie, a town where many survivors lived. Illinois law requires public schools to teach about the Holocaust. Many Jews say never again, a promise to remember and to stand against hatred.

From Jesus to the Reformation and Beyond

Christianity
Big questionHow did a small movement in Roman Judea become the world's largest religion, and why did it split into so many churches?
The story

Two Letters from Two Jails

Almost two thousand years apart, two prisoners write letters that are read aloud to crowds.

Around 60 CE, a man named Paul sits in a rented house in Rome with a Roman soldier guarding him. The book of Acts says he stayed there two years, welcoming everyone who came. Paul was a Jewish teacher who had come to believe that Jesus of Nazareth was the Messiah. He wrote letters to small groups of believers in cities around the Mediterranean.

His letters were not read quietly by one person. They were read aloud when believers gathered, often in someone's home. In one letter, to the believers at Philippi, the writer says his chains have helped spread his message. Scholars debate where each letter was written. But Christians have read these letters aloud in worship ever since.

In April 1963, another prisoner sat in a jail cell in Birmingham, Alabama. He was Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., a Baptist minister. He had been arrested for leading a peaceful march against segregation. Local clergy had called the protests unwise and untimely. King answered them in a long letter, written at first in the margins of a newspaper.

King's letter was soon printed and read aloud in churches and meetings across the country. He pointed to Paul, who carried his message from town to town. Between these two jails lie almost two thousand years of Christian history. This chapter follows that history from Judea to Chicago.

Jesus and the First Believers

18.1

Judea Under Rome

Main ideaJesus lived in Judea and Galilee while the Roman Empire ruled, a time when many Jews hoped for a Messiah.

Roman coins from the first century show the face of the emperor. People in Judea paid taxes with coins like these. Rome had taken control of the region in 63 BCE. It ruled through local kings, such as Herod, and through Roman governors, such as Pontius Pilate, who governed from about 26 to 36 CE.

Many Jews at this time hoped for a , a word meaning anointed one. In Jewish tradition, the Messiah would be a leader from King David’s family who would bring justice and peace. Jewish groups disagreed about how and when that would happen. Some waited and prayed. Some wanted to fight Rome.

Into this world, around 4 BCE, Jesus was born. He grew up in the village of Nazareth in Galilee. He was Jewish. He went to the synagogue, kept the festivals and taught from the Hebrew scriptures. Christians later came to believe that he was the Messiah. In Greek, Messiah is Christos, which gives us the word Christ.

18.2

Jesus in the Gospels

Main ideaFour Gospels tell Jesus' story; Christians believe he died on a cross and rose from the dead.

The are four books in the New Testament: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. Gospel means good news. They were written in Greek, most likely between c. 65 and 100 CE. Each tells Jesus’ story in its own way. Christians read them aloud in worship every week.

The Gospels say Jesus taught in parables, short stories with a lesson. In the parable of the Good Samaritan, a stranger from a rival group helps a hurt traveler whom others walked past. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus blesses the poor, the grieving and the peacemakers. He tells his followers to love even their enemies.

The Gospels say that Jesus was crucified by Roman order in Jerusalem, around 30 CE. Christians believe that he rose from the dead three days later. This is called the . Christians celebrate it at Easter. Jews and Muslims honor Jesus in different ways, or not as Messiah, and do not share this belief about him.

Paul and the Early Church

Main ideaPaul's travels and letters helped spread the faith to non-Jews across the Roman world.

After Jesus’ death, his followers kept meeting in Jerusalem. At first they were Jews who believed Jesus was the Messiah. The book of Acts tells how the message spread. A key figure was Paul, who had first opposed the believers. Acts says he changed after a vision of Jesus on the road to Damascus.

Paul traveled by road and by ship to cities like Corinth, Ephesus and Philippi. He taught that non-Jews, called Gentiles, could join without first becoming Jewish. His letters, called , are the oldest writings in the New Testament. They date to c. 50 to 60 CE.

The early believers came to be called the , from a Greek word for an assembly. They met in homes, shared a meal and baptized new members with water. Paul wrote that differences of people and status should not divide them. By 100 CE, small churches existed in many cities of the empire.

From Persecution to Power

18.4

Martyrs, Constantine and the Creed

Main ideaChristians were at times persecuted until Constantine allowed their faith in 313 CE; church councils then wrote creeds.

In Rome, you can still walk through catacombs, underground burial tunnels. Early Christians painted pictures on the walls, such as a shepherd carrying a sheep. At times Roman rulers punished Christians for refusing to worship the emperor and the gods. Those who died for their faith are honored as .

Then things changed. In 312 CE, the general Constantine won a battle for control of Rome. He later said he had seen a sign of Christ before it. In 313 CE, Constantine and his co-emperor issued the Edict of Milan. It allowed Christians to worship freely. By 380 CE, a later emperor made Christianity the empire’s official faith.

Christians disagreed about how to describe Jesus and God. In 325 CE, bishops met at Nicaea. They wrote a , a short statement of belief. The Nicene Creed says Christians believe in one God: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. This belief is called the Trinity. Many churches still say this creed every Sunday.

18.5

East, West and the Middle Ages

Main ideaIn 1054 the church split into Catholic and Orthodox, while monasteries, cathedrals and universities shaped medieval Europe.

Over time, the church in the east, centered on Constantinople, and the church in the west, centered on Rome, grew apart. They spoke different languages, Greek and Latin. They disagreed about the power of the , the bishop of Rome. In 1054, leaders of the two sides excommunicated each other. This is often called the Great Schism.

Today the western church is the Roman Catholic Church, led by the pope. The eastern churches are the Eastern churches, led by patriarchs and bishops. Orthodox worship uses icons, painted images of Christ and the saints. In 1965, a pope and a patriarch lifted the old excommunications.

In medieval Europe, monasteries were communities of monks or nuns who lived by a rule of prayer and work. Many followed the Rule of Saint Benedict, from c. 530 CE. Monks copied books by hand. Towns built great cathedrals. Church schools grew into the first universities, such as Bologna and Paris.

Section 3

Reformation and Response

18.6

Luther and the Printing Press

Main ideaIn 1517 Martin Luther challenged church practices, and the printing press spread his ideas fast, starting the Protestant Reformation.

Around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg in Germany built a printing press with movable metal letters. Books that once took months to copy by hand could now be printed by the hundreds. Pamphlets could spread across a country in weeks. This new tool changed how ideas moved.

In 1517, a monk and teacher named Martin Luther wrote the . They criticized the sale of indulgences, papers said to reduce punishment for sin. Luther taught that people are saved by God’s grace through faith, not by buying anything. He also taught that the Bible was the highest authority. He translated the New Testament into German so ordinary people could read it.

Printers copied Luther’s writings and spread them fast. The church leadership condemned his ideas, and he was excommunicated in 1521. His followers came to be called , from their protest. The movement is called the Reformation, an effort to reform, or change, the church.

Think it through
Old Idea, New Tool

In 1517, Luther writes 95 arguments in Latin for scholars. Within weeks, printers turn them into German and sell copies in many towns. Tradespeople who have never met Luther are soon arguing about his ideas.

How did the printing press change what happened to Luther's ideas? What tool plays a similar role today?

18.7

Many Churches, Three Families

Main ideaAfter Luther came Calvin, other Protestant churches and a Catholic reform, leaving three large Christian families today.

Other reformers followed Luther. John Calvin led the church in Geneva, Switzerland, from the 1540s. His ideas shaped Presbyterian and Reformed churches. In England, King Henry VIII broke with the pope in 1534, and the Church of England formed. Later came Baptists, Methodists and many more , separate church groups.

The Catholic Church also reformed itself. The Council of Trent met from 1545 to 1563. It restated Catholic teaching and set new rules for training priests. New orders, such as the Jesuits, founded schools and sent missionaries around the world. The Reformation years also brought religious wars, and many people died.

Today, Christianity is often described in three large families. Catholics are the largest. Protestants include thousands of denominations. The Orthodox churches are strong in Eastern Europe, Greece and Russia. All three share the Bible, and the belief that Jesus is the Christ, but they differ on leadership, worship and some beliefs.

Section 4

Christianity in America and Chicago

18.8

The Americas and the Black Church

Main ideaChristianity came to the Americas with European colonizers, and enslaved Africans and their descendants built churches of their own.

From 1492 on, Spanish, Portuguese and French explorers brought Catholic missionaries to the Americas. English settlers brought Protestant churches. Some missionaries defended Native peoples, but conquest and forced conversion caused great suffering. Many Native Americans today are Christian, and many also keep their own traditions.

Enslaved Africans were brought to the Americas by force. Many were taught a Christianity that told them to obey. They heard their own message in the Bible too, especially in the story of the Exodus. They sang , songs of faith and freedom, like Go Down, Moses. Some worshiped in secret gatherings.

Free Black Christians founded their own churches. In Philadelphia, Richard Allen led the founding of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, organized in 1816. The became a center of worship, schooling and community life. It gave leaders and meeting places to the fight against slavery and, later, segregation.

Think it through
One Story, Two Hearers

A slaveholder reads the Bible and chooses verses about servants obeying. An enslaved woman hears the same Bible and remembers Moses telling Pharaoh, let my people go. That night she sings about it in the fields.

How can two people read the same book so differently? Why might the Exodus story matter so much to enslaved people?

18.9

Civil Rights and Chicago's Churches

Main ideaBlack churches helped lead the civil rights movement, and Chicago today holds churches of every Christian family.

In the 1950s and 1960s, the worked to end segregation and win voting rights. Many of its meetings were held in Black churches. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. was a Baptist minister. In his Letter from Birmingham Jail, he quoted the prophets and pointed to Paul. He argued that unjust laws must be resisted peacefully.

Chicago has its own church history. Thomas Dorsey, called the father of gospel music, led a choir at Pilgrim Baptist Church on the South Side from the 1930s. spread from Chicago churches across the country. King himself lived on Chicago’s West Side in 1966 to protest unfair housing.

Today Chicago holds churches of every family. Holy Name Cathedral is the seat of the Catholic archbishop. There are Greek, Serbian and Ukrainian Orthodox churches. There are Lutheran, Methodist, Baptist and Pentecostal churches, and Mass in Spanish, Polish and many other languages. Many Chicagoans also belong to other faiths or to none.
