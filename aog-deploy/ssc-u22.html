U.S. History: Civil Rights to Today

1954–Present

In September 1955 a Chicago mother opened her murdered son's casket and made the country look. In November 2008 a crowd of more than two hundred thousand stood in Grant Park to watch a senator from Illinois become president. Between those two nights lie the years this unit covers: the civil rights movement and the movements it inspired, the collapse of trust after Vietnam and Watergate, the oil shocks, the conservative turn, the end of the Cold War, the internet, September 11, the crash of 2008, a decade of polarization and a pandemic. Much of it happened on the streets and in the courtrooms of Chicago, and some of it happened to your own family.

This is the hardest history in the course, for two reasons. The first is that it is not finished: the arguments about what these events meant are still going on, and the people making them are still alive. The second is that the evidence is strange. Some of it is on tape in the National Archives; some of it is a deleted post or a text on a phone that no longer turns on. To study this era you have to learn what historians do when the records are incomplete, the outcomes unknown and the writer is also a witness.

By the end of the unit you should be able to explain how a movement with no army forced a nation to change its laws, how each shock since 1969 changed what Americans expected from their government, and how to weigh a claim about the recent past against the evidence that exists now and the evidence still to come. You should also be able to say, with reasons, which of these events changed the country and which only seemed to at the time.

The big question

From the civil rights movement to the present, which events actually changed the United States, and how can we judge a past whose evidence is still arriving and whose witnesses are still arguing?

The story

The Open Casket

In September 1955 a Chicago mother made one decision about her son's funeral, and the whole country was forced to look.

Mamie Till-Mobley lived on the South Side of Chicago and worked as a clerk for the Air Force. In August 1955 she put her only child, fourteen-year-old Emmett Till, on the train south to visit relatives near Money, Mississippi. Emmett was a city kid who liked jokes and sharp clothes. His mother warned him that the rules were different in the Delta, that a Black boy had to be careful around white people. He promised he would be.

A few days later Emmett went into a small country store and, by the accounts that survive, said something or whistled at the white woman behind the counter. In the early hours of August 28, her husband Roy Bryant and his half-brother J. W. Milam took Emmett from his great-uncle's house at gunpoint. Three days later his body was pulled from the Tallahatchie River, beaten beyond recognition, a heavy cotton-gin fan tied to his neck with barbed wire. Mississippi officials wanted him buried fast, in the ground, out of sight.

Mamie Till-Mobley refused. She demanded the body be shipped home to Chicago. When the sealed box arrived at the rail station, she insisted it be opened. Then she made the decision the world remembers. There would be no closed lid and no makeup. She said she wanted the world to see what they had done to her boy. For four days in September, tens of thousands of Chicagoans filed past the glass-covered casket at Roberts Temple Church of God in Christ on State Street. Jet, a Black magazine published in Chicago, printed the photograph. It traveled everywhere.

Later that month an all-white jury in Sumner, Mississippi, took about an hour to find Bryant and Milam not guilty. The next year, safe from a second trial, the two men sold their confession to a magazine for money. No one was ever punished. But something had shifted. In Montgomery, Alabama, a seamstress named Rosa Parks later said she was thinking of Emmett Till when she refused to give up her bus seat that December. A generation of Black teenagers who saw the photo in Jet would fill the sit-ins and the Freedom Rides five years later.

This chapter is about that generation and the movement they built. It began in courtrooms and church basements, moved into buses, lunch counters and jail cells, and ended in the laws of the United States. It also came north, to Chicago, where it met a different kind of wall. Through all of it runs the question Mamie Till-Mobley answered in 1955: what happens when people refuse to let injustice stay hidden?

50.1

Brown v. Board of Education

Main ideaIn 1954 the Supreme Court ruled that racially segregated public schools are unconstitutional, overturning the separate-but-equal rule from 1896.

Every school morning in Topeka, Kansas, a girl named Linda Brown walked past a white elementary school a few blocks from her house to catch a bus to a Black school across town. In 1950 her father, Oliver Brown, tried to enroll her in the closer school and was turned away. The Brown family joined a lawsuit organized by the NAACP, the nation’s oldest civil rights organization. Similar cases from South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware and Washington, D.C., were bundled with it. Together they became Brown v. Board of Education.

The law they were fighting went back to 1896. In Plessy v. Ferguson the Supreme Court had ruled that a state could require separate railroad cars for Black and white passengers as long as the cars were roughly equal. That decision became the legal backbone of , the system of separating people by race in schools, parks, buses, restrooms and hospitals across the South. In practice the facilities were rarely equal. Black schools often had used textbooks, no buses and half the money per student.

The NAACP’s lead lawyer, Thurgood Marshall, argued that even if the buildings were identical, forced separation itself did harm. His team offered evidence from social scientists, including a study in which many Black children chose a white doll over a Black one when asked which was nicer. On May 17, 1954, Chief Justice Earl Warren read a decision, all nine justices agreeing. Separate educational facilities, he wrote, are inherently unequal. Segregated schools violated the Fourteenth Amendment’s promise of equal protection.

A decision is not the same as a change. The next year the Court ordered schools to desegregate with all deliberate speed, a vague phrase that many Southern districts read as permission to wait. Some states closed schools rather than mix them. In 1957 the governor of Arkansas used the National Guard to block nine Black students from Little Rock Central High School, and President Eisenhower sent Army paratroopers to walk them in. Ten years after Brown, most Black children in the Deep South still attended all-Black schools. The ruling gave the movement a legal foundation. People would have to build on it.

50.2

Emmett Till and Chicago

Main ideaThe murder of a Chicago teenager in Mississippi in 1955, and his mother's decision to show it, turned a private crime into a national reckoning.

By 1955 Chicago’s South Side was home to hundreds of thousands of Black families who had come north in the Great Migration. Many still had relatives in Mississippi and Arkansas and sent their children south each summer. That is why Emmett Till, fourteen years old and raised on Chicago’s streets, was in the Mississippi Delta in August 1955. The rules there were enforced not only by law but by the constant threat of violence. A Black person who crossed a line could be beaten or killed, and the killers almost never faced a court.

That threat was called : murder by a mob or a group, outside the law, meant to terrorize a whole community. Between the 1880s and the 1950s thousands of Black Americans were lynched across the South, often in public, sometimes with photographs sold as postcards. Congress debated anti-lynching bills for decades and Southern senators blocked every one. Emmett Till’s murder fit an old pattern. What broke the pattern was what his mother did next.

Mamie Till-Mobley’s open casket in Chicago and the photographs in Jet magazine reached Black homes across the country. Many people later said that image was the moment they decided to act. The trial in Mississippi added fuel. Emmett’s great-uncle, Mose Wright, a sharecropper in his sixties, stood up in a Southern courtroom, pointed at the two white defendants and identified them as the men who took the boy. It was an act of enormous courage. The jury freed the defendants anyway.

The case did not change Mississippi law. It changed what people were willing to accept. Mamie Till-Mobley spent the rest of her life speaking about her son and became a Chicago public school teacher. In 2022 Congress finally made lynching a federal crime, and named the law after Emmett Till. Roberts Temple church still stands on State Street, and the Till home on South St. Lawrence Avenue is now a Chicago landmark. How we know this story is itself part of it: the photographs, the trial transcript, the magazine, and the words of the witnesses who dared to speak.

50.3

Montgomery Walks

Main ideaThe 1955 Montgomery bus boycott showed that a whole Black community, organized and patient, could defeat segregation with its feet and its dollars.

On Thursday, December 1, 1955, Rosa Parks finished her shift as a seamstress at a Montgomery, Alabama, department store and boarded a bus home. City law reserved the front rows for white riders, and the driver could order Black riders to give up seats further back when the white section filled. When the driver told Parks to move, she said no. She was arrested. Parks was not simply tired, as the story is often told. She was a longtime secretary of the local NAACP and had trained in organizing that summer.

That night Jo Ann Robinson, a college English professor who led the Women’s Political Council, stayed up copying a flyer on a mimeograph machine. It called on Black riders to stay off the buses on Monday, the day of Parks’s trial. Ministers announced it from every pulpit on Sunday. On Monday the buses ran almost empty. That evening thousands packed a church meeting and chose a twenty-six-year-old new pastor, Martin Luther King Jr., to lead a new organization, the Montgomery Improvement Association. They voted to keep the going.

The boycott lasted 381 days. Black residents were about three quarters of the bus company’s riders, so their absence emptied its cash box. People walked miles in every weather. Churches ran a carpool of about three hundred cars. Black taxi drivers charged bus fare. White employers who supported segregation found themselves driving their maids to work. The city fought back: it fined the carpool drivers, indicted King and dozens of others under an old anti-boycott law, and someone bombed King’s house with his wife and baby inside.

Victory came from two directions at once. The economic pressure hurt, but the legal blow came from a separate lawsuit, Browder v. Gayle, filed by four Black women riders. In November 1956 the Supreme Court affirmed that bus segregation in Montgomery was unconstitutional. On December 21, 1956, Black riders returned and sat where they chose. The lessons were clear to organizers everywhere: mass participation, careful planning, economic pressure, a court case running alongside, and a strict commitment to even under attack.

50.4

Sitting In

Main ideaBeginning in 1960, Black college students used sit-ins at segregated lunch counters to take the movement into their own hands.

On February 1, 1960, four freshmen from North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College walked into the Woolworth’s store in Greensboro, bought some school supplies, and then sat down at the lunch counter, which served only white customers. They asked for coffee. They were refused, and they stayed until closing. The next day they came back with more students. Within a week hundreds were sitting in. Within two months had spread to more than fifty cities across the South.

The tactic was simple and hard. Sit quietly, dress well, bring a book, ask to be served. Do not hit back when someone pours ketchup on your head or burns you with a cigarette. Be arrested politely and fill the jails. In Nashville, a minister named James Lawson had spent months training students, including Diane Nash and John Lewis, in the discipline of nonviolent : confronting an unjust rule in person instead of waiting for a court. They practiced being shoved and insulted until they could take it without flinching.

The sit-ins mattered because of who led them. The students were not waiting for the NAACP’s lawyers or the ministers of Montgomery. In April 1960 Ella Baker, a veteran organizer who distrusted leader-centered movements, helped them form their own group, the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, known as SNCC. It would send young organizers into the most dangerous counties of Mississippi and Alabama for the rest of the decade.

The results came fast by the movement’s standards. Woolworth’s in Greensboro served its first Black customers at the counter in July 1960. Nashville’s downtown stores desegregated that spring after the mayor, confronted on the courthouse steps by Diane Nash, admitted in public that segregation was wrong. Sit-ins did not end all segregation, but they proved a lesson: a business that depends on customers cannot afford a daily scene at its counter, and young people could create that scene anywhere.

50.5

The Freedom Rides

Main ideaIn 1961 integrated groups rode buses into the Deep South to test a federal ruling, and the violence they met forced the government to enforce it.

The Supreme Court had already ruled that segregation in bus stations serving interstate travel was illegal. The problem was that no one in the South enforced it. In May 1961 the Congress of Racial Equality, or CORE, led by James Farmer, decided to test the ruling. Thirteen riders, Black and white together, boarded two buses in Washington, D.C., bound for New Orleans. They planned to use the whites-only waiting rooms and restrooms at each stop. They expected trouble and got it.

On May 14, Mother’s Day, one bus was attacked outside Anniston, Alabama. A mob slashed the tires and, when the bus stopped, threw a firebomb through a window. The riders escaped with smoke in their lungs just before the bus exploded into flames. The other bus reached Birmingham, where a mob armed with pipes and chains beat the riders while police stayed away. A photograph of the burning bus went around the world. CORE, its riders in the hospital, decided to stop.

The Nashville students refused to let the ride end. Diane Nash argued that if violence could stop a Freedom Ride, the movement was finished. New riders, John Lewis among them, went to Birmingham and then to Montgomery, where another mob beat them on the station platform. Attorney General Robert Kennedy, the president’s brother, sent federal marshals. When the riders reached Jackson, Mississippi, they were arrested and sent to the state penitentiary. More kept coming all summer. More than three hundred people served time in Mississippi prisons for the crime of sitting in the wrong waiting room.

The Freedom Rides won on a technical point that changed daily life. In September 1961, under pressure from the Kennedy administration, the Interstate Commerce Commission ordered the signs down in every interstate bus and train station. The rides also taught the movement something about power. Segregationist violence, when captured on camera, embarrassed the United States in front of the world during the Cold War. That embarrassment could move a president who would rather not be moved.

50.6

Birmingham, 1963

Main ideaIn Birmingham, the movement chose the most segregated city in America and let the nation watch police attack children, which pushed a president to act.

By early 1963 King’s organization, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, needed a win. A long campaign in Albany, Georgia, had fizzled because the police chief there arrested protesters calmly and out of sight. Birmingham, Alabama, was chosen because it was the opposite. Its public safety commissioner, Eugene Connor, known as Bull, was a hot-tempered segregationist. The city had seen so many bombings of Black homes and churches that people called it Bombingham. Organizers believed that if Connor reacted the way he always did, the cameras would show the country what segregation really was.

The campaign began in April with marches and sit-ins downtown, and arrests followed. King himself was jailed on Good Friday. In his cell he wrote a long letter on scraps of newspaper and toilet paper, answering white clergymen who had called the protests unwise and untimely. He argued that people who have waited more than three hundred years are told to wait too easily by those who have never had to. The Letter from Birmingham Jail became the movement’s most famous defense of nonviolent direct action and of breaking unjust laws openly and lovingly, ready to accept the penalty.

By May, adult volunteers were running out. Organizer James Bevel proposed sending students. On May 2 more than a thousand children, some as young as six, marched out of the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church and were arrested. The next day Connor turned high-pressure fire hoses on them, strong enough to strip bark from trees, and released police dogs. The photographs and television footage shocked the country. Within days Birmingham’s business leaders agreed to desegregate downtown stores and hire Black workers.

President Kennedy had avoided a civil rights bill for two years. On June 11, 1963, he went on national television and called civil rights a moral issue as old as the Scriptures and as clear as the Constitution. He asked Congress for a law ending segregation in public places. Hours later, in Jackson, Mississippi, NAACP leader Medgar Evers was shot dead in his driveway. In September a bomb at the Sixteenth Street Baptist Church killed four girls at Sunday school. Birmingham had forced the question. It had not answered the violence.

50.7

The March on Washington

Main ideaOn August 28, 1963, about a quarter of a million people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial to demand jobs and freedom, and made the movement a national majority cause.

The idea was old. In 1941 A. Philip Randolph, leader of the Black railroad porters’ union, had threatened a march on Washington to demand defense jobs, and President Roosevelt had opened war plants to Black workers to head it off. In 1963 Randolph, now in his seventies, revived the plan, and the organizing fell to Bayard Rustin, a brilliant pacifist strategist who had been kept in the background because he was gay and a former Communist. Rustin had eight weeks to move a small city’s worth of people into the capital and out again in one day.

The full name was the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. The demands were economic as well as legal: a two-dollar minimum wage, a federal jobs program, an end to discrimination in hiring, and passage of Kennedy’s civil rights bill. On August 28 roughly 250,000 people arrived by train, bus and car, about a quarter of them white. Labor unions, churches and civil rights groups marched together. Kennedy, who had worried about violence, watched on television as the crowd stayed orderly and left the Mall spotless.

The speeches ran all afternoon. John Lewis, the twenty-three-year-old chairman of SNCC, had to soften a draft that called Kennedy’s bill too little, too late. Gospel singer Mahalia Jackson, who lived in Chicago, sang. Then King, the last speaker, set aside his prepared text partway through and spoke of a dream in which children would be judged by their character and not their color. The speech, delivered in the shadow of the Lincoln statue on the hundredth anniversary of the Emancipation Proclamation, became the best known words of the century.

Historians point out what the memory leaves out. The day was organized by a union leader and a gay pacifist, not only ministers. No woman gave a major speech, though women like Daisy Bates and Rosa Parks were on the platform. The jobs demands were mostly not met. And within three weeks the Birmingham church bombing showed that words at the Lincoln Memorial had not reached Alabama. Still, the march did what marches are for. It showed the country and Congress that the movement was large, disciplined and impossible to ignore.

Freedom Summer

Main ideaIn 1964 civil rights workers brought hundreds of volunteers into Mississippi to register voters and open schools, at the cost of three lives and dozens of bombings.

In 1964 Mississippi had the lowest share of Black registered voters in the country, about seven percent of those eligible. The tools of exclusion were the , in which a county clerk could ask a Black applicant to explain any section of the state constitution and fail them for any reason, the , and plain fear. A sharecropper who tried to register might be fired, evicted or shot at. SNCC organizer Bob Moses had spent three years in the state and had watched local people beaten and killed for trying.

Moses and the coalition of groups called COFO made a hard calculation. If Black Mississippians died, the country did not notice. If white college students from the North were hurt, it would. So Freedom Summer recruited about a thousand volunteers, most of them white students, to spend the summer registering voters and teaching in Freedom Schools. On June 21, the first day, three workers vanished near Philadelphia, Mississippi: James Chaney, a Black Mississippian, and Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, two white New Yorkers. Their bodies were found in an earthen dam in August. Local police and Klansmen had murdered them.

The summer went on. Volunteers lived with Black families who risked everything to host them. More than thirty Black churches were bombed or burned. Freedom Schools taught Black history and the Constitution to thousands of children who had never seen either in a Mississippi classroom. Registration gains were small because the clerks did not budge, but the project built something else: the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, an open party to challenge the all-white official Democrats.

In August the MFDP sent a delegation to the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City and asked to be seated in place of the segregationists. Fannie Lou Hamer, a former sharecropper who had been beaten in jail for trying to register, testified on live television about what had been done to her. President Johnson, afraid of losing the South, cut in with a press conference to knock her off the air, but the networks replayed her that night. The party offered the MFDP two seats without votes. They refused. Many young activists left Atlantic City convinced that appeals to the conscience of the powerful were not enough.

Selma and the Bridge

Main ideaThe 1965 Selma campaign, and the attack on marchers at the Edmund Pettus Bridge, produced the Voting Rights Act within five months.

Selma, Alabama, sat in Dallas County, where Black residents were about half the population and about two percent of the registered voters. The county registrar opened two days a month and failed almost every Black applicant. In January 1965 King and the SCLC joined SNCC organizers already in town for a campaign aimed squarely at the vote. Marchers lined up at the courthouse and were arrested by the hundreds. In February, in nearby Marion, a state trooper shot a young man named Jimmie Lee Jackson as he tried to shield his mother. He died days later.

Activists planned a march from Selma to the state capital in Montgomery, fifty-four miles, to protest the killing. On Sunday, March 7, about six hundred marchers led by John Lewis and Hosea Williams walked out of Selma and over the Edmund Pettus Bridge, named for a Confederate general and Klan leader. On the far side waited state troopers and county deputies on horseback. They gave the marchers two minutes, then attacked with clubs and tear gas. Lewis’s skull was fractured. Television networks interrupted their Sunday evening programs to show the footage. The day became known as Bloody Sunday.

Hundreds of clergy and ordinary citizens flew to Selma in response. A white minister from Boston, James Reeb, was beaten to death on a Selma street. On March 15 President Lyndon Johnson addressed a joint session of Congress, called the events at Selma a turning point like Lexington and Concord, and told the lawmakers that the cause of Black Americans must be everyone’s cause. Then he closed with the movement’s own words, and we shall overcome. Watching on television, King wept. On March 21, under federal protection, the march finally set out. Five days later about twenty-five thousand people reached the capitol in Montgomery.

The night the march ended, Klansmen shot and killed Viola Liuzzo, a Detroit mother of five who had driven marchers home. The bill moved anyway. On August 6, 1965, Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act. Selma showed the pattern of the whole movement in one town: local people who had been organizing for years, national leaders who brought attention, violence that the nation could not look away from, and a law that followed. It also showed the cost, paid mostly by people whose names are on no monument.

Two Laws That Changed the Country

Main ideaThe Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 put the federal government behind equal treatment and the ballot, and the numbers changed fast.

After Kennedy’s assassination in November 1963, Lyndon Johnson, a Texan who had spent decades in Congress, took up the civil rights bill as his own. Southern senators launched a , endless speeches meant to keep the bill from ever coming to a vote, that ran for more than two months. Johnson and Senate leaders of both parties gathered the two-thirds needed to shut off debate. The Civil Rights Act was signed on July 2, 1964. It banned segregation in restaurants, hotels, theaters and other public places, outlawed discrimination in hiring based on race, color, religion, sex or national origin, and let the government cut off funds to schools that stayed segregated.

The word sex was added to the employment section by a Virginia congressman who opposed the bill and may have hoped to sink it. It stayed. The new law’s job provisions, enforced by a new Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, would become the main legal weapon of the women’s movement over the next decade. A law written for one struggle turned out to be a tool for many.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 attacked the machinery of exclusion directly. It banned literacy tests in states with a history of discrimination, sent federal examiners to register voters where local clerks refused, and required those states to get federal approval, called , before changing any voting rule. The effect was immediate. In Mississippi, Black registration rose from about seven percent of eligible adults in 1964 to nearly sixty percent by 1968. Across the South, Black sheriffs, mayors and legislators began to win office for the first time since the 1870s.

Both laws have had long afterlives. Courts have spent decades deciding what the Civil Rights Act’s ban on discrimination covers. In 2013 the Supreme Court, in Shelby County v. Holder, struck down the formula that decided which states needed preclearance, saying it was based on outdated data. Several states changed voting rules soon after. Whether those changes protect elections or restrict voters is argued today along party lines. What the evidence of 1965 shows plainly is that when the federal government enforced the right to vote, millions of citizens used it.

King Comes to Chicago

Main ideaIn 1966 the movement turned north to Chicago, where segregation was enforced not by law but by housing practices, and found it far harder to break.

In January 1966 Martin Luther King Jr. rented an apartment in North Lawndale on Chicago’s West Side and moved in with his family. The building was run-down and cold; the landlord rushed to fix it once reporters arrived. King had come at the invitation of local organizers to launch the Chicago Freedom Movement. The target was not a law. Chicago had no segregation statute. Yet the city was among the most segregated in the country, and Black families paid more for worse housing than white families paid for better.

The tools were quieter than Bull Connor’s dogs. For decades federal maps had marked Black neighborhoods in red as bad risks for loans, a practice called , so banks would not lend there. Real estate agents steered Black buyers away from white areas. Because they could not get mortgages, Black families bought homes on contract, paying installments for years with no ownership until the last payment and losing everything if they missed one. White homeowners’ groups and, at times, mobs made sure the boundaries held. Public schools followed the neighborhoods, so they were segregated too.

In July 1966 King addressed a rally at Soldier Field, then marched to City Hall and taped the movement’s demands to the door. In August marchers walked into Marquette Park on the Southwest Side to demand open housing. Thousands of white residents met them with rocks, bottles and Nazi signs. King was struck in the head by a rock and said he had never seen mobs as hateful, even in Mississippi and Alabama. Late that summer Mayor Richard J. Daley signed an agreement promising fair housing steps. King left. Most of the promises went unkept.

The Chicago campaign is often called a failure, and by the measure of Birmingham it was. But it shifted the movement’s understanding. Northern segregation was built into banking, real estate and city planning, and no single law could undo it. In April 1968, days after King was assassinated in Memphis, Congress passed the Fair Housing Act, which banned discrimination in selling and renting homes. Chicago’s housing patterns, though, still trace the old redlining maps. You can see them in the census today.

Malcolm X and Black Power

Main ideaMalcolm X and the Black Power movement argued that self-defense, pride and control of Black communities mattered as much as integration.

Malcolm Little was born in Omaha in 1925. His father, a preacher who followed Marcus Garvey, died violently when Malcolm was six; his mother was committed to a mental hospital; Malcolm drifted to Boston and Harlem and into prison for burglary at twenty. There he read constantly and joined the Nation of Islam, a Black religious movement led by Elijah Muhammad from its headquarters on Chicago’s South Side. He took the name Malcolm X, the X standing for the African family name that slavery had erased.

Through the 1950s and early 1960s Malcolm X became the Nation’s most powerful speaker. He rejected the goal of integration and the strategy of nonviolence. Why, he asked, should Black people beg to sit beside those who hated them, and why should they let themselves be beaten without striking back? He called for , Black-owned businesses and pride in African history. Many civil rights leaders considered him dangerous. Many young Black people in Northern cities, where the movement’s Southern victories changed little, found him the only leader saying what they felt.

In 1964 Malcolm broke with the Nation of Islam and made the pilgrimage to Mecca, where he prayed beside Muslims of every color and began to revise his views on race. He founded a new organization and started talking with civil rights leaders he had once mocked. On February 21, 1965, he was shot dead while speaking at the Audubon Ballroom in New York. Three members of the Nation of Islam were convicted; two of them were cleared decades later. His autobiography, published that year, became one of the most read American books of the century.

In June 1966, during a march through Mississippi, SNCC’s new chairman Stokely Carmichael gave the mood a name: . It meant different things to different people, from electing Black officials to building separate institutions. In Oakland that October, Huey Newton and Bobby Seale founded the Black Panther Party, which patrolled police with loaded guns, which was legal in California, and ran free breakfast programs for children. In Chicago the Panthers’ twenty-one-year-old leader, Fred Hampton, built alliances with poor white and Latino groups. On December 4, 1969, Chicago police killed him in his bed during a pre-dawn raid coordinated with the FBI. Black Power frightened much of white America. It also reshaped how Black Americans saw themselves.

The Movements That Followed

Main ideaThe methods and laws of the Black freedom struggle were taken up by women, Latinos, Native Americans, people with disabilities and gay Americans.

The Civil Rights Act’s one-word addition, sex, gave the women’s movement a legal lever. In 1966 Betty Friedan and others founded the National Organization for Women to make the government enforce it. Title IX in 1972 barred sex discrimination in schools that took federal money and transformed girls’ sports. Congress passed the Equal Rights Amendment that year, but it fell short of ratification by enough states before its deadline; Illinois did not ratify it until 2018. Women’s share of law and medical students, tiny in 1960, was near half by the century’s end.

In California, farmworkers led by Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta struck the grape growers of Delano in 1965 and asked the nation to boycott grapes. The strike lasted five years and won the first union contracts for field workers. In Chicago, Puerto Rican young people in Lincoln Park formed the Young Lords in 1968 to fight displacement of their neighborhood. Native American activists founded the American Indian Movement in 1968, occupied Alcatraz Island in 1969, and in 1975 won a federal law giving tribes more control over their own programs.

People with disabilities used the sit-in directly. In 1977 protesters occupied a federal building in San Francisco for nearly a month until the government issued rules under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, which banned discrimination by programs that received federal money. Judy Heumann, who had been denied a teaching license because she used a wheelchair, was a leader. In 1975 Congress required public schools to educate every child with a disability, the law now called IDEA, which is why students have individualized education plans today. In 1990 the Americans with Disabilities Act extended the ban to private businesses, public transit and employment.

In June 1969 patrons of the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in New York, fought back against a police raid, and the anniversary became the first pride marches the next year, including one in Chicago. Illinois had been the first state to repeal its law against private homosexual conduct, in 1961. Over the following decades the movement won the removal of homosexuality from the list of mental illnesses, protections against discrimination in many states, and in 2015 the Supreme Court’s ruling in Obergefell v. Hodges that same-sex couples have the right to marry. Each of these movements borrowed the sit-in, the march, the lawsuit and the boycott, and each argued that the Fourteenth Amendment’s promise belonged to it too.

Strategies and Costs

Main ideaThe movement won by combining courts, direct action, media and the vote, and it paid in lives, jobs, and years that its participants never got back.

Step back and the movement’s toolkit becomes visible. Lawyers built the legal foundation, from Brown to Browder to the cases that enforced the 1964 and 1965 acts. Direct action created crises that could not be ignored: the empty buses, the occupied counters, the burned bus, the children in Birmingham. Organizers did the slow work in between, registering voters door to door and training people to endure. And television carried the images to a national audience whose opinion moved Congress. No single tool worked alone. The debates inside the movement, between lawyers and marchers, between King and SNCC, between nonviolence and Black Power, were arguments about which tool to use next.

The costs were not spread evenly. Medgar Evers, Jimmie Lee Jackson, James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, Michael Schwerner, James Reeb, Viola Liuzzo, the four girls in Birmingham, Malcolm X, Fred Hampton and Martin Luther King Jr. were all killed. Hundreds of others were beaten or bombed. Thousands lost jobs, homes and farms for registering to vote or hosting a volunteer. Many young organizers left the South in the late 1960s with injuries, nightmares and broken health. The FBI, under J. Edgar Hoover, wiretapped King, tried to discredit him, and ran a program to disrupt Black organizations.

The results were real and incomplete. Legal segregation is gone; a Black student from Chicago can ride any bus and eat at any counter in Mississippi. Black voters and officeholders exist in numbers unthinkable in 1954. Yet the problems King found in Chicago, housing, schools tied to housing, and the wealth gap that both produce, remain. Historians measure the movement by both columns: what changed in the law, and what the law could not reach.

The best way to weigh it is with evidence. Compare the registration tables of 1964 and 1968. Read the Kerner Commission’s warning of two societies in the same year as the Fair Housing Act. Look at a map of Chicago neighborhoods by race today next to a redlining map from the 1930s. Listen to an oral history from a Freedom Summer volunteer, now in their eighties. The movement is not a finished story with a moral. It is a set of choices people made under pressure, and the same choices are still available.

Watergate

Main ideaWatergate began as a burglary and ended a presidency because tape recordings proved the president had obstructed justice, and because courts, Congress and the press each did their job.

At about two in the morning on June 17, 1972, a security guard named Frank Wills noticed tape holding a door latch open at the office complex in Washington, D.C. He removed it. When he came back, the tape was there again, so he called the police. Officers arrested five men inside the headquarters of the Democratic National Committee carrying cameras, bugging equipment and cash. One of them, James McCord, worked for the Committee to Re-elect the President, Richard Nixon’s campaign. The White House called it a third-rate burglary. That November Nixon won forty-nine states. The break-in itself was a small crime. What destroyed Nixon was the : the effort to hide who had ordered it and paid for it.

Two young reporters at the Washington Post, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, followed the money from the burglars back to the campaign, helped by a secret source inside the FBI whom they called Deep Throat. In 1973 the Senate opened televised hearings led by Sam Ervin of North Carolina. Nixon’s former lawyer, John Dean, testified that the president had joined the cover-up. Then, in July, an aide named Alexander Butterfield revealed that Nixon secretly recorded his Oval Office conversations. The , Archibald Cox, demanded the tapes. On October 20, 1973, Nixon ordered him fired. The attorney general and his deputy resigned rather than do it, in what the press called the Saturday Night Massacre.

On July 24, 1974, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in United States v. Nixon that a president’s , his right to keep advice confidential, was real but not absolute, and that he had to hand over the tapes. Days later the House Judiciary Committee, with votes from both parties, approved articles of for obstruction of justice, abuse of power and contempt of Congress. On August 5 the White House released a tape from June 23, 1972, on which Nixon told aides to have the CIA block the FBI’s investigation. Republican senators told him he would be convicted. Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974, the only president ever to do so. Gerald Ford took office and, a month later, pardoned him.

How do we know all this? The tapes sit in the National Archives, and anyone can listen to them online. The hearings were broadcast; the court opinion is public; dozens of officials were convicted and their trial records survive. Historians also point out that the system worked only because individuals chose to act: a night guard, two reporters, a judge named John Sirica who pressed the burglars to talk, senators of both parties, and a Court that included justices Nixon himself had appointed. Remove any one, and the story might have ended differently.

Trust After Watergate

Main ideaVietnam, Watergate and revelations about the CIA and FBI drove public trust in government down sharply, and Congress answered with laws that limit presidents and open records.

Since 1958 pollsters have asked Americans whether they trust the government in Washington to do what is right most of the time. In 1964 about three in four said yes. By 1974 barely one in three did. Watergate was only part of the reason. In 1971 the New York Times published the Pentagon Papers, a secret Defense Department history leaked by an analyst named Daniel Ellsberg, which showed that presidents of both parties had misled the public about Vietnam. Nixon’s response was to create a secret unit, nicknamed the Plumbers, that broke into Ellsberg’s psychiatrist’s office. The same men later broke into the Watergate. Reporters called the gap between what officials said and what was true the .

Congress moved to rein in the presidency. The of November 1973, passed over Nixon’s veto, requires a president to notify Congress within forty-eight hours of sending troops into combat and to withdraw them within sixty days unless Congress approves. A 1974 budget law created the Congressional Budget Office so lawmakers no longer depended on the White House for numbers. New campaign finance rules set limits on contributions and created the Federal Election Commission. Amendments to the , passed over Ford’s veto in 1974, made it far easier for citizens and reporters to demand government files. The Twenty-sixth Amendment, ratified in 1971, had already lowered the voting age to eighteen.

In 1975 a Senate committee led by Frank Church of Idaho investigated the intelligence agencies. The found that the CIA had plotted to assassinate foreign leaders, opened Americans’ mail and tested drugs on people without consent, and that the FBI’s COINTELPRO program had spied on and tried to break up civil rights groups, including the effort against Martin Luther King Jr. described in the last chapter. The result was permanent intelligence oversight committees in both houses and, in 1978, a special court that must approve spying on people inside the United States.

Gerald Ford, the only president never elected to either the presidency or the vice presidency, paid for the pardon at the polls. In 1976 voters chose Jimmy Carter, a Georgia peanut farmer and former governor who promised he would never lie to them. Carter brokered the Camp David peace accords between Egypt and Israel in 1978, but inflation and a hostage crisis in Iran wore down his presidency. Trust never returned to 1960s levels. It spiked briefly after September 11, 2001, then fell to about one in five by the 2010s. Some argue that healthy suspicion is what the framers intended. Others point out that low trust makes it harder to govern in an emergency. Both sides are reading the same numbers.

Oil Shocks and Stagflation

Main ideaIn the 1970s two oil shocks and rising prices combined with high unemployment into stagflation, a problem the old economic playbook could not solve, and the cure of the early 1980s was painful.

In October 1973, during a war between Israel and its Arab neighbors, the Arab members of , the cartel of oil-exporting countries, cut off shipments to the United States for supporting Israel. The lasted five months. The price of a barrel of crude oil roughly quadrupled, from about three dollars to about twelve. Gas stations ran dry. Some states allowed drivers to buy gas only on odd or even days, depending on their license plate. Congress set a national speed limit of fifty-five miles per hour to save fuel. For the first time since the Second World War, Americans discovered that a decision made overseas could empty their tank.

Economists had believed that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions: fight one and you got more of the other. The 1970s broke that rule. Prices rose about eleven percent in 1974 while unemployment climbed past eight percent the next year. The combination got a new name, , stagnation plus inflation. Oil was the reason it spread so fast, because oil is an input to almost everything: fertilizer, plastics, trucking, heating. Nixon had tried freezing wages and prices in 1971; Ford handed out buttons reading Whip Inflation Now. Neither worked, because once people expect prices to rise, they raise their own prices and wages first, and the expectation feeds itself.

A second shock came in 1979, when a revolution in Iran cut its oil exports and prices doubled again. That July, Carter gave a televised speech arguing that the country’s real crisis was one of confidence, not just energy; critics called it the malaise speech. In August he appointed Paul Volcker to lead the , and Volcker raised interest rates until borrowing nearly stopped. The prime lending rate passed twenty percent in 1980. The recession that followed pushed unemployment to almost eleven percent in late 1982, the worst since the Depression. Inflation fell to about three percent by 1983 and stayed low for decades.

The pain landed hardest on the industrial Midwest, soon called the . In March 1980 Wisconsin Steel on Chicago’s Southeast Side shut its gates without warning, throwing roughly three thousand people out of work; many never received the pay they were owed. U.S. Steel’s South Works, which had employed about twenty thousand at its peak, shrank through the 1980s and closed in 1992. Whether Volcker’s cure was worth the cost is still argued. To judge it, compare the inflation of 1980 with what came after, then walk the empty lakefront where the mills stood.

The Crash of 2008

Main ideaA housing bubble built on risky mortgages and complex financial bets burst in 2008, causing the worst downturn since the Depression and a fierce argument over bailouts.

From 2000 to 2006 the price of an average American house roughly doubled. Lenders, confident prices would keep rising, offered to borrowers with weak credit, often with low starting payments that jumped after two years. Banks did not keep these loans. Through they bundled thousands of mortgages into bonds and sold them to investors around the world, and rating agencies stamped many of the bundles as safe as government debt. Other firms sold insurance on those bonds without setting money aside to pay claims. The whole structure assumed that home prices in Chicago, Phoenix and Miami could not all fall at once.

They did. Prices began sliding in 2006, payments jumped, and notices spread through neighborhoods, hitting Black and Latino families, who had been steered toward the riskiest loans, hardest. In March 2008 the investment bank Bear Stearns collapsed and was sold with government help. On September 15, 2008, Lehman Brothers filed the largest bankruptcy in American history and no rescue came. The next day the government lent eighty-five billion dollars to the insurer AIG to keep it from failing. Credit froze: banks stopped lending even to each other. On September 29 the House voted down a rescue plan and the Dow fell 777 points in a day. Four days later Congress passed , a seven-hundred-billion-dollar that bought stakes in banks and later rescued General Motors and Chrysler.

The officially ran from December 2007 to June 2009, but the damage lasted longer. The economy lost nearly nine million jobs, and unemployment reached ten percent in October 2009. Millions of families lost homes. In February 2009 Congress passed a stimulus of about eight hundred billion dollars in spending and tax cuts, and the Federal Reserve held interest rates near zero for seven years. Anger ran in two directions. On February 19, 2009, a television reporter on the floor of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange denounced help for struggling borrowers, a rant credited with sparking the Tea Party movement. In 2011 Occupy Wall Street protested the rescue of banks whose executives were never prosecuted. Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Act in 2010 to tighten rules on lenders.

Whose fault was it? The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, a federal body, concluded in 2011 that the crisis was avoidable and blamed regulators, lenders and the rating agencies; dissenting members blamed government housing policy and a global glut of savings. Polarization, Screens and a Pandemic

Main ideaSince about 2010 American politics has grown more polarized, social media has changed how people learn and argue, and the COVID-19 pandemic tested government and trust at the same time.

means the two parties moving apart. The evidence is in the numbers. In 1994 about one voter in five held a very unfavorable view of the other party; by 2022 more than half did. Votes in Congress fall along party lines far more often than in the 1970s. Explanations compete: the sorting of conservatives into one party and liberals into the other, primaries that reward the loudest candidates, safe districts, and cable news. In 2016 Donald Trump won the presidency in the Electoral College while losing the popular vote. In 2020 Joe Biden defeated him; Trump’s claims of a stolen election lost in more than sixty court cases, and on January 6, 2021, a crowd of his supporters stormed the Capitol to stop the count. The House impeached Trump twice; the Senate acquitted him both times. In 2024 he won a second, non-consecutive term.

The screens changed in the middle of this. Facebook opened to the public in 2006, the iPhone arrived in 2007, and within a decade most Americans carried a feed in their pocket. Platforms make money from attention, so an chooses what each person sees, favoring posts that get a reaction. Researchers disagree about how much this drives polarization; some studies find feeds mostly confirm what people already think, others find real effects on anger and . A Senate committee found that a Russian agency ran fake American accounts during the 2016 campaign. Doctors debate the effect on teenagers’ mental health. What is not in dispute is that the way most people first learn about an event changed more between 2005 and 2015 than in the previous fifty years.

Then a virus arrived. The second known American case of COVID-19 was a Chicago woman, confirmed in January 2020. On March 11 the World Health Organization declared a ; on March 21 Illinois ordered residents to stay home, and schools went online. Unemployment hit nearly fifteen percent in April 2020, the highest since the Depression, and Congress passed a rescue package of about two trillion dollars. On May 25 a Minneapolis police officer killed George Floyd, and protests filled cities including Chicago. Vaccines, developed in under a year, were authorized in December 2020. By 2023 more than a million Americans had died, with deaths falling unevenly by age, race, income and region. Masks and vaccines became partisan markers, and students lost months of learning. Trust in institutions, already low, fell further for many.

These are events you or your family lived through, which makes them hard to see as history. Claims about them contradict one another, and some of the people who made those claims are still making them. One tool is the count of , the number of people who died beyond what would be expected in a normal year, which cuts through arguments about labels on death certificates. Another is to ask what a historian in 2075 would need: the emails, the school board recordings, the texts between friends, the memory of the empty expressway. Most of that will vanish unless someone saves it.

Writing History While It Happens

Main ideaThe recent past is the hardest history to write because the sources are incomplete, the outcomes unknown and the historian is a participant, so historians use rules to keep themselves honest.

Harry Truman left office in 1953 with one of the lowest approval ratings ever recorded; historians now rank him among the better presidents. Reagan was dismissed by many scholars in 1989 and reassessed after his diaries and letters were published. Distance changes the view, and so do the records. Under the Presidential Records Act, a president’s papers go to the National Archives and open in stages, with the most sensitive files closed for twelve years or more, and classified documents reviewed after twenty-five. Since the 1990s most of those records are : emails, texts, drafts saved over drafts. A tweet can be deleted; a web page can change. The Internet Archive has saved copies of pages since 1996, but only some of them.

Historians working close to the present follow rules. Source every claim: who said it, when, and what did they want. : find a second, independent record before believing the first. Separate journalism, the first draft, from history, which waits for the drafts underneath. Watch for , the trap of judging a 2003 decision by what was learned in 2004. Watch for , judging the past by today’s values without first understanding its own. And count the silent: the warehouse worker in 2020 who left no essay, the family in 1980 whose mill closed, the grandmother who watched Watergate on a black-and-white set. Oral history projects, including ones at Chicago museums and libraries, exist to record them before they are gone.

The chapter’s own events show how interpretations move. In 1974 Watergate seemed to prove the system worked; a year later the Church Committee showed how much of the system had failed unseen. In 2008 the crash was blamed on greedy borrowers, then on greedy banks, then, by a federal commission, on regulators who looked away; the arguments continue with better data. In 2020 the pandemic’s toll was fought over daily; the excess-death counts settled some of it, and more will be settled when hospital and government records open. None of these stories is finished. Each got clearer as evidence arrived and as the people writing it were no longer the people living it.

That makes you a . What you keep from this year, a screenshot, a diary, a recording of a grandparent describing a gas line in 1979 or a classroom on September 11, 2001, or an empty street in March 2020, is what a historian will have. The next chapter of this course asks you to choose a question about your own place and pursue it through evidence. This chapter’s lesson is the starting rule: the recent past is not yet history, but it is already evidence, and someone has to save it.
