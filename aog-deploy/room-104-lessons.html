How to Use the Closed-Loop Routing Tables

A one-page “Score → Intervention Crosswalk” opens every unit of Book 4 · 9–10.

1 · Screen. Run the Grace Compass check-in. It scores three domains — A (Regulation & Well-Being), B (Self-Compassion & Growth), C (Social Competency & Repair).

2 · Route to the lowest domain. A student’s lowest-scoring domain is where support is needed most. Find that domain on the unit’s crosswalk; the rows beneath it are this unit’s targeted lessons, novel scenes, and anchor charts.

3 · Run the Teacher Move. The last column gives a one-line, low-prep way to deliver the row — usually a scaffold so students fill content, not structure.

4 · Mind the red flag. A red ★ (elevated) or ★★ (highest-sensitivity) badge means safety steps apply: counselor co-facilitation, posted crisis resources, and — for ★★ — pre/post consultation. Never route a flagged row without them.

Doing well? If all three domains score 75+, use the ◆ enrichment row — an integration capstone — instead of a support row.

Important: deliver lessons in order. The crosswalk tells you where a student’s need is met within the unit — it is not a license to reorder the curriculum. The same routing logic runs the Grace Compass digital screener, so print and screen always agree.

Beyond the Brand

★ PPRA — COUNSELOR AWARENESS

OBJECTIVE

Students explain the gap between the curated persona and the authentic self and identify one specific cost they personally pay to maintain a curated performance.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness (primary) · Self-Management (supporting)

DURATION

45–50 min · August Week 1 · First lesson of the year

KEY VOCABULARY

curated self · authentic self · performance tax · persona · gap · integrity · self-monitoring

MATERIALS

Student journals · Anchor chart paper · Markers · Scenario Cards B4-1A and B4-1B

OPENING REVIEW

None — first lesson of year. Deliver Year-Start Introduction Protocol first.

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 9–10 · EARLY HIGH SCHOOL

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1A.4a Analyze how thoughts and emotions affect decision making and responsible behavior.

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2A.4a Analyze similarities and differences between one’s own and others’ perspectives.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

★ PPRA — COUNSELOR AWARENESS REQUIRED

PPRA-designated. Psychological self-examination — students examine the gap between public persona and private self. Advance parental notification required (minimum 10 days prior). Standard 3-tier opt-out protocols apply. Brief counselor at least 2 days before delivery. Do not ask students to share their curated vs. authentic gap with the whole class — journal first, always.

OPENING RITUAL

3 minutes · Year-Start Version 'Both feet on the floor. One breath slower than usual.' [Pause 10 seconds. Do not fill it.] 'This is how this class begins. Every time. Not because it is a routine for its own sake — because what this class asks of you takes a moment to actually arrive for. You will understand why by the end of today.' [Post the essential question on the board: 'Who are you when no one is watching?'] 'That is the question this unit is built around. Not who you perform for other people. Not who your applications say you are. Who are you when the audience is gone? Today we start looking at that gap — and what it costs to maintain it.'

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During the journal prompt in Step 2, a student who writes immediately, intensely, and at length — clearly accessing well-developed and familiar content without pausing.

MAY SIGNAL This student has a large, established gap and may be relieved to have language for it — or activated by naming it directly. A well-rehearsed gap at 9–10 is often accompanied by significant shame material underneath.

ACTION Note privately. Do not acknowledge in class. Flag for counselor awareness before U2-L7 (Backpack Audit). |

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During Step 3, a student who stares at the page for the full time and leaves it blank.

MAY SIGNAL Either: the gap is so defended that even a private prompt feels dangerous. Or: genuine alignment between curated and authentic selves (less common at this age).

ACTION Do not approach during the lesson. After class: 'How did today feel for you?' A low-pressure check-in opens more than any direct question. |

GRADE-BAND DIFFERENTIATION
GRADES 9 · 10

GRADES 11 · 12

Grades 9–10: Keep gap concept concrete and scenario-grounded. Journal prompts carry personal work privately. The essential question is planted today — it does not need to be answered today.

Grades 11–12: The gap has typically been maintained for years — the Performance Tax is higher and more entrenched. Push further: 'What has the sustained performance cost you in relationships where you actually wanted to be known?'

EXIT TICKET

"The gap between my curated self and my authentic self right now is ___. The performance tax I pay for it shows up as ___. One thing I would have more of if the gap were smaller is ___." Journal — private. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

First lesson: resist the urge to push personal depth. Scenario cards carry the emotional weight. The journal prompts are where the real work happens — protect them even if everything else runs short. Total composure about the performance-authenticity gap content is almost always protective at this age — note students who appear entirely unaffected. The Performance Tax is not abstract for students managing identity gaps around race, sexuality, immigration status, or family circumstances — hold that awareness even if it is not named today.

FAMILY CONNECTION · CONVERSATION STARTER

★ PPRA LESSON Dear Families, Today in Architecture of Grace, your student examined the concept of the curated self — the gap between the identity we perform for others and who we actually are. Using scenario cards, students explored what it costs to maintain a version of yourself that is technically accurate but not fully honest. No student was asked to disclose personal information. All reflection was private and journal-based. A conversation-starter that might open this naturally: "Is there a version of you that people see that feels different from how you actually are inside?" You do not need to pursue this if your student isn't ready. The fact that you asked it — and meant it — is enough.

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 3 · read aloud or in your own words

01THE CURATED SELF — NAME THE GAP10 MIN

'Open your phones for ten seconds. Look at your profile photo or your most recent public post.' [10 second pause.] 'Now: is the person in that image the same person who woke up this morning? Same mood? Same worries? Same doubts?' [Take 2–3 student responses — brief, no pressure.] 'We all manage how we appear. That is not dishonesty — that is being human. The question is not whether you have a gap between your curated and authentic self. The question is: what is it costing you?' Point to the anchor chart. 'This curriculum calls that cost the Performance Tax. Today we start measuring yours.'

02THE PERFORMANCE TAX — HOW IT WORKS8 MIN

Write on board: TAX = GAP × TIME. 'The performance tax is real and measurable. Psychologists call the underlying mechanism self-monitoring: the cognitive effort of continuously tracking how you appear across every context. It runs on working memory — the same working memory you use for everything else that matters. The wider your gap, and the longer you maintain it, the more it consumes.' [Pause.] 'Kameron in the scenario card is anxious and sometimes lonely at home. His curated self is confident and effortlessly social. That gap is not free.' Students journal 3 minutes privately: 'Where am I spending the most performance energy right now? What would I have more of if that gap were smaller?' Do not ask for sharing.

03IS THE GAP WORTH IT?7 MIN

'Not all curation is a problem. Choosing what to share with an employer is different from performing a false identity for your closest friends. The question is not whether you adjust your presentation — you do, and so does everyone. The question is whether the gap is costing you more than it is giving you.' Write on board: SIGNAL QUESTION — 'Is the gap costing you more than it is giving you?' 'This is not a yes or no question you answer today. It is a question you carry through this unit.' Students write privately: 'One thing my curated self shows that my authentic self doesn't fully believe is ___.' Private. Not shared.

SCENARIO CARDS

Print and laminate before unit. Two cards per lesson.

B4-1A BEYOND THE BRAND

THE SITUATION

Kameron runs a school Instagram account with 2,300 followers. His posts are perfectly lit, captioned with wit, and carefully curated to show him as confident, funny, and effortlessly social. At home he is often anxious, sometimes lonely, and spends hours crafting posts that look spontaneous. His best friend — someone who has known him since fifth grade — says: 'I barely recognize you on there. It's weird because it's all technically true, but it doesn't feel like you.'

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What is the gap between Kameron's curated self and his authentic self — and what is it costing him? The best friend says 'it's all technically true.' Can something be factually accurate and still misrepresent you? How? Kameron's posts show the highlights. What is systematically absent? Is there a version of showing up online that is both genuine and selective? Where is the line?

THE PERFORMANCE TAX Sustained self-monitoring — constantly tracking how you appear — consumes cognitive and emotional energy. The performance tax is real: the wider the gap between the curated self and the authentic self, the heavier the toll. The question is not whether we present differently in different contexts — that is human. The question is whether the gap is costing you more than it is giving you.

B4-1B BEYOND THE BRAND

THE SITUATION

In a college interview, Priya is asked: 'Tell me something about yourself that isn't on your application.' She freezes. Her application is excellent — every accomplishment, activity, and honor documented. But in that moment she realizes she doesn't know how to answer because every version of herself she has practiced presenting is achievement-based. The interviewer smiles: 'Take your time. There's no wrong answer.' Priya's mind is blank.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What has Priya practiced so thoroughly that she can no longer access what lies beneath it? Why is 'tell me something about yourself' so much harder than 'here are my accomplishments'? What would Priya say if the question was: 'What do you love that you have never put on an application?' Priya's application tells the interviewer what she has done. What would it take to tell them who she is?

THE HIGHLIGHT REEL VS. THE REAL SELF The highlight reel is not dishonest — it is selective. But over time, if we practice the highlight reel long enough, we can lose fluent access to what lies beneath it. Identity is not a résumé. The architecture of who you are is built from your values, your contradictions, your private joys, and your genuine struggles — not your credentials alone.

MAY SIGNAL This student may be experiencing significant social exhaustion or performance anxiety at school. A battery consistently depleted across the full school environment with no institutional charge source is a wellbeing concern.

ACTION Note privately. After class: 'I noticed today that school feels draining across a lot of different contexts for you. That's important to know — I'd like to check in with you this week.' Brief counselor if the pattern appears in the Tier 2 worksheet as well. |

GRADE-BAND DIFFERENTIATION
GRADES 9 · 10

GRADES 11 · 12

Grades 9–10: Keep battery concept behavioral and concrete. Focus on Charges/Drains lists and the Misread Problem. Land the introversion/extroversion spectrum clearly before moving to the grace extension.

Grades 11–12: Push further — 'Have you ever overridden your battery so consistently that you stopped being able to tell what you actually felt?' This connects to the Authenticity Gradient (L4) and the Integrity Anchor (L3).

EXIT TICKET

"My social battery charges when I ___. It drains most when I ___. One environment I have been misreading — assuming something about a person there that the battery concept reframes — is ___." Journal — private. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Most important teaching move: resist valorizing either introversion or extroversion — both are neutral. Watch for students who list school as universally draining — this is a wellbeing signal worth a counselor brief if it also appears in the worksheet. The Misread Problem has an equity dimension: students managing code-switching or cultural performance carry a higher and less-acknowledged performance tax. Name this implicitly in the debrief.

FAMILY CONNECTION · CONVERSATION STARTER

★ PPRA LESSON Dear Families, Today students explored the Social Battery — a framework for understanding the energy available for genuine social engagement, and what charges or drains it. Students examined how introverted and extroverted patterns are neither flaws nor virtues, but information about how a person is wired. A conversation-starter: "When you've had a full day of being around people, what do you need afterward to feel like yourself again?" This question works regardless of where your student falls on the introvert/extrovert spectrum. There is no correct answer — the goal is simply for them to know their own.

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 3 · read aloud or in your own words

01WHAT THE BATTERY IS — AND WHAT IT IS NOT8 MIN

Draw a battery on the board. 'The social battery is not a metaphor for shyness. It measures authentic engagement capacity — the energy available for genuine connection rather than performance. Some environments charge it — they require less performance and more genuine connection. Others drain it — they require sustained self-monitoring.' Students list two columns privately: Charges / Drains. 3 minutes. Private.

02INTROVERT / EXTROVERT — THE ACTUAL DISTINCTION10 MIN

'Research on introversion and extroversion shows something more specific than the popular version. Introvert-dominant people charge in lower-stimulation environments — often solitude or small groups. Extrovert-dominant people charge in higher-stimulation social environments. Neither is better. And crucially: you can be an extrovert who is completely drained by performance-heavy environments — because performance and genuine connection are not the same thing.' Write: INTROVERT dominant → charges in LOW stimulation. EXTROVERT dominant → charges in HIGH stimulation. Pairs: 'Where do you land — and does it match what actually charges and drains you?'

03THE MISREAD PROBLEM — AND THE GRACE EXTENSION7 MIN

'Fatima thinks Zoe is unfriendly. Zoe thinks Fatima is exhausting. Neither is correct — they are managing different batteries and using their own battery as the universal standard. The skill: knowing your own battery, honoring it without judgment, and extending the same courtesy to people whose batteries work differently.' Pairs: 'Name one person whose battery seems to work very differently from yours. What have you assumed about them that this reframes?'

SCENARIO CARDS

Print and laminate before unit. Two cards per lesson.

B4-2A THE SOCIAL BATTERY

THE SITUATION

After three weeks of orientation activities, college fairs, and mandatory social events, Diego finds himself dreading lunch. He likes his classmates. He is genuinely interested in them. But he has been 'on' — attentive, engaged, witty — for so long that he can barely carry a conversation. He starts eating in a bathroom stall with headphones in. 'I'm not antisocial,' he tells himself. 'I'm just empty.'

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What is Diego's social battery doing — and what does his behavior suggest about what charges it? Diego says 'I'm not antisocial.' What is the distinction he is making — and is he right? What environments or interactions appear to charge Diego's battery versus drain it? What would it mean for Diego to honor his battery's state rather than override it?

THE SOCIAL BATTERY The Social Battery is a metaphor for the energy available for authentic social engagement. Some environments charge it — they require less performance and more genuine connection. Others drain it — they require sustained monitoring of how you appear. Knowing which is which is not a luxury — it is data you need to function well over time.

B4-2B THE SOCIAL BATTERY

THE SITUATION

Fatima gets energy from being around people. After a long weekend alone studying, she feels depleted and slightly gray. Her roommate Zoe is the opposite — a long social weekend leaves Zoe quiet, needing hours of solo time to return to herself. Both of them are confused by the other. Fatima thinks Zoe is unfriendly. Zoe thinks Fatima is exhausting. Neither is wrong about themselves.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What does Fatima's battery charge on — and what drains Zoe's? Why does neither experience invalidate the other? Fatima interprets Zoe's withdrawal as unfriendliness. What is she missing? Zoe interprets Fatima's energy as exhausting. What is she missing? What would it look like for both of them to honor their own battery needs without making the other feel deficient?

CHARGING VS. DRAINING There is no correct social battery configuration. What matters is knowing your own — and not using your battery's needs as a judgment about others' character. Fatima is not shallow for needing social connection. Zoe is not antisocial for needing solitude. Both are managing their own authentic selves as best they know how.

The Integrity Anchor

45–50 min · August Week 3
OBJECTIVE

Students name three personal non-negotiable values and demonstrate how an integrity anchor holds without requiring moral pressure on others.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness (primary) · Self-Management (supporting) · Responsible Decision-Making (supporting)

DURATION

45–50 min · August Week 3

KEY VOCABULARY

integrity anchor · non-negotiable · values · quiet integrity · anchor test · internal standard

OPENING REVIEW

'Last lesson: the Social Battery. Quick — one environment that charges yours?' [Answer.] 'Today: when social pressure pushes hard enough to override even a charged battery — what holds you steady?'

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 9–10 · EARLY HIGH SCHOOL

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1A.4a Analyze how thoughts and emotions affect decision making and responsible behavior.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3A.4a Demonstrate personal responsibility in making ethical decisions.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

ANCHOR CHART

THE INTEGRITY ANCHOR A RULE: Imposed from outside. Holds because of consequences. AN ANCHOR: Examined from inside. Holds because you chose it. CHARACTERISTICS: Holds quietly. Does not announce itself. Does not require others to change. Simply tells you where your own line is. THE ANCHOR TEST: 'Does this hold when the cost of living it is real and immediate?' An anchor that only holds when it is costless is not an anchor. It is a preference. POST: Before class. KEEP: All of Unit 1 and beyond — the Integrity Anchor is PERMANENT.

OPENING RITUAL

60–90 seconds 'Both feet on the floor. One breath slower than usual.' [Pause 10 seconds.] 'Last lesson: the Social Battery — what charges it, what drains it. Today: when everything is loud and the pressure is real — what holds you in place?' [Post: 'What holds you steady when social pressure pushes — and does it require anyone else to change?']

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During the pair sharing in Step 2, a student who cannot name a single genuine anchor — who lists only rules or things others expect, with no internally-chosen value.

MAY SIGNAL This student may not yet have examined their values as genuinely their own — operating primarily from external rules or social expectations. Developmentally understandable at 9–10, not a clinical signal. Worth noting for Unit 2 context — students lacking internal anchors tend to be more dependent on external validation.

ACTION In the worksheet, prompt more gently: 'Is there anything you would hold onto even if you were the only one who cared about it?' |

SCENARIO CARDS · FACILITATION NOTES

B4-3A and B4-3B B4-3A (MARCUS): Whole-class. Key: 'What is Marcus's anchor — and how does it hold without moralizing at others?' The insight: the anchor is specific, not abstract. B4-3B (YUSUF): Pairs. Key: 'Is Yusuf's position principled or convenient? How would you tell the difference?' This is the Anchor Test in practice.

GRADE-BAND DIFFERENTIATION
GRADES 9 · 10

GRADES 11 · 12

Grades 9–10: Keep the anchor concrete and behavioral — name the specific value, name the specific test, name what held. The Quiet Anchor concept (holds without announcing itself) is the key insight.

Grades 11–12: Push philosophical: 'Is an integrity anchor the same as a moral position? What is the difference between a value you have examined and a belief you have absorbed?' Connects directly to B5-U1-L6: The Moral North Star.

EXIT TICKET

"My three integrity anchors are ___, ___, and ___. The one most tested right now is ___. What held it in place was ___. The Anchor Test question I will carry into this week is ___." Journal — private. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Most important move: push from abstract values ('honesty,' 'kindness') toward specific behavioral anchors ('I will not represent someone else's work as mine'). Abstract values do not function as anchors under pressure — they are too easy to rationalize around. Students who list only rules or externally-imposed standards have not yet developed genuine internal anchors. Normal at 9–10 — note for Unit 2 context. The Integrity Anchor is permanent — stays up for the rest of the year.

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 3 · read aloud or in your own words

01RULE VS. ANCHOR — THE CORE DISTINCTION8 MIN

'Marcus doesn't use AI to cheat on homework — not because of the rules, but because he wants to actually know the material. That is the difference between a rule and an anchor.' Write: RULE = external / consequence-driven. ANCHOR = internal / value-driven. 'A rule holds because something bad happens if you break it. An anchor holds because breaking it means becoming someone you don't want to be.' Students journal 3 minutes: 'Name three things you would not compromise even under real social pressure. Not rules — not what you're supposed to say. What is actually true for you right now?' Private.

02THE QUIET ANCHOR — HOW IT WORKS WITHOUT PRESSURE10 MIN

'Notice what Marcus does NOT do when his friends start cheating. He doesn't lecture. He doesn't leave. He explains his reason once — clearly, without judgment — and they stop pressuring him. That is what a well-articulated anchor does: it doesn't require others to change. It simply holds you in place, clearly enough that others understand it.' Pairs: 'Share one of your three anchors. Just the anchor. Then: how would you explain it to someone who might push back — without moralizing at them?'

03THE ANCHOR TEST7 MIN

Write: ANCHOR TEST — 'Does this hold when it costs me something specific, right now?' 'Yusuf fills out his peer evaluation honestly even though staying silent would also benefit him. His anchor — honesty — costs him something real in that moment. It holds. That is an anchor.' Students complete the Integrity Anchor Worksheet (Tier 2): name three anchors, describe the last time each was tested, write what held them in place.

B4-3A THE INTEGRITY ANCHOR

THE SITUATION

When Marcus's friend group started cheating on homework using AI tools, they assumed Marcus would join. He didn't. They assumed he was being judgmental. He wasn't. He explained: 'I just don't want to go into the exam not knowing the material. It's not about the rules — it's about what I want to be able to do at the end of the semester.' His group didn't entirely understand. But they didn't pressure him again.

B4-15A THE 24-HOUR RULE

THE SITUATION

After a group project meeting dissolved into conflict — disagreements about workload, accusations about effort, raised voices — Marcus sent three texts in rapid succession that he immediately regretted. By the next morning, the messages had been screenshot and shared. What started as a conflict between four people was now a social situation involving fifteen. DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What happened in the gap between Marcus's emotional flooding and his first text — and what was missing there? Marcus's texts may have been accurate — he may have had genuine grievances. Does accuracy make the timing right? The 24-hour rule does not say 'never say the hard thing.' What does it say — and why does the distinction matter? Redesign Marcus's approach: what does a response (not a reaction) look like for this situation?

RESPONSE VS. REACTION A reaction is immediate, emotion-driven, and often accurate in content and wrong in timing. A response is deliberate — it contains the same honest content delivered after the emotional flooding has subsided. The 24-hour rule is not about suppressing what needs to be said. It is about choosing when to say it in a way that the other person can actually hear.

B4-15B THE 24-HOUR RULE

THE SITUATION

Nia received feedback from a teacher on an essay she had worked hard on. The feedback was detailed and included several significant criticisms. Nia's first impulse was to go to the teacher that period and explain why every criticism was wrong. She stopped herself. She waited until the next day. When she returned, she had three genuine questions and one point of respectful disagreement. The conversation was productive. Her teacher raised her grade.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What changed between Nia's first impulse and the next-day conversation? Nia still had a point of disagreement on day two — the 24-hour rule didn't make her opinion change. What did it change? What would have happened if Nia had followed her first impulse — and what would she not have been able to hear? Identify a recent situation where you reacted instead of responding. What would a 24-hour pause have changed?

THE WINDOW OF DELIBERATION Emotional flooding impairs the prefrontal cortex — the part of the brain responsible for deliberate reasoning and long-term consequence evaluation. The 24-hour rule creates a window in which that capacity returns. During that window, honest disagreement becomes possible without the content being filtered through someone else's defensiveness. The goal is not to manufacture agreement — it is to create conditions where honest exchange can actually happen.

'They're living rent-free in my head,' she thought. 'And they're not even aware of it.'

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What is the cost of Amara's sustained resentment — and who is paying it? Amara organized her life around people who have moved on. What does that tell you about how resentment works? Forgiveness is sometimes described as 'evicting the tenant who stopped paying.' What does this mean in Amara's situation? What specific things would Amara be able to do if she stopped letting the old friend group occupy her mental real estate?

FORGIVENESS AS SELF-DETERMINATION Forgiveness is not a gift to the person who harmed you. It is an act of self-determination — choosing not to let someone else's past behavior continue to define, limit, and organize your present life. The rent-free metaphor captures something real: the person you resent is often not thinking about you, while you are thinking about them constantly. Evicting that tenant is not for them. It is for you.

B4-19B FORGIVENESS AS POWER

THE SITUATION

B4-28A UNIT REFLECTION — CULTURE CELEBRATION

THE SITUATION

At the end of the year, Amara's teacher asks: 'What is one thing this class did this year that you want to name out loud?' Students take turns. The answers are specific: 'We didn't let Jaylen get left out when his group changed.' 'We told Marcus when we thought he was wrong and didn't make it weird afterward.' 'We celebrated Nia's regional win like it mattered, even though some of us didn't make the team.' None of these are in the curriculum. All of them are evidence of it.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What do the specific moments students name tell you about what the class actually built this year? None of the things students name are formal curriculum activities. What does that reveal about where culture lives? What is the difference between a class that performs grace and a class that actually builds it? Name one specific thing your class did this year — not a formal activity, but a real moment — that evidenced the culture you were building.

THE EVIDENCE OF CULTURE Culture is not built in curriculum — it is evidenced by it. The specific, small moments students recall at year-end are the real data: not what the class studied, but what the class actually did when no one was evaluating. These moments are the architecture of grace in practice. They cannot be planned, assigned, or performed. They can only be grown — slowly, in a community that takes them seriously.

THE SITUATION

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What did the class create that allowed Marcus to know people differently — and how did it get created? Marcus says none of it was in a lesson plan. What was in the lesson plan that made the unplanned things possible? A room that makes it 'okay to be real' does not happen automatically. What conditions produce it? As you leave this class, what is one thing you know about another person in this room that you want to carry forward?

THE ROOM THAT MADE IT OKAY Safety — psychological, relational safety — is the foundation on which genuine growth becomes possible. The curriculum creates structure. The room — the specific community of people who show up to it — creates the conditions in which the structure produces something real. Marcus's journal captures what the curriculum is ultimately trying to build: a room where being real is okay. That room is not the lesson. It is what the lessons are for.
