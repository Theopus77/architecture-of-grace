Unit 1

Identity & The Weight of Perception

13 cards

Lesson 1

The Mask vs. The Mirror

1-A

New School, New Mask

Amara
The situation

Amara transfers to a new school and carefully constructs a social mask — new name spelling, different interests, carefully curated lunch-table conversations. Six months in, she writes in her journal: "I don't know which one is me anymore."

Discussion questions
  1. What was Amara trying to protect by building the Mask?
  2. At what point does a protective strategy become a trap?
  3. What would it cost Amara to show one real thing about herself to one person?
  4. What is the difference between adapting and disappearing?

Teacher note — not read to students. Use if time allows. Best for classes that engage quickly.

1-B

The Class Clown

Jordan
The situation

Jordan is the funniest person in every room. Teachers describe him as "a handful." His closest friend knows Jordan reads philosophy books in private and cries at wildlife documentaries. Jordan has never told anyone what he actually wants to do with his life.

Discussion questions
  1. What does the Mask give Jordan? What does it cost him?
  2. Is Jordan's humor a Mask, a real part of his identity, or both?
  3. What would "identity foreclosure" look like for Jordan in five years?
  4. What's the smallest step toward letting someone see the Mirror version?

Teacher note — not read to students. Grades 7-8 or faster classes. Introduces the identity-foreclosure concept.

Lesson 2

The Anatomy of a Mistake

1-C

"I Am Stupid"

Maya
The situation

Maya failed a math test. She tells her friend: "I am just stupid. I've always been stupid. There's no point in trying harder." She avoids the next test by pretending to be sick.

Discussion questions
  1. Is Maya experiencing shame or guilt? What evidence supports your answer?
  2. What is the difference between "I failed the test" and "I am stupid"?
  3. What does the shame response (avoidance) cost Maya?
  4. What would a guilt response open up for her?

Teacher note — not read to students. Prioritize this card. Work through as whole class. It illustrates the core distinction most cleanly.

1-D

Keeps Paying for It

Darius
The situation

Darius said something genuinely cruel to a classmate six months ago. He apologized. The classmate forgave him. But Darius still refuses to join group projects, avoids social events, and tells himself he "doesn't deserve" good friendships.

Discussion questions
  1. Darius already did the guilt work (he apologized). What is he doing now?
  2. What is he protecting himself from by continuing to self-punish?
  3. If accountability is complete, what does continuing to pay the price achieve?
  4. What would "self-forgiveness" look like as a practical next step for Darius?

Teacher note — not read to students. Grades 7-8 after Card 1-C debrief. Deepens accountability vs. self-punishment distinction.

The situation

Today we studied shame vs. guilt. At home: "That was a dishonest choice" (guilt) is more useful than "You are a liar" (shame). Focus on the behavior, not the person.

Lesson 3

The Digital Backpack

1-E

Perfect Digital Life, Hidden Pain

Kezia
The situation

Kezia's feed shows a perfect life — coffee shops, friends, sun on her face. Underneath, she is dealing with significant anxiety and hasn't slept properly in three months. She told a close friend: "If I posted what I actually feel, I'd lose everything."

Discussion questions
  1. What is Kezia's digital backpack carrying vs. what is actually in her real backpack?
  2. Why does the gap between them feel necessary to her?
  3. What does it cost her to maintain that gap?
  4. What is the smallest thing she could do to close it — even slightly?

Teacher note — not read to students. Equity: be sensitive to students from cultures where public display of struggle carries real social consequences.

1-F

Deleted Post, Still Circulating

Marcus
The situation

Marcus posted a heated comment during an argument, regretted it immediately, and deleted it within ten minutes. Fourteen months later, a screenshot of the comment appears during a different conflict. He had no idea it had been saved.

Discussion questions
  1. What does this tell us about how the digital environment works?
  2. Is Marcus responsible for the post even though he deleted it? Why or why not?
  3. What is the difference between "I deleted it" and "it's gone"?
  4. What decision-making filter might Marcus use before posting in the future?

Teacher note — not read to students. Do NOT ask students to share real examples of posts they regret.

Lesson 4

Peer Pressure & The Volume of Integrity

1-G

Didn't Stop the Mockery

Sofia
The situation

Sofia's friend group began mocking a quieter classmate at lunch. Sofia didn't join in — but she also didn't stop it. She laughed once when she didn't mean to. Afterwards, she felt sick about it. She told herself: "I'm not a bad person — I just didn't do anything."

Discussion questions
  1. What peer pressure mechanism was at work?
  2. What was the integrity response Sofia did not take?
  3. What was the smallest possible action she could have taken without major social risk?
  4. Is "not joining in" the same as "not participating"? Why or why not?

Teacher note — not read to students. Work through all four questions as a whole class first. Do not let students dismiss Sofia as simply weak.

1-H

The Lunch Table Vote

Group Scenario
The situation

A group of friends is deciding whether to exclude someone from their lunch table going forward. It's framed as a "vote." Most have already said yes. One person hasn't voted yet. That person privately thinks the exclusion is unfair.

Discussion questions
  1. What pressure mechanism is being used — and what makes it particularly powerful?
  2. What are the options available to the person who hasn't voted yet?
  3. What is the integrity response — and what would it cost?
  4. What is the difference between loyalty and compliance?

Teacher note — not read to students. Faster classes or debrief extension after Card 1-G. Introduces the "vote" as social coercion mechanism.

Lesson 4

Accountability Without Self-Punishment

FT-1

The Essay

Friend Test Priya
The situation

Priya worked hard on an essay. She received a C+. She spent the next three days telling herself she was stupid, stopped doing homework for another class, and skipped the teacher's feedback session because "what's the point."

Discussion questions
  1. Is this an accountability response or self-punishment? How can you tell?
  2. Apply the Friend Test: what would Priya say to her best friend in this exact situation?
  3. What does accountability look like here? (acknowledge → repair → commit → move on)
  4. What is self-punishment costing Priya that accountability would not?

Teacher note — not read to students. Work through as a whole class first, modeling the four accountability steps.

FT-2

The Argument

Friend Test Marcus
The situation

Marcus said something cruel to his sister during an argument. She cried. He apologized the same day and she accepted. Two weeks later, Marcus still won't eat dinner with the family, refuses to talk about it, and says: "I'm a terrible person — this is just what I am."

Discussion questions
  1. Marcus completed the accountability steps. What is he doing now?
  2. What is the Friend Test response to Marcus's current self-talk?
  3. What does it mean that self-punishment continues after accountability is complete?
  4. What would moving forward actually look like for Marcus?

Teacher note — not read to students. Pairs or small groups after Card 1. Connects to Darius from U1-L2.

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students learned the four-step accountability structure: acknowledge → repair → commit → move on. At home, model it: "I made a mistake. Here's what I'm doing about it. Now I'm moving on."

Lesson 5

The Friend Test

FT-A

Sports Tryout

Friend Test Pair Jamie
The situation

Jamie didn't make the team. She had worked harder than anyone she knew. In the week after, she quits the gym, stops sleeping properly, and tells her mom: "I'm just not athletic. I never will be. It's embarrassing that I even tried."

Discussion questions
  1. Apply the Friend Test: what would Jamie say to her best friend in this situation?
  2. What is the gap between how Jamie is treating herself and how she would treat someone she cares about?
  3. Write out Jamie's internal critic statement and then a Compassionate Witness response.
  4. What does "being on your own side" look like for Jamie right now?

Teacher note — not read to students. Partner A reads the scenario aloud. Partner B applies the Friend Test first. Then swap.

FT-B

The Social Rejection

Friend Test Pair Theo
The situation

Theo was not invited to a birthday party that most of his friend group attended. He saw the photos online. He spent the weekend telling himself he was "annoying," "too much," and "nobody actually likes me." He cancelled plans the following week.

Discussion questions
  1. Is the internal critic's conclusion ("nobody likes me") accurate? What evidence would you need to test it?
  2. Apply the Friend Test: what would Theo say to his best friend making the same conclusion?
  3. What is the Compassionate Witness response to this situation?
  4. What is one small action Theo could take that is evidence-based rather than conclusion-based?

Teacher note — not read to students. Social exclusion is high-frequency for this age group. Do not ask if this resonates personally.

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students practiced the Friend Test. At home: when your child is being hard on themselves, ask "Would you say that to your best friend?" Then listen.

Empathy & Forgiving Others

Lesson 1

Impact vs. Intent

II-A

The Missed Invitation

Impact/Intent — Layla & Nadia
The situation

Layla forgot to invite her close friend Nadia to a small group hangout - not intentionally, she simply forgot. Nadia found out on social media. She is devastated and tells people Layla "chose to exclude" her. Layla is confused: "I didn't mean to hurt her - why is she acting like I did this on purpose?"

Discussion questions
  1. What was Layla's intent? What was the impact on Nadia?
  2. Does Layla's good intent make Nadia's pain less real? Why or why not?
  3. What does repair address - the intent, the impact, or both?
  4. What would a repair conversation look like between them?

Teacher note — not read to students. Work through as whole class. Confirm before closing: "Impact is not determined by intent."

II-B

The Joke That Landed Wrong

Impact/Intent Theo & Amara
The situation

During group work, Theo made a joke that the whole group laughed at. One student — Amara — went quiet and didn't participate for the rest of class. Theo had no idea the joke had a cultural meaning he didn't know about.

Discussion questions
  1. Did Theo intend to target Amara? What was the actual impact?
  2. Is "I didn't know" the same as "I didn't cause harm"?
  3. What is repair in this situation — and who is responsible for initiating it?
  4. How does this scenario change if the joke touched on the student's culture or background?

Teacher note — not read to students. Equity alert. Do not ask any student to identify whether they have been in Amara's position.

II-C

The Group Project

Impact/Intent Cameron
The situation

Cameron took over the group project presentation without consulting anyone. The group got a high grade. But two members felt invisible and one told the teacher: "Cameron always does this — he acts like we don't matter." Cameron is genuinely surprised — he thought he was helping.

Discussion questions
  1. What did Cameron intend? What was the impact?
  2. Can an action with a positive outcome still cause harm?
  3. What accountability response is available to Cameron?
  4. Does the good grade change the answer? Why or why not?

Teacher note — not read to students. Best for pairs after whole-class Card A debrief. Positive outcomes do not erase harmful impact.

Family connection

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students learned: impact is what repair addresses, regardless of intent. At home: instead of defending intent, try acknowledging impact. "I didn't mean it that way — and I can see it landed hard. What can I do?"

Lesson 2

Three Sides of Every Story

3S-A

The Broken Friendship

Three Sides Kezia & Priya
The situation

Kezia and Priya were close friends. Priya started a new group of friends and spent less time with Kezia. Kezia told people Priya "abandoned" her. Priya feels Kezia is "too clingy" and was "holding her back." Both feel misunderstood and hurt.

Discussion questions
  1. What is Side One (Kezia's experience and story)?
  2. What is Side Two (Priya's experience and story)?
  3. What does the Compassionate Observer see that neither of them can?
  4. Is one side "right"? Can both experiences be real at the same time?

Teacher note — not read to students. Friendship drift is high-frequency for Grades 6-7. Do not ask if this maps to their own friendships.

3S-B

The Teacher Complaint

Three Sides Marcus
The situation

Marcus complained to his parents that his teacher "hates him." The teacher said Marcus "disrupts class constantly." A third student in the class sees both — Marcus is often anxious, and the teacher gives harsher feedback to some students than others.

Discussion questions
  1. What is Marcus's side? What is the teacher's side?
  2. What does the third student (the Compassionate Observer) see that the two main parties cannot?
  3. Does the third student's view mean Marcus has no legitimate grievance?
  4. What would repair or clarity look like from each of the three positions?

Teacher note — not read to students. Authority figure on one side — handle carefully.

Family connection

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students practiced the Three Sides framework. At home: when your child describes a conflict, ask: "What do you think the other person's side was? And what would someone outside the conflict — who cares about both of you — see?"

Lesson 3

Forgiveness vs. Reconciliation

FVR-A★

The Betrayal

Forgiveness Darius
The situation

Two years ago, Darius's closest friend shared a private secret with the whole grade. The friendship ended. The friend has since apologized multiple times and is clearly remorseful. Darius has processed the hurt and genuinely no longer carries resentment but he has no desire to rebuild the friendship.

Discussion questions
  1. Has Darius forgiven his friend? How can you tell?
  2. Is Darius required to reconcile because he has forgiven?
  3. What is the difference between "I release the resentment" and "I want you back in my life"?
  4. What would it mean to make forgiveness a condition of reconciliation?

Teacher note — not read to students. Counselor on-site for the lesson. Do not ask students to share personal forgiveness situations.

FVR-B★★

The Family Situation

Forgiveness High Sensitivity
The situation

A student is asked by a well-meaning adult: "Have you forgiven your family member yet? It's important to forgive." The student is confused — they don't feel ready. The family member has not apologized or changed the harmful behavior.

Discussion questions
  1. Is forgiveness appropriate here? What information would you need to decide?
  2. What is the difference between forgiving someone who is still causing harm vs. someone who is not?
  3. Is it ever okay to NOT forgive?
  4. What would you say to the well-meaning adult about the pressure to forgive?

Teacher note — not read to students. HIGH SENSITIVITY. Do NOT use if a student is in a known ongoing harmful family situation.

Lesson 4

The Digital Echo

DE-A

The Screenshot

Digital Echo Layla
The situation

Layla sent a private message venting about a conflict with a classmate. Her friend showed the screenshot to three others. By the next morning it had been shared widely. Layla's first instinct was to post a detailed public response defending herself.

Discussion questions
  1. What happens if Layla posts the public response immediately — walk through the echo chain.
  2. What is the difference between react and respond here?
  3. What information does Layla need before doing anything?
  4. What is the minimum effective response — the smallest action that addresses the harm without amplifying it?

Teacher note — not read to students. The "minimum effective response" is the key concept. De-escalation is strategic, not weakness.

DE-B

The Group Chat

Digital Echo Group Scenario
The situation

A group chat begins mocking a student not in the chat. Someone screenshots and sends it to the student being mocked. The student responds. The original poster escalates. Now it's spread to a larger group.

Discussion questions
  1. At what point could someone have interrupted this chain?
  2. What would RESPOND (not REACT) look like at each stage?
  3. What does "audience collapse" look like in this scenario?
  4. If you were in the group chat watching this happen, what is one specific thing you could do?

Teacher note — not read to students. Equity note: consider who is most likely to be the person mocked.

DE-C

The Callout Post

Digital Echo Public Accountability
The situation

Someone posted publicly calling out a classmate by name for something they did six months ago. The post has been liked and shared. The classmate has not responded. A third student who witnessed the original incident knows the callout post is partially inaccurate.

Discussion questions
  1. What are the three available responses for the classmate being called out?
  2. What is the third student's responsibility — if any?
  3. Does the partial inaccuracy of the callout change what the right response is?
  4. What would "minimum effective response" look like for each person?

Teacher note — not read to students. Grades 7-8. Public accountability vs. public attack.

Family connection

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students studied digital conflict and the Flame vs. Pause framework. At home, model the pause before responding online: "Let me think about what I actually want to happen before I say anything."

Lesson 7

Limits from Self-Respect

BD-A

The Pressure Loop

Boundary Sofia
The situation

Every time Sofia says she doesn't want to do something, her friend group applies the same pressure: "You're being so boring. You used to be fun. Don't you trust us?" She always gives in to end the discomfort.

Discussion questions
  1. What limit does Sofia need to set — and what value does it protect?
  2. Write a boundary script using the formula: "When you ______ / I ______ / Going forward, I need ______."
  3. What is the difference between this limit and retaliation against her friends?
  4. What happens to Sofia's identity if she never sets this limit?

Teacher note — not read to students. Connects to U1-L4 peer pressure. Students who remember the Integrity Statement should recognize this as values-in-action.

BD-B

The Oversharer

Boundary Marcus
The situation

Marcus has a friend who shares Marcus's private information with others — not maliciously, just carelessly. Marcus has hinted that he doesn't like it. The friend doesn't pick up on the hints and keeps doing it.

Discussion questions
  1. Why have hints not worked? What does a clear limit look like instead?
  2. Write a boundary script for Marcus to deliver directly to his friend.
  3. What value is Marcus protecting with this limit?
  4. What does Marcus lose if he never sets this limit clearly?

Teacher note — not read to students. The shift from hinting to direct communication is key for this age group.

Family connection

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students practiced setting boundaries as acts of self-respect. At home, model boundary-setting and name it: "I am setting a limit on that because I need to take care of myself."

Grace & Generosity

Lesson 1

What Is Grace?

GR-A

The Unexpected Kindness

Grace Nadia
The situation

Nadia had been excluded by her friend group for three weeks. On a particularly bad day, a classmate she barely knew sat next to her at lunch — not because anyone told him to. He just noticed. He didn't say anything about the exclusion. He just sat there.

Discussion questions
  1. Was this grace? What makes it grace rather than just politeness?
  2. What did it cost the classmate? What did Nadia receive?
  3. Why does grace that costs something feel different from grace that doesn't?
  4. What would have to be true about the classmate's character for this to happen naturally?

Teacher note — not read to students. Opening card for grace definition. The "cost" element is key.

GR-B

The Second Chance

Grace Marcus
The situation

Marcus was removed from the school play after a serious mistake in rehearsal. The drama teacher who had every right to keep him out — quietly invited him back two weeks later with one condition: "Show me with your actions, not your words." Marcus never missed another call.

Discussion questions
  1. Was the teacher's action grace, mercy, or both?
  2. What did the single condition add to the grace? Would it be grace without the condition?
  3. What does "show me with your actions" do to Marcus's relationship with the second chance?
  4. What does this tell us about the difference between grace and having no standards?

Teacher note — not read to students. "The condition is not punishment — it is dignifying expectation."

Lesson 2

What Does Mercy Look Like?

MR-A

The Overdue Apology

Mercy Priya & Jamie
The situation

A year ago, Priya publicly embarrassed Jamie during a class presentation. She didn't apologize until recently. The apology was genuine. Jamie is in a position to exclude Priya from a group project that Priya really needs and Jamie has every social right to do so.

Discussion questions
  1. Is mercy appropriate here? What would it look like?
  2. What would justice require? Can Jamie have both mercy and justice?
  3. What does mercy cost Jamie — and what might it give Priya?
  4. Does the late apology change what mercy is worth here?

Teacher note — not read to students. Whole-class model card. The mercy-justice tension is the destination — do not resolve it.

MR-B

The Repeated Mistake

Mercy Theo
The situation

Theo keeps making the same mistake — arriving late to group rehearsals. He is genuinely remorseful each time. His group is deciding whether to ask him to leave. They have given him two chances already.

Discussion questions
  1. Is a third chance mercy or is it enabling a pattern?
  2. What is the difference between mercy and having no accountability?
  3. What would mercy WITH a clear condition look like here?
  4. At what point does mercy become unfair to everyone else?

Teacher note — not read to students. Pairs after whole-class Card A. The "mercy as enabling" question is developmentally important.

MR-C

The Power Difference

Mercy Equity
The situation

A teacher had the power to fail a student for an assignment that was late due to a documented family crisis. Instead, they accepted it without penalty. The student never asked — the teacher just quietly decided.

Discussion questions
  1. Is this mercy? What makes it mercy rather than just rule-breaking?
  2. How does power difference change what mercy means — and what it costs?
  3. Equity: is this mercy available equally to all students?
  4. What does this tell us about who gets to be merciful — and why that matters?

Teacher note — not read to students. Equity alert — mercy is not equally distributed.

Family connection

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students studied mercy. At home, look for a mercy moment this week and name it: "I am choosing to extend some mercy here."

Lesson 3

Giving the Benefit of the Doubt

CH-A

The Ignored Message

Charitable Reading Layla
The situation

Layla sent a message to a close friend and got no reply for two days. Her first interpretation: "She's ignoring me. She's mad. I must have done something." A week later, she finds out her friend's grandmother had been hospitalized.

Discussion questions
  1. What was the negative interpretation? What did it cost Layla in that week?
  2. What is the charitable interpretation? How available was it before Layla knew the truth?
  3. Apply The Pause: what would each of the four steps have looked like here?
  4. Does practicing charitable interpretation mean you're always right? What does it give you even when you're wrong?

Teacher note — not read to students. Whole-class model. The Pause steps should be worked through explicitly.

CH-B

The Short Answer

Charitable Reading Cameron & Nadia
The situation

During group work, Cameron gave a short, flat answer when Nadia tried to include him. Nadia interpreted it as: "He thinks I'm annoying." Cameron was actually anxious about presenting and barely heard the question.

Discussion questions
  1. What negative interpretation did Nadia form? What does it lead her to do next?
  2. What is the charitable interpretation?
  3. What is the smallest action that tests the charitable interpretation rather than acting on the negative one?
  4. How does this change if Cameron often gives short answers to Nadia specifically?

Teacher note — not read to students. Final question is important — charitable interpretation has limits when a pattern is clearly directed at one person.

Family connection

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students practiced charitable interpretation. At home, model the pause: "That could mean a few different things. What is another explanation besides the obvious one?"

Lesson 5

Grace With Hard People

HP-A

The Persistent Critic

Hard People Priya
The situation

Every time Priya contributes in class, a classmate makes a dismissive comment — a small eye roll, a quiet sigh, a one-word rebuttal. It happens consistently. Priya finds this person irritating and demoralizing, but there is no physical threat and no ongoing harassment outside class.

Discussion questions
  1. Where does this person fall on the difficult-to-unsafe spectrum?
  2. Is grace the goal here, or is safety?
  3. What does grace look like in this situation — specifically?
  4. What would grace NOT look like? What is grace NOT asking Priya to accept?

Teacher note — not read to students. Whole-class model. Final question is critical — grace is not acceptance of mistreatment.

HP-B

The Complicated History

Hard People Two Students
The situation

Two students have a history — one hurt the other significantly two years ago. They are now in the same class. The student who was hurt has done their own processing. Now they notice the other student struggling socially and isolated. They don't want to be friends again — but they feel something.

Discussion questions
  1. What is that feeling? Is it grace — or something else?
  2. Is there a gracious posture available that does NOT require rebuilding the relationship?
  3. What is the difference between "I want good things for you" and "I want to be close to you again"?
  4. What would the smallest possible act of grace look like — if any?

Teacher note — not read to students. Builds on U3-L3 (forgiveness without reconciliation). Grace-at-a-distance is the key takeaway.

Family connection

A Note Home

For Caregivers
The situation

Today students studied grace toward difficult people. The curriculum is clear: grace is not required toward people who are unsafe. We practice grace toward people who are merely difficult. That is a meaningful distinction.
