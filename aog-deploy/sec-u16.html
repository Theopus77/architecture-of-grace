Real Government Secrets

Documented Secrets

Many stories about secret plots are false. Some government secrets were real. In the 1950s and 1960s, the CIA secretly tested drugs on people who did not know, the FBI secretly worked to break up American political groups, and top military leaders wrote a plan for fake attacks to start a war with Cuba. We know these things today not from rumor but from records: payment files, stolen FBI papers, court cases, Senate reports and a memo released in 1997.

This unit looks at those documented secrets and at the people who brought them to light. You will meet a family who learned the truth about their father's death twenty-two years later, eight citizens who broke into an FBI office, a reporter who asked what one strange word meant, and a young leader in Chicago whose death led to a long fight in court. You will also see how Congress changed its rules so that secret agencies would have to answer to elected officials.

By the end, you will be able to explain what MKUltra, COINTELPRO and Operation Northwoods were, sort claims about them into documented, contested and not supported, and use the same test of documents to judge other claims about hidden power. Real secrets leave a paper trail. Learning to follow it is a skill you can use for life.

The big question

How do we know when a government secret is real, and what does it take to bring one to light?

MKUltra
Big questionWhen a government hides what it has done, what does it take for the public to learn the truth, and how much can still be proven?
The story

The Boxes in the Money Files

In 1977 the CIA finds records of a secret program that were supposed to have been destroyed four years earlier.

It is 1977. A writer named John Marks has asked the CIA, under the Freedom of Information Act, for its records on mind-control research. The agency has an easy answer ready. Its leaders say the key files were destroyed years ago. There is nothing left to give. Still, the law requires a search, so CIA staff go back into the stored records.

The search reaches a place almost no one thought about: the money files. Every secret program still has to pay its bills. Checks go to universities, hospitals and research foundations. Receipts, contracts and payment records get filed by accountants, not by the scientists who ran the work. Those financial files were stored separately. Nobody had included them when the order came to destroy the program's papers.

Box after box, the paperwork is still there. Together the boxes hold about 20,000 documents. They name a program called MKUltra. They show payments for experiments with drugs, including LSD, and with ways to change how people think and behave. Some of the people tested never knew they were part of an experiment.

On 3 August 1977, the new CIA director, Admiral Stansfield Turner, sits before a joint hearing of two Senate committees. He tells senators what the boxes contain. Much of the program had already been exposed by investigators in 1975 and 1976. Now there is more paper. A secret that powerful people tried to erase has been rescued by the dullest records of all: the bills.

Talk about itThe CIA's leaders tried to destroy MKUltra's records, but the payment files survived. Why might ordinary records like receipts be harder to erase than a program's main files?
Section 1

A Secret Program Begins

31.1

Fear in the Early Cold War

Main ideaMKUltra began in 1953 because U.S. leaders feared the Soviet Union and its allies had found ways to control people's minds.

In the early 1950s, the United States and the Soviet Union were locked in the . This was a long rivalry between two powers that competed for influence around the world without fighting each other directly. Each side spied on the other. Each side feared the other was about to gain a secret advantage.

The Korean War (1950–1953) added a new fear. Some American prisoners held by North Korea and China signed false confessions or spoke on enemy radio. Newspapers used a new word, , to describe what might have happened. The idea was that captors could strip away a person’s beliefs and put new ones in their place. Researchers later found that harsh treatment, hunger and pressure explained most of it. At the time, though, many officials were alarmed.

CIA leaders asked a question: if the other side could control minds, could the United States learn to do it too, or at least learn to defend against it? On 13 April 1953, CIA Director Allen Dulles approved a program to study the question in secret. It was called MKUltra. The CIA is the , created in 1947 to gather and analyze information about other countries.

Understanding the fear does not excuse what came next. It helps explain it. Historians try to see a decision the way the people inside it saw it, and then judge it by what it did to real people. Both steps matter. Explaining a choice is not the same as approving it.

Think it through
Two Ways to Read One Decision

View A: In 1953, leaders believed the enemy might have a mind-control weapon. They felt they had to study it fast and in secret to protect the country. View B: Fear does not give anyone the right to test drugs on people who have not agreed. A good goal cannot make a harmful method acceptable.

Explain how a historian can take View A seriously as an explanation while still using View B to judge what happened.

31.2

What MKUltra Paid For

Main ideaMKUltra secretly paid outside researchers to study drugs, hypnosis and other ways to change how people think and act.

MKUltra was run by the CIA’s Technical Services staff. Its best-known leader was Sidney Gottlieb, a chemist. The program did not build one big laboratory. Instead, it paid outside researchers at universities, hospitals, prisons and private companies. Those projects were called . Investigators later counted about 150 of them.

Many researchers did not know the CIA was paying them. The money often passed through a , a group that looks independent but secretly serves someone else. A foundation with a scientific-sounding name could hand out grants while hiding where the money came from.

The subprojects studied many things. Some tested LSD, a powerful drug that changes perception, mood and thinking. Some studied hypnosis, sleep, stress and ways to get information from people during questioning, called . Some were ordinary science that simply had a secret sponsor. Others crossed clear lines, as the next lesson shows.

Because the program was spread across so many places and hidden behind other names, it was hard to see from the outside. That same spread later helped investigators. Each subproject left payment records behind. Money records name who was paid, when, and for what. They are hard to hide completely.

31.3

Consent and the Unwitting

Main ideaThe worst harm in MKUltra came from testing drugs on people who did not know and had not agreed.

After World War II, judges at Nuremberg, Germany, tried Nazi doctors who had done cruel experiments on prisoners. In 1947 the court set out rules for research on people. These rules are called the Nuremberg Code. The first rule is about . That means a person agrees to take part, freely, after being told what will happen and what the risks are.

MKUltra broke that rule in some of its work. The CIA wanted to know how drugs would affect people who did not expect them, as an enemy’s victim would not expect them. So some people were given LSD without being told. The word for such people is : they did not know. Records show tests on unwitting people in ordinary places, including a CIA-run apartment in San Francisco.

Other subjects were patients, prisoners or people with addictions. They might have signed a form, but they had little real choice or little real information. Researchers call such people : their situation makes it hard to say no or to understand the risk.

Not every MKUltra subproject harmed anyone. But the unwitting tests are why the program became a lasting symbol. They show what can happen when secrecy removes the usual checks on research. Rules like informed consent exist so that no one, not even a government agency, can decide alone that a person’s body may be used for a test.

From the source
The First Rule of the Nuremberg Code
The voluntary consent of the person being studied is absolutely essential. The person must be legally able to consent, free to choose without force or trickery, and must understand enough about the experiment to make an informed decision.
The Nuremberg Code, Principle 1, from the judgment in United States v. Karl Brandt et al., Nuremberg Military Tribunal, 1947 (paraphrase) (paraphrased)

Section 2

The Death of Frank Olson

31.4

A Drink at a Retreat

Main ideaIn November 1953, Army scientist Frank Olson was secretly given LSD, and nine days later he died after a fall from a New York hotel window.

Frank Olson was a scientist who worked for the U.S. Army at Camp Detrick in Maryland, studying germs that could be used as weapons. He had a wife and three children. In November 1953, he went to a work retreat at Deep Creek Lake in western Maryland with Army and CIA colleagues.

At the retreat, CIA officers from Sidney Gottlieb’s group put LSD into a drink and gave it to several of the men. Olson did not know. In the days that followed, he became deeply troubled and anxious. His colleagues took him to New York City to see a doctor who worked with the CIA.

Early on 28 November 1953, Olson fell from a tenth-floor window of the Statler Hotel in New York and died. For more than twenty years, his family was told only that he had suffered a breakdown. They were not told about the LSD. His death was treated as a private tragedy, not as the result of a secret .

Olson’s story matters because it puts a face on the word unwitting. He was a trained scientist and a government employee. He still had no chance to say no. His family would not learn the truth for more than twenty years. The next lesson tells how they found out.

31.5

The Truth Comes Out in 1975

Main ideaOlson's family learned about the LSD in 1975, received an apology from President Ford, and still disputes how he died.

In June 1975, a presidential commission released a report on CIA activities inside the United States. One passage described an Army employee who had been given LSD without his knowledge in 1953 and who died days later after jumping or falling from a hotel window. The report did not give his name. The Olson family read it and recognized their father.

The family spoke publicly. In July 1975, President Gerald Ford met them at the White House and apologized on behalf of the government. Congress later approved a payment to the family. For the first time, the government admitted in public that one of its own secret tests had led to a death.

The story did not end there. Olson’s sons came to believe the official account was still incomplete. In the 1990s they had his body examined again. They argue that he did not jump but was killed to keep secrets. Others hold that the drug and his distress explain the fall. No court or official inquiry has settled this .

So there are two kinds of fact here. It is that Olson was secretly drugged and died days later. How exactly he died remains contested. Keeping those two kinds of fact apart is one of the most useful habits a reader of history can build. Both matter.

From the source

Report to the President by the Commission on CIA Activities within the United States (Rockefeller Commission), June 1975 (paraphrase) (paraphrased)

Section 3

Destroyed, Then Found

31.6

The Order of 1973

Main ideaIn January 1973, CIA Director Richard Helms ordered MKUltra's files destroyed, which is why much of the program can never be fully known.

In January 1973, Richard Helms was leaving his job as director of the CIA. Before he left, he ordered MKUltra’s files destroyed. Sidney Gottlieb, who was retiring too, carried out the order. Most of the program’s working papers and reports were destroyed.

The timing matters. The Watergate scandal was growing in Washington. Congress and reporters were starting to ask hard questions about what the government had done in secret. Records that were destroyed could not be demanded later. Helms and Gottlieb later gave reasons, such as protecting the privacy of the researchers and subjects. Critics saw a simpler motive: removing the evidence.

Historians call the destruction of records a . It is a real hole, not a sign of anything in particular. A gap tells you that something was there. It does not tell you what was there. That difference is important for the rest of this chapter.

Destroying records is also a kind of evidence in itself. The order shows that leaders knew the files could cause trouble if the public saw them. The 1973 order shows intent to hide. It does not tell us exactly what the lost pages said. Hiding and proving are different things.

31.7

Saved by the Accountants

Main ideaAbout 20,000 documents of MKUltra records survived because they were filed with financial records rather than with the program's main files.

Helms’s order reached the program’s main files. It did not reach every piece of paper MKUltra had created. Money leaves a trail. Each subproject needed contracts, invoices and payment approvals. Those were handled by the CIA’s finance staff and stored in a different place, among retired .

In 1977, a search for records turned up these files. The search followed a Freedom of Information Act request from writer John Marks. The , passed in 1966 and strengthened in 1974, lets anyone ask a federal agency for its records. The agency must search and must explain what it holds back.

The surviving papers came to about 20,000 documents. They did not contain the full research reports. They did show who was paid, how much, for which subproject, and often a short description of the work. That was enough to identify many universities, hospitals and researchers.

The lesson is practical. When you look for evidence of a hidden program, look where money moved. Budgets, receipts and payroll are dull, but they are hard to erase, because many different offices keep copies. Many copies in many hands make a secret much harder to erase.

31.8

The Hearing of 3 August 1977

Main ideaAt a joint Senate hearing on 3 August 1977, CIA Director Stansfield Turner told senators what the recovered MKUltra records showed.

On 3 August 1977, two Senate groups met together: the new Select Committee on Intelligence and the Subcommittee on Health and Scientific Research, led by Senator Edward Kennedy. The witness was Admiral Stansfield Turner, whom President Jimmy Carter had made CIA director that year.

Turner did not defend the program. He told the senators that the recovered papers had been found in financial files and described what they covered: drug tests, including on unwitting people, research on behavior, and funding hidden through outside groups. He said the CIA would try to identify the institutions involved and let them know.

A is a meeting where lawmakers question witnesses in public or in private and create an official record. This one was printed as a government document. It became one of the main on MKUltra. A primary source is a record made at the time by people involved in the events.

The hearing also showed a new kind of relationship. A CIA director was answering questions from a permanent committee whose job was to watch his agency. Before 1976, no such Senate committee had existed. The hearing itself was a sign that the rules had changed.

Watching the Watchers

31.9

A Story and a Commission

Main ideaA December 1974 newspaper story about CIA spying at home led President Ford to appoint the Rockefeller Commission in January 1975.

In December 1974, reporter Seymour Hersh wrote in The New York Times that the CIA had spied on thousands of Americans inside the United States, mostly people who opposed the Vietnam War. The CIA’s charter was meant to keep it focused on other countries, not on Americans at home. The story caused an uproar.

In January 1975, President Gerald Ford named a commission to look into it. Vice President Nelson Rockefeller led it, so it is called the . Its report came out in June 1975. It confirmed a CIA program, called Operation CHAOS, that had gathered files on American protesters. It also described drug testing, including the case that turned out to be Frank Olson’s.

Critics said the commission was too close to the government it was studying. Several members were former officials. It worked for only a few months. Supporters said it still put key facts on the public record for the first time. Commissions like this are one way a president can answer public worry.

Notice the chain. A reporter’s story led to a commission. The commission’s report led a family to the truth. Each step made the next one possible. This is how often works: in steps, not all at once. A single story rarely tells the whole truth, but it can start the search.

31.10

The Church Committee

Main ideaThe Senate's Church Committee (1975–1976) studied the CIA, FBI and other agencies and published the fullest public account of their secret abuses.

On 27 January 1975, the Senate created a special committee to study the government’s intelligence agencies. Its chairman was Senator Frank Church of Idaho, so it is known as the . The House of Representatives set up its own inquiry, called the Pike Committee.

The Church Committee had staff, a budget and the power to demand documents and question witnesses under , a legal order to appear or to hand over records. It looked at the CIA, the FBI, the National Security Agency and others. It held public hearings and many private ones.

Its final report was issued on 29 April 1976, in six books totaling about 2,700 pages. It covered MKUltra and other drug testing, CIA plots against foreign leaders, and the FBI’s secret campaign against American groups, which the next chapter explores.

The committee’s members did not all agree. Some worried that revealing secrets could hurt the country or put agents at risk. Others said that hidden abuses were the greater danger to a free country. That debate, between and oversight, has never fully ended.

31.11

Proven and Not Proven

Main ideaThe records prove that MKUltra drugged unwitting people and hid its funding, but they do not prove that it achieved real mind control.

Because MKUltra was real and hidden, it has become the base for many larger stories. A careful reader sorts the claims into three groups. First, what is documented: the program existed from 1953; it paid about 150 subprojects; it tested drugs on some people without their knowledge; Frank Olson was drugged and died; most files were destroyed in 1973.

Second, what is : how Olson died; how many people were harmed in total; exactly what some subprojects did. Honest researchers disagree, because the evidence is incomplete. In Canada, patients at a Montreal psychiatric hospital who went through harsh treatments in work partly paid for by MKUltra later sued, and some won settlements.

Third, what is not supported by the records: that MKUltra built a working method to control people’s minds, or that it created programmed assassins. Investigators found no evidence of this. Many who worked on the program said the results were disappointing.

The destroyed files leave real gaps. Some people fill those gaps with the most dramatic answer. Historians leave a gap as a gap and say what is and is not known. Saying "we do not know" is honest. It is also a strong position when others claim to know more than the records show.

31.12

How Oversight Changed

Main ideaAfter 1975, Congress built permanent intelligence committees and new laws so that secret agencies would answer to elected officials.

The investigations did more than tell stories. They changed how the government works. In May 1976, the Senate created a permanent Select Committee on Intelligence. In 1977, the House created its own permanent intelligence committee. Agencies now had to report to these committees on a regular basis.

In 1976, President Ford issued an executive order that banned U.S. government employees from taking part in political assassination. In 1978, Congress passed the , known as FISA. It requires the government to get approval from a special court before it listens in on people inside the United States for intelligence purposes.

Research rules changed too. In the 1970s, federal rules required review boards to approve research on human beings before it starts. These boards check for informed consent. The shock of cases like MKUltra helped push those protections forward. Consent became a rule to check, not a favor to ask.

Oversight is not a machine that runs itself. It depends on members of Congress asking hard questions, on judges, on reporters and on people inside agencies who speak up. The system of the 1970s made abuse harder to hide. It did not make it impossible.

From the source
What FISA Requires

What COINTELPRO Did

32.4

From Watching to Disrupting

Main ideaCOINTELPRO went beyond investigating crimes: the FBI secretly worked to disrupt and discredit lawful groups and their members.

Investigating a crime means gathering evidence so a court can decide if someone broke the law. COINTELPRO did something different. Its goal was to groups, meaning to break them apart, weaken them and damage their reputations, whether or not their members had committed any crime.

The Church Committee described the methods. Agents sent anonymous letters to stir up quarrels between members or between rival groups. They gave damaging stories to friendly reporters. They tried to get people fired from jobs. They placed , people who secretly report to the police, inside groups.

Hoover and his agents saw themselves as defending the country. They believed some of these groups were controlled by foreign enemies or were heading toward violence. Some groups did include people who broke laws. But the FBI also targeted people for their ideas and for lawful speech and protest.

The Church Committee concluded that many of these methods would be wrong in a free country even if every target had been violent. The problem was not only who was targeted. It was that a secret agency was acting as judge and punisher without any court.

Real secrets leave traces. Money is paid, memos are written, and people remember and sometimes speak. Those traces can be found by reporters, courts, Congress and ordinary citizens. Why: The FBI added white hate groups, including the Klan, in 1964, alongside groups on the left.

4. What did lawsuits show about the FBI's role in the Hampton raid?

Why: Records from Hampton v. Hanrahan showed informant William O'Neal gave the FBI a floor plan.

5. How are COINTELPRO and Operation Northwoods different?

Why: The FBI ran COINTELPRO from 1956 to 1971; Northwoods was a 1962 military proposal Kennedy's government rejected.

6. Why did the Northwoods memo stay hidden until 1997?

Why: The memo was classified and released in November 1997 by the Assassination Records Review Board.

7. Which statement is an example of evidence-based reasoning about Northwoods?

Why: That claim matches the evidence exactly; the others reach too far or ignore the document.

8. Which of these helped expose secrets in this chapter?

Why: Carl Stern's FOIA lawsuit and the Church Committee's report brought COINTELPRO's details to light.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Which fear helped lead the CIA to start MKUltra in 1953?

Why: Reports of 'brainwashing' in the Korean War made officials fear the other side had a mind-control method.

2. What made MKUltra's tests on unwitting people wrong under the Nuremberg Code?

Why: The Nuremberg Code's first rule requires voluntary, informed consent, which unwitting subjects never gave.

3. Which fact about Frank Olson is still contested?

Why: The drugging and death are documented; his sons argue he was killed, and no inquiry has settled it.

4. Why did some MKUltra records survive the 1973 destruction order?

Why: About 20,000 pages of payment records were stored separately and found in 1977.

5. Who told senators about the recovered MKUltra files on 3 August 1977?

Why: CIA Director Stansfield Turner testified at the joint Senate hearing.

6. What was the Church Committee?

Why: Senator Frank Church led the Senate committee that published a six-book report in 1976.

7. Which reform came out of the 1970s investigations?

Why: The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 put a court between agencies and spying inside the U.S.

8. How was the word COINTELPRO first discovered by the public?

Why: A routing slip among the Media files carried the word, and reporters began to ask about it.

9. Which best describes COINTELPRO's methods?

Why: The Church Committee described anonymous letters, planted stories, job pressure and informants.

10. Which statement about COINTELPRO's targets is accurate?

Why: Programs began between 1956 and 1968 against the Communist Party, the Klan, Black-led groups and the New Left.

11. What did the case Hampton v. Hanrahan reveal?

Why: Documents from the lawsuit showed informant William O'Neal gave the FBI a floor plan before the raid.

12. How was the Hampton case finally ended in 1982?

Why: The federal government, Cook County and Chicago each paid one-third of $1.85 million.

13. What happened to the Operation Northwoods proposal?

Why: Kennedy said military force was not under discussion; the memo was released in November 1997.

14. How are MKUltra and Operation Northwoods different?

Why: MKUltra ran from 1953; Northwoods was a 1962 proposal that was never carried out.

15. Which conclusion best matches the evidence in this unit?

Why: The unit shows real secrets were exposed by records, which is also the test for other claims.
