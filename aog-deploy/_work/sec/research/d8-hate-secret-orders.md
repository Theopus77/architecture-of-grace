## Summary

1. The **first Ku Klux Klan** began as a social club of six Confederate veterans in **Pulaski, Tennessee (late 1865 or spring 1866)**. By 1868 it was a terror network that used secrecy, disguise and night raids to stop Black citizens from voting, learning, owning land and holding office.
2. Congress fought back. The **Enforcement Acts (1870-71)**, including the **Ku Klux Klan Act of 20 April 1871**, were followed by the **Joint Select Committee's 13-volume report (1872)**. That report holds hundreds of sworn statements from survivors, recorded in their own words. It is the greatest primary source on this topic.
3. The **second Klan** was launched by **William J. Simmons on Stone Mountain, Georgia, on Thanksgiving night 1915**. The film ***The Birth of a Nation*** (released February 1915) had just glorified the first Klan. From 1920 professional recruiters sold Klan membership like a lodge.
4. The ***New York World*** **exposé (September 1921)** led to **House Rules Committee hearings (October 1921)** and a **Pulitzer Prize**. It also, ironically, made the Klan more famous.
5. Illinois had a large 1920s Klan: tens of thousands in Chicago, and the **Williamson County "Klan war" (1923-26)** in southern Illinois. Chicago was also home to one of the loudest anti-Klan groups, the **American Unity League**.
6. The Klan marched down Pennsylvania Avenue on **8 August 1925**. That same year, **Indiana Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson** was convicted of the murder of **Madge Oberholtzer**. Her sworn **dying declaration** helped bring the Klan down.
7. **Stetson Kennedy** said he went undercover inside the Georgia Klan in 1946-47 and fed its secrets to the *Adventures of Superman* radio show. Later researchers showed that parts of his story came from other informants. How much he embellished is still **DISPUTED**.
8. The **Know-Nothings (Order of the Star Spangled Banner, 1850s)** were a secret, oath-bound, anti-Catholic and anti-immigrant order. In 1855 it elected **Chicago's mayor, Levi Boone**. His policies set off the **Lager Beer Riot**.
9. **Ida B. Wells**, the **NAACP**, Black newspapers, federal prosecutors, reporters, Catholic and Jewish civic groups, and ordinary witnesses exposed these groups. Wells did her later work from Chicago.
10. The ***Protocols of the Elders of Zion*** is a forged "secret society" document. It was first printed in Russia in **1903**. **Philip Graves (*The Times*, August 1921)** proved it was copied from **Maurice Joly's 1864 satire**. A **Bern court (1935)** called it a forgery. **Henry Ford** spread it in America and then publicly retracted in **1927**. It is still the key case study in how a lie about a secret society spreads. Note for the owner: Bill Cooper's *Behold a Pale Horse* (1991) reprinted the *Protocols* (see dossier D1). That is a reason to teach this unit with extra care.

### A. The first Klan and Reconstruction

**1. Joint Select Committee to Inquire into the Condition of Affairs in the Late Insurrectionary States, *Report* and testimony, 13 vols.** Washington: Government Printing Office, 1872. 42nd Congress, 2nd Session. Printed as both a House and a Senate report; commonly cited as H. Rpt. 22 / S. Rpt. 41. Title, 13 volumes and GPO 1872 VERIFIED 2026-09-30 (https://dlg.usg.edu/record/dlg_klan); report numbers STILL UNVERIFIED (checked 2026-09-30: not in search results).
- The volumes are usually described as follows. Vol. 1 holds the majority and minority reports. Vol. 2 is North Carolina, vols. 3-5 South Carolina, 6-7 Georgia, 8-10 Alabama, 11-12 Mississippi, and 13 Florida. UNVERIFIED volume split; check.
- Where: HathiTrust and archive.org (full scans of every volume; search "Condition of Affairs in the Late Insurrectionary States"); the ProQuest Congressional Serial Set; many Federal Depository libraries (in Illinois, UIUC and the Chicago Public Library government documents collections are likely holders).
- What it shows: sworn testimony from hundreds of Black and white Southerners. Survivors describe raids, whippings and murders, and name their attackers. The Democratic minority report denies that the Klan was organized, so the same volumes hold both sides of the argument.
- Band: 9-10 for selected testimony (Elias Hill, Hannah Tutson, Abram Colby). The introduction to the report works at 6-8.

**2. Testimony of Elias Hill, York County, South Carolina (July 1871), in the Joint Select Committee's South Carolina volumes.** Attack (6 May 1871), testimony to the committee sitting in York County, and emigration to Arthington, Liberia, in autumn 1871 VERIFIED 2026-09-30 (https://ldhi.library.cofc.edu/exhibits/show/after_slavery_educator/unit_nine_documents/document_9; Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Hill). Month of testimony, volume and page STILL UNVERIFIED (checked 2026-09-30: search results did not give them). Hill died in Liberia, 28 March 1872 (Wikipedia).
- What it shows: Hill was a Black Baptist preacher and schoolteacher. Childhood illness had left him unable to walk or use his arms. Klansmen dragged him from his cabin and beat him in May 1871. He testified in detail, and later he led a group from York County who emigrated to Liberia.
- Why it matters for this site: he is a disabled teacher at the centre of the story. Present him as a leader and witness, not as a figure of pity.
- Band: 9-10.

**3. Testimony of Hannah Tutson, Clay County, Florida (Florida volume).** KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.
- What it shows: a Black woman describes being whipped by disguised men who wanted her family's land. It is one of the clearest accounts showing that the violence was also about land and work.
- Band: 9-10.

**4. Testimony of Abram Colby, Georgia (Georgia volumes).** KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.
- What it shows: a formerly enslaved Georgia legislator describes being whipped in 1869 because he would not stop supporting Republicans. It is often quoted for his refusal to be bribed.
- Band: 9-10.

**5. Testimony of Nathan Bedford Forrest before the Joint Select Committee (27 June 1871).** KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.
- What it shows: Forrest denies knowing about the order or claims he cannot remember. Set it beside his 1868 *Cincinnati Commercial* interview, where he spoke confidently of Klan numbers. Together they make a good exercise in comparing sources.
- Band: 9-10.

**6. The Klan *Prescript* (1867) and the *Revised and Amended Prescript* (1868).** Small printed rulebooks with the order's titles (Grand Wizard, Grand Dragon, and so on), its structure and its oath. They are reprinted in Lester and Wilson (1905 edition, with Fleming's introduction) and in Fleming's other writings. KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.
- Where: surviving originals are reported at the Tennessee State Library and Archives and in a few rare-book collections (UNVERIFIED). The reprint is on archive.org and HathiTrust.
- What it shows: the Klan copied the ritual forms of fraternal lodges (see dossier D3) and put them to the use of terror.
- Band: 11-12 (structure only; do not read the oath aloud).

**7. J. C. Lester and D. L. Wilson, *Ku Klux Klan: Its Origin, Growth and Disbandment*.** Nashville: Wheeler, Osborn & Duckworth, 1884. 2nd ed., New York and Washington: Neale, 1905, introduction by Walter L. Fleming. KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.
- What it shows: the founders' own account (Lester was one of the six). It is self-serving: it calls the Klan a prank that "got out of hand". Teach it as a source to be questioned.
- Band: 11-12.

**8. The Enforcement Acts and the Ku Klux Klan Act.** Act of 31 May 1870 (16 Stat. 140); Act of 28 Feb. 1871 (16 Stat. 433); Act of 20 April 1871 (17 Stat. 13). 16 Stat. 140 and 17 Stat. 13 VERIFIED 2026-09-30 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enforcement_Act_of_1870; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ku_Klux_Klan_Act); 16 Stat. 433 STILL UNVERIFIED (checked 2026-09-30: not in search results).
- Where: *U.S. Statutes at Large* (Library of Congress "A Century of Lawmaking" collection; govinfo).
- What it shows: section 1 of the 1871 Act survives as **42 U.S.C. § 1983**, the law most civil-rights lawsuits use today. A short, true "then and now" link.
- Band: 9-10.

**9. Ulysses S. Grant, proclamation suspending habeas corpus in nine South Carolina counties, 17 October 1871.** VERIFIED 2026-09-30 (Proclamation 201, https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/proclamation-201-suspending-the-writ-habeas-corpus-certain-counties-south-carolina).
- Where: the *Messages and Papers of the Presidents* (Richardson); the American Presidency Project (UC Santa Barbara).
- Band: 9-10.

### B. The second Klan (1915-1930s)

**10. U.S. House of Representatives, Committee on Rules, *The Ku-Klux Klan: Hearings*, 67th Congress, 1st Session, October 1921.** Washington: GPO, 1921. VERIFIED 2026-09-30 (https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/100479396; hearings opened 11 Oct. 1921; 184 pp.; Arno Press reprint 1969).
- Where: HathiTrust, archive.org, Google Books; Serial Set libraries.
- What it shows: reporters from the *New York World* and critics give evidence, and so does William J. Simmons. Simmons claims the Klan is a patriotic fraternal order. Students can test that claim against the evidence of the hearing itself.

## Story hooks

1. **Elias Hill waits for the committee (York County, South Carolina, July 1871).** Hill cannot walk. He is carried in to give his testimony. The committee's clerk writes it down word for word: what happened on the night the disguised men came, and why he still means to teach and preach. A few months later he leads neighbours who sail for Liberia. *(9-10)*
2. **A pamphlet printed in exile (Memphis to New York, 1892).** Ida B. Wells is in New York when she learns that a mob has wrecked the office of her paper, *Free Speech*, and threatened her life. Instead of going home, she gathers the white press's own lynching reports into *Southern Horrors*. *(6-8)*
3. **Thanksgiving night on the mountain (Stone Mountain, Georgia, 25 Nov. 1915).** A few men climb a granite dome in the cold and set up a cross. Their leader, a failed preacher and lodge organizer, is selling a new "fraternal order". Ask: what was he really selling? *(9-10)*
4. **Reporters at a desk with the Klan's price list (New York, September 1921).** *World* reporters lay out membership forms and fees ($10 a head). Then they tally a list of floggings and tar-and-featherings. The series runs for three weeks, and Congress calls hearings. *(6-8)*
5. **The cigar store in Herrin (Illinois, 24 January 1925).** Two armed men, the Klan's raid leader S. Glenn Young and Deputy Ora Thomas, meet in a cigar store in a coal town. Within seconds both are dead, and so are two of Young's companions (CORRECTED 2026-09-30: was "both are dead" only; four died, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williamson_County_Jail). The whole county had been choosing sides for a year. *(9-10; describe it without detail of the injuries)*
6. **A dying woman's statement (Indianapolis, April 1925).** Madge Oberholtzer, 28, who ran a state programme against illiteracy, gives lawyers a sworn statement about what the state's most powerful man did to her. Her words send him to prison. *(11-12 for the full account; 9-10 for "one testimony ended an empire")*
7. **The battered little book in Constantinople (1921).** A Russian refugee hands *The Times* correspondent Philip Graves an old French book with its title page missing. Graves opens it and finds whole passages of the "secret Jewish plan", written 40 years earlier as a satire about a French emperor. *(9-10)*
8. **Superman fights the Klan on the radio (1946).** Children listening at home hear the Klan's secret words mocked on air. Later we learn the story behind it is more tangled than the hero's version. *(6-8; add the Kennedy debate at 9-10)*
9. **The German beer gardens of Chicago (21 April 1855).** A Know-Nothing mayor raises the saloon licence fee and orders Sunday closing. Immigrant saloon keepers are arrested. Crowds march on the courthouse and meet police at the Clark Street bridge. *(6-8)*
10. **Notre Dame students against the Klan (South Bend, Indiana, May 1924).** Catholic students go downtown to face a Klan rally in a state the Klan largely ran. *(9-10; the details need checking)*

## Hard-to-find finds

1. **The minority report inside the 1872 Klan volumes.** Most lessons quote only survivor testimony. Volume 1 also holds the Democratic minority's rebuttal, which denies that any organized conspiracy existed. The 13 volumes let students watch the same evidence argued both ways in 1872. Few classrooms ever see this pairing.
2. **Elias Hill's testimony.** A disabled Black teacher and preacher is the central witness. For a special-education site, this is the most powerful single source in the unit: he is a man of courage and learning, not a victim to be pitied.
3. **Graves's parallel columns (*The Times*, 16-18 Aug. 1921).** Printed side by side, Joly's 1864 lines and the *Protocols* lines make the forgery visible to a 14-year-old in under a minute. It is the best "how do we know it's fake?" exhibit in the whole course.
4. **The 1964 Senate committee print on the *Protocols*.** It is little known: a U.S. Senate committee, at the height of the Cold War, formally called the document a fabrication and traced its spread.
5. **The 1921 House Rules Committee hearings.** William J. Simmons's own testimony, with his theatrical denials and claims to be a fraternal order, sits in the same record as the *World*'s evidence. It is a free government document, and few people read it.
6. **The Ford-Marshall apology papers.** The draft of Ford's 1927 retraction was written by the Jewish leader Louis Marshall. Among the papers is a document that asked a powerful man to put his name to the truth.
7. **Stephenson's dying-declaration case.** It is an Indiana Supreme Court opinion that ended a Klan empire on the testimony of one woman. Treat it with care and use it at 11-12 only.
8. **"Hoodwinked?" (2006) against *I Rode with the Ku Klux Klan* (1954).** This is a rare case in which the tellers of a famous story publicly corrected it, which makes it good material on memoir, heroism and evidence.
9. **Lincoln's "I am not a Know-Nothing."** Flagged quotation. Checked 2026-09-30 by search only: the wording "I am not a Know-Nothing. That is certain." and "When the Know-Nothings get control, it will read 'all men are created equal, except negroes, and foreigners, and catholics.'" appeared in search text drawn from the Papers of Abraham Lincoln (https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/documents/D200860) and House Divided (https://housedivided.dickinson.edu/sites/lincoln/letter-to-joshua-speed-august-24-1855/). Every page itself was blocked, so this is STILL UNVERIFIED against a scan; open *Collected Works* vol. 2, pp. 320-323 once before printing. Third pass (2026-09-30): pages still blocked (quod.lib.umich.edu refused). Search text from the NPS Lincoln Home page (https://home.nps.gov/liho/historyculture/knownothingparty.htm) and the Massachusetts Historical Society (https://www.masshist.org/database/456) gives the fuller run "I am not a Know-Nothing. That is certain. How could I be? How can any one who abhors the oppression of negroes, be in favor of degrading classes of white people?" and "As a nation, we began by declaring that 'all men are created equal.' We now practically read it 'all men are created equal, except negroes.'" Still STILL UNVERIFIED word for word against a primary transcription. Note that the Declaration line begins "When the Know-Nothings get control, it will read". The widely reproduced text includes the line *"I am not a Know-Nothing. That is certain."* and continues that under Know-Nothing rule the Declaration would "read 'all men are created equal, except negroes, and foreigners, and catholics.'" (Basler, vol. 2; page to confirm). The word "negroes" is Lincoln's 1855 usage. Tell students this before they read it.
