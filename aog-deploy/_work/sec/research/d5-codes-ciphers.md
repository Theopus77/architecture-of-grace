# Codes, Ciphers, Signs and Passwords of Secret Societies

Dossier D5 for the K-12 course "Secret Societies" (Architecture of Grace). Compiled 2026-09-30.

**How to read this file.** Every item carries a status:

- **DOCUMENTED**: a primary source exists and a findable catalogue record or digitized copy was located.
- **SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS**: peer-reviewed or specialist scholarship agrees.
- **DISPUTED**: people disagree; who says what is given.
- **UNSUPPORTED / DISPROVEN**: no evidence, or evidence against.
- **UNVERIFIED**: seen only in secondary reporting; a team member must open the original before it goes into a lesson.

**Research limits, stated honestly.** During this session the network proxy blocked direct page fetches from archive.org, HathiTrust, Library of Congress, Project Gutenberg, ACL Anthology, Wikipedia and most other sites. Quotations are therefore listed in a separate "to verify" table, never as final text. Before any lesson is published, someone must open each linked original.

## Summary

1. Secret societies protected their words in three ways: **written ciphers** (pigpen, the Copiale cipher), **body signs** (grips, handshakes, ways of holding a tea cup), and **spoken passwords and verse** (catechisms, recited poems).
2. The **pigpen cipher** (also called the Masonic or Freemasons' cipher) swaps each letter for a piece of a tic-tac-toe grid or an X. A version appears in Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* (1530s). Freemasons used it widely from the 1700s.
3. The best classroom artifact is the **James Leeson gravestone** (d. 1794) in Trinity Churchyard, New York. Its pigpen line reads "REMEMBER DEATH." The Trinity Record newspaper reported the solution in 1889.
4. The **Copiale cipher** is a 105-page, roughly 75,000-character German manuscript from about 1730 (CORRECTED 2026-09-30: was "about 1760-1780"; the Stockholm University project page says it "can be dated back to around 1730"; early 2011 reports gave 1760-1780; https://www.su.se/english/research/research-catalogue/research-projects/d/decipherment-of-historical-manuscripts/the-copiale-cipher). In 2011 Kevin Knight (USC), Beáta Megyesi and Christiane Schaefer (Uppsala) broke it with computers and language skills. It holds the rituals of the **Oculists**, a society built around eyes and seeing.
5. The **"Templar cipher"** is a modern name for a pigpen variant. No medieval evidence ties it to the Knights Templar.
6. **Rosicrucian and alchemical symbols** (e.g., *Geheime Figuren der Rosenkreuzer*, Altona, 1785-88) are picture-languages more than ciphers. They are digitized at the Wellcome Collection and Internet Archive.
7. **Grips and passwords** of Freemasonry were printed by critics in exposés: Morgan's *Illustrations of Masonry* (1826/27) and Duncan's *Masonic Ritual and Monitor* (1866). Morgan's disappearance sparked the Anti-Masonic movement.
8. The **Tiandihui (Heaven and Earth Society)**, founded in Fujian in 1761, used hand signs, tea-cup arrangements, split characters and recited verses. Dutch interpreter Gustaaf Schlegel published seized documents in 1866. Singapore's National Museum holds William Stirling's notes on "forms of recognition."
9. **Hobo signs** are a sign system, not a secret society. 1890s newspapers printed charts of them. But writer Josiah Flynt, who rode with tramps, called most of the signs a "fabrication." Treat them as **DISPUTED**.
10. For **K-2**, the core lesson is the difference between a **fun secret or surprise** (a code game, a birthday gift) and an **unsafe secret** (one that makes you feel worried, or that an adult says to keep from your grown-ups). Always tell a safe adult about unsafe secrets.

### B. The Copiale cipher

**P7. The Copiale manuscript (c.1730; CORRECTED 2026-09-30: was c.1760-1780; https://www.su.se/english/research/research-catalogue/research-projects/d/decipherment-of-historical-manuscripts/the-copiale-cipher).**
- Physical: 105 pages, about 75,000 characters, about 90 distinct cipher symbols, bound in gold-and-green brocade paper (VERIFIED 2026-09-30: Stockholm University project page, "a 105 pages manuscript containing around 75,000 characters, beautifully bound in green and gold brocade cover ... two different watermarks"). The society is the "high enlightened" (Hocherleuchtete) Oculist order of Wolfenbüttel; a parallel manuscript is reported at the Staatsarchiv Wolfenbüttel (Wikipedia only). Found in the East Berlin academy after the Cold War; reported now in a private collection.
- Stockholm/Uppsala project page: https://www.su.se/english/research/research-catalogue/research-projects/d/decipherment-of-historical-manuscripts/the-copiale-cipher
- Status: DOCUMENTED (the manuscript); current owner UNVERIFIED.
- Band: **3-5** for the "eye society" story and symbol-matching; 6-8 for how it was cracked.

**P8. Kevin Knight, Beáta Megyesi, Christiane Schaefer, "The Copiale Cipher," *Proceedings of the 4th Workshop on Building and Using Comparable Corpora* (BUCC), ACL, Portland, OR, June 2011, pp. 2-9.**
- URL: https://aclanthology.org/W11-1202/ (free PDF). Venue and pages VERIFIED 2026-09-30 (ACL Anthology record in search results: Proceedings of the 4th Workshop on Building and Using Comparable Corpora, Portland, Oregon, June 2011, pp. 2-9). PDF itself not openable (host blocked).
- Shows: method. Scholarly consensus summary: the plain Roman letters were mostly decoys/spaces; the real text was German hidden in symbols and accented letters, with several symbols per common letter (a homophonic cipher). The breakthrough came when a symbol turned out to mean "eye."
- Status: DOCUMENTED; the method summary is SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS but should be checked against the PDF.
- Band: 9-10 (method); 6-8 (story).

**P9. Knight, Megyesi, Schaefer, "The Secrets of the Copiale Cipher," *Journal for Research into Freemasonry and Fraternalism* 2.2 (2011, issued 2012).**
- URL: https://journal.equinoxpub.com/JRFF/article/view/7999
- Shows: the content: initiation rituals of the Oculists, linked to Count Friedrich August von Veltheim.
- Status: DOCUMENTED.
- Band: 6-8.

**P10. Press coverage with translated excerpts: NPR (2011) transcript https://www.npr.org/transcripts/142284553 ; USC news release https://today.usc.edu/?p=6245 ; NBC News https://www.nbcnews.com/science/cosmic-log/secret-societys-code-cracked-flna6C10402805**

### C. The "Templar cipher" legend

**P11. No primary source.** The "Templar cipher" is a pigpen variant built on a Maltese-cross/cross pattée shape. Sources such as dCode (https://www.dcode.fr/templar-cipher) and Histories & Castles (https://historiesandcastles.com/blogs/knights-templar/templar-secret-codes-myth-or-masonic-invention) agree no medieval document shows it.
- Status: attribution to the medieval Templars **UNSUPPORTED**.
- Band: 6-8 (great "how do legends start?" lesson).

### D. Rosicrucian and alchemical symbols

**P12. *Geheime Figuren der Rosenkreuzer, aus dem 16ten und 17ten Jahrhundert* (Altona: J. D. A. Eckhardt, 1785-1788).**

## Story hooks

1. **The riddle in the churchyard (New York, 1794/1889).** Wall Street traffic roars past a small old stone. Among the carved compasses and an hourglass is a line of little boxes and dots. For almost a hundred years people walk by without reading it. Then the church's own newspaper prints the answer: REMEMBER DEATH. *(Band 3-5; can soften to "a message on a stone" for K-2.)*

2. **The eyebrow test (Germany, 1700s; decoded 2011).** A candidate is led in. He is shown a blank page and asked, "Can you read the writing?" He cannot. He is given glasses. Still nothing. His eyes are wiped with a cloth. Finally the master says an "operation" is needed, and plucks one hair from his eyebrow. That is the whole "operation." The Oculists were teaching about learning to see. *(Band 3-5. Quote text UNVERIFIED; see table.)*

3. **The word "eye" (Uppsala and Los Angeles, 2011).** A computer tells Kevin Knight the hidden language is German. Beáta Megyesi notices one symbol keeps landing where "eye" (*Auge*) should be. Christiane Schaefer links it to a real society of the "Oculists." Almost 300 years of silence end with one small word. (CORRECTED 2026-09-30: was "260 years", which assumed a 1760s date; the book is now dated about 1730, https://www.su.se/english/research/research-catalogue/research-projects/d/decipherment-of-historical-manuscripts/the-copiale-cipher) *(Band 6-8.)*

4. **The book that got a man kidnapped (Batavia, NY, 1826).** William Morgan tells people he will print the secret words and handshakes of the Masons. Before the book is out, he is taken from the jail in Canandaigua and never seen again. The book sells anyway, and a new political party forms against secret societies. *(Band 9-10.)*

5. **Three fingers on a tea cup (Southeast Asia, 1800s).** A traveler walks into a tea shop in a strange town. He lifts the cup's cover with three fingers and sets it down just so. Across the room, someone answers with a sign of his own. Two strangers now know they are "brothers." *(Band 6-8.)*

6. **The interpreter's desk (Batavia, 1863).** A 22-year-old Dutch interpreter, Gustaaf Schlegel, is handed papers taken from an arrested man's house. They are covered in split characters, poems and diagrams. His translation becomes the first big Western book on the Heaven and Earth Society. *(Band 6-8. Schlegel's age derived from his 1840 birth; check.)*

7. **The officer's briefcase (Singapore, 1920s).** William Stirling carries a briefcase of notes on how triad members recognized each other. It is now in the National Museum of Singapore, and you can see it online. *(Band 6-8.)*

8. **"A-No. 1" was here (Los Angeles, 1914/2016).** Under a bridge, anthropologist Susan Phillips shines a light on grease-pencil writing. It is the road name of America's most famous hobo, with a date: 1914. *(Band 3-5.)*

9. **The man who said "it's made up" (1899).** Josiah Flynt rode the rails with tramps for years. Newspapers were printing charts of secret tramp signs. Flynt said most tramps did not use them. Who do you believe: the newspaper or the man who was there? *(Band 6-8.)*

## Illinois & Chicago

Only connections confirmed in this session:

- **Chicago's Hobohemia.** Nels Anderson's *The Hobo* (University of Chicago Press, 1923) studies homeless and migrant men on the "main stem" at Halsted and West Madison. It describes a Hobo College ("Hobo University") with teachers, supported by Dr. Ben L. Reitman. Good 6-8 source for "was there really a hobo code?" UIC's Maxwell Street/Halsted local history site: https://maxwellhalsted.uic.edu/home/immigrants-in-chicago/hobohemia/index.html
- **Not confirmed, do not use yet:** an Illinois pigpen gravestone; Chicago holdings of Masonic cipher books; a Chicago Tiandihui/tong code source. Worth asking the Chicago History Museum and the Newberry Library (the Newberry has large fraternal-order collections; holdings not checked here).

## Hard-to-find finds

1. **William Stirling's notes on "forms of recognition" (National Museum of Singapore, 1996-02885-038) and his briefcase.** A police officer's actual working papers on triad hand and tea signs, online with images. Almost never used in US classrooms. Lets students see how an outsider tried to learn a secret sign language.
2. **Pocket cipher books at the Scottish Rite Masonic Museum & Library (Lexington, MA).** A lodge member's 6¼ x 4 inch cipher manuscript, a Blue Lodge cipher book, a Scottish Rite cipher of 1870, and a whole ritual book in cipher from about 1920. Shows ciphers as a living memory tool, not just a legend. Images can be requested.
3. **John Browne's *Master-Key* (1798/1802) with Slater's 2019 decipherment study.** A printed book, openly sold, where the key lived only with members. A perfect contrast with exposés like Morgan and Duncan.
4. **The 1893-94 "tramp sign" newspaper articles vs. Josiah Flynt's 1899 denial.** A ready-made source-conflict lesson with public-domain texts on both sides.
5. **The Copiale technical paper (ACL 2011), free online.** Rarely used in schools, yet short and clear. Its tables let older students see how letter-frequency counting cracked a real cipher.
6. **"Freemasonry in China," *Freemasons' Quarterly Review* (1845).** A very early Western report of tea-cup and finger signs; also a good example of outsiders over-reading a link between two societies.
7. **The NSPCC autism and Makaton guides to the PANTS rule.** Not rare, but little known in the US, and a close fit for this site's learners.
