# Initiation Societies of the World: Poro and Sande, the Heaven and Earth Society, Ekpe and Nsibidi, and Others

Dossier D6 for the Architecture of Grace course "Secret Societies". Compiled 2026-09-30.

**How this was researched, and its limits.** Web search worked this session. Direct fetches of archive sites did not: archive.org, HathiTrust, the British Museum, the RAI archive, Zenodo, Oregon Digital, the Art Institute API and Chronicling America were all blocked by the network proxy. So every citation below was confirmed through search-engine catalogue records and museum and publisher pages, **not** by opening the scanned page. Each item is labelled:

- **CONFIRMED (catalogue)**: the title, date and publisher showed up in a library, museum, publisher or journal record during this session.
- **KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED**: standard in the scholarship, but not confirmed this session. Check it before a lesson prints it.
- **UNVERIFIED**: plausible, but check it before use.

No direct quotations appear, because none could be checked word for word against a digitized original (see Open questions). No URL here was guessed. Every URL came back from a search result.

## Summary

1. "Secret society" is an outsider's label. Most groups in this dossier are **initiation societies**: schools, courts, and places for mutual aid and worship. Only the initiates hold some of the knowledge, and that is where much of the groups' authority comes from.
2. **Poro** (men) and **Sande/Bondo** (women) of Sierra Leone, Liberia and Guinea run "bush schools". There, young people learn adult roles, crafts, law and how to behave. Historically, Poro also held judicial and political power (Little 1965–66).
3. The **Sande sowei helmet mask** is one of the very few masks in Africa owned and danced by women. Male carvers make it. Many museums hold examples, including the **Art Institute of Chicago**.
4. The **Tiandihui (Heaven and Earth Society)**, ancestor of the "Hongmen" and later the "Triads", claimed a 1670s founding by Shaolin monks loyal to the Ming dynasty. Qing government archives show a mutual-aid brotherhood founded around **1761–62** in Zhangpu county, Fujian (Murray and Qin 1994).
5. The Qing state first "discovered" the Tiandihui during the **Lin Shuangwen uprising in Taiwan (1786–88)**. Much of what we know comes from the confessions of arrested members.
6. **Ekpe (Leopard society)** of the Cross River region (Efik, Ejagham, Efut; now Nigeria and Cameroon) governed towns, enforced trade debts and judged disputes. It was central to Old Calabar's Atlantic trade, including the slave trade.
7. **Nsibidi** is a system of signs from the Cross River region. It has public forms and forms kept secret by initiates. British officers first wrote it down in **1909 and 1911** (Macgregor; Dayrell).
8. Enslaved Cross River people rebuilt Ekpe in Cuba as **Abakuá**. The first lodge was in **Regla, 1836**, and the society is still active today.
9. Brief extras: the **Hidden Christians of Japan** (a faith kept secret to survive), and the **Bwami** of the Lega and **Ogboni** of the Yoruba (flagged for more checking).
10. **Respect rule:** members of these societies still live and practise today. Lessons describe what communities share openly. They never reproduce sacred songs, secret signs, or rites that are closed to outsiders, and they never ask students to act out initiation.

### A. Poro and Sande (Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea)

**A1. Sande helmet mask (sowei), Art Institute of Chicago, object 146941.** Wood, early to mid-20th century, Mende or Sherbro, Sierra Leone. 48.9 × 28 × 30.5 cm. Arts of Africa collection, currently off view.
URL: https://www.artic.edu/artworks/146941/helmet-mask-sowei
Shows: the museum label says senior Sande officials danced such masks at initiations, at funerals and for visiting guests. It calls this "one of the rare instances in Africa when women danced with sculpted masks". It also notes that the civil war (1991–2002) disrupted this practice. A **Chicago** object students can look up. CONFIRMED (museum page via search).
Band: **3-5** (looking at the art: hair, neck rings, a shining black surface as ideals of beauty and dignity). The initiation context is for **6-8**.

**A2. Sande helmet masks, British Museum, Af1901,0722.10.a-b and Af1956,27.18.**
URLs: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1901-0722-10-a-b and https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1956-27-18
Shows: masks that entered a British collection in 1901. This is during early colonial rule of the Sierra Leone Protectorate (declared 1896) and just after the 1898 Hut Tax War. Good for a lesson on *how objects travelled*. Records found; details not opened (blocked). The 1901 date is inferred from the registration number and still needs checking.
Band: **6-8**.

**A3. Other museum sowei masks found this session** (records seen in search; not opened): Princeton University Art Museum (object 56351); Cornell, Johnson Museum (objects 24143, 27269, 31949); Museum of Fine Arts Boston (object 4729); Smith College ("Sande Helmet Mask (Sowo-wui), Dance Mask of Ligba Rank (Ndoli Jowei)", https://sites.smith.edu/interpreting-collections/?p=190); Emory, Carlos Museum (object 3820); North Carolina Museum of Art; Pitt Rivers Museum, Oxford (object 156853, catalogued as "of the type associated with Sande or Bundu"); Penn State "African Brilliance" catalogue no. 29.
**Sierra Leone Heritage** (sierraleoneheritage.org, ids 2121, 2122, 2124) is a digital project that brings together Sierra Leonean objects held in the Sierra Leone National Museum and in UK collections. **This is the best source that comes from Sierra Leone itself.** UNVERIFIED as to who runs it now (the page was blocked).
Band: **3-5** (comparing masks); **6-8** (where they are now, and why).

**A4. Smith College label on the "Ndoli Jowei" mask.** Names the specific dancing spirit (ndoli jowei, "the dancing sowei") and the ligba rank. Useful because it uses Mende terms rather than "mask". CONFIRMED (catalogue title). Band: **6-8**.

**A5. George W. Harley, *Masks as Agents of Social Control in Northeast Liberia*** (Papers of the Peabody Museum, Harvard, vol. 32 no. 2, 1950). Harley was a medical missionary at Ganta, Liberia, who collected Poro-related masks and recorded their use in law and governing. His collection went to the Peabody Museum. It is a primary field record by an outsider. Read it critically: it is the work of an outsider collector. KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED. Band: **11-12** (teacher background).

### B. Tiandihui / Hongmen (Qing China and the diaspora)

**B1. Qing Grand Council and palace memorial archives on the Tiandihui (1780s onward)**, at the **First Historical Archives of China** (Beijing) and the **National Palace Museum** (Taipei). These are the confessions and reports that Murray and Qin, and Cai Shaoqing before them, used to date the founding to 1761–62. Primary, in Chinese. Mostly reached through the 1994 book. Stated in the publisher description (CONFIRMED). Band: **9-10** (as an idea: "why a police file is better evidence than a legend").

**B2. Gustaaf Schlegel, *Thian Ti Hwui. The Hung-League or Heaven-Earth-League: A Secret Society with the Chinese in China and India*.** Batavia: Lange & Co., 1866.
Where: HathiTrust (https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/100881170); Oregon Digital / e-Asia Digital Library (https://od1.oregondigital.org/catalog/oregondigital:df72rs65p); WorldCat OCLC 3989454. The Dutch Freemasonry Museum also catalogues a copy (collecties.vrijmetselarijmuseum.nl, item 17170). That tells us Western Freemasons were reading it as a "Chinese Masonry". CONFIRMED (catalogue).
Shows: the first full Western study. It prints translated membership certificates, oaths, secret hand signs, poems and banners. Schlegel was an interpreter at the Batavia court and wrote from seized society papers. His work was "found useful by both the Dutch and British colonial administrations" (Sinological Profiles, paraphrase). **That makes it a colonial policing document as much as scholarship.** Because it is public domain (1866), its illustrations of certificates and banners could be shown.
Band: **6-8** for the illustrations (codes, poems, symbols); **11-12** for the text.

**B3. J. S. M. Ward and W. G. Stirling, *The Hung Society, or The Society of Heaven and Earth*** (3 vols, London: Baskerville Press, 1925–26). A Freemason author read the Hung rituals as a kind of Masonry. That reading is a good example of outsiders seeing their own society in someone else's. KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED. Band: **11-12**.

**B4. Copperplate engravings, "Pacification of Taiwan" (1786–1788)**, made for the Qianlong emperor. Dealer catalogue: https://swaen.com/mapping-of/taiwan/pacification. This is the court's own picture of crushing a Tiandihui-led rising. Impressions are in major libraries. CONFIRMED (dealer page appeared in results; plate details not opened). Band: **6-8**.

**B5. "Shaolin Temple legends"** (PDF, Grand Lodge of British Columbia and Yukon history pages, https://www.freemasonry.bcy.ca/history/chinese_freemasons/shaolin_temple_legends.pdf). A modern fraternal retelling of the founding legend, as published by a Masonic body that studies the "Chinese Freemasons" (Chee Kung Tong). Useful as "the story as members tell it", set beside B1. Not a primary 18th-century source. Band: **6-8**.

### C. Ekpe, Nsibidi and Abakuá (Cross River and Cuba)

**C1. The Diary of Antera Duke (1785–88).** Written at Old Calabar in trade ("pidgin") English by an Efik trader and Ekpe member. Modern scholarly edition: Stephen D. Behrendt, A. J. H. Latham and David Northrup, *The Diary of Antera Duke, an Eighteenth-Century African Slave Trader* (Oxford UP, 2010). The title was CONFIRMED in a library catalogue; the editors and year are KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.
**Hard-to-find note:** the original manuscript is widely reported to have been destroyed in the Second World War. The text survives in an early 20th-century transcription of extracts published in Daryll Forde (ed.), *Efik Traders of Old Calabar* (1956). KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.
Shows: Ekpe "plays", Ekpe fines and the society's role in trade, written by an African member in his own words. It also records the slave trade he took part in (about twenty Liverpool ships "slaved", per secondary summary).
Band: **9-10** (short, teacher-picked entries only; the slave-trade content needs care).

**C2. J. K. Macgregor, "Some Notes on Nsibidi"**, *Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute* 39 (Jan.–Jun. 1909): 209–219. DOI 10.2307/2843292 (JSTOR 2843292). A free scan may be on Zenodo (record 1449627; blocked here, UNVERIFIED). CONFIRMED (DOI).
Shows: the first published list of nsibidi signs, with meanings as Macgregor was told them. He proposed an origin among particular groups; later scholars dispute this.
Band: **3-5** (public signs as pictures: "love", "a meeting", "a house"). Use only signs already printed in public sources, and say they are the *public* kind.

**C3. Elphinstone Dayrell, "Further Notes on 'Nsibidi Signs with Their Meanings from the Ikom District, Southern Nigeria'"**, *JRAI* 41 (Jul.–Dec. 1911): 521–540. CONFIRMED. Band: **6-8**.

**C4. Dayrell, Elphinstone (1869–1917), manuscript "Anthropology", Royal Anthropological Institute Archives, MS 23** (GB 1446 MS 23). https://therai.org.uk/archives-and-manuscripts/manuscript-contents/0023-dayrell-elphinstone-ms-23/ ; AIM25 record https://atom.aim25.com/index.php/dayrell-elphinstone-1869-1917-anthropology. The field notes of a colonial district officer, behind his published nsibidi work. CONFIRMED (catalogue). Contents not opened. Band: **11-12** / teacher.

**C5. Kenneth Little, "The Political Function of the Poro, Part I"**, *Africa* 35(4) (1965): 349–365, and **Part II**, *Africa* 36 (1966). Cambridge Core. Scholarship, not primary, but the key source for the claim that Poro governed. CONFIRMED. Band: teacher; **9-10** for summarised ideas.

**C6. Ivor L. Miller, *Voice of the Leopard: African Secret Societies and Cuba*** (University Press of Mississippi, 2009). Miller did fieldwork with Abakuá in Cuba and was initiated into Ékpè in Nigeria, so he writes with insider permission. He now teaches at the University of Calabar. CONFIRMED. Band: teacher; **9-10** for excerpts.

## Story hooks

1. **The dancing sowei comes out (Sierra Leone, 20th century).** Girls who have spent weeks in the Sande bush school come back into town as grown women. Leading them is a masked figure, shining black and utterly silent, with a tall carved hairstyle. That is the sowei. She does not speak; her helper speaks for her. (Phillips 1995; Art Institute label.) *Band 3-5*, told as a graduation, a celebration and a lesson in dignity.
2. **The mask in Chicago (Art Institute, object 146941).** It is off view. What would it mean to the women who once danced it that it sits in a storeroom in Chicago? *Band 6-8.*
3. **A pavilion in Fujian, around 1761.** In a small Buddhist pavilion to Guanyin at Gaoxi, a monk called Tixi gathers poor men who need each other: travellers, peddlers, men far from family. Their brotherhood will later claim a heroic story it did not have. *Band 6-8.*
4. **The cloth peddler (Taiwan, 1780s).** Yan Yan, a peddler from Fujian, carries the society's secrets across the strait along with his cloth. A few years later the governor's arrests set off the largest uprising Taiwan saw under Qing rule. *Band 9-10.*
5. **A trader's diary (Old Calabar, 1785).** Antera Duke writes in trade English about the day's business, the Ekpe drums and fines, and the European ships in the river. It is one of very few 18th-century diaries by an African trader. It is also an honest record of the slave trade he profited from. *Band 9-10.*
6. **A missionary's list of signs (Cross River, published 1909).** A Scottish missionary, J. K. Macgregor, asks local people to show him nsibidi signs and publishes the ones they chose to share. *Band 3-5*, framed as "what they chose to share". Where Macgregor worked (possibly the Hope Waddell Institution, Calabar) is UNVERIFIED.
7. **Regla, across the bay from Havana, 1836.** Africans from the Cross River, enslaved or recently freed in Cuba, found a lodge to rebuild the society they knew at home. It still exists nearly two centuries later. *Band 6-8.*
8. **Schlegel's desk in Batavia (1860s).** A Dutch court interpreter translates confiscated Hung League papers: membership certificates, oath poems, hand signs. His book helps colonial police, and it also preserves documents that would otherwise be lost. *Band 9-10.* The seizure details are UNVERIFIED.
9. **The picture you must step on (Japan, 1600s–1800s).** Officials made villagers step on a Christian image (fumi-e) each year to prove they were not Christian. Hidden Christians kept their faith secret for over two centuries by disguising prayers and images. *Band 6-8* (religious persecution; handle with care). KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED.

## Hard-to-find finds

1. **Dayrell's manuscript notes, RAI MS 23 (London).** The unpublished field record behind the 1911 nsibidi article. Few people know it exists; it shows what the published version left out.
2. **Schlegel's *Thian Ti Hwui* (Batavia, 1866), digitized on HathiTrust and Oregon Digital.** Public-domain plates of Hung League certificates, banners and code-poems. The copy catalogued by the Dutch Freemasonry Museum is a small story in itself: European Masons collected it as "Chinese Masonry".
3. **The Qianlong "Pacification of Taiwan" copperplates (1786–88).** The emperor's victory pictures of the campaign against a Tiandihui-led rising. The *state's* view of a secret society, in art.
4. **Antera Duke's diary, a lost original surviving in a transcription.** An African member's own voice from inside Ekpe in the 1780s.
5. **Sierra Leone Heritage's sowei records.** A way to show students Sande masks through a Sierra Leonean-centred project rather than only Western museum pages.
6. **Smith College's "Ndoli Jowei, Ligba rank" label.** One of the few museum records naming the mask's specific role and rank in Mende terms.

## Respect: what outsiders should not publish

- **Poro and Sande:** do not show or describe secret songs, words, medicines or rites inside the bush. Do not show any Poro mask or object that the society restricts from women or non-initiates. Some Poro spirits must not be seen by the uninitiated at all, so a museum photo can itself be an offence to members. Prefer Sande masks shown openly in public dances, and museum labels that say so. Never ask students to role-play initiation.
- **Ekpe/Mgbe and nsibidi:** use only nsibidi signs already printed in public sources, and say that there are sacred signs students will not see. Do not reproduce Ekpe ritual language or the voice of the leopard.
- **Abakuá:** the same applies. Its ritual language and signatures (anaforuana) are for initiates.
- **Hongmen:** historical oaths and hand signs are widely published (Schlegel, Ward and Stirling). They are fine to *describe* for 9-12. Do not teach students secret hand signs; K-2 and 3-5 lessons make up their own.
- **K-2 link:** clubhouses, special handshakes and passwords are fun. A good secret is a surprise that will be shared. An unsafe secret is one that makes you feel scared or hurts someone, and you always tell a trusted grown-up.

## Other examples (brief; check before use)

- **Japan, Kakure Kirishitan (Hidden Christians):** a faith kept in secret under persecution from the 1600s to the 1870s. Nagasaki sites were listed by UNESCO in 2018. It is a *hidden faith community*, not an order, but it teaches why people keep secrets for safety. KNOWN, NOT RE-CHECKED. Band 6-8.
- **Japan, Genyōsha (1881) and Kokuryūkai / "Black Dragon Society" (1901):** political nationalist societies. They were not secret initiation orders, despite their English nicknames. **Leave them out** unless another dossier covers them (band 11-12).
- **Lega (DR Congo), Bwami society:** a graded association whose ivory and wooden figures teach proverbs. Main source: Daniel Biebuyck. UNVERIFIED this session. Band 6-8.
- **Yoruba, Ogboni:** an earth-cult society with judicial power. Paired brass *edan* figures are in many museums. The "Reformed Ogboni Fraternity" was founded 1914. UNVERIFIED this session. Band 6-8.
