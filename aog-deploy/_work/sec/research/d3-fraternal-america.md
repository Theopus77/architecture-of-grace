## Summary

1. From about 1865 to 1930, joining a lodge was one of the most ordinary things an American adult did. A widely cited estimate (Beito) is that about one in three adult men belonged to a lodge around 1910.
2. Most of these "secret societies" were not about power. They were about ritual, friendship, and money for a funeral, a sick week, or a widow. Many were insurance companies with a ritual attached.
3. The ritual was secret; the lodge was not. Lodges published member lists, proceedings, rulebooks, and newspaper notices, and put their names on buildings and gravestones.
4. African Americans built a parallel fraternal world that started with Prince Hall in Boston during the Revolution. Scholars (Skocpol, Liazos, Ganz) show those lodges trained leaders and paid lawyers in the civil-rights struggle.
5. Groups shut out of mainstream orders built their own: Catholics (Knights of Columbus, 1882), Black Americans (Prince Hall Masons, Black Elks, Black Pythians), and women (Eastern Star, Rebekahs, Royal Neighbors, Independent Order of St. Luke).
6. Opposition was real and organized: the Anti-Masonic Party after the 1826 Morgan affair, and Wheaton, Illinois's National Christian Association (1868), whose Chicago paper *The Christian Cynosure* attacked lodges for decades.
7. Illinois is central to this story. The Grand Army of the Republic began in Decatur (1866). Chicago had the 1892 Masonic Temple skyscraper, Medinah Temple (1912), the Black Pythians' National Pythian Temple in Bronzeville (1928), and Mooseheart near Batavia (1913). Royal Neighbors of America is based in Rock Island.
8. Lincoln was never a Mason. A popular story says he applied and then withdrew in 1860; that story is DISPUTED and has no corroboration from the Grand Lodge of Illinois. Stephen A. Douglas was a Mason, and that is documented.
9. Decline: membership peaked around the 1950s-60s and then fell steeply. Putnam (1995, 2000) and Skocpol (2003) document the drop and argue about what it means.
10. Best teaching angle: "What did people get from belonging, and who was left out?" It works from K-2 (clubhouses, symbols, safe secrets) up to 11-12 (insurance economics, segregation litigation, civic decline data).

### Prince Hall and African Lodge

1. **Petition of Prince Hall and others to the Massachusetts General Court, January 1777** (anti-slavery petition).
   Where: Massachusetts Archives (original); transcribed in *Collections of the Massachusetts Historical Society*, 5th ser., vol. 3 (1877) (UNVERIFIED volume and page; confirm). Teaching transcriptions: National Constitution Center Historic Document Library; Facing History (PDF).
   URL: https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/historic-document-library/detail/prince-hall-petition-to-the-massachusetts-legislature
   Shows: a lodge founder turning Revolutionary language ("natural and inalienable right") against slavery. Band: **3-5** (excerpt), full text 6-8.

2. **Prince Hall, *A Charge Delivered to the African Lodge, June 24, 1797, at Menotomy*** (Boston, 1797). Evans Early American Imprints no. N24354. The OTA record notes it was advertised in the *Boston Gazette*, 28 Aug 1797.
   Where: Evans-TCP (University of Michigan), Oxford Text Archive; NYPL Research Catalog b11802969.
   URL: https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/evans/N24354.0001.001 ; https://ota.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/repository/xmlui/handle/20.500.12024/N24354
   Shows: a Black Masonic leader's public speech. It names street harassment in Boston and cites the Haitian Revolution as hope. Band: **6-8**.

3. **Petition of Prince Hall and others for schooling, 17 Oct 1787** (Massachusetts Archives). Shows that Black families paid school taxes but were shut out of Boston's free schools. Hall later ran a school in his own home (about 1800). Band: **3-5**.
   URL (summary): https://whenandwhereinboston.org/entry/schools-for-african-american-children-are-demanded

4. **Petition of Prince Hall on the kidnapping of three free Black men, dated 27 Feb 1788**, and the Massachusetts act of 26 Mar 1788 "to prevent the Slave Trade, and for granting Relief to the Families of such unhappy Persons as may be Kidnapped or decoyed away from this Commonwealth."
   Where: Massachusetts Archives; National Park Service image record ("Prince Hall's 1788 petition to end the slave trade"); MHS blog essay on the case.
   URLs: https://npgallery.nps.gov/AssetDetail/41976e7d-96aa-4ed8-82a2-713a8644b5a8 ; https://www.masshist.org/beehiveblog/2022/01/what-then-are-our-lives-and-lebeties-worth-the-18th-century-kidnapping-case-that-shook-boston

5. **Warrant of Constitution for African Lodge No. 459, 29 Sep 1784**, issued under the Duke of Cumberland, Grand Master; signed by R. Holt (Deputy GM) and William White (Grand Secretary).

   URLs: https://srmml.libraryhost.com/repositories/2/resources/2653 ; https://princehall.org/african-lodge-459/

   URL: https://museumfreemasonry.org.uk/node/771

## Claims ledger

| Claim | Status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Prince Hall and 14 other Black men were initiated by a British military lodge in Boston in March 1775. | DOCUMENTED tradition / **DISPUTED date** | The standard Prince Hall histories give 6 Mar 1775, Lodge No. 441 (Irish), 38th Foot. Alleyne (AQC 139) argues the evidence points to 6 Mar 1778. Teach "1775 (some historians now argue 1778)." |
| African Lodge No. 459 received a warrant from the Grand Lodge of England in 1784, delivered in 1787. | DOCUMENTED | Warrant dated 29 Sep 1784; delivered 29 Apr 1787 by Capt. James Scott (lodge records; SRMML facsimile). |
| Prince Hall was born in Barbados. | DISPUTED / UNVERIFIED | Commonly stated (the CPL finding aid says "believed to"). Birth records are not established; some older claims about his origins are unreliable. Say "possibly." |
| Prince Hall fought at Bunker Hill. | DISPUTED | Repeated in popular sources. Several men named Prince Hall appear in Massachusetts service records, and identification is uncertain. Do not teach as fact. |
| Prince Hall Masonry is "clandestine" (illegitimate). | DISPROVEN as a matter of record; historically a live dispute | White grand lodges long called it clandestine. Since 1989 (Connecticut first) most U.S. grand lodges recognize Prince Hall grand lodges. UGLE recognizes Prince Hall Grand Lodge of Massachusetts (verify date before stating). |
| The Chicago Masonic Temple (1892) was the tallest building in the world. | DISPUTED | Skyscraper Museum: it had the highest occupied floor, but New York's World Building (1890), with its lantern, was taller. It was Chicago's tallest from 1895 (when the Board of Trade tower came down) to 1899. Say "one of the tallest buildings in the world." |
| The Masonic Temple was demolished in 1939 partly because of the State Street subway. | SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS | Preservation Chicago and architectural historians cite outdated services plus subway foundation costs. |
| Lincoln was a Freemason. | DISPROVEN | No lodge record of his initiation. Masonic historians (for example, the Grand Lodge of British Columbia & Yukon's "wannabe" list) state he was not. |
| Lincoln applied to Tyrian Lodge, Springfield, in 1860 and withdrew to avoid seeming political. | DISPUTED / UNVERIFIED | A widely repeated story, with a supposed Tyrian Lodge resolution of 17 Apr 1865. Masonic sources note it has not been corroborated by the Grand Lodge of Illinois or Tyrian Lodge. *Lincoln Lore* (Jan 1971) discusses the question. Present it as "a story, not proven." |
| Stephen A. Douglas was a Mason. | DOCUMENTED | Springfield Lodge No. 4, admitted 1839/1840. Wayne C. Temple, *Stephen A. Douglas, Freemason* (1982). |
| Knights of Columbus was founded partly because Catholics were barred from other fraternal orders. | SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS, with nuance | The Catholic Church forbade membership in Freemasonry (papal condemnations beginning 1738). Many fraternal insurers were Protestant in culture. McGivney wanted a Catholic alternative that gave widows insurance (Kauffman 1982). "Barred" is often both exclusion by others and prohibition by the Church; teach both. |
| The Knights of Columbus "Fourth Degree oath" pledges to kill Protestants. | DISPROVEN | It is a forgery. It was printed in the Congressional Record (15 Feb 1913) only as evidence in *Bonniwell v. Butler*. The House Committee on Elections condemned "the spurious Knights of Columbus Oath." Pamphlets later cut that context out. |
| Masons murdered William Morgan. | DISPUTED | Kidnapping is DOCUMENTED: several Masons were convicted in 1827-28, with sentences from about 30 days to 28 months. That he was killed is widely believed but never proven in court. No body was conclusively identified. |
| The Anti-Masonic Party won electoral votes in 1832. | DOCUMENTED | William Wirt carried Vermont (7 electoral votes). |
| Around 1910, about one-third of adult American men belonged to a lodge. | SCHOLARLY ESTIMATE | Beito (2000) calls it "conservative." Estimates vary with double-counting (many men belonged to several orders). |
| Fraternal societies were mainly insurance and welfare providers. | SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS (for benefit orders) | AOUW (1868) paid death benefits (started at $500, soon $2,000). Modern Woodmen, WOW, Royal Neighbors and K of C sold life insurance. Beito documents lodge doctors, orphanages and old-age homes. Masons and Elks were more social and charitable than insurance-based. |
| The Woodmen of the World gave every member a tree-stump gravestone. | DOCUMENTED, with dates | Free marker about 1890-1900. After that, a $100 rider was needed (about 1900-1920s). Program discontinued in the 1920s (Stott 2003). Not every WOW grave is a tree stone. |
| Royal Neighbors of America was the first fraternal society to insure women's lives. | Organization's claim; plausible, UNVERIFIED as a "first" | Chartered 21 Mar 1895 in Illinois; it began in 1888 as an auxiliary to Modern Woodmen. Treat "first" as the organization's own claim. |
| Maggie Lena Walker was the first Black woman to charter a bank in the U.S. | DOCUMENTED (NPS) | St. Luke Penny Savings Bank, 1903; it grew out of the Independent Order of St. Luke. |

## Story hooks

1. **The school in the kitchen (Boston, 1787-1800).** Prince Hall and other Black fathers pay school taxes, but their children are turned away from Boston's free schools. They petition the legislature in October 1787 and lose. So Hall opens a school in his own home, and Harvard students teach there. *Band 3-5.* Lesson: a club can be a place where people help each other learn.

2. **Locked below deck (Boston Harbor, February 1788).** A man named Avery hires three free Black Bostonians, Cato Newell, Wenham Carey and Luke Russell, to repair a ship. He sends them below, locks them in, and sails for the Caribbean to sell them. Their families write to the government. Prince Hall petitions, and Governor Hancock writes to other governors. A month later Massachusetts outlaws the slave trade. MHS reports the men eventually came home (confirm details in the MHS essay). *Band 6-8.*

3. **"Patience, I say" (Menotomy, 24 June 1797).** An old man stands before his lodge on St. John's Day. He talks about the insults his brothers face in Boston's streets, and about news from Haiti that enslaved people have won their freedom. Then he publishes the speech. *Band 6-8.*

4. **The skyscraper with secrets on top (Chicago, 6 Nov 1890).** A parade marches down State Street to lay a cornerstone. Within two years, a 21- or 22-story tower rises at State and Randolph. Shops fill the bottom floors, offices the middle, and lodge rooms the top, with a public observation deck and rooftop scenic theaters (1894). In 1939 it is torn down for a two-story drugstore. *Band K-2* (tallest building), *6-8* (skyline and money).

5. **A tree on every grave (anywhere in the Midwest and South, 1890s).** A child in a country cemetery finds a gravestone carved like a cut tree, with an axe and the words "Dum Tacet Clamat." It was part of an insurance policy. *Band K-2 / 3-5.* Illinois cemeteries have them (find a local example before teaching; not confirmed here).

6. **The children's city (Batavia area, Illinois, 1913).** A dairy farm becomes Mooseheart, a town built for the children of Moose members who had died. James J. Davis recruited members with that promise; he later became U.S. Secretary of Labor. *Band K-2 / 3-5.*

7. **"A long list of beasts, birds, fishes" (New York court, 1912).** Black Elks turned away by the white Elks build their own order. The white order sues over the name, and a judge tells them to pick another animal. *Band 9-10.* (Quotation needs verification; see below.)

8. **The oath that Congress called a fake (Washington, February 1913).** A candidate who is a Knight of Columbus loses an election in Pennsylvania. A forged "oath" had been passed around saying Catholics pledged violence. The House prints it in the Congressional Record to prove it is false. Then pamphleteers reprint it as "Copied from the Congressional Record." *Band 9-10.* A strong media-literacy lesson.

9. **Bronzeville's skyscraper (37th & State, 1928).** Walter T. Bailey, the first licensed Black architect in Illinois, finishes an eight-story Egyptian Revival temple for the Black Knights of Pythias. It had a 1,500-seat theater and a rooftop garden and cost $850,000. It was demolished in 1980. *Band 6-8.*

10. **Masons "at sight" on the Mississippi (Nauvoo, Illinois, 15-16 March 1842).** The Grand Master of Illinois comes upriver and makes a new religion's prophet a Master Mason in two days, above a red-brick store. Five months later he suspends the lodge. *Band 9-10.*

## Illinois & Chicago

Only items confirmed in this pass are listed here.

- **Grand Army of the Republic**, Decatur, 6 Apr 1866: the Union veterans' fraternal order, with ritual and posts. See Sangamon County History, "Benjamin Stephenson and the Grand Army of the Republic."
- **Prince Hall Masonry in Illinois**: North Star Lodge No. 12, Chicago (1852); Griffin T. Watson No. 16, Alton (1856); Central No. 19, Springfield (1857). The Grand Lodge was formed at Springfield in 1867, with Benjamin Franklin Rogers of Springfield as Grand Master (per MWPHGL of Illinois history). Archive: CPL Harsh Collection, 1902-2006.
- **Chicago Masonic Temple**, State & Randolph: cornerstone 6 Nov 1890; opened 1892; Burnham & Root; Norman T. Gassette was the moving figure; demolished 1939.
- **Medinah Temple**, 600 N. Wabash: 1912; Huehl & Schmid (both Shriners); Moorish Revival; auditorium seating about 4,200. Later a Bloomingdale's Home store. WBEZ (2023) reports later casino use (verify current status before teaching).
- **Shriners Hospital, Chicago**, opened 20 Mar 1926 at Oak Park & Belden. Medinah Temple nobles decided to build it in 1925. It was the 11th Shriners hospital.
- **National Pythian Temple**, Bronzeville: 1928, Walter T. Bailey, demolished 1980.
- **Mooseheart**, near Batavia: farm bought 14 Dec 1912; opened 1913.
- **Royal Neighbors of America**, Rock Island (chartered 1895). **Modern Woodmen of America** later headquartered in Rock Island (founded 1883 in Lyons, Iowa).
- **Order of the Eastern Star**: Elizabeth Butler, Miriam Chapter No. 1 (Illinois), was the first Most Worthy Grand Matron (1876-78). Illinois Grand Chapter proceedings from 1875.
- **Knights of Columbus**: Chicago Council No. 182, 10 Jul 1896, first in Illinois. See Logan T. Ludwig, *Faith, Service and Inspiration: The Knights of Columbus in Illinois*.
- **Anti-secret-society movement**: Jonathan Blanchard (Wheaton College), the National Christian Association (1868), and *The Christian Cynosure* (Chicago).
- **Nauvoo, 1842**: Illinois Grand Lodge and the Mormon lodges.
- **Lincoln**: never a Mason (DOCUMENTED absence of record). The Tyrian Lodge story is DISPUTED. Douglas was a Mason (DOCUMENTED).
- **John Jones** (1816-1879), Chicago abolitionist and tailor, first Black elected official in Illinois (Cook County Board, 1871). His Masonic membership is **UNVERIFIED**; searches did not confirm it. Do not claim it.

## Hard-to-find finds

1. **The 1890 cornerstone program for the Chicago Masonic Temple (SRMML, Lexington).** A printed ceremony and parade order for the skyscraper. Almost no classroom material uses it. It shows the "secret" society at its most public. Request a scan from SRMML.

2. **Chicago Public Library's Prince Hall Freemasonry Archives (Harsh Collection 1997/03).** Sixteen boxes of 20th-century Black Chicago lodge material, unrestricted, 20 minutes from many Illinois classrooms. A real archive students can use.

3. **Prince Hall's eleven letters at the Library and Museum of Freemasonry, London.** The only direct correspondence between the founder of Black Freemasonry and the Grand Lodge that chartered him. Most U.S. teachers don't know it exists.

4. **The *Bonniwell v. Butler* record (1913).** A primary source in which the U.S. House itself debunks a forgery. It is also a later primary source (the pamphlets) showing how the debunking was cut off. A perfect "check the source" lesson.

5. **Alleyne's 2025-26 AQC paper on John Batt.** Brand-new archival scholarship that may move the founding date of Prince Hall Masonry from 1775 to 1778. It lets students watch history being revised in real time. (It is in copyright; cite, don't reproduce.)

6. **The Voorhis Prince Hall research papers (SRMML).** A white Masonic scholar's mid-century files on whether Black Masonry was "legitimate." Good for 11-12 students on how institutions justified segregation.

7. **The fraternal regalia trade catalogs (SRMML, 1924 Borris ribbon catalog).** Mail-order badges for a dozen orders. They show the scale of the movement at a glance, and they are great for K-2 design activities.

8. **The Woodmen monument program (Stott, *Markers XX*).** It connects insurance economics to objects students can find in Illinois cemeteries.

9. **The Eastern Star ritual (1890) on Project Gutenberg.** A complete, openly published women's ritual. It is the easiest way to show that "secret society" often meant "private ceremony."

## Grade-band guide (quick view)

- **K-2**: clubhouses and club names (Elks, Moose, Woodmen, "Jolly Corks"); badges, colors and symbols (SRMML catalogs); the tree-stump gravestone; Mooseheart as a place that cared for children; the tall Chicago Masonic Temple. Also *safe vs. unsafe secrets*: a club's handshake is a fun private thing, while a secret that makes you feel scared or hurt is one you tell a grown-up.
- **3-5**: Prince Hall's school petition and home school; the 1784 charter as an object; Maggie Lena Walker's bank; women's orders (Rebekahs, Eastern Star, Royal Neighbors); "insurance in a lodge" explained with a class piggy bank.
- **6-8**: the kidnapping case of 1788; Prince Hall's 1797 Charge; the Anti-Masonic backlash; Blanchard and the critics; the Pythian Temple and Walter T. Bailey; the GAR in Decatur.
- **9-10**: Nauvoo 1842; the bogus K of C oath (media literacy); Morgan trials; the Black Elks name fight; Harwood and Stevens as period sources.
- **11-12**: *Creswill* (1912); recognition and "regularity" (Voorhis papers, 1989 Connecticut); Putnam vs. his critics; Skocpol's "membership to management"; Beito on mutual aid vs. the welfare state.

Note: this dossier's groups are not hate or criminal groups. The Ku Klux Klan of the 1920s *copied* fraternal forms and sold memberships through the same lodge culture. It belongs in a separate 6-12 dossier, and the anti-Catholic bogus-oath material above should be taught only with its debunking.
