Close Reading the Bhagavad Gita

The Gita I

A warrior sits down in his chariot and refuses to fight. His charioteer answers him in about 700 verses. That conversation, the Bhagavad Gita, is one of the most read and most translated books in the world. Many Hindus recite it daily, read it at funerals, and turn to it in hard moments.

In this unit you will read Gita chapters 1 to 6 closely. You will follow Arjuna's grief, Krishna's first answers about the self, the steady mind, and the discipline of action. You will compare Telang's prose translation (1882) with Arnold's verse (1885), and meet readers such as Gandhi and Tilak who drew very different lessons from the same verses.

By the end you will be able to cite a Gita verse by chapter and number, explain what it says and who is speaking, and describe how different readers have understood it, without deciding for anyone which reading is true.

The big question

What does the Gita teach about action and duty, and why do its readers disagree about what that means?

Arjuna's Despair and Krishna's Answer

Gita 1–2
Big questionWhat does Krishna say to a person who cannot bring himself to act, and how have readers understood his answer?
The story

The Bow Falls

In the middle of two armies, the greatest archer of his age sits down and refuses to fight.

Two armies face each other on the plain of Kurukshetra. Conch shells blow. Horses stamp. The Bhagavad Gita opens here, inside the Mahabharata, at the last moment before a great war between cousins. The blind king Dhritarashtra asks his minister Sanjaya what is happening, and Sanjaya describes it.

Arjuna, the finest archer among the Pandava brothers, asks his charioteer to drive into the open space between the lines. His charioteer is Krishna. Arjuna wants to see who he must fight. He looks across and sees his teachers, his great-uncle Bhishma, his cousins, and the sons of friends.

His limbs go weak. His mouth goes dry. His bow, Gandiva, slips from his hand. He says he does not want victory or a kingdom if it costs the lives of his own family. What good would a throne be, he asks, if the people he loves are dead?

At the end of the first chapter, Arjuna sits down in the chariot, overcome with grief. In the second chapter he says plainly that he will not fight. Then he asks Krishna to teach him what is right. The other seventeen chapters of the Gita are Krishna's answer.

Hindus have read this scene for more than two thousand years. Some see a real warrior on a real field. Others see every person who stands frozen before a hard duty. In this chapter you will read Gita 1 and 2 closely and meet both kinds of reader.

Talk about itArjuna refuses to act because he loves his family. Is his refusal weakness, wisdom, or something else? What would you need to know to decide?
Section 1

The Frame of the Poem

23.1

A Poem Inside an Epic

Main ideaThe Gita is eighteen chapters of the Mahabharata's Bhishma Parva, told by Sanjaya to a blind king.

The Bhagavad Gita, or "Song of the Lord," is not a separate book in its origin. It sits inside the , the great Sanskrit epic, in the section called the Bhishma Parva. It has 18 chapters and about 700 verses. Most of the verses are in a meter called shloka.

The Gita has layers of speakers. The blind king Dhritarashtra asks a question in verse 1.1. His minister Sanjaya, who has been given special sight, reports what he sees on the field. Inside Sanjaya’s report, Arjuna speaks and Krishna answers. So the whole reaches us through a narrator.

Scholars usually date the Gita to about the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE, while noting it is hard to be exact. Hindu tradition places the war at the start of the Kali Yuga, often dated 3102 BCE, and names the sage Vyasa as the author of the epic. Both dates are worth knowing, because readers use them for different purposes.

23.2

Who Stands on the Field

Main ideaArjuna's crisis begins when he sees that the enemy is his own family and teachers.

In Gita 1.21 to 1.23, Arjuna asks Krishna to place the chariot between the two armies. Krishna is his friend, cousin and charioteer. Arjuna wants to look at the men who have come to fight for the Kauravas, the sons of Dhritarashtra. The Kaurava army is led by Duryodhana, the eldest of those sons.

What he sees, in verses 1.26 to 1.27, is a list of relatives. There are fathers and grandfathers, teachers, uncles, brothers, sons, grandsons and friends. Bhishma raised him. Drona taught him archery. The war is a war, and that is the heart of his pain.

The Mahabharata tells how the war came about: a stolen kingdom, a rigged dice game, years of exile and failed peace talks. Arjuna’s side believes it has a just claim. Yet the Gita shows him feeling that even a just war is unbearable when it is fought against those you love.

23.3

Arjuna's Arguments

Main ideaArjuna gives reasons for refusing: killing kin is wrong, and it will destroy families and their traditions.

Arjuna does not only feel sad. He argues. In Gita 1.31 to 1.46 he says he wants no victory or pleasure if it comes from killing kin. He says even if his enemies are greedy, he should not become like them. He also says that killing them would bring sin on his own side, not only on theirs.

He then warns about the wider damage. When families are destroyed, he says, their family is lost. Dharma here means duty and right order, the ways a family keeps its rites and its care for the dead. Arjuna fears that disorder will spread when the elders are gone.

Close readers notice something. Arjuna uses the language of dharma to argue against fighting. Krishna will later use the language of dharma to argue for it. The Gita stages a real argument about what duty requires, and it lets Arjuna’s case be heard in full before any answer comes.

Section 2

The Self Is Not Slain

23.4

Krishna's First Reply

Main ideaKrishna begins by telling Arjuna that the wise do not grieve for the living or the dead.

In Gita 2.1 to 2.10, Arjuna says he is confused about his duty. He asks Krishna to teach him, calling himself Krishna’s student. This is a turning point. The friend in the chariot now becomes a , a teacher. In Hindu tradition a guru is honored as the one who removes the darkness of not knowing.

Krishna’s first words surprise many readers. In 2.11 he says Arjuna grieves for those who should not be grieved for, yet speaks words that sound wise. The wise, he says, grieve neither for the living nor for the dead. It is a gentle rebuke: Krishna says Arjuna’s words sound wise, but his grief is misplaced.

Krishna then begins to explain why. His first argument is about the self, called in later teaching and dehin, "the embodied one," in these verses. The Gita says this self was never born and never dies. The rest of chapter 2 builds on that claim.

23.5

Never Born, Never Dying

Main ideaIn Gita 2.19 to 2.21 Krishna says the self neither kills nor is killed, because it is unborn and eternal.

Gita 2.19 says that anyone who thinks the self kills, or thinks it is killed, does not understand. The self does not kill and is not killed. Verse 2.20 adds that it is not born and does not die. It is unborn, everlasting and ancient, and it is not slain when the body is slain.

These verses are among the most quoted in the Gita. Many Hindus recite them at funerals and in times of loss. Readers find comfort in them because they describe death as the end of a body, not of the person’s deepest self. Families may hear the verses in Sanskrit, in Hindi or Tamil, or in English, depending on where they live.

Some readers also notice a hard edge. Krishna uses this teaching to answer a man who does not want to kill. Critics, including some modern Hindu thinkers, ask whether the teaching could excuse violence. Defenders reply that the Gita is teaching about the self, not giving a license to harm. You will meet this debate again.

23.6

The Clothing Image

Main ideaGita 2.22 compares the self changing bodies to a person changing worn-out clothes.

The Gita often teaches through pictures. In 2.22 Krishna says that as a person throws off worn-out clothes and puts on new ones, so the embodied self throws off worn-out bodies and enters new ones. This is the . Students often find it the easiest verse in chapter 2 to remember.

The image carries the idea of , the cycle of birth, death and rebirth. Hindus who hold this teaching believe the self passes through many lives. The body is like a garment: needed, worn for a time, then set aside. The person wearing it, the text says, is not the same as the garment.

Verses 2.23 and 2.24 add more pictures. Weapons do not cut the self, fire does not burn it, water does not wet it, wind does not dry it. The poet takes the four things that destroy bodies and says none of them reach the self. When you read closely, watch how the Gita moves from an argument to a picture that makes the argument easy to remember.

Section 3

The Steady Mind

23.7

Your Right Is to the Work

Main ideaGita 2.47 says a person's concern is the action itself, not its fruits.

Gita 2.47 may be the single most quoted verse of the book. Krishna tells Arjuna that his right, or concern, is with action alone, never with its fruits. He should not let the fruits be his motive, and he should not cling to doing nothing either. Each clause is short, and each one closes a door that Arjuna might try to walk through.

The word here means action, the deeds a person does. The "fruits" are the results. The verse does not say results do not matter. It says a person should not let the hope of reward, or fear of loss, drive the action. Readers call this acting without attachment.

Verse 2.48 follows. It asks Arjuna to act while steady in , letting go of attachment and staying the same in success and failure. It then gives a short definition: evenness of mind is called yoga. In the Gita, yoga is much more than exercise; it names a disciplined way of acting and seeing.

23.8

The Person of Steady Wisdom

Main ideaIn Gita 2.54 to 2.72 Arjuna asks what a person of steady wisdom is like, and Krishna describes one.

In 2.54 Arjuna asks a practical question. How does a person of steady wisdom speak, sit and walk? The Sanskrit term is , one whose wisdom stands firm. Arjuna wants a picture of a real person, not a theory. His question tells us the Gita cares about how wisdom looks in daily life.

Krishna answers in verses 2.55 to 2.72. Such a person gives up desires that race through the mind. They are not shaken by sorrow and do not crave pleasure. They are free from fear and anger. Verse 2.58 uses a tortoise that draws its limbs into its shell as a picture of drawing the senses back from their objects.

Gandhi said these closing verses of chapter 2 were the verses he returned to most. They were recited daily at his ashram prayers. For many readers, the sthitaprajna is a goal to work toward slowly, one habit at a time. Readers from many traditions have also found in these verses a portrait of calm under pressure.

23.9

From Desire to Ruin

Main ideaGita 2.62 to 2.63 traces a chain from dwelling on things to desire, anger, confusion and ruin.

Gita 2.62 and 2.63 form a famous chain. When a person dwells on the objects of the senses, attachment grows. From attachment comes desire. From desire comes anger. From anger comes confusion. From confusion, memory wanders; from that, understanding is lost; and from that, the person is ruined.

This is a : each step causes the next. The Gita uses it to show why steadiness matters. The point is not that feelings are bad. It is that an unchecked thought can grow step by step into something that harms a person. The chain moves inward first, into memory and understanding, before it ends in ruin.

Teachers of many backgrounds have compared this chain to ideas in psychology. Some Hindu teachers use it in talks on anger. When you study it, notice the poem’s method: it names a small first step, then shows how far the path can go. That method, a short list with a clear direction, makes the teaching easy to recite and remember.

Section 4

Translators and Readers

23.10

Arnold's Song Celestial

Main ideaEdwin Arnold's 1885 verse translation brought the Gita to many English readers, including the young Gandhi.

In 1885 the English poet Edwin Arnold published The Song Celestial, a translation of the Gita in English verse. He made it rhyme and flow like Victorian poetry. It became popular in Britain and the United States. By 1900 it had gone through many editions, and it remains in print today.

Gandhi wrote in his autobiography that he first read the Gita as a law student in London, in Arnold’s version, when two friends invited him to read it with them. He had not read it in Sanskrit or Gujarati before. That encounter, he said, began his lifelong study of the book.

A verse like Arnold’s is beautiful but free. It adds words to fit rhyme and meter. K.T. Telang’s prose translation of 1882, in the Sacred Books of the East series, stays closer to the Sanskrit. Close readers compare both, and they always name which one they are quoting.

23.11

An Outer War or an Inner One

Main ideaSome readers take Kurukshetra as a real battle; others read it as the struggle inside every person.

Many Hindus read the Gita as a record of real events. On this , Arjuna was a real warrior, the war was real, and Krishna taught him on the field. Kurukshetra, a town in the Indian state of Haryana, is visited by pilgrims as the place where the Gita was spoken.

Other readers, Hindu and not, read the battle as an , a story whose parts stand for something else. Gandhi wrote that the Gita’s war stands for the struggle between good and evil in the human heart. Some teachers read the two armies as good and bad tendencies inside a person.

The two readings can live side by side. A reader may believe the war happened and still find the inner meaning central. A careful student describes each reading and who holds it, and does not declare one of them the correct one. Students may hold a view of their own, and still describe others’ views in their holders’ terms.

Big questionCan a person do their work fully while letting go of what they will get from it?
The story

The Night Train

After a double shift, a nurse opens a small paperback to chapter 3.

It is almost midnight on a Metra train heading west out of Chicago. Priya, a nurse, has just finished a sixteen-hour double shift. Her feet hurt. One of her patients did not make it through the evening, though the whole team did everything they could.

In her bag is a worn paperback Gita that her grandmother gave her. She opens it to chapter 3, a habit she has kept since nursing school. Tonight her eyes land on a line about no one being able to stay without action, even for a moment.

She thinks about the families she spoke to today. She did her work well, and still the outcome was not what anyone hoped. The Gita, as she reads it, tells her to give full care to the work and to let go of the results she cannot control.

Priya is a made-up person, but readers like her are real. Many Hindus, and many people of other backgrounds, have turned to Gita 3 to 6 while thinking about work, duty and burnout. Others argue with these chapters and ask whether "doing your duty" can go too far.

In this chapter you will read Krishna's teaching on action, knowledge and meditation, see how Gandhi read it, and practice arguing with a text respectfully. You will also ask where the idea of duty needs limits, and how Hindu readers have answered.

Section 1

No One Can Stop Acting

24.1

Why Not Just Withdraw?

Main ideaIn Gita 3 Arjuna asks why he must act at all, and Krishna answers that no one can stay without action.

Chapter 3 opens with a fair question. In 3.1 and 3.2 Arjuna says: if knowledge is better than action, why are you urging me into this terrible deed? Your words confuse me. Tell me one thing clearly. Arjuna is not being rude. He is asking a real student’s question, and Krishna takes it seriously.

Krishna’s answer in 3.4 and 3.5 is that a person does not become free from action by simply not starting it, nor reach perfection just by giving things up. No one can remain even for a moment without acting. The body breathes, eats and moves; nature itself pushes a person to act.

So the question is not whether to act, but how. The Gita’s answer is , the discipline of action. Verse 3.8 says to do one’s assigned work, because action is better than inaction; without action, even the body could not be kept going. Karma yoga does not ask a person to stop working. It asks a person to change how they hold their work.

From the source
Not for a Moment
No one ever remains even for a moment without performing some action; for everyone, being without free will in the matter, is made to act by the qualities born of nature.
Bhagavad Gita 3.5, paraphrased from K.T. Telang's translation (SBE 8, 1882) (paraphrased)

List three actions you do without choosing them. How does this verse change the question "Should I act?"

24.2

Work as Offering

Main ideaGita 3.9 and 3.19 teach acting without attachment, as an offering rather than for private gain.

Gita 3.9 says that action binds a person unless it is done as . Yajna first meant the Vedic fire sacrifice. The Gita widens it. Any work done as an offering, not only for oneself, can be yajna. This widening lets a farmer, a teacher or a nurse see ordinary labor as a kind of offering.

Verse 3.19 then gives the plain instruction: therefore, without attachment, always do the work that should be done; by working without attachment a person reaches the highest. Many readers see this as the center of karma yoga. Readers note that the verse does not say to work less. It says to work without clinging.

The Gita then points to King Janaka, a wise ruler in the Upanishads, who kept ruling while being free of attachment (3.20). Krishna adds that a person should act for , the holding-together of the world. Leaders are copied by others, so their example matters.

Think it through
Janaka on the Throne

Gita 3.20 names Janaka, a king remembered as a wise man who kept ruling his kingdom. Krishna says Janaka and others reached perfection through action, and that one should act with an eye to holding the world together.

Why might the Gita choose a king, not a hermit, as its example? What does that suggest about ordinary jobs?

24.3

One's Own Duty

Main ideaGita 3.35 says it is better to do one's own duty imperfectly than another's duty well.

Gita 3.35 is short and much debated. Krishna says one’s own duty, even done imperfectly, is better than another’s duty done well. Death in one’s own duty is better; another’s duty brings danger. The word is , one’s own dharma. Sva means "one’s own." So the word points to a duty that belongs to a particular person.

In the setting of the poem, Arjuna is a , a member of the warrior class, and his svadharma is to fight a just war. Traditional readers tied svadharma closely to varna, the four social classes named in the Vedic texts. That reading linked duty to birth.

Many modern Hindus read svadharma more broadly, as the calling that fits a person’s nature and gifts. Critics note that the verse was used to defend a fixed social order. Reformers answer that the Gita also says, in 4.13, that varna was made by qualities and actions. The debate is ongoing inside the tradition.

Think it through
Two Readings of Svadharma

Reading A: svadharma is the duty of the social class one is born into. Reading B: svadharma is the calling that fits one's own nature and gifts. Both readings cite Gita 3.35; Reading B often also cites 4.13 on qualities and actions.

What would each reading tell a student who wants a career different from her parents'? Describe each fairly.

the warrior and ruler class in the Vedic four-varna scheme; Arjuna is a kshatriya
varna
one of the four social classes named in Vedic texts: priests, warriors, merchants, laborers

Section 2

Age After Age

24.4

When Dharma Declines

Main ideaIn Gita 4.7 to 4.8 Krishna says he comes into the world age after age to protect dharma.

In chapter 4 Krishna says something new about himself. He tells Arjuna that they have both had many births; Krishna knows them all, but Arjuna does not. Then comes one of the Gita’s best-known passages. The claim that Krishna remembers past births sets up the famous verses that follow.

In 4.7 and 4.8, Krishna says that whenever dharma declines and adharma, its opposite, rises, he sends himself forth. To protect the good, to destroy wrongdoers and to establish dharma, he comes into being age after age. The phrase "age after age" translates the Sanskrit yuge yuge.

Vaishnava Hindus, who worship Vishnu, read these verses as the Gita’s statement of the teaching: Vishnu descends in forms such as Rama and Krishna. The Gita itself does not list the avatars; the lists come from the Puranas. As students, we describe what the Gita says and how devotees read it.

From the source
Age After Age
Whenever there is a decline of dharma and a rise of adharma, then I create myself. To protect the good, to destroy evildoers, and to establish dharma, I am born age after age.
Bhagavad Gita 4.7–4.8, paraphrased from K.T. Telang's translation (SBE 8, 1882) (paraphrased)

Who is speaking, and to whom? What three purposes does the speaker name?

avatar
a "descent"; a form in which Vishnu is believed by his devotees to come into the world

Vaishnava
a Hindu who worships Vishnu or his forms, such as Krishna and Rama, as supreme

24.5

Action in Inaction

Main ideaGita 4.18 says the wise see inaction in action and action in inaction.

Gita 4.18 reads like a riddle. The one who sees inaction in action and action in inaction is wise among people and is a true yogi, even while doing all kinds of work. What can it mean? The verse sounds like a puzzle on purpose. It asks the reader to slow down and think.

Commentators give different answers. One common reading: a person acting without attachment is, inwardly, not bound by the action, so there is a kind of stillness inside the work. And a person who sits idle while wanting results is still bound, so there is hidden action in the inaction.

This is a good place to practice . Look at each word. Ask who is being described, and what the verse is contrasting. Then compare what commentators say. The same line can bear more than one careful reading. Commentators such as Shankara and Ramanuja read many verses in different ways, as you will see in a later unit.

Think it through

Describe a person who is busy on the outside but calm inside, and a person who is still on the outside but restless inside. Which fits each half of the verse?

24.6

The Fire of Knowledge

Main ideaGita 4 praises knowledge, jnana, as a fire that burns away the binding effects of action.

Later in chapter 4 the Gita turns to knowledge, or . Verse 4.37 says that as a blazing fire reduces fuel to ashes, so the fire of knowledge reduces all actions to ashes. Readers take this to mean that knowledge frees a person from the binding results of karma.

Verse 4.34 says how to gain this knowledge: by humble respect, by questions, and by service to teachers who have seen the truth. The Gita pictures learning as a relationship. That is what Arjuna himself is doing with Krishna. Knowledge in the Gita is not only facts. It is understanding passed from teacher to student.

So far the Gita has praised action and knowledge. Some readers see a tension: which is better? Krishna answers in chapter 5 that both lead to the same goal, but that the discipline of action is easier for most people. The Gita tends to join paths rather than choose only one.

Think it through

Section 3

The Meditator

24.7

A Seat, Neither High nor Low

Main ideaGita 6 describes how a person of the text sets up for meditation and steadies the mind.

Chapter 6 is often called the chapter of meditation, . In verses 6.10 to 6.14 Krishna describes how a yogi prepares. The yogi goes to a clean place, sets a firm seat neither too high nor too low, and covers it with grass, a deer skin and a cloth.

The yogi holds body, head and neck straight and still, and gazes at the tip of the nose without looking around. The mind is to be calm and fearless. These details show that the Gita treats meditation as a practice with a body and a setting, not only an idea.

In this course we describe what the text says about meditation. We do not practice it in class. Students can notice how carefully the text builds its scene, from the seat to the gaze to the mind. Notice also what the text leaves out: it does not name a set time or a count of breaths here.

24.8

The Lamp and the Restless Mind

Main ideaGita 6.19 pictures the steady mind as a lamp in a windless place, and 6.34–6.35 admit the mind is hard to hold.

Verse 6.19 gives a lovely image. The mind of a yogi who has mastered it is like a lamp in a windless place that does not flicker. The flame stands still, because nothing blows it about. Readers remember the picture because anyone who has watched a candle knows how easily a flame moves.

Arjuna does not simply accept this. In 6.34 he says the mind is restless, turbulent, strong and stubborn; holding it seems as hard as holding the wind. It is one of the most honest moments in the book. A student is allowed to say, "This is hard." He does not pretend to be calm when he is not.

Krishna agrees. In 6.35 he says the mind is indeed hard to control, but it can be held by , steady practice, and , letting go. The Gita does not promise a quick fix. It describes slow, patient effort. Many readers find comfort in that honesty from both speakers.

Think it through

Both images use air. How does the second image answer the first? What does Krishna's reply in 6.35 add?

Section 4

Readers and Arguments

24.9

Gandhi's Gospel of Selfless Action

Main ideaGandhi read Gita 2 to 6 as a guide to selfless service and nonviolent action.

Gandhi translated the Gita into Gujarati and called his version Anasaktiyoga, "the yoga of non-attachment" (published 1929 and 1930). For him the key teaching was acting without desire for the fruits. He saw this as the heart of public service. The book was widely read in the Indian freedom movement.

Gandhi read the Gita’s battle as an allegory, and so he did not see it as a call to physical war. He argued that a person who truly acts without attachment could not harm others for gain. For him the Gita pointed toward , not harming. Not every reader accepts this step, but it shaped Gandhi’s whole public life.

Other readers disagreed. The nationalist leader Bal Gangadhar Tilak wrote a long study, Gita Rahasya (1915), reading the Gita as a call to energetic action in the world, including resistance. Both men were devout readers. Their debate shows how one text can guide very different lives.

24.10

Duty and Conscience

Main ideaReaders argue about whether the Gita's call to duty leaves room for conscience, and the text itself stages the debate.

Arjuna’s refusal in chapter 1 can look like conscience: he feels killing kin is wrong. Krishna’s answer calls him back to duty. So a hard question follows. Does the Gita teach that duty should override conscience? Readers have given at least three careful answers, and each rests on verses in the text.

Some readers say yes, within limits: Arjuna’s war is a just war, fought after peace failed, and his grief is personal, not moral. Others say the Gita reshapes conscience rather than overriding it: Krishna asks Arjuna to act from a steady, unselfish mind, and 18.63 ends with "do as you choose." Still others, like some modern critics, remain uneasy about any text used to justify war.

Arguing with a sacred text is an old Hindu practice. Commentators disagreed sharply for centuries. When you argue, name the verse, name the reader, and give their reasons fairly. That is how a scholar enters the conversation without mocking anyone. It is also how a reader shows respect for people who hold the text sacred.

Think it through
