Structure, Style and Recitation

The Text

Pick up a printed Qur'an and you hold a book that Muslims say began as a voice. This unit looks at the book as a made thing: how its 114 chapters are arranged, why the short ones ring like songs and the long ones read like law, what the strange letters at the top of some chapters are, and how the whole thing was gathered, written, dotted, printed and recited over fourteen centuries.

You will learn the words readers use: surah and ayah, juz' and hizb, Meccan and Medinan, tajwid and qira'at. You will meet the people tradition names, Zayd ibn Thabit, Abu Bakr, Uthman, and the scholars who added the marks. You will also meet the evidence historians use, including two pages of parchment in an English library that turned out to be older than anyone expected.

This is the study of a scripture in a public school. We describe what the Qur'an says and how Muslims and scholars read it; we do not decide whether it is true. By the end you should be able to open the book to any surah, say what kind of chapter it is, and explain how the words on the page got there.

Three Lines and Forty

Two surahs sit on facing pages. One is over in a breath. The other runs on and on.

Dario opened the borrowed copy near the back, where his teacher said the short chapters live. On the left page, one *surah* took up three lines. Its name at the top said Al-Kawthar, 'Abundance.' He counted the verse markers: three small circles. That was the whole chapter. On the facing page, another surah began and did not stop. He turned the page. It kept going. The heading said it had forty verses.

The copy was in Arabic, but the class had a public-domain English translation open beside it. Pickthall's 1930 version printed the surah names in English, so Dario could match them up. The three-line surah spoke of a gift and a prayer. The long one, Surah 78, An-Naba, 'The Tidings,' opened with a question: what are they asking one another about? Then it listed mountains, sleep, night, day and rain, one after another, like a drumbeat.

His teacher walked past and stopped. 'Notice anything?' she asked. Dario said the short one felt like a song and the long one felt like a speech. She nodded. 'Both come from the Mecca years, Muslims say. The really long ones, near the front, mostly come from Medina. The book is not in the order it was first spoken.' Dario flipped to the front. Surah 2 filled page after page: rules about fasting, debts, marriage, and a story about a cow.

That night Dario tried to explain it to his older brother. 'It is like a playlist,' he said. 'The tracks are not in the order they were recorded. The long ones are first. The short ones with the strongest beat are at the end. And some chapters start with letters that nobody is sure about.' His brother asked what the letters meant. Dario said that was the point. Nobody agrees, and scholars have argued about it for more than a thousand years.

A Map of the Book

15.1

One Hundred Fourteen Chapters

Main ideaThe Qur'an has 114 surahs, arranged roughly from longest to shortest, and each surah is made of ayahs, or verses.

Open any printed copy of the Qur’an and you will find 114 chapters. Each chapter is called a . The word is Arabic. Some scholars connect it to a word for a row or a wall of a city, a set-off section. Each surah is divided into short units called ayahs. An is a verse, but the word also means a sign, a mark that points to something. The Qur’an uses the same word for the verses on the page and for signs in nature, like rain or the stars.

The surahs are not all the same size. Surah 2, Al-Baqarah (The Cow), is the longest with 286 ayahs. Surah 108, Al-Kawthar (Abundance), is the shortest with 3. Most Muslim scholars count about 6,236 ayahs in the whole book, though older counting systems differ by a few dozen, because they mark verse endings in slightly different places. The count of surahs does not change: 114 in every copy.

Very roughly, the book runs from long surahs to short ones. Surah 2 opens the long stretch. By Surah 90 or so, most chapters fit on one page, and the last ten fit on a page together. This is not a strict rule. Al-Fatiha, the first surah, has only seven ayahs, and a few long surahs sit among shorter ones. But the general slope, long to short, is one of the first things a reader notices.

Every surah except one begins with the : In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful. Allah is the Arabic word for God, used by Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews as well as Muslims. The exception is Surah 9, At-Tawbah (Repentance), which has no Bismillah at its head. Muslim scholars offer several reasons. One common view is that it continues Surah 8, and another is that its sharp opening did not fit a line about mercy.

15.2

How Surahs Got Their Names

Main ideaSurah names usually come from a striking word or story inside the chapter, and many surahs have more than one name.

Surah names are not titles the way a novel has a title. Most come from a memorable word or story somewhere in the chapter, often near the start. Surah 2 is called Al-Baqarah, The Cow, because of a story in verses 67 to 73 about the Israelites being told to sacrifice a cow. The cow is not the main subject of the 286 verses. It is simply a name people could remember and say.

Other names work the same way. Surah 16 is An-Nahl, The Bee, from a passage that says God inspired the bee to build its home in the hills and trees (16:68). Surah 29 is Al-Ankabut, The Spider, from a line that compares those who trust in anything besides God to a spider building a flimsy house (29:41). Surah 105 is Al-Fil, The Elephant, from a story about an army with an elephant. Surah 19 is Maryam, named for the mother of Isa (Jesus).

Some surahs have several names. The first surah is Al-Fatiha, The Opening. Muslim tradition also calls it Umm al-Kitab, the Mother of the Book, and the Seven Oft-Repeated. Surah 17 is called both Al-Isra, The Night Journey, and Bani Israil, The Children of Israel. Most Muslim scholars hold that the names came from the Prophet or his companions. In this view the names are not part of the revealed text itself. A few scholars argue for a stronger view.

In a printed copy, the heading of each surah usually gives the name, the number of verses, and whether tradition places it in Mecca or Medina. That heading is a scholar’s label, added to help readers, like a table of contents. Knowing this helps a student read the page: the name is a handle, not a summary.

15.3

Parts for Reading: Juz' and Hizb

Main ideaBesides surahs, the Qur'an is divided into 30 equal parts (juz') so the whole book can be read in a month, and each juz' into halves and quarters.

Surahs are the Qur’an’s own chapters. But readers long ago added a second set of divisions that has nothing to do with subject. The book is cut into 30 nearly equal parts, each called a juz&#x27; (plural ajza’). A juz’ can start or end in the middle of a surah, because the goal is equal length, not meaning. Read one juz’ a night and you finish the whole Qur’an in the 30 nights of Ramadan, the month of fasting.

Each juz’ is split again into two halves, each called a , giving 60 in all. Each hizb is marked at its quarters. A printed copy shows these divisions in the margin with small symbols, so a reader can stop at a quarter, a half, or a whole part and know exactly where to begin again tomorrow. Many Muslims use a hizb as a daily reading portion outside Ramadan.

There is also a division into seven parts, the manazil, for those who want to finish the book in a week. It is less common today. What all these systems share is that they are practical. They answer a reader’s question, how much should I read tonight, rather than a scholar’s question about themes. The surah and the ayah belong to the text. The juz’ and hizb belong to its readers.

During Ramadan nights, many mosques hold a special prayer called tarawih. A reciter moves through one juz’ each night. Over the month the congregation hears the whole book. In Chicago, as in cities across the world, some families choose a mosque partly by who leads these nights. The sound of the recitation matters to them.

Two Voices: Mecca and Medina

15.4

Short Surahs, Strong Beats

Main ideaSurahs that Muslim tradition places in Mecca tend to be short, rhythmic and urgent, calling people to one God and warning of judgment.

Muslim tradition divides the surahs by where the Prophet Muhammad was living when they were first recited. Mecca was his home from about 610 to 622. Medina was his home from 622 until his death in 632. Printed copies label each surah Meccan or Medinan. About 86 are counted as Meccan and 28 as Medinan. Scholars debate a few, and some surahs mix verses from both periods.

Meccan surahs are usually short. Their lines are brief and end in rhyme or near-rhyme. They speak with urgency about one God, about the signs of God in the sky and earth, about the Last Day when people are judged, and about caring for the poor and the orphan. Surah 81, At-Takwir (The Overthrowing), opens with a chain of images: when the sun is folded up, when the stars fall, when the mountains are set moving.

Read aloud in Arabic, these surahs have a beat. Lines are close in length and their endings ring. Scholars of Arabic style call the effect saj&#x27;, rhymed prose, and note that Meccan surahs use it most. Muslims say the sound is part of the meaning: a warning should sound like a warning. Historians note that early listeners in Mecca were used to poets and oral speech, so the form spoke to its first audience.

Muslim tradition tells that these years were hard. The early Muslims were a small group in a city that mostly rejected them. Many scholars connect the Meccan surahs’ themes, patience, trust in God, judgment coming, to that setting. Reading a surah with its period in mind is one of the first tools students of the Qur’an learn to use.

15.5

Oaths and the Last Day

Main ideaMany Meccan surahs open with oaths sworn by things in nature, a form early Arabic listeners knew, leading into a warning or a promise.

A striking feature of Meccan surahs is the oath. A surah may open by swearing by the morning light, the night, the sun, the fig and the olive, or the galloping horses of a dawn raid. Surah 91, Ash-Shams (The Sun), swears by the sun and its brightness, the moon that follows it, the day that shows it, and the night that covers it. Surah 103, Al-Asr, swears by the passing time.

In Arabic, the oath is an old and serious form. A speaker swears by something to make what follows carry weight. In the Qur’an the oath almost always leads to a claim: that the human soul faces a choice, that people are in loss unless they do right, that the dead will be raised. Muslim commentators such as al-Tabari (d. 923) discuss what each thing sworn by means and why God would swear by a created thing. Several answers are offered.

The Last Day is the topic these oaths most often introduce. The Qur’an describes it with pictures of the world coming apart, the sky splitting, graves opening, and every person seeing what they did. Muslims believe this day will come. The surahs of this kind, such as 99, Az-Zalzalah (The Earthquake), and 101, Al-Qari’ah (The Calamity), are among the shortest and most vivid in the book.

For a student of the text, the oath is a signal. When a surah opens with ’By the...’ you can expect a short, intense chapter. It is likely from Mecca and likely about judgment or the human heart. Recognizing the form is like recognizing a sonnet by its fourteen lines. It tells you what kind of thing you are reading before you read it.

15.6

Medina: Law and Community

Main ideaSurahs placed in Medina are longer and speak in a calmer voice about law, family, the new community and its dealings with other peoples.

After the , the move from Mecca to Medina in 622, Muslim tradition says the surahs changed. The Muslims were no longer a small rejected group but a growing community with a city to run. The Medinan surahs are mostly long. They talk about fasting, prayer, charity, marriage, divorce, inheritance, debts, contracts and war. They address the believers directly: ’O ye who believe.’

The lines are longer, and the rhyme is looser. Surah 2, Al-Baqarah, is the clearest example: it lays out the fast of Ramadan (2:183–185), the rules for writing down a loan (2:282, the longest single ayah in the book), and the direction of prayer. Surah 4, An-Nisa (The Women), deals with orphans, inheritance shares and marriage. Surah 5, Al-Ma’idah, deals with food, oaths and relations with Jews and Christians.

Medinan surahs also tell of events in the community’s life. There is the battle of Badr in 624, the battle of Uhud in 625, and the treaty of Hudaybiyyah in 628. They speak about the Jewish tribes of Medina and about Christians. Sometimes the tone is argument and sometimes respect, as People of the Book. Scholars read these verses with their occasions, the moments tradition says they answered. A verse about a specific dispute reads differently from a general rule.

The two voices are not two books. Muslims read Meccan and Medinan surahs as one Qur’an. But knowing the period helps explain why a surah sounds the way it does. A short, ringing chapter about the Last Day and a long chapter about loans are both there, and the label Meccan or Medinan is one of the first things a careful reader checks.

15.7

A Refrain That Repeats

Main ideaSurah 55, Ar-Rahman, repeats one question 31 times, a refrain that shapes the whole surah like a chorus.

Some surahs use repetition on purpose. The clearest case is Surah 55, Ar-Rahman (The Beneficent). It lists gifts: the sun and moon on their courses, the stars and trees, the earth spread out with fruit and grain, the two seas, pearls and coral, ships like mountains. After nearly every pair of verses comes the same question, addressed to two listeners at once: Which is it, of the favors of your Lord, that ye deny?

The line appears 31 times in 78 verses. Read aloud, it works like a in a song: the verses change, the chorus returns. Muslim commentators say the ’two’ addressed are humans and jinn, unseen beings the Qur’an describes as made of fire. The repeated question makes each gift a challenge. A listener cannot hear the list without being asked, again and again, whether they will call any of it false.

Refrains appear elsewhere too. Surah 77, Al-Mursalat, repeats ’Woe unto the repudiators on that day’ ten times. Surah 54, Al-Qamar, repeats a line asking whether anyone will take heed. Scholars of literature compare these to refrains in the Psalms and in oral poetry across many cultures. Muslims often say Ar-Rahman is among the most beautiful surahs to hear, and reciters are known for the way they handle its return line.

The refrain is one answer to a student’s question: how can a book meant to be heard hold a listener’s attention? Repetition gives the ear something to hold. Muslims have memorized the Qur’an for fourteen centuries, and features like this one, tradition says, are part of what makes that possible.

15.8

Parables and Signs

Main ideaThe Qur'an teaches through parables (amthal), short comparisons drawn from everyday life, and by pointing to signs in nature.

The Qur’an often explains an idea with a picture. A good word, it says, is like a good tree, its root firm and its branches reaching to the sky (14:24). Those who trust in anything besides God are like a spider building a house, and the spider’s house is the frailest of houses (29:41). Those who spend for show are like a smooth rock with a little soil on it, washed bare by rain (2:264). Each of these is a , in Arabic a mathal.

The Qur’an says openly that it uses such comparisons. It says God does not shy away from using even a gnat as an example (2:26). Commentators note the pictures come from the life of the first listeners. There are date palms, trade caravans, wells, rain on dry ground, a lamp in a niche. Surah 24:35, the Light Verse, compares God’s light to a lamp in a glass. Sufi readers over the centuries have written whole treatises on that one image.

Closely related is the idea of the sign. The same word, ayah, names a verse and a sign in the world. The Qur’an tells its listeners to look: at the camel, how it was created. At the sky, how it was raised. At the mountains and the earth (88:17–20). The difference of human languages and colors is called a sign (30:22). Muslims read nature as pointing beyond itself. Historians of science note that this idea shaped how later Muslim scholars valued studying the natural world.

For a reader, parables and signs are the Qur’an’s way of being concrete. An abstract claim about trust is hard to hold. A spider’s web in a corner is not. Students can practice by finding a parable, naming the picture, and then stating the idea in their own words.

Order and Mystery

15.9

The Mysterious Letters

Main ideaTwenty-nine surahs open with single Arabic letters whose meaning is not known; Muslim scholars have offered many readings and no single answer.

Surah 2 begins with three Arabic letters: Alif, Lam, Mim. They are not a word. They are simply letters, read out by their names. Twenty-nine surahs start this way. Some have one letter, like Surah 50 (Qaf) and Surah 38 (Sad). Some have five, like Surah 19, which opens Kaf Ha Ya Ayn Sad. Fourteen different letters are used in all, exactly half the Arabic alphabet. Scholars call them the muqatta&#x27;at, the disconnected letters.

What do they mean? The honest answer from Muslim tradition is: God knows. Al-Tabari, in the tenth century, listed the many views he had gathered and did not settle on one. Some said the letters are names of God or of the surah. Some said they stand for words. Some said they are a challenge: the Qur’an is made of these same ordinary letters, so try to make its like. Some said they are a call to attention, like clearing the throat before speaking.

Modern scholars, Muslim and not, have added ideas. Some link the letters to the sound of the opening lines that follow. Some note that in most cases the letters are followed by a mention of the Book or the Qur’an itself, as if the letters introduce the text. None of these has become the settled view. A student who says ’nobody knows for sure’ is stating the mainstream position accurately.

The letters matter for reading the page. They are recited exactly as letters, drawn out with their full names. They count as verses in some counting systems and not in others. That is one reason ayah totals differ slightly between old systems. It is also a good example of something students of any scripture meet. Here is a feature of the text that its own tradition treats as an open question.

Seven Oft-Repeated Verses

Main ideaAl-Fatiha, seven short verses, opens the book and is recited in every unit of daily prayer; tradition calls it the Seven Oft-Repeated.

The book opens with a prayer. Surah 1, Al-Fatiha (The Opening), is seven ayahs long. It praises God as Lord of the worlds, the Beneficent and Merciful, Owner of the Day of Judgment. It says: Thee alone we worship, Thee alone we ask for help. It asks to be shown the straight path, the path of those God has favored, not of those who earn anger or go astray. Muslim tradition connects this surah with Qur’an 15:87, which speaks of seven of the oft-repeated and the great Qur’an. Most commentators read the seven as Al-Fatiha, and the name stuck. The surah is repeated because it is recited in every rak’ah, every unit, of the five daily prayers. A Muslim who prays the required prayers says it at least seventeen times a day. Why Not in Date Order?

Main ideaThe Qur'an is not arranged in the order Muslims say it was revealed; tradition holds the surah order was fixed early, and scholars have built separate lists of the order of revelation.

Muslim tradition says the Qur’an was revealed piece by piece over about 23 years, from c. 610 to 632. The Qur’an itself says it was sent down in stages so that it could be recited slowly and settle the heart (17:106, 25:32). But the book is not printed in that order. The first words revealed, by tradition, are 96:1–5, near the end of the book. Surah 2, at the front, is Medinan and late.

Sunni tradition generally holds that the Prophet set the order of the surahs on instruction. The companions then kept that order when they collected the text. Some early scholars allowed that the companions arranged parts of the order. Either way, the arrangement is old and shared. Sunni and Shia copies have the same 114 surahs in the same order. Scholars note that grouping by length was a common way to arrange collections in the ancient world.

Because readers want to know the order of revelation, lists were made. The 1924 Cairo edition prints a traditional chronological number in each surah heading, drawn from early Muslim reports. In 1860 the German scholar Theodor Nöldeke proposed a four-period order using style as a clue: early Meccan, middle Meccan, late Meccan, Medinan. The two lists agree broadly and differ in details, and both are used by scholars today.

Why does this matter? Reading in book order, a student meets law before warning, community before the lone voice in Mecca. Reading in revelation order, the story of the first community unfolds. Muslims mostly read in book order, and memorize in it. Historians often read in the other order. A good student can do both and say why each order shows something the other hides.

The story

Two Pages Older Than Anyone Thought

In a university library in England, two sheets of parchment turn out to be among the oldest Qur'an pages in the world.

The University of Birmingham keeps a collection of old Middle Eastern manuscripts gathered in the 1920s. For decades, two leaves of parchment sat filed with a later Qur'an manuscript. The writing on them was Arabic, in a tall, slanted early style with almost no dots. A researcher working on her doctorate, Alba Fedeli, looked closely and thought the two leaves did not belong with the pages around them. They looked older.

The library sent a tiny piece of the parchment to a radiocarbon lab at Oxford. Radiocarbon dating measures how much of a certain kind of carbon is left in something that was once alive, like the animal skin that became parchment. In 2015 the result came back. With about 95 percent probability, the animal whose skin made the pages had died between 568 and 645 CE. That range overlaps the lifetime of Muhammad, who died in 632.

The text on the two leaves is parts of Surahs 18, 19 and 20. When scholars compared it with the standard printed Qur'an, the wording matched, though the old pages had no vowel marks and few dots, and the verse divisions were shown with simple clusters of strokes. Newspapers around the world ran the story. The library put the pages on display, and thousands came to see them, many of them Muslims from Birmingham's large Muslim community.

Scholars were careful about what the date proved. Radiocarbon dates the skin, not the ink. A scribe could have written on older parchment. Some argued the pages were made a little later than the range suggests, based on the script. But most agreed the leaves were from the first decades of Islam and that they showed a text close to the one Muslims read today. The find did not settle every question. It gave everyone, believers and historians alike, something solid to look at.

16.1

A Recitation Before a Book

Main ideaThe word Qur'an means recitation; Muslims say its words were spoken and heard for years before they were gathered into a single written book.

16.2

Memorizers and Scribes

Main ideaIn the Prophet's lifetime, by Muslim tradition, companions memorized the Qur'an and scribes wrote pieces of it on whatever they had, so the text lived in two forms at once.

Muslim tradition names people who learned the whole text by heart in the Prophet’s lifetime. Such a person is called a , a guardian. Reports in the hadith collections of al-Bukhari and Muslim name several, including Ubayy ibn Ka’b, Abdullah ibn Mas’ud and Zayd ibn Thabit. The Prophet, tradition says, would recite each year’s revelation with the angel Jibril (Gabriel) during Ramadan, and twice in his final year.

Alongside the memorizers were scribes. Tradition reports that the Prophet dictated verses to companions who wrote them on what was available: pieces of parchment, flat stones, the shoulder blades of camels, the ribs of palm leaves. Zayd ibn Thabit, a young man from Medina, is named as a chief scribe. Historians cannot check each report, but they generally accept that some writing took place during the Prophet’s life, since Arabic writing was in use.

What did not exist yet, by all accounts, was one complete bound book with the surahs in order. The pieces were spread among many people and objects. When someone asked what the Qur’an said, the answer lived in the memory of the huffaz (plural of hafiz) and in scattered written sheets that could be checked against them. Memory and writing checked each other.

This double life is the key to what came next. When a written copy was finally gathered, the collectors did not simply copy sheets. Tradition says they required both a written piece and the testimony of those who had heard it from the Prophet. A student can see why: a book that lives in voices needs the voices to vouch for the pages.

Gathering the Pages

16.3

Abu Bakr's Collection

Main ideaMuslim tradition says the first caliph, Abu Bakr, had the Qur'an gathered into one set of sheets after many memorizers died in battle, c. 633.

The Prophet Muhammad died in 632. Abu Bakr, his close companion, became the first , the leader of the Muslim community. Within a year, by tradition, the Muslims fought a hard battle at Yamama in central Arabia against a rival claiming prophethood. Many companions who had memorized the Qur’an were killed. A well-known report in al-Bukhari’s collection tells what happened next.

In that report, Umar ibn al-Khattab went to Abu Bakr and urged him to gather the Qur’an before more huffaz died and parts were lost. Abu Bakr hesitated, saying the Prophet had not done this. Umar pressed him, and Abu Bakr agreed. He called Zayd ibn Thabit and gave him the task. Zayd, the report says, at first felt that moving a mountain would have been easier, but he set to work.

Zayd collected the text from written pieces and from the memories of the companions. Tradition says he required both kinds of evidence. The result was a set of sheets, called suhuf. They were kept with Abu Bakr, then with Umar. After Umar’s death they went to his daughter Hafsa, who had been a wife of the Prophet. It was a reference copy, not a book sent out to the community.

Historians treat this account with respect and with caution. It comes from reports written down a century or more after the events, and the details vary between versions. Most scholars accept that some early collection took place under the first caliphs. What they debate is how complete and how official the collection was, and how much the standard text owes to this stage versus the next one.

Main ideaAround 650, by Muslim tradition, the caliph Uthman had a standard copy made, sent copies to major cities and ordered other versions destroyed, giving the text one form.

They match the Uthmanic text closely in wording, which supports the tradition’s outline. The early script had no dots and vowels. That left room for slight differences in reading. 16.5

Old Manuscripts Speak

Main ideaEarly Qur'an manuscripts, such as the Birmingham leaves radiocarbon-dated to the seventh century, let historians check the tradition against physical evidence.

For most of history, what we knew about the early Qur’an text came from Muslim reports. In the past few decades, scientists have added a new kind of evidence: the parchment itself. measures the decay of a form of carbon in anything that was once alive. Parchment is animal skin, so it can be dated. The method gives a range, not a single year, and it dates the skin, not the writing.

The Birmingham leaves are the best-known case. Two folios, catalogued as Mingana 1572a, hold parts of Surahs 18 to 20. In 2015 the University of Birmingham announced a radiocarbon date of 568 to 645 CE with 95.4 percent probability. The script is , an early Arabic hand that slants to the right, with almost no dots. Scholars connected the leaves to a manuscript in Paris written in the same hand.

Other early manuscripts sit in Istanbul, Cairo, St. Petersburg, Paris and Tübingen. A folio in Tübingen was dated to 649 to 675 CE with 95 percent probability. Together they show that by the mid-seventh century, the Qur’an was being copied on large, carefully prepared parchment in a text that matches the standard reading in wording, though not in spelling conventions, dots or vowel marks.

What these finds do not do is settle every debate. A date range that begins before the Prophet’s death does not mean the pages were written then. The skin may have been prepared and used later. Historians and Muslim scholars both point this out. What the manuscripts give is a floor: the text Muslims read today was already on parchment within a generation or two of the events tradition describes.

Marks on the Page

16.7

Dots and Vowel Marks

Main ideaEarly Arabic script had few dots and no vowel signs; over the first Islamic centuries, scholars added marks so readers who did not already know the text could read it correctly.

Look at the Birmingham leaves and you see a problem. Arabic letters share shapes. The letters for b, t, th, n and y look alike and are told apart by dots above or below. Early Qur’an manuscripts have very few of these dots. They also have no vowel marks, the short signs for a, i and u that sit above and below letters. A reader who already knew the text by heart could read the page. A stranger could not.

Muslim tradition tells how the marks were added. A grammarian of Basra named Abu al-Aswad al-Du’ali (d. c. 688) is credited with the first system: colored dots placed to show vowels. In the time of the governor al-Hajjaj (c. 700), two of his students are said to have added dots to distinguish the consonants. Later, al-Khalil ibn Ahmad (d. c. 786), the founder of Arabic dictionary-making, designed the small strokes and marks for vowels still used today.

Historians note that the details of these stories are uncertain. Manuscripts show the changes happening gradually over a century or more. What is certain is the direction. The script began as a bare memory aid. It became a fully marked script that anyone who knew Arabic could read. Printed copies today show every dot and every vowel. They even mark where to pause and how long to hold a sound.

This history explains something students notice: why a Qur’an page looks so different from an Arabic newspaper. The newspaper leaves out the short vowels, since fluent readers do not need them. The Qur’an keeps every mark, because getting a single sound wrong in recitation matters to Muslims. The page is designed so the voice can be exact.

The Readings: Hafs and Warsh

Main ideaWithin the Uthmanic text, Muslim scholars recognize several accepted ways of reciting, the qira'at; most of the world uses the reading of Hafs, and North and West Africa use Warsh.

The Uthmanic text fixed the letters but not every sound. Small differences in vowels, in lengthening, and occasionally in a word remained among the teachers of recitation in different cities. These traditions of recitation are called qira&#x27;at, readings. Each is passed from teacher to student in a chain going back, Muslims say, to the companions of the Prophet. A hadith says the Qur’an was revealed in seven ahruf, or modes, which scholars connect to this variety in different ways.

In the tenth century, a scholar in Baghdad named Ibn Mujahid (d. 936) selected seven readings as authoritative, one from each of the main cities: Medina, Mecca, Damascus, Basra and three from Kufa. Later scholars accepted three more, making ten. Each reading is named for a master reciter and is known through two of his students. The reading of Asim of Kufa, as transmitted by his student (d. c. 796), is one of the seven.

Today the reading of Hafs is used by the great majority of Muslims, from Turkey to Indonesia to Chicago. The reading of Nafi of Medina, as transmitted by his student (d. c. 812), is common in Morocco, Algeria and parts of West Africa. Copies of the Qur’an are printed in each. The differences are mostly in pronunciation. A small number affect the sense of a word, and commentators discuss those cases.

For Muslims, the readings are not rival Qur’ans. They are accepted ways of sounding one text, all going back to the Prophet’s teaching, in the tradition’s view. Historians see in them the traces of the regional teaching that the early script allowed. Both agree the readings are old, limited and carefully recorded. A trained reciter can often tell you which reading is being used within a few verses.

The Rules of Tajwid

Main ideaTajwid is the set of rules for pronouncing the Qur'an exactly, learned from a teacher, so that every sound comes out as tradition says it should.

means to make something good or beautiful. In Qur’an study it names the rules for correct recitation. The rules say where each sound is made in the mouth and throat. They say how long a vowel is held, when a sound is nasal, when two sounds merge, and where to pause. Muslim teachers tie it to Qur’an 73:4, which tells the Prophet to recite in slow, measured tones. Scholars described the rules from the ninth century on. They are taught the same way today.

Some rules a beginner meets early. is lengthening: certain long vowels are held for two, four or six counts, and printed copies mark them with a small wavy sign. is a nasal hum on certain n and m sounds. Idgham is merging one letter into the next. Qalqalah is a slight echo on five letters when they close a syllable. Each rule has a name and a sign or a place on the page.

Tajwid is learned by ear from a teacher, then checked by the page. A student recites. The teacher corrects. The student repeats. This is how the Qur’an has been passed on, Muslims say, since the Prophet taught his companions, in an unbroken chain of listening. A certificate that a student has recited the whole Qur’an correctly to a teacher is called an ijaza, and it lists the teacher’s own chain of teachers.

Non-Arabic speakers learn tajwid too. Many Muslim children in Chicago speak Urdu, Bosnian, Somali or English at home. They attend weekend classes where they learn to sound the Arabic exactly. Often this comes before they know what the words mean. Muslims explain that the sound itself is the revealed form. Meaning is learned alongside it, not in place of it.

16.10

The Cairo Edition

Main ideaIn 1924 a committee in Cairo printed a carefully checked Qur'an in the Hafs reading; it became the model for most printed copies in the world.

Printing came late to the Qur’an. Muslims long preferred hand-copied mushafs, partly out of respect and partly because early printed Arabic was ugly and full of errors. A Venice printing of about 1537 is thought to have failed for that reason. Printed Qur’ans made by Muslims appeared in St. Petersburg in 1787 and later in India and Istanbul, but they varied in spelling and verse numbering from press to press.

In Egypt, a committee of scholars from al-Azhar, the great teaching mosque of Cairo, set out to produce one authoritative printed text. They worked from the reading of Hafs from Asim and from the classical books on spelling and verse counting. The result appeared in 1924 under King Fuad I and is known as the or the King Fuad edition. It uses the Kufan verse count, giving 6,236 ayahs.

The Cairo edition did not create a new text. Its aim was to print the Uthmanic text in the Hafs reading with full tajwid marks, standard spelling and clear surah headings, including the traditional Meccan or Medinan label and the chronological number. Because it was careful and widely accepted, later presses copied it. The Medina printing complex in Saudi Arabia, which has printed hundreds of millions of copies since 1985, follows the same text.

Historians call 1924 a turning point. For the first time, most Muslims in the world could hold an identical printed page. Scholars in universities use the Cairo verse numbers too. So a citation like 2:255 means the same thing everywhere. Older European translations, including Rodwell’s of 1861, sometimes use a different verse count. That is why a reference can be off by one or two when you compare them.

16.11

Sound First, Still Today

Main ideaEven with printed and digital copies everywhere, Muslims still treat the Qur'an as sound first: memorized, recited aloud and heard.

Everything in this chapter, the collection, the dots, the readings, the printing, served one goal in Muslim eyes: keeping the recited word exact. The Qur’an says of itself that God sent down the Reminder and will guard it (15:9). Muslims read this as a promise that the text will be preserved. Historians describe the same result in different words: a text kept by memory and writing together, with unusual care, from a very early date.

The proof, Muslims often say, is the huffaz. Today millions of people, children and adults, on every continent, have memorized the entire Qur’an. Competitions are held each year in which young reciters from dozens of countries are judged on memory and tajwid. In Ramadan, mosques from Jakarta to Chicago hear the whole book recited over the month. If every printed copy vanished, the tradition says, the text would survive in voices, as it did at first.

Technology has followed the sound. Famous reciters such as Abdul Basit Abdus Samad and Mahmoud Khalil al-Husary were recorded in Egypt in the twentieth century. Their recordings are still listened to daily. Apps show the text with each word highlighted as a reciter reads it, in Hafs or in Warsh. A student in Illinois can hear the same recitation a student in Cairo hears. That is what Uthman’s copies were meant to secure, in a form he could not have imagined.

For a public-school reader, the takeaway is not about belief. It is about how a text can be kept. The Qur’an is a case study in transmission by voice and page together, checked against each other for fourteen centuries, and now checked, too, against parchment in laboratories. Knowing how it got from a cave near Mecca to a phone in your pocket is knowing something real about the book.
