Clauses, Parallel Structure and Usage

The band closes where a sentence is actually built. A sentence is a hierarchy: whatever sits in the main clause is what it ASSERTS, and whatever is tucked into a subordinate clause is handed over as already agreed. So the same two facts in the same sentence can put either one beyond question, depending only on which half a writer dropped it into — and that choice is the last and finest version of the move this whole band has been tracking. On top of that sit the repairs the tests actually ask for: parallel structure, dangling modifiers, agreement across a long subject, and the usage pairs worth owning. A sentence decides what you get to argue with

Two facts in one sentence: the survey was flawed, and the plan was approved. Both are here. Both are true. They are not being treated the same way.

A sentence is a hierarchy of claims. Whatever sits in the main clause is ASSERTED — offered to you to weigh. Whatever sits in a subordinate clause is ASSUMED — handed over as already agreed, and skimmed past on the way to the main verb. Use the swap test: rewrite with the clauses reversed, keep every fact, and whatever changes is the work the structure was doing.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

Both facts are in the sentence, so the sentence is even-handed.

Presence is not rank. Readers weigh what a sentence asserts and skim what it presupposes, because presupposition is how language handles ground that is meant to be shared — nobody could read at all if they stopped at every assumption. So a contested fact in a subordinate clause is fully visible and practically invisible at once. Nothing was hidden; it was ranked below the level at which people object.

Ask what this sentence assumed in order to say what it said.

⚠️ People often think…

Parallel structure is just a style preference.

Parallel form signals that items belong to one set and can be measured against each other. Break the shape and the signal breaks, and a reader stumbles without knowing why. Usually the writer had stopped holding the items in one category too — “her results were better than the other schools” compares results with institutions, which is not a coherent comparison at all.

Fix the list and you often find an item that never belonged in it.

⚠️ People often think…

Everyone knows what a dangling modifier means, so it does not matter.

True only when the accidental reading is absurd. Rain that walks to school gets repaired silently by every reader. But “Having failed the inspection, the council closed the pool” reads as an accusation against the council, and it is entirely believable, so nothing alerts anyone. The rule is mechanical: whatever noun follows the comma is what the phrase describes.

The ones that make you laugh are harmless. Worry about the plausible ones.

Find the main clause, then swap

1 Watch one

Apply the swap test to: “While attendance fell slightly, the new timetable was judged a success.”

  1. Find the two facts: attendance fell, and the timetable was judged a success. Both are present.
  2. Which could stand alone? “The new timetable was judged a success.” So that is the main clause, and it is what is asserted. While demotes the other one.
  3. Swap, keeping every fact: “While the new timetable was judged a success, attendance fell slightly.”
  4. What changed: the original is a report of a success with a caveat; the swap is a report of falling attendance despite a favorable judgement. Neither is untrue, and they are different news.

Helpful Hints

⚡ The one idea

A sentence is a hierarchy of claims. Whatever sits in the main clause is what the sentence ASSERTS and invites you to weigh; whatever sits in a subordinate clause is handed over as already agreed. “Although the survey was flawed, the council approved the plan” asserts the approval and assumes the flaw. Swap them — “Although the council approved the plan, the survey was flawed” — and the flaw becomes the news. Same two facts, and a writer decided which one you would get to argue with.

📐 Two kinds of clause

ClauseTestWhat it does
Independentcould stand alone as a sentenceasserts — you are invited to weigh it
Subordinatestarts with although, because, when, if, that…assumes — handed over as settled

🪜 Parallel structure

Items joined in a list or a comparison must share a grammatical shape. “She likes reading, hiking and to cook” stumbles because the third item changed form. It is not only a style rule: a broken parallel is usually a sign that the writer stopped thinking of the items as members of one set, and a reader feels the logic slip before they can name it.

🪂 The dangling modifier

An opening phrase attaches to whatever noun comes next. “Having failed the inspection, the council closed the pool” says the council failed the inspection. “Walking to school, the rain started” has the rain walking to school. The rule is mechanical: whatever follows the comma is what the phrase describes, so put the right noun there.

📋 Usage pairs worth owning

PairHow to choose
affect / effectaffect is usually the verb; effect is usually the noun
fewer / lessfewer for things you can count; less for quantities you cannot
who / whomhe → who; him → whom
its / it’sit’s expands to it is; its is possessive
between / amongbetween for two or for distinct pairs; among for an undivided group
lie / laylie takes no object; lay puts something down
