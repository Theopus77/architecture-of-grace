Pronouns, Reflexives and Commands

Pronouns

A text from a cousin says "¿Me lo mandas?" Four short words, and you know exactly what she wants: send it to me. A grandmother on the phone says "Sécalo bien" and "No lo pongas todavía," and a pot of rice comes out right. This unit is about the small words that carry the weight in Spanish sentences: lo, la, le, se, me, te, nos, and about the verb forms that tell someone what to do.

You will learn to replace nouns with direct and indirect object pronouns, to put them in the right place before a verb or attached to its end, and to write the accent that appears when a word grows. You will tell your daily routine with reflexive verbs, say that two people help each other, and then move on to commands: the familiar tú forms, the eight irregulars, the polite usted and ustedes forms, and let's with nosotros.

By the end you will be able to follow a recipe, give directions to a stranger in Pilsen, tell a friend not to worry, and explain why dámelo has an accent and no me lo des does not. You will also have most of the present subjunctive in your hands without knowing it yet, which is exactly where the next unit begins.

The big question

How do a few small words and a handful of verb endings let Spanish say who does what to whom, and tell people what to do?

The story

Exam Day, 6:40 a.m.

Daniela has a chemistry final at 8:00, and everything that can go wrong does.

Daniela's alarm rings at 6:00. She turns it off and thinks, "Cinco minutos más" (five more minutes). At 6:40 her little brother shakes her shoulder. "¡Despiértate! ¡Es tarde!" (Wake up! It's late!) She sits up fast. Her chemistry final starts at 8:00, and the bus leaves at 7:15.

She runs to the bathroom. "Me ducho en tres minutos," (I shower in three minutes) she promises herself. She showers, brushes her teeth, and gets dressed: *me ducho, me cepillo los dientes, me visto*. Every one of those verbs has a little word in front of it: me. In Spanish, when you do something to yourself, the verb says so.

In the kitchen her mother holds out a folder. "Tus apuntes. No los olvides." (Your notes. Don't forget them.) Daniela grabs them. Her mother also hands her a sandwich. "¿Me lo preparaste?" (You made it for me?) "Sí, te lo preparé anoche." (Yes, I made it for you last night.) Two tiny words, te and lo, carry a whole sentence of meaning: for you, it.

At the bus stop she checks her bag. The calculator is not there. She texts her brother: "¿Viste mi calculadora?" (Did you see my calculator?) He answers: "La tienes en la mochila, bolsillo de adelante." (You have it in the backpack, front pocket.) She looks. There it is. She did not have to write calculadora again; la did the job.

On the bus, Daniela and her friend Tomás quiz each other. "Nos ayudamos siempre," (We always help each other) Tomás says. By 7:55 she is in her seat, notes read, calculator found, sandwich half eaten. Nothing about the morning went the way she planned, and yet every small word landed exactly where it belonged.

Talk about itDaniela's morning is full of tiny words: me, te, lo, la, los, nos. Which of them mean the person doing the action, and which mean someone or something else?
Section 1

Direct Object Pronouns

33.1

Finding the Direct Object

Main ideaThe direct object receives the action, and Spanish replaces it with lo, la, los or las.

Tomás texts his sister: "¿Tienes la calculadora?" (Do you have the calculator?) She answers, "Sí, la tengo." (Yes, I have it.) The word la is standing in for la calculadora. It is a (direct object pronoun). The direct object is the noun that receives the action of the verb. Ask "what?" or "whom?" after the verb, and the answer is the direct object: I have what? The calculator.

Spanish has four third-person direct object pronouns, and they match the noun in gender and number: (him, it, masculine), (her, it, feminine), (them, masculine or mixed), (them, feminine). "¿Compraste los boletos?" (Did you buy the tickets?) "Sí, los compré." (Yes, I bought them.) "¿Conoces a Marta?" (Do you know Marta?) "Sí, la conozco." (Yes, I know her.) People and things use the same pronouns.

The pronouns for me, you and us are easy because they never change for gender: me (me), te (you, familiar), nos (us). "Mi mamá me despierta." (My mom wakes me.) "Te llamo mañana." (I’ll call you tomorrow.) "El profesor nos escucha." (The teacher listens to us.) The common English-speaker mistake is to translate it as lo every time. If the noun is feminine, it is la: la tarea (the homework) → la hago (I do it).

33.2

Where the Pronoun Goes

Main ideaA direct object pronoun goes right before the conjugated verb, and no before the pronoun.

In English the pronoun follows the verb: I see it. In Spanish it comes first: Lo veo. The pronoun sits directly in front of the (conjugated verb), the verb with a person ending. "Mi hermano me despierta." (My brother wakes me.) "¿La calculadora? La tengo." (The calculator? I have it.) Nothing squeezes between the pronoun and the verb.

When the sentence is negative, goes before the pronoun, not between the pronoun and the verb. "No lo tengo." (I don’t have it.) "No te escucho." (I can’t hear you.) English speakers often write "Lo no tengo" because English puts the negative next to the verb. Remember the order: no + pronoun + verb.

In a question the order stays the same; only the intonation and the question marks change. "¿Me escuchas?" (Are you listening to me?) "¿Los viste?" (Did you see them?) Subject pronouns, when they appear, go before everything: "Yo no la conozco." (I don’t know her.) Say the chain out loud a few times until it feels natural: yo no la conozco, tú no la conoces, ella no la conoce.

Think it through

33.3

Attached to Infinitives and Gerunds

Main ideaWith an infinitive or a gerund the pronoun may attach to the end, and a gerund then needs a written accent.

Daniela says, "Voy a leer los apuntes." (I’m going to read the notes.) With a pronoun she has two choices, and both are correct: "Los voy a leer" or "Voy a leerlos." The pronoun can go before the conjugated verb (voy) or attach to the end of the (infinitive, the -ar, -er, -ir form). Attached, it is written as one word: leerlos, comprarla, verte.

The same two choices exist with the (gerund, the -ando / -iendo form) after estar. "Estoy leyendo los apuntes" becomes "Los estoy leyendo" or "Estoy leyéndolos." Notice the accent that appeared on leyéndolos. A gerund is stressed on the syllable before -ndo: le-YEN-do. Adding -los makes the word longer, so Spanish writes an accent to keep the stress where it was: leyéndolos, comiéndola, esperándote.

Infinitives usually do not need an accent when one pronoun attaches (leerlos, verte), because the stress already falls on the last syllable and one extra syllable does not break the rules. English speakers often forget the gerund accent, or add one to an infinitive where it does not belong. Rule of thumb: gerund + pronoun always takes an accent; infinitive + one pronoun does not.

Section 2

Indirect Object Pronouns

33.4

To Whom and For Whom

Main ideaThe indirect object pronouns le and les mark the person who receives something, and Spanish usually adds them even when the noun is present.

Daniela’s mother made her a sandwich: "Le preparé un sándwich a Daniela." (I made a sandwich for Daniela.) The sandwich is the direct object, the thing made. Daniela is the (indirect object), the person it was made for. Ask "to whom?" or "for whom?" after the verb, and you have found the indirect object.

The indirect object pronouns are me, te, le, nos, les. Only the third person differs from the direct ones: (to him, to her, to you formal) and (to them, to you all). They do not change for gender. "Le escribo a mi abuela." (I write to my grandmother.) "Les mando fotos a mis primos." (I send photos to my cousins.) "Te doy el libro." (I give you the book.)

Spanish often keeps both the pronoun and the noun in the same sentence: "Le preparé un sándwich a Daniela." This looks like a repeat to English speakers, but it is normal Spanish. The phrase a Daniela clears up who le means. Verbs of giving, telling and asking almost always take an indirect object: dar (to give), decir (to say), pedir (to ask for), mandar (to send), regalar (to give as a gift).

A: Les mando una tarjeta. (I'm sending them a card.) In each line find the thing (direct object) and the person (indirect object).

Why does B say les and not le in the second question? What would change if the gift went to one grandfather only?

33.5

Two Pronouns Together

Main ideaWhen a sentence has both pronouns, the indirect one comes first, and le or les becomes se before lo, la, los or las.

"¿Me lo preparaste?" (Did you make it for me?) "Sí, te lo preparé." (Yes, I made it for you.) When both objects are pronouns, the indirect one comes first, then the direct one, and both go before the conjugated verb: me lo, te la, nos los. Think of the order as person first, thing second.

Now the twist. Spanish does not allow le lo or les la. When le or les comes right before lo, la, los or las, it changes to . "Le doy el libro a Marta" becomes "Se lo doy." (I give it to her.) "Les mando las fotos a mis primos" becomes "Se las mando." (I send them to them.) This se is not reflexive; it is just le or les in disguise, changed so two l-words do not bump together.

Because se can mean to him, to her, to you (formal) or to them, Spanish often adds a clarifying phrase: "Se lo doy a ella" (I give it to her). Two English-speaker mistakes to watch for: writing le lo, and putting the direct pronoun first (lo se doy). The chain is always se + lo/la/los/las + verb.

33.6

Double Pronouns on the End

Main ideaTwo pronouns can attach together to an infinitive or gerund, and the longer word always takes a written accent.

Both pronouns can also attach to an infinitive or a gerund, in the same order, as one word: "Voy a dártelo." (I’m going to give it to you.) "Está explicándonoslo." (She is explaining it to us.) The pronouns never split up. Either both go before the conjugated verb (Te lo voy a dar) or both go on the end (Voy a dártelo).

When two pronouns attach, a written accent is always needed, even on an infinitive. Say dar-te-lo: the stress stays on dar, now three syllables from the end, so Spanish writes . The same goes for comprársela (to buy it for her), decírselo (to tell it to him), and any gerund: diciéndoselo, mandándotelas. Find the syllable that was stressed in the plain verb and put the accent there.

The famous example is the command (give it to me), which you will meet again in the next chapter. Da is the command, me is to me, lo is it. The stress stays on da, so the accent goes there. If you can build dámelo, cómpramela and explícanoslo, you have the whole system: verb + indirect + direct, one word, accent on the original stressed syllable.

Section 3

Reflexive Verbs

33.7

Doing It to Yourself

Main ideaA reflexive verb sends the action back to the subject, and its pronoun (me, te, se, nos, os, se) must match the subject.

"Me ducho en tres minutos," Daniela says. Ducharse means to shower, to wash oneself. The subject (Daniela) and the object (Daniela) are the same person, so the verb is (reflexive). In the dictionary a reflexive verb ends in -se: levantarse (to get up), ducharse (to shower), vestirse (to get dressed), acostarse (to go to bed).

To conjugate one, drop the -se, conjugate the verb as usual, and put the matching (reflexive pronoun) in front: me levanto, te levantas, se levanta, nos levantamos, os levantáis, se levantan. The pronoun and the ending must agree with the same subject. "Me levanto a las seis" (I get up at six), never "Me levanta."

Some of these verbs also have stem changes, which show in all forms except nosotros and vosotros: despertarse (e → ie): me despierto, te despiertas, se despierta, nos despertamos; acostarse (o → ue): me acuesto, nos acostamos; vestirse (e → i): me visto, nos vestimos. The pronoun goes right before the conjugated verb, just like the object pronouns you already know.

33.8

The Daily Routine

Main ideaSpanish tells a routine with a chain of reflexive verbs and uses the definite article, not a possessive, for parts of the body and clothes.

Here is Daniela’s normal morning: "Me despierto a las seis, me levanto a las seis y cuarto, me ducho, me cepillo los dientes, me visto y me peino. Desayuno a las siete." (I wake up at six, get up at six fifteen, shower, brush my teeth, get dressed and comb my hair. I eat breakfast at seven.) Notice the last verb: desayunar is not reflexive, because breakfast is not done to yourself.

Look at me cepillo los dientes (I brush my teeth). English says "my teeth"; Spanish says "the teeth," because me already tells you whose they are. The same happens with clothes: me pongo la chaqueta (I put on my jacket), me quito los zapatos (I take off my shoes), me lavo la cara (I wash my face). Writing "mis dientes" is a common English-speaker habit; the article is the natural choice.

Some verbs change meaning when they become reflexive. Ir means to go; means to leave. Dormir means to sleep; dormirse means to fall asleep. Poner means to put; ponerse means to put on. Quedar means to remain; quedarse means to stay. "Me voy a las siete y cuarto" (I leave at seven fifteen) does not say where you are going, only that you are leaving.

33.9

Reflexive or Not?

Main ideaThe same verb is reflexive when the action stays with the subject and not reflexive when it goes to someone else.

Daniela’s brother wakes her up: "Mi hermano me despierta." On a normal day she wakes up by herself: "Me despierto a las seis." Both sentences have me, but they are different. In the first, me is a direct object: he wakes me. In the second, me is reflexive: I wake myself. The verb ending tells you which: despierta (he) versus despierto (I).

Many verbs work both ways. Lavar el carro (to wash the car) versus lavarse (to wash oneself). Acostar al bebé (to put the baby to bed) versus acostarse (to go to bed). Vestir a la niña (to dress the little girl) versus vestirse (to get dressed). The test: does the action land on the subject or on someone else? "La mamá viste a la niña" (the mom dresses the girl), but "La niña se viste" (the girl gets dressed).

The placement rules are the ones you already know. Before a conjugated verb: "Me quiero acostar." Attached to an infinitive: "Quiero acostarme." Attached to a gerund with an accent: "Estoy vistiéndome." The pronoun must still match the subject even when it is attached: "Vamos a levantarnos temprano" (we are going to get up early), not levantarse, because the subject is nosotros.

33.10

Each Other

Main ideaWith a plural subject, nos, os and se can mean each other, and context or el uno al otro makes it clear.

On the bus Tomás says, "Nos ayudamos siempre" (we always help each other). The pronoun nos is (reciprocal): I help you and you help me. Reciprocal meaning only works with plural subjects, so the pronouns are nos, os and se. "Se escriben todos los días." (They write to each other every day.) "Nos vemos mañana." (We’ll see each other tomorrow, a very common goodbye.)

Some of these sentences could also be reflexive. "Se miran" can mean they look at each other or they look at themselves. Usually the situation makes it obvious, but Spanish can add (each other) for reciprocal or a sí mismos (themselves) for reflexive. "Se ayudan el uno al otro." (They help each other.) "Se hablan a sí mismos." (They talk to themselves.)

Common reciprocal verbs: conocerse (to meet, to know each other), abrazarse (to hug each other), saludarse (to greet each other), quererse (to love each other), llamarse (to call each other), and verse (to see each other). The story

Abuela's Arroz con Pollo

Marcos wants to cook his grandmother's rice and chicken by himself, and she coaches him by phone from Humboldt Park.

Marcos is seventeen and has never cooked anything harder than eggs. His grandmother's arroz con pollo (rice with chicken) is the dish every birthday in his family is built around. Tonight his parents are working late, and he wants to surprise them. He calls his abuela, who lives near Humboldt Park, and puts the phone on speaker.

"Primero, lava el pollo y sécalo bien," (First, wash the chicken and dry it well) she says. "Después, pon aceite en la olla y caliéntalo." (Then put oil in the pot and heat it.) Marcos notices something. Every instruction is a verb with no subject: lava, seca, pon, calienta. And when she wants to say "it," the pronoun sticks to the end of the verb: sécalo, caliéntalo.

He gets nervous and pours the rice in too early. "¡No lo pongas todavía!" (Don't put it in yet!) she says, hearing the sound. "No tengas prisa. Espera." (Don't be in a hurry. Wait.) Now the pronoun has jumped. In "no lo pongas" the lo is in front of the verb, and the verb itself has changed shape: pon became pongas.

His older cousin Elena walks in and starts helping. Abuela switches: "Marcos, Elena, corten la cebolla y agréguenla al pollo." (Marcos, Elena, cut the onion and add it to the chicken.) Talking to two people, she uses corten and agreguen instead of corta and agrega. Then she laughs: "Vamos a ver si les sale." (Let's see if it comes out for you.)

Forty minutes later the kitchen smells right. Marcos sends his abuela a photo. "Dime la verdad," (Tell me the truth) he writes. "¿Está bien?" She answers: "Sírvelo con limón y no le digas a tu mamá que yo te ayudé." (Serve it with lime and don't tell your mom I helped you.) He has just learned the whole system of Spanish commands over one pot of rice.

Section 1

Affirmative Tú Commands

34.1

Borrowing the Él Form

Main ideaAn affirmative tú command is the same as the present-tense él/ella form of the verb.

"Lava el pollo. Corta la cebolla. Come." (Wash the chicken. Cut the onion. Eat.) These are commands, and the form is called the (imperative). For a regular verb the affirmative tú command is simply the present-tense él/ella form: hablar → habla (speak), comer → come (eat), escribir → escribe (write). "Ella habla" and "¡Habla!" look identical; only the context and the tone differ.

Because it borrows the él form, the command keeps any stem change: cerrar → cierra (close), volver → vuelve (come back), pedir → pide (ask for), dormir → duerme (sleep). "Cierra la puerta." (Close the door.) "Vuelve pronto." (Come back soon.) English speakers sometimes use the infinitive as a command (cerrar la puerta), which sounds like a sign, not a person talking.

Spanish often softens a command with (please) or with the person’s name: "Marcos, corta la cebolla, por favor." It is also common to add the pronoun tú after the verb for emphasis: "Hazlo tú" (You do it). Tú commands are for one person you address as tú: a friend, a sibling, a classmate, a child. For a teacher or a stranger, you will use the usted forms later in this chapter.

34.2

The Eight Irregulars

Main ideaEight common verbs have short one-syllable tú commands: di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven.

"Pon aceite en la olla." (Put oil in the pot.) Pon does not come from the él form (pone). It is one of eight irregulares (irregulars) that every Spanish speaker uses daily: decir → (say, tell), hacer → (do, make), ir → (go), poner → (put), salir → sal (leave, go out), ser → (be), tener → (have), venir → (come).

Most of them are the él form with the last syllable cut off: pone → pon, tiene → ten, viene → ven, sale → sal. That trick does not work for di, haz, ve and sé, which you simply memorize. Notice the accent on sé (be): it separates the command from se the pronoun. "Sé bueno." (Be good.) "Ten paciencia." (Have patience.) "Ven aquí." (Come here.) "Haz la tarea." (Do the homework.)

Ve is doubly tricky: it is the tú command of ir (go) and also the él form of ver (to see). "Ve a la tienda" means go to the store; the a after it is your clue. English speakers often produce "hace la tarea" or "dice la verdad" as commands; those are statements (he does, he says). The commands are haz and di. Say the eight in a row until they sound like one word: di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven.

34.3

Pronouns on the End

Main ideaObject and reflexive pronouns attach to the end of an affirmative command, and an accent keeps the stress in place.

"Sécalo bien." (Dry it well.) "Caliéntalo." (Heat it.) In an affirmative command, the pronoun attaches to the end of the verb as one word. Seca + lo → sécalo. Calienta + lo → caliéntalo. Compra + la → cómprala. Because the word is now longer, Spanish writes an accent on the syllable that was stressed before: SE-ca → SÉ-ca-lo.

The one-syllable irregulars also take pronouns: di + me → dime (tell me), haz + lo → hazlo (do it), pon + te → ponte (put on), ven + te → vente (come along). With one pronoun on a one-syllable command, no accent is needed. With two pronouns, the accent returns: dímelo (tell it to me), (give it to me), pónselo (put it on him). Indirect pronoun first, then direct, exactly as in the last chapter.

Reflexive verbs make some of the most common commands in a home: "Levántate" (get up), "Siéntate" (sit down), "Despiértate" (wake up), "Ponte los zapatos" (put on your shoes), "Lávate las manos" (wash your hands). The reflexive pronoun is always te with tú commands. A frequent mistake is writing the pronoun separately (levanta te) or forgetting the accent (levantate); both are wrong.

Negative Tú Commands

34.4

Flip the Vowel

Main ideaA negative tú command comes from the yo form: drop the -o, add -es for -ar verbs and -as for -er and -ir verbs.

"¡No lo pongas todavía!" (Don’t put it in yet!) The verb changed: pon became pongas. Negative commands do not borrow the él form. Instead they start from the present-tense yo form, drop the -o, and add an ending with the opposite vowel: -ar verbs take -es, and -er / -ir verbs take -as. Hablar → hablo → no hables. Comer → como → no comas. Escribir → escribo → no escribas.

Starting from the yo form matters because it carries every irregularity. Poner → pongo → . Tener → tengo → no tengas. Hacer → hago → no hagas. Decir → digo → no digas. Salir → salgo → no salgas. Venir → vengo → no vengas. Conocer → conozco → no conozcas. Stem changes carry over too: cerrar → cierro → no cierres; pedir → pido → no pidas.

Some verbs need a spelling change to keep the sound: -car → -ques (buscar → no busques), -gar → -gues (llegar → no llegues), -zar → -ces (empezar → no empieces). Four verbs do not end in -o in the yo form and have their own negative commands: ir → no vayas, ser → no seas, dar → no des, estar → no estés. "No seas tímido." (Don’t be shy.) "No vayas solo." (Don’t go alone.)

34.5

Pronouns Move to the Front

Main ideaIn a negative command every pronoun goes between no and the verb, never on the end.

Compare the two sides: "Ponlo" (put it) but "No lo pongas" (don’t put it). "Levántate" (get up) but "No te levantes" (don’t get up). "Dímelo" (tell it to me) but "No me lo digas" (don’t tell it to me). In an affirmative command the pronouns attach to the end. In a negative command they move in front of the verb, right after , in the usual order: indirect, then direct.

Because the pronouns are separate words in the negative, there is never an accent to add. English speakers often keep the attached form and write "No ponlo" or "No levántate"; both break the rule. The pattern is always no + pronoun(s) + negative command verb. "No se lo digas a tu mamá." (Don’t tell it to your mom.) "No te preocupes." (Don’t worry.)

Some of the most useful daily phrases follow this pattern: "No te preocupes" (don’t worry), "No me digas" (you don’t say, no way), "No lo olvides" (don’t forget it), "No te vayas" (don’t leave). Try making an affirmative and a negative pair for the same idea: Cómelo / No lo comas; Hazlo / No lo hagas; Siéntate / No te sientes.

Usted, Ustedes and Nosotros

34.6

Polite Commands

Main ideaUsted and ustedes commands use the flipped-vowel form for both affirmative and negative: hable, coma, hablen, coman.

A waiter to a customer: "Pase, por favor. Siéntese aquí." (Come in, please. Sit here.) A doctor to a patient: "Respire hondo." (Breathe deeply.) These are commands, for one person you address formally. They use the same flipped-vowel form as the negative tú command, minus the final -s: hablar → hable, comer → coma, escribir → escriba. And they use it whether affirmative or negative: hable / no hable.

For , two or more people, add -n: hablen, coman, escriban. In Latin America ustedes is the plural for everyone, formal or not, so Abuela says "corten la cebolla" to her two grandchildren. All the irregular yo-form verbs behave as expected: tenga / tengan, ponga / pongan, haga / hagan, diga / digan, salga / salgan, venga / vengan. The four special ones: vaya / vayan, sea / sean, dé / den, esté / estén.

Pronouns follow the rule you already know: attached with an accent on the affirmative, in front on the negative. "Siéntese." (Sit down.) "No se siente allí." (Don’t sit there.) "Dígame." (Tell me, also how many people answer the phone in Spain.) "No me diga." (You don’t say.) "Pásenlo bien." (Have a good time, to a group.) The reflexive pronoun for usted and ustedes is se.

34.7

Let's Do It

Main ideaNosotros commands mean let's and end in -emos or -amos, with vamos as the everyday form for let's go.

"Vamos a ver si les sale," Abuela says: let’s see. When the speaker includes herself in the command, Spanish uses the nosotros command, which means let’s. It is built with the same flipped-vowel form: -ar verbs take -emos, -er and -ir verbs take -amos. Hablar → hablemos (let’s talk). Comer → comamos (let’s eat). Escribir → escribamos (let’s write).

For let’s go, Spanish uses plain in the affirmative, not vayamos. "¡Vamos!" (Let’s go!) In the negative it switches back: "No vayamos." (Let’s not go.) The very common phrase vamos a + infinitive also means let’s: "Vamos a comer" (let’s eat), "Vamos a empezar" (let’s begin). Both comamos and vamos a comer are correct; the vamos a form is more casual in speech.

One spelling detail: when the reflexive pronoun nos attaches to an affirmative nosotros command, the final -s drops before it. Sentemos + nos → (let’s sit down), not sentémosnos. Levantemos + nos → levantémonos (let’s get up). Vamos + nos → vámonos (let’s leave), one of the most useful words in the language. The accent appears because the word got longer.

34.8

The Whole Command Map

Main ideaAll commands except affirmative tú use the flipped-vowel form, and pronouns attach only to affirmative commands.

Giving Directions

Main ideaDirections to a stranger use usted commands like siga, doble and cruce, plus a few fixed place words.

A visitor in Pilsen asks, "Perdón, ¿dónde está la biblioteca?" (Excuse me, where is the library?) You answer a stranger with usted commands: " derecho dos cuadras. a la izquierda en la esquina. la calle. Está a la derecha." (Go straight two blocks. Turn left at the corner. Cross the street. It’s on the right.)

The verbs are the usual flipped-vowel forms with their spelling changes: seguir → sigo → siga; doblar → doble; cruzar → cruzo → cruce (z → c before e); subir → suba; bajar → baje. The direction words: (to the right), (to the left), derecho or todo recto (straight ahead), (the block), la esquina (the corner), el semáforo (the traffic light).

To a friend you switch to tú: "Sigue derecho, dobla a la izquierda, cruza la calle." The negative warns: "No cruces aquí" (don’t cross here), "No doble todavía" (don’t turn yet, formal). Watch the pair derecho / derecha: derecho means straight ahead, and a la derecha means to the right. English speakers mix them constantly, and one wrong letter sends someone the wrong way.

Think it through
From the Bus Stop

Un turista: Perdón, ¿cómo llego al museo? (Excuse me, how do I get to the museum?) Tú: Siga derecho tres cuadras. En el semáforo, doble a la derecha. Cruce la calle y suba la escalera. El museo está a la izquierda. Un turista: ¿No doblo en la esquina? (Don't I turn at the corner?) Tú: No, no doble en la esquina. Doble en el semáforo.

Rewrite the directions as if you were talking to a friend (tú). Then draw the route in words: how many turns, and in which directions?

Words to know
siga
continue, go on (usted) — from seguir: sigo → siga; Siga derecho (Go straight)
doble
turn (usted) — Doble a la izquierda (Turn left)
cruce
cross (usted) — from cruzar with the z → c spelling change
a la derecha
to the right — not derecho, which means straight ahead
a la izquierda
to the left — Doble a la izquierda en la esquina
la cuadra
the block — Siga dos cuadras (Go two blocks)
Check yourself

1. Which means "Turn left" to a stranger?

Why: Usted command doble; a la izquierda is to the left.

2. What does "Siga derecho" mean?

Why: Derecho means straight ahead; a la derecha would be to the right.

3. Which is the correct usted command of cruzar?

Why: Cruzar → cruzo → cruce, with z changing to c before e.

Commands

1. Which is the affirmative tú command of escribir?

Why: Affirmative tú commands match the él form: escribe.

2. Which of these is one of the eight irregular tú commands?

Why: Ten is the irregular tú command of tener; the others are regular él forms.

3. Which is correct: "Don't tell it to her"?

Why: Negative: no + se (from le) + lo + digas.

4. Which is correctly written?

Why: The reflexive pronoun attaches and an accent keeps the stress on -ván-.

5. Which form is the usted command of hacer?

Why: Usted commands use the flipped-vowel form from hago: haga.

6. Which means "Let's eat"?

Why: The nosotros command of an -er verb ends in -amos: comamos.

7. Which sentence tells a stranger to go straight?

Why: Usted command siga; derecho means straight ahead.

8. Which form would you expect in a written recipe?

Why: Written recipes usually address the reader as usted: mezcle.

Unit test

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Which pronoun replaces "el sándwich" in "Preparo el sándwich"?

Why: Sándwich is masculine singular, so the direct object pronoun is lo.

2. Which sentence has the correct order?

Why: No comes first, then the pronoun, then the conjugated verb.

3. Which of these is correct?

Why: The pronoun attaches to the gerund and the accent keeps the stress on -cién-.

4. Which pronoun fits: "___ escribo una carta a mis abuelos."

Why: Mis abuelos are the people who receive the letter, plural: les.

5. How do you say "I give them (las fotos) to her"?

Why: Le becomes se before las; fotos is feminine plural.

6. Which form is correct for "we get dressed"?

Why: Nos matches the nosotros ending, and nosotros keeps the stem e: vestimos.

7. What does "Me pongo los zapatos" mean?

Why: Ponerse means to put on; the article los stands in for my.

8. What does "Mis amigos se abrazan" mean?

Why: With a plural subject and abrazarse, se is reciprocal: each other.

9. Which is the affirmative tú command of dormir?

Why: The tú command matches the él form, including the stem change: duerme.

10. Which is the tú command of poner?

Why: Pon is one of the eight irregular tú commands.

11. Which is correct for "Give it to me" (el libro)?

Why: Da + me + lo in one word with an accent because two pronouns attached.

12. Which is the negative tú command of salir?

Why: Start from salgo, drop -o, add -as: no salgas.

13. Which is correct: "Don't sit there" to a friend?

Why: Negative tú command with te in front and the flipped vowel: no te sientes.

14. Which is the ustedes command of venir?

Why: Ustedes: from vengo, flip the vowel, add -n: vengan.

15. Which means "Let's leave"?

Why: Vamos + nos with the -s dropped and an accent: vámonos.

Write it

Escribe un párrafo de 6 a 8 oraciones: describe tu rutina de la mañana con verbos reflexivos y luego dale tres consejos a un amigo con mandatos (uno afirmativo, uno negativo y uno con un pronombre). Write a paragraph in Spanish: your morning routine with reflexive verbs, then three commands of advice to a friend, one affirmative, one negative, and one with a pronoun attached or in front.

  • Use at least four reflexive verbs from this unit, and make each pronoun match the subject.
  • For a negative command, start from the yo form and flip the vowel; put the pronoun before the verb.
  • If a pronoun attaches to an affirmative command or a gerund, check whether it needs a written accent.
  • Use the article, not a possessive, for parts of the body and clothes: me lavo la cara.
  • Read it aloud; if a sentence sounds like English word order, fix it.
