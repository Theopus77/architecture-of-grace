Studying Religion Like a Historian

Method

How do you learn about a religion that is not your own, or study your own with fresh eyes? Historians of religion have a toolkit. They read sacred texts as primary sources. They sort writings by genre, place events on a timeline and trace how traditions spread on a map. They also watch what people do: the rituals they repeat, the journeys they take and the buildings they raise.

This unit gives you that toolkit. In the first chapter you will read sources closely, learn what BCE, CE and c. mean, and see what a historian can and cannot say. In the second you will compare rites of passage, pilgrimages, houses of worship and holy days across many traditions, including ones you can visit in Chicago.

By the end, you will be able to describe a belief fairly by saying who holds it, read a text or a building for clues, and compare traditions without ranking them. These skills will guide every unit that follows.

The big question

How can we study the world's religions fairly and accurately, through their texts, their times and their practices?

The story

Two Dates for One Teacher

Two students find two different birth dates for the Buddha and learn what a small letter c. is doing.

Maya and Leo were making a timeline for class. Their job was to add the Buddha, the teacher whose ideas became Buddhism. Maya opened a library book. It said he was born in 563 BCE. Leo checked a museum website. It said he lived from about 480 to 400 BCE. They stared at the two screens. Which one was right?

They asked Ms. Ortiz, the school librarian. She pointed to a tiny mark on the museum page: c. 480 BCE. "That c. stands for circa," she said. "It is Latin for about. It means historians are making a careful estimate." She explained that no birth certificate survives from ancient India. Stories about the Buddha were told aloud for generations before anyone wrote them down.

"So scholars compare clues," Ms. Ortiz went on. "They look at lists of kings, old texts from Sri Lanka and India, and the order of events. Different clues point to different dates. Older books often used the earlier date. Many newer books use the later one. Both are careful guesses. Neither one changes what Buddhists believe he taught."

Maya and Leo decided to put both dates on their timeline, each with a c. and a short note. Under it they wrote a rule for themselves: say what we know, say how we know it, and say when we are not sure. Ms. Ortiz smiled. "That," she said, "is how a historian works."

Talk about itWhy might two careful books give two different dates for the same person? Is that a problem or a clue?
Section 1

Where Evidence Comes From

15.1

Primary and Secondary Sources

Main ideaA primary source comes from the time or people being studied; a secondary source explains or studies it later.

Picture an old prayer book in a museum case, its pages worn soft by many hands. A historian would call it a . That means it was made by the people being studied, at the time they lived. Letters, sacred texts, carvings, songs and even buildings can be primary sources.

Now picture a modern textbook chapter that explains that same prayer book. It tells who used it and why. That chapter is a . It was written later, by someone studying the past. Secondary sources are helpful because they gather many clues. But they are only as good as the primary sources behind them.

In the study of religion, scripture is often the most important primary source. The Hebrew Bible, the New Testament, the Qur’an, the Vedas and the Guru Granth Sahib all tell us what communities believed and practiced. A historian reads them closely and asks what they show about the people who kept them.

15.2

Who, When, Why and for Whom

Main ideaEvery source has an author, a time, a purpose and an audience, and asking about them helps a reader understand it.

Historians ask four questions about any source. Who made it? When? Why? And for whom? These questions do not judge the source. They help a reader see it clearly. The answers are called the source’s , the setting that surrounds it.

Take the letters of Paul in the New Testament. Paul was an early follower of Jesus who traveled around the Roman Empire in the 50s CE. He wrote to small groups of Christians in cities such as Corinth and Rome. His letters answer their questions and settle their arguments. Knowing that helps a reader understand why he writes the way he does.

Sometimes the author is unknown or debated. Many ancient texts were passed down by a community and shaped over time. Jewish and Christian tradition, for example, has long named Moses as the author of the Torah. Many modern scholars think it was gathered from several sources over centuries. A historian reports both views and does not choose between them.

Many Kinds of Writing

15.3

Law and Story

Main ideaSacred books often hold different kinds of writing, called genres, such as laws that guide life and stories that teach through events.

Open the Torah, the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, and you will find more than one kind of writing. Genesis is mostly , or story: the creation of the world, Abraham’s journey, Joseph in Egypt. Leviticus is mostly rules for worship, food and daily life. A kind of writing is called a .

Jewish tradition calls the Torah’s commandments . By one well-known count there are 613 of them. They cover prayer, holidays, food, farming, fairness in business and care for strangers. Many Jews study these laws closely, along with the later rabbinic writings that explain how to live them.

Knowing the genre changes how a reader reads. A law asks, what should a person do? A story asks, what happened, and what does it mean? A historian notices which is which. That helps explain how a community uses each part of its scripture.

15.4

Poetry and Prophecy

Main ideaPoetry expresses feeling through images, and prophecy, as traditions describe it, is a message spoken on behalf of God.

The book of Psalms is a collection of 150 songs and prayers in the Hebrew Bible. Jews and Christians have sung and prayed them for centuries. They use : images, rhythm and repeated lines. Poems praise, give thanks, grieve and ask for help.

is a different genre. In the Hebrew Bible, prophets such as Isaiah and Amos speak messages that, according to the text, come from God. They often warn rulers to treat the poor fairly. Some speak of a hoped-for future of peace. Islam also honors prophets, and Muslims believe Muhammad was the final one.

Poetry and prophecy are not read like a list of rules. Readers look at pictures in the words and ask what they point to. That is why the same poem can comfort one reader at a funeral and inspire another at a protest.

15.5

Letters and Teachings

Main ideaSome sacred writings are letters to real communities, and others are collected sayings of a teacher.

More than twenty books of the New Testament are , a word that means letters. Most were written to early Christian groups or leaders. They were read aloud when believers gathered. They give advice, answer questions and encourage readers facing hard times.

Other texts collect teachings, the sayings of a respected teacher. The Analects gather sayings of Confucius, a Chinese teacher who lived c. 551 to 479 BCE. His students wrote them down after he died. The Dhammapada gathers verses that Buddhists trace back to the Buddha.

A letter and a book of sayings are built differently. A letter follows one writer’s thoughts to one audience. A collection of sayings jumps from topic to topic, like a notebook. A historian notices these shapes. They show how a text was made and used.

Section 3

Putting Time in Order

15.6

BCE and CE

Main ideaBCE and CE count years before and after year 1 in the common calendar, and historians use them so every tradition can share one timeline.

Look at a timeline in a history book. You will often see two labels: , Before the Common Era, and , the Common Era. Years in BCE count down toward year 1. So 500 BCE is earlier than 400 BCE. Years in CE count up: 610 CE comes before 1469 CE.

The system comes from a Christian calendar. Around 525 CE, a monk named Dionysius Exiguus counted years from the birth of Jesus as he calculated it. Those labels were BC, Before Christ, and AD, Anno Domini, Latin for in the year of the Lord. Many scholars now think Jesus was born a few years earlier, c. 6 to 4 BCE.

Many historians use BCE and CE because the numbers stay the same while the labels do not name any one faith. Many people still use BC and AD. Some traditions also keep their own calendars. The Jewish calendar counts from creation as tradition reckons it. The Islamic calendar counts from 622 CE, when Muhammad moved to Medina.

15.7

When Dates Are Uncertain

Main ideaThe mark c. means about, and it tells a reader that a date is a careful estimate from the evidence.

Many ancient events were not written down when they happened. Stories were passed on by memory for generations. So historians often cannot give an exact year. They write c., short for the Latin , meaning about. A date like c. 1200 BCE is an honest estimate.

Scholars make these estimates from clues. They compare old texts, lists of rulers, coins, carvings and events in nearby lands. Sometimes the clues disagree. That is why the Buddha’s life has two common date ranges: c. 563 to 483 BCE and c. 480 to 400 BCE. Different scholars weigh the clues differently.

Traditions may also keep their own dates. Some Hindus date the start of the present age to 3102 BCE, based on ancient calculations. A historian can report that date as a belief of the tradition. Then the historian can also report what archaeologists have found. Both are useful facts about the world.

15.8

When Traditions Began

Main ideaThe major world religions began at different times across more than three thousand years, and many grew from older roots.

A timeline of world religions starts long ago. Hinduism has no single founder. Its oldest texts, the , were composed c. 1500 to 1000 BCE and passed on by memory. Judaism traces itself to Abraham and Moses. Historians see Israelite religion taking shape in the land of Israel c. 1000 BCE and after.

Later teachers started new movements. The Buddha taught in northern India c. 500 to 400 BCE. Confucius taught in China at about the same time. Jesus taught in Judea c. 30 CE, and Christianity grew from his followers. Muslims believe Muhammad received the first revelation of the Qur’an in 610 CE.

Newer traditions continue the story. Guru Nanak, the first Sikh Guru, was born in 1469 CE in Punjab. The Bahá’í Faith began in Iran in the 1800s with Bahá’u’lláh. Its House of Worship in Wilmette, Illinois, opened in 1953. A timeline shows that religions often grow out of the ones around them.

Section 4

Maps and Limits

15.9

Where Religions Began and Live Now

Main ideaReligions often begin in one small region and later spread worldwide, so a map of beginnings looks very different from a map of today.

Look at a map of beginnings. Judaism, Christianity and Islam all began in Southwest Asia, the region some call the Middle East. Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism began in South Asia. Confucianism and Daoism began in China. All began within a few thousand miles of each other.

Now look at a map of today. Buddhism is now found mostly in East and Southeast Asia, not in India where it began. Most Christians today live in Africa, the Americas and Europe. The country with the most Muslims is Indonesia, far from Arabia. Religions travel with traders, teachers, rulers and families. This spreading is called .

Chicago shows this too. In 1893 the city hosted the World’s Parliament of Religions, one of the first large meetings of leaders from many faiths. Today Chicago has churches, synagogues, mosques, temples and gurdwaras. Many were built by immigrants who carried their traditions with them.

15.10

What a Historian Can and Cannot Say

Main ideaA historian can describe what people believed and did, but cannot use evidence to decide whether a belief is true.

A historian can say a great deal. Christians believe Jesus rose from the dead. Muslims believe the Qur’an is the word of God given to Muhammad. The Buddha, according to Buddhist tradition, reached enlightenment under a tree at Bodh Gaya. Each sentence reports a belief and names who holds it. That is called .

A historian cannot use evidence to prove or disprove those beliefs. Texts, coins and ruins show what people thought and did. They cannot measure God or test a teaching about the soul. So a historian writes in a way. That means describing fairly, without taking a side.

This matters in a public school. Students may belong to many faiths, or none. Studying religion as a historian lets everyone learn together. You learn what each tradition teaches in its own words. No one is asked to believe it or reject it.

The story

Four Buildings on One Bus Route

A class rides one Chicago bus and learns to read four houses of worship from the outside in.

Mr. Kim's class climbed onto a city bus with notebooks and pencils. Their task was simple to say and hard to do. They would look at four houses of worship along the route, from the outside only. They would write what they saw, and guess what each shape might mean. Then they would check.

The first was a stone church with a tall tower and a cross on top. Seen from above, its floor plan made a cross too. Two blocks later came a mosque with a dome and a slim tower called a minaret. Leila, whose family prays there, said the prayer hall faces Mecca. "Even in Chicago," she said, "everyone faces the same direction."

Next was a synagogue with Hebrew letters over the door and a carved scroll-shaped symbol. Then the bus passed a gurdwara flying a tall orange flag. Arjun pointed. "There is a free meal inside every day," he said. "Anyone can eat." The class wrote down the flag and the open doors.

Back at school, Mr. Kim asked what they had learned. Sofia raised her hand. "A building is like a book," she said. "The shape tells you what the people inside care about." Mr. Kim wrote her words on the board. Then he added one more line: "And the best way to check is to ask them."

Section 1

Actions Done on Purpose

16.1

What Makes a Ritual

Main ideaA ritual is an action done the same way on purpose, and adherents say it connects them to something they hold sacred.

At a Jewish Friday night dinner, a family lights two candles and says a blessing over bread and wine. They do it the same way every week. An action repeated on purpose, in a set pattern, is called a . Rituals can be large, like a festival, or small, like a bow before a meal.

Rituals often use . A symbol is an object or action that stands for something bigger. The candles can stand for the rest and holiness of the Sabbath. In many Christian churches, water in baptism stands for washing and new life. A symbol says a lot without many words.

Scholars notice that rituals do several jobs. They mark time. They bind a group together. They pass beliefs from old to young without a lecture. Adherents usually describe rituals in their own terms, as ways of honoring God or following a teacher’s path.

16.2

Birth and Coming of Age

Main ideaMany traditions mark birth and growing up with rites of passage that welcome a person into the community.

A marks a move from one stage of life to the next. Birth is often the first. In many Muslim families, a parent whispers the call to prayer into a newborn’s ear. Many Christian churches baptize babies. Sikh families often choose a baby’s name from a letter found by opening the Guru Granth Sahib.

Coming of age is another step. In Judaism, a boy becomes a at 13, which means son of the commandment. In many communities a girl becomes a bat mitzvah at 12 or 13. Often the young person reads from the Torah before the congregation. In many churches, teens take part in confirmation.

Other traditions mark growing up in their own ways. In some Hindu families, a boy receives a sacred thread in a ceremony called upanayana. In Myanmar, many Buddhist boys spend a short time as novice monks. Sikhs of any age may choose Amrit, a ceremony of commitment to the faith.

16.3

Marriage and Death

Main ideaTraditions mark marriage and death with rituals that express their teachings about love, family and what comes after life.

At a Sikh wedding, the couple walks four times around the Guru Granth Sahib while hymns are sung. This is the , the ceremony of bliss. At a Hindu wedding, the couple often takes seven steps together around a sacred fire. At many Jewish weddings, the couple stands under a canopy called a chuppah.

Death rituals also differ. Many Hindus cremate the body. Some families carry the ashes to a holy river such as the Ganges. Muslims and Jews usually bury the dead quickly, often within a day. In Islam, the body is placed facing Mecca. Christians often hold a funeral that speaks of hope in the resurrection.

After a Jewish funeral, close family , seven days of mourning at home. Friends bring food and pray with them. A historian compares these rituals without ranking them. Each shows what a tradition teaches about life, loss and community.

Journeys to Sacred Places

16.4

Mecca, Jerusalem and Rome

Main ideaA pilgrimage is a journey to a sacred place, and three cities draw pilgrims from Islam, Judaism and Christianity.

Each year, about two million Muslims travel to Mecca in Saudi Arabia for the . It is one of the Five Pillars of Islam. Muslims who are able, in health and money, are expected to go once in their lives. Pilgrims wear simple white clothing and walk seven times around the , a cube-shaped building at the center of the Great Mosque.

Jerusalem is sacred to three traditions. Many Jews pray at the Western Wall, a remaining wall from the area of the ancient Temple. Christians visit the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, where tradition places Jesus’s burial. Muslims pray at the al-Aqsa Mosque, near the Dome of the Rock. One city holds many memories.

Rome draws Catholic Christians to St. Peter’s Basilica in Vatican City. Catholic tradition holds that the apostle Peter was buried there. A is more than a trip. Pilgrims describe it as a journey of the heart as well as the feet.

16.5

Varanasi, Bodh Gaya and Amritsar

Main ideaSouth Asia holds pilgrimage places sacred to Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs, each tied to a river, a teacher or a Guru.

At dawn in , Hindu pilgrims walk down stone steps into the Ganges River. Many Hindus regard the Ganges as sacred and bathe in it to purify themselves. Varanasi is one of the oldest living cities in the world. Many Hindus hope to have their ashes placed in the river there.

In Bodh Gaya, Buddhists from around the world sit beneath a large fig tree. According to Buddhist tradition, the Buddha reached enlightenment under a Bodhi tree on this spot. The present tree is said to grow from a descendant of the first. Monks and visitors meditate, chant and leave flowers.

In Amritsar, Sikhs visit the Harmandir Sahib, often called the Golden Temple. It sits in a pool of water and has four doors, one on each side. Sikhs say the doors show that people from every direction and background are welcome. Its kitchen serves free meals to tens of thousands of visitors a day.

16.6

Church and Mosque

Main ideaA church and a mosque are shaped by how their communities worship, from a cross-shaped floor to a wall that points to Mecca.

Many old churches in Europe and America are built in the shape of a cross when seen from above. The long hall where people sit is the . At the far end is the altar, where many Christians celebrate communion, a meal of bread and wine that remembers Jesus’s last supper. Tall towers and stained glass often draw eyes upward.

A is shaped by prayer. Inside, one wall points toward Mecca. In it is a small arched niche called the . Muslims pray in straight rows facing that wall. Many mosques have a minaret, a tower from which the call to prayer is given.

Mosques usually contain no pictures of people. Many Muslims hold that God should not be shown in images. Instead, walls are decorated with geometric patterns and Arabic writing from the Qur’an. A historian can read each building as a statement of how its people worship.

16.7

Synagogue and Gurdwara

Main ideaA synagogue centers on the Torah scroll, and a gurdwara centers on the Guru Granth Sahib and a shared kitchen.

In a , the most important object is the Torah scroll. It is kept in a cabinet called the ark, usually on the wall facing Jerusalem. A light often burns above it. During services, the scroll is carried to a raised platform called the bimah and read aloud. The building points the community toward its text.

In a , which means doorway to the Guru, the Guru Granth Sahib rests on a raised seat under a canopy. Sikhs treat it with the respect given to a living teacher. Everyone covers their head and removes their shoes. People sit on the floor together, which Sikhs say shows that all are equal.

Every gurdwara has a , a free community kitchen. Volunteers cook and serve a vegetarian meal to anyone who comes. Outside stands the Nishan Sahib, a tall flag with the Sikh emblem. It tells travelers that food and shelter can be found inside.

16.8

Mandir and Stupa

Main ideaA Hindu mandir leads worshippers toward a small inner shrine, and a Buddhist stupa is a dome that pilgrims walk around.

A Hindu temple, or , is often built as a path inward. Worshippers pass through halls toward a small, dim inner room. It holds the , a sacred image of a deity. Above it rises a tower. Many Hindus see the temple as a home for the deity and come for darshan, seeing and being seen by the divine.

A is a Buddhist monument shaped like a dome. Many hold relics, remains or objects linked to the Buddha or a respected teacher. The Great Stupa at Sanchi in India dates in part to the 200s BCE. Pilgrims walk around a stupa clockwise, often chanting or praying as they go.

The two shapes teach different movements. A mandir draws people inward, toward the image at its heart. A stupa invites people around, in a circle of respect. A historian reads both shapes as clues to what their communities practice.

Section 4

Sacred Time

16.9

A Holy Day Each Week

Main ideaJudaism, Christianity and Islam each set apart one day of the week, but they choose different days and keep them differently.

The , or Shabbat, is the Jewish day of rest. It begins at sunset on Friday and ends after dark on Saturday. Many Jews do not work, and families share meals, pray and study. The Torah connects the day to God resting after creation.

Most Christians gather on Sunday, the day they believe Jesus rose from the dead. Many attend worship and rest from work. Muslims gather on Friday for Jumu&#x27;ah, the midday congregational prayer at a mosque. Many Muslims work on Friday, but they leave time for the prayer and sermon.

So three related traditions set apart three different days. Each one ties its day to a teaching or a story. A calendar, then, is more than a list of dates. It shows what a community remembers together.

From the source
Rest on the Seventh Day
And on the seventh day God finished His work which He had made; and He rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had made.
Genesis 2:2, The Holy Scriptures, Jewish Publication Society (1917)

16.10

Fasts and Feasts

Main ideaMany traditions follow a time of fasting with a time of feasting, and both teach through the body what a tradition values.

During , the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, many Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. They take no food or drink during daylight. The Qur’an names Ramadan as the month it was first revealed. At the end comes Eid al-Fitr, a feast of prayer, food and gifts.

On , the Day of Atonement, many Jews fast for about 25 hours and pray for forgiveness. Many Christians keep Lent, forty days before Easter, by giving something up. A means going without food or other things for a religious reason. Adherents describe fasts as a time to focus on God and on others.

Feasts bring people together. Hindus, Sikhs and Jains celebrate Diwali with lamps and sweets. Jewish families retell the Exodus at the Passover meal, called the seder. Sikhs mark Vaisakhi in spring. Because the Islamic calendar follows the moon, Ramadan moves about eleven days earlier each year.
