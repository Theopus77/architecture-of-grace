Close Reading the Suttas

Suttas

This unit reads two suttas slowly, line by line. The first is the talk Buddhists call the first sermon, set in a deer park near Varanasi. The second is the long account of the Buddha's last journey and death. Buddhists remember one as the beginning of the teaching and the other as its passing on.

Both texts come from the Pali Canon, and both were translated by T.W. Rhys Davids in 1881. That translation is in the public domain, so you will read its exact words. You will also compare it with modern translations, and see how one Pali word can become two different English words.

By the end you will be able to explain the four noble truths as the sermon states them, trace how the Book of the Great Decease answers the question of who leads after a teacher dies, and describe how Theravada, Mahayana and modern readers read these texts. You will describe beliefs and attribute them; you will not be asked to share them.

The big question

How do a tradition's accounts of its first teaching and its teacher's death shape what its followers do?

Setting the Wheel in Motion

The First Sermon
Big questionWhy do Buddhists treat one short talk to five listeners as the start of their whole tradition?
The story

The Five Who Meant to Ignore Him

Five men agree to give their old companion no welcome, and then find they cannot keep their promise.

The later tradition tells the scene this way. Five wandering ascetics are living in a deer park at Isipatana, near Varanasi. Once they practiced hard fasting with a man named Gotama. Then he gave up fasting and ate a meal. The five decided he had quit, and they left him.

Now they see him walking toward them. They agree among themselves not to stand, not to take his bowl and not to greet him. They will only set out a seat, since he was once of good family. In the Vinaya's telling, their plan falls apart as he comes near. One takes his bowl, one sets out water and one prepares a seat.

At first they still call him by his name and speak to him as an equal. The Buddha, as the text presents him, tells them not to address him that way. He says he has found what they were looking for. They doubt him. He asks whether he has ever spoken like this before. They admit he has not, and they agree to listen.

What he says next is the talk Buddhists call the first sermon, the Setting in Motion of the Wheel of the Dhamma. Buddhists in every school recite it. Theravada Buddhists remember it each year on Asalha Puja. This chapter reads it closely, line by line, in the 1881 translation by T.W. Rhys Davids.

Section 1

The Setting of the Sermon

23.1

Where and to Whom

Main ideaThe first sermon is set in a deer park near Varanasi and is spoken to five former companions of the Buddha.

Every sutta opens by saying where it happened and who was there. The first sermon names the deer park at Isipatana, today called Sarnath, near the city of Varanasi. It names the listeners as the group of five monks. The Pali name of the talk is , the discourse on setting the wheel of the Dhamma in motion.

The sutta appears in the Samyutta Nikaya, at 56.11, in the collection of talks on the truths. A longer version, with the story of the journey to Sarnath, appears in the Vinaya, in the book called the Mahavagga. Rhys Davids translated the sutta in Sacred Books of the East volume 11 in 1881. That English version is now in the public domain.

Scholars date the Buddha’s life to about the fifth century BCE, often c. 480 to c. 400 BCE. Theravada tradition gives earlier dates, placing his death in 544 or 543 BCE. The words were passed down by memory for centuries. They were written down in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE, as Sri Lankan chronicles report. So the text we read is the as its reciters kept it, not a recording.

23.2

The Two Extremes

Main ideaThe sermon opens by naming two extremes a person who has left home should avoid.

The sermon does not begin with the truths. It begins with a warning. Rhys Davids renders the opening this way: there are two extremes which one who has given up the world ought to avoid. The first is a life given to pleasures, which the text calls low and unprofitable. The second is a life given to self-torment, which the text calls painful and unprofitable.

The listeners knew the second extreme well. They had fasted with Gotama for years. The sermon’s first move, then, speaks to their own past. It tells them that the hard path they admired did not lead where they hoped. Readers often point out that the Buddha, as the text presents him, had tried both extremes himself: a palace life and harsh fasting.

Between the two, the sutta says, lies a middle path. It says this path opens the eyes, gives understanding, and leads to peace, insight and . Buddhists across schools call their way the for this reason. The phrase later took on other meanings too, as when Mahayana thinkers spoke of a middle between being and non-being.

23.3

Reading a Spoken Text

Main ideaRepetition and numbered lists in the suttas come from a tradition of memory and recitation.

Read the sermon aloud and you notice repetition. Phrases return again and again with one word changed. Each truth is stated, then stated again in the same frame. To a silent reader today this can feel slow. To a monk reciting with others, it was a way to keep every word exact.

The suttas also love numbered lists: two extremes, four truths, an eightfold path, three turnings, twelve aspects. Scholars such as Rupert Gethin note that lists work like hooks for memory. A reciter who knows there are eight parts will notice if one is missing. Buddhist teachers today still teach through these lists.

So a close reader asks two questions. What does the passage say? And how does its shape help it survive? The sermon’s repetition is not padding. It is how an guarded the text for centuries before it was written. It is also why the sutta still works well when chanted, as monks do at Asalha Puja.

Section 2

The Four Truths, Line by Line

23.4

The Truth of Suffering

Main ideaThe first truth names dukkha, the unsatisfactory side of life, through a list of human experiences.

Rhys Davids renders the first truth as the noble truth concerning suffering. The sutta then lists what it means. Birth is suffering. Decay is suffering. Illness and death are suffering. Union with the unpleasant and separation from the pleasant are suffering. Not getting what one wishes is suffering.

The Pali word is . Many translators now say that suffering is too narrow. Dukkha can also mean stress, unease or unsatisfactoriness. Walpola Rahula argued in 1959 that no single English word covers it. The list in the sutta ranges from great pain, like death, to ordinary frustration, like wanting something and not getting it.

Buddhist teachers stress that this truth is a diagnosis, not a complaint. Many compare the four truths to a doctor’s method: name the illness, its cause, its cure and the treatment. The sutta itself does not use the doctor image. Later commentators and modern teachers do. Readers should keep the text and its later readings apart.

The First Truth Stated
Now this, O Bhikkhus, is the noble truth concerning suffering.
Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta 5, tr. T.W. Rhys Davids, Buddhist Suttas, Sacred Books of the East 11 (1881)

23.5

The Cause: Craving

Main ideaThe second truth names tanha, craving or thirst, as the origin of suffering.

The second truth, in the sutta’s words, concerns the origin of suffering. The Pali word is , literally thirst. Rhys Davids renders it as craving. The text names three kinds: craving for sense pleasures, craving for existence, and craving for non-existence.

The image of thirst is worth pausing on. Thirst returns after it is satisfied. The sutta describes craving as leading to renewed becoming and delighting now here, now there. Theravada teachers read this as a claim about how karma and rebirth continue. Some modern readers focus instead on how craving shapes a single day.

Notice what the sutta does not name as the cause. It does not blame the world, the body or other people. It points to a pattern of wanting inside experience. Buddhists believe this is why the problem can be addressed: if craving is the cause, then its ending is possible. That idea leads straight to the third truth.

23.6

The End and the Path

Main ideaThe third truth names the ending of craving, and the fourth names the eightfold path that leads there.

The third truth concerns the cessation of suffering. The sutta describes it as the complete fading away and ending of that very craving, letting it go and being free of it. Buddhists call this state nibbana in Pali, nirvana in Sanskrit. The word suggests a flame going out.

The fourth truth names the path. The sutta lists eight parts: right views, right aspirations, right speech, right conduct, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right rapture, in Rhys Davids’ wording. Modern translators often render the last as right concentration. Buddhist teachers group the eight into wisdom, ethics and meditation.

The word right translates samma, which can also mean complete or whole. So the path is not a checklist done in order. Teachers describe the eight as supporting one another, like strands in a rope. Here the sermon returns to its start: this is the middle path it named at the opening.

Think it through
Two Translations of One Step

Rhys Davids (1881) renders the eighth step 'right rapture.' Many modern translators render the same Pali word, samadhi, as 'right concentration.' One stresses a joyful state; the other stresses steady focus.

Section 3

Three Turnings and a Witness

23.7

Three Turnings of Each Truth

Main ideaThe sutta says each truth was known in three ways, making twelve aspects in all.

After naming the four truths, the sutta has the Buddha describe his own knowledge. For each truth, he says, there were three stages. First: this is the truth. Second: this truth is to be done in a certain way. Third: this has been done. Readers call these the three turnings or three phases.

Each truth has its own task. Suffering is to be fully understood. Craving is to be abandoned. Cessation is to be realized. The path is to be developed. So the four truths are not only facts to know. As the sutta frames them, each asks for a different response.

The Buddha, as the text presents him, then says that until this knowledge in twelve aspects was complete, he did not claim full awakening. Once it was, he did. Buddhist commentators read this passage as the heart of the sermon. It shows the truths as a practice, not only a teaching.

23.8

Kondanna Understands

Main ideaThe sutta ends by saying one listener, Kondanna, understood, and tradition counts him as the first to do so.

At the end of the talk, the sutta turns to the listeners. It says that the eye of truth arose in Kondanna, one of the five. What arose was the insight that whatever has the nature to arise also has the nature to cease. It is a short line, but Buddhists treat it as a turning point.

The Buddha, in the text, exclaims that Kondanna has understood. From this, the text says, Kondanna gained the name Annata Kondanna, Kondanna who knows. Theravada tradition counts him as the first person to enter the path through the Buddha’s teaching. In the Vinaya’s fuller story, he then asks to be ordained, and the begins.

Notice the content of his insight. It is not a new fact about the four truths. It is a general pattern: what begins, ends. Theravada commentators call this the stream entry of the first disciple. Later the other four understand too, over the following days, after more teaching.

23.9

The Cry Through the Worlds

Main ideaThe sutta ends with gods proclaiming the event from world to world, a sign of how its tellers valued it.

The sermon does not end with the five. The text says that earth-dwelling gods raised a cry: the wheel of the Dhamma has been set in motion at Benares, and no one in the world can turn it back. The cry passes up through heaven after heaven, each group of gods repeating it, until it reaches the Brahma world.

The sutta then says the ten-thousand-fold world system shook and a great light appeared. Here the text uses the language of cosmology, the Buddhist picture of many worlds and many kinds of beings. Buddhists have read the passage in several ways. Many traditional readers take it as an account of events. Some modern readers read it as a way of saying the event was cosmic in importance.

A careful student does not need to decide. The task is to describe what the text says and why its tellers included it. The repeating cry uses the same memory technique as the rest of the sutta. It also gives the talk its name: the has begun to turn, like a wheel that has started rolling.

Section 4

Why It Is Called First

23.10

The Three Jewels Complete

Main ideaBuddhists call it the first sermon because, as tradition tells it, the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha were all present for the first time.

Buddhists speak of the Three Jewels or Three Refuges: the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha. Before the sermon, the tradition says, there was a Buddha but no community that followed his teaching. After it, the five became the first monks. So Buddhist teachers often say the Three Jewels were complete for the first time at Sarnath.

This is one reason Buddhists call it the first sermon, even though the tradition reports that the Buddha had spoken with others before. Two merchants, Tapussa and Bhallika, offered him food earlier and took refuge. But they received no teaching of the truths. The sermon at Sarnath is the first teaching of the Dhamma as a path.

For this reason Sarnath became a place of pilgrimage. The emperor Ashoka set up a pillar there in the third century BCE. Its lion capital is now the national emblem of India. The wheel on the Indian flag also recalls the wheel of Dhamma, a sign of how widely the image traveled.

23.11

One Sermon, Many Readers

Main ideaTheravada, Mahayana and modern readers all honor the first sermon but read its place differently.

Theravada Buddhists treat the first sermon as a foundation. It is chanted at ordinations and at Asalha Puja. Monks in Thailand, Sri Lanka and Myanmar learn it by heart. For Theravada teachers, the four truths and eightfold path are the core of the whole Dhamma.

Many Mahayana traditions honor it too, but some describe it as the first turning of the wheel. Some Mahayana sutras speak of a second turning, the teaching of emptiness, and a third turning, the teaching found in texts such as the Samdhinirmocana Sutra. In that view the first sermon is true and needed, but not the last word.

Modern readers add their own emphases. Some present the four truths as practical psychology. Others, such as Thich Nhat Hanh in The Heart of the Buddha’s Teaching (1998), focus on transforming suffering in daily life. Scholars ask how the text grew over time. A careful reader describes each view without ranking them.

23.12

Key Terms in Context

Main ideaThe terms dukkha, tanha and nibbana form a chain in the sermon: the problem, its cause and its ending.

Three Pali words carry the sermon’s argument. names the problem. names its cause. names its ending. The fourth truth, the path, names how to get from one to the other. Seen this way, the four truths make one chain of reasoning.

Each word is hard to translate. Dukkha can be suffering or unease. Tanha can be craving or thirst. Nibbana can be blowing out, as of a flame, or release. Translators choose, and each choice shapes a reader’s picture. That is why a close reader keeps the Pali word in view beside the English.

Spelling also varies. Pali forms are dukkha, kamma, nibbana and dhamma. Sanskrit forms are duhkha, karma, nirvana and dharma. English books use both. Theravada writers tend to use Pali; many Mahayana writers use Sanskrit. Neither spelling is more correct. They point to the same terms in two languages of Buddhist texts.

The Buddha's Last Days

Mahaparinibbana Sutta
Big questionWhat does the account of the Buddha's death teach its readers about how a community goes on without its teacher?
The story

Three Times He Asked

An old teacher lies between two trees and gives his students one more chance to ask.

The sutta sets the scene in a grove of sal trees near Kusinara, a small town in northern India. The Buddha, now about eighty years old in the tradition's telling, is lying on his right side between two trees. The text says the trees burst into bloom out of season, and flowers fell on his body.

Monks have gathered. Ananda, his cousin and attendant for many years, has already gone off to weep. He leaned on a door frame, the text says, and cried that his teacher was passing away before his own training was complete. The Buddha sent for him and praised his years of kindness.

Now the Buddha turns to all the monks. It may be, he says, that one of you has a doubt about the Buddha, the teaching or the community. Ask now, so you will not regret later that you had the chance and missed it. No one speaks. He asks a second time. Silence. He asks a third time. Still no one speaks.

Then, the sutta says, he gives his final words. This chapter reads the Book of the Great Decease, Digha Nikaya 16, in T.W. Rhys Davids' 1881 translation. It is the longest sutta in the Pali Canon, and one of the most loved.

Section 1

The Book of the Great Decease

24.1

The Longest Sutta

Main ideaThe Mahaparinibbana Sutta, Digha Nikaya 16, tells the Buddha's final journey and death as the tradition remembers it.

The is the sixteenth discourse in the Digha Nikaya, the Long Discourses. Its name means the great discourse on the final nibbana. Rhys Davids called it the Book of the Great Decease in Sacred Books of the East 11 (1881), and divided it into six chapters with numbered verses.

The sutta follows the Buddha’s last months. He travels from Rajagaha north toward Kusinara, stopping in towns and teaching along the way. He falls ill, recovers, eats a final meal, and dies. Then the text describes the funeral and how the relics were shared. Few suttas follow a story over so many places and weeks.

Scholars think the sutta grew over time. Parts may be very early, and others were likely added as the community developed. Versions survive in Chinese and Sanskrit too, which lets scholars compare. For Buddhists, it is the account of the , the final passing of one who has already reached nibbana.

24.2

Seven Conditions of Welfare

Main ideaEarly in the sutta, the Buddha lists conditions that keep a community strong, first for a people and then for monks.

The sutta opens with politics. King Ajatasattu of Magadha plans to attack the Vajjians, a people governed by assemblies. He sends a minister to ask the Buddha’s view. The Buddha, as the text presents him, does not answer directly. He asks Ananda questions about the Vajjians instead.

Do they meet often and in full numbers? Do they meet in harmony and act in harmony? Do they respect their elders and their customs? Do they honor their shrines and protect holy people? As long as they do, the Buddha says, they may be expected to prosper. These are the seven .

Then he gathers the monks and gives them a parallel list for the . Meet often. Meet and act in harmony. Keep the rules. Respect elders. Readers often note the link: the sutta that ends with the teacher’s death opens with how a group stays whole. The Vinaya-minded tradition read this as guidance for the order after him.

24.3

No Closed Fist

Main ideaIn the sutta, the Buddha says he has held nothing back and does not see himself as the one who leads the order.

At Beluva the Buddha falls seriously ill. When he recovers, Ananda says he was comforted by the thought that the Buddha would not pass away without leaving instructions for the order. The Buddha’s reply, as the text gives it, is one of the most quoted passages in the sutta.

He says he has taught without any difference between inner and outer teachings. The teacher, he says, has no closed fist that keeps some things back. He adds that he does not think of himself as the one who leads the order or that the order depends on him. So why would he leave special instructions?

Readers have taken this in several ways. Theravada teachers see it as a sign that the Pali teachings are complete and open. Some Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions, which value later or esoteric teachings, read the passage in its context: the Buddha held back nothing his listeners needed. Scholars note it as a clue to early debates over authority.

Section 2

Lamps and Refuges

24.4

Be Ye Lamps

Main ideaThe Buddha tells Ananda to be a lamp and a refuge to himself, with the Dhamma as lamp and refuge.

The passage right after the closed fist gives the sutta’s best-known line. The Buddha, as the text presents him, says he is old and his body is worn like a cart held together with straps. Therefore, he tells Ananda, be lamps unto yourselves. Be a refuge to yourselves. Hold fast to the truth as a lamp and as a refuge.

The Pali word dipa can mean lamp or island. Rhys Davids chose lamp. Many modern translators, such as Maurice Walshe (1987), choose island: be an island to yourselves, a safe place in a flood. Both readings make sense. A lamp gives light to see by. An island gives safe ground. The translator’s choice colors the verse.

The line does not tell Ananda to rely on himself instead of the teaching. It pairs self and Dhamma: take the self as refuge, and the Dhamma as refuge, nothing else. The sutta then explains how: through mindfulness of body, feelings, mind and mental objects. Teachers across schools quote this passage when students fear losing a teacher.

From the source

Therefore, O Ananda, be ye lamps unto yourselves. Be ye a refuge to yourselves. Betake yourselves to no external refuge. Hold fast to the truth as a lamp. Hold fast as a refuge to the truth.

24.5

Ananda's Grief

Main ideaThe sutta shows Ananda weeping and the Buddha comforting him, a scene readers value for its honesty.

Ananda is the most human figure in the sutta. He was the Buddha’s cousin and his attendant for about twenty-five years, tradition says. At Kusinara, the text says, he went into a building, leaned against the door frame and wept. He had not yet reached awakening, and his teacher was leaving.

The Buddha sends for him. As the text gives it, he tells Ananda not to weep. Has he not taught that we must part from all that is dear? Whatever is born must end. Then he praises Ananda to the monks. For years, he says, Ananda served him with kind acts, kind words and kind thoughts. He tells Ananda to keep striving, and he will soon be free.

Buddhist readers draw two lessons. The teaching on impermanence holds even for the Buddha. And grief is met with kindness, not scolding. Tradition adds that Ananda reached awakening on the night before the , where he recited the suttas from memory. So the one who wept becomes the one who keeps the teaching.

24.6

The Dhamma as Teacher

Main ideaBefore he dies, the Buddha says his teaching and discipline will be the monks' teacher after him.

Near the end, the Buddha anticipates a worry. Some may think, he says, that the word of the Teacher is ended and they have no teacher. They should not think so. The truths and the rules he has taught, the and the , will be their teacher after he is gone.

This answers the question the sutta has asked from its start: who leads when the leader is gone? The answer is not a successor. It is a body of teaching and a code of discipline. Buddhist communities read this as the reason the texts matter so much. The recited words take the place of the living teacher.

Different traditions built on this in different ways. Theravada monasteries center the Vinaya and the suttas. Many Mahayana and Vajrayana communities also stress living teachers and lineages, while honoring the texts. Zen speaks of transmission from teacher to student. Each claims continuity with the Buddha, but they locate it in different places.

Section 3

The Last Meal and Last Words

24.7

Cunda's Meal

Main ideaThe Buddha eats a final meal from Cunda the smith, falls ill, and then makes sure Cunda is not blamed.

At Pava, a smith named Cunda invites the Buddha and the monks for a meal. The text says Cunda served a dish called sukara-maddava. The Buddha asks that it be served only to him and that the rest be buried. After eating, he falls ill with severe pain, but he continues on toward Kusinara.

The dish’s name is uncertain. It may mean something like pig’s delight. Rhys Davids translated it as dried boar’s flesh, a common reading. Other readers, ancient and modern, think it was a kind of mushroom or a food that pigs like. The Pali commentaries already record different views. Scholars today still do not agree.

What the sutta stresses is Cunda. The Buddha tells Ananda that some may blame Cunda. He should be told instead that his gift carries great merit. Two meals, the text says, are equal in fruit: the one before the Buddha’s awakening and the one before his final passing. Readers see this as care for a layperson’s feelings, even at the end.

24.8

Compounded Things Decay

Main ideaThe Buddha's last words, as the sutta records them, are that all compounded things decay and his followers should strive with diligence.

After asking three times for questions and hearing none, the Buddha speaks his last words. Rhys Davids renders them: decay is inherent in all component things; work out your salvation with diligence. In Pali, the words are short: vayadhamma sankhara, appamadena sampadetha.

means things that are put together, formed or conditioned. Rhys Davids calls them component things. The claim is that anything made of parts will come apart. means heedfulness or diligence, not being careless. The Dhammapada’s second chapter praises the same quality. Salvation is Rhys Davids’ word; many modern translators prefer strive or bring about the goal.

Readers note how the last words gather the sutta’s themes. The body is like the worn cart. The dear must part. Even the Buddha’s life is conditioned and ends. Yet the response is not despair. It is effort. The sutta then describes him entering deep meditative states and passing away, as its tellers remember.

Relics, Stupas and Readers

24.9

Dividing the Relics

Main ideaThe sutta ends with the cremation and the sharing of relics among eight groups who build stupas.

The last chapter describes the funeral. The Mallas of Kusinara honor the body for six days with music, garlands and perfume, the text says. The body is cremated. Then kings and clans from other regions send messengers. Each claims a share of the remains, and a dispute threatens.

A brahmin named Dona calms them. The Buddha taught patience, he reminds them. It would not be right to fight over his remains. He divides the relics into eight equal portions. Each group takes one home and builds a , a mound for relics. Dona keeps the vessel, and a late arrival takes the ashes of the fire.

The sutta says the Buddha had earlier told Ananda that his remains should be treated like those of a universal king, with a stupa at a crossroads. Buddhists across Asia have built stupas ever since. Archaeologists at Piprahwa, in India, found a relic vessel in 1898 with an inscription that many scholars read as naming the Buddha’s relics.

24.10

Four Places to Visit

Main ideaThe sutta names four places a devoted person may visit: where the Buddha was born, awakened, first taught and passed away.

In chapter 5, Ananda worries that after the Buddha’s death the monks will no longer see him. The Buddha, as the text presents him, names four places worth visiting with reverence. Where the Buddha was born. Where he awakened. Where he set the wheel of the Dhamma in motion. Where he passed away.

Buddhists identify these as Lumbini, in today’s Nepal; Bodh Gaya; Sarnath; and Kusinara, today Kushinagar. All four are still sites. Pilgrims from Sri Lanka, Thailand, Japan, Tibet and the United States visit them. The emperor Ashoka marked Lumbini with a pillar in the third century BCE, and its inscription names the place as the Buddha’s birthplace.

The passage shows the sutta thinking ahead. The teacher will be gone, but places can hold his memory. The text says those who die while on such a journey with a devoted heart will be reborn in a heavenly world. Buddhists read the four places as reminders; the practices at them are described in guides to Buddhist pilgrimage.

Think it through
Four Places, Four Events

Lumbini: birth. Bodh Gaya: awakening. Sarnath: first sermon. Kushinagar: final passing. The sutta itself does not name the towns in this passage; it names the events, and Buddhists match them to places.
