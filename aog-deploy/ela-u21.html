Diction, Syntax and Style

Language

Two students describe the same hospital waiting room with the same facts, and one paragraph holds its breath while the other floods. A teacher takes a pencil to three hundred words and leaves one hundred twenty, with every idea still standing. In both rooms the question is the same: not what the writing says, but how it says it, and why that how changes what a reader feels.

This unit is about style as a set of choices you can see, name and make. Chapter one takes apart diction and syntax: the level of a word, the feeling it carries, the length and shape of a sentence, where a sentence puts its weight, when a fragment is a tool, and how to say precisely what a passage is doing instead of saying 'the author uses diction.' You will imitate sentences by writers who knew exactly what they were doing, and you will learn that a voice is not a gift but a pattern.

Chapter two turns the same eye on your own drafts. You will learn to cut redundancy and filler, to free the verbs hiding inside nouns, to choose the exact word, to vary rhythm, to make paragraphs cohere, and to decide between the plain style and the ornate one on purpose. You will edit a classmate's paragraph without stealing their voice and build a seven-point checklist short enough to use. By the end you will read like an editor and write like someone who has one.

The big question

How do the choices a writer makes about words and sentences carry meaning that the facts alone cannot?

The story

Two Paragraphs, One Room

A teacher hands out two descriptions of the same hospital waiting room, written by two students, and asks the class which one is nervous.

Ms. Okafor put two paragraphs on the board without names. Both described the same thing: a hospital waiting room at two in the morning, a boy waiting to hear about his father. The facts were identical. Same room, same boy, same clock on the wall, same vending machine humming in the corner. She asked the class one question: which writer is more afraid? Nobody could point to a single word that said afraid. Yet within thirty seconds, every hand pointed to the first paragraph.

The first paragraph was built the way Ernest Hemingway built his: short, plain sentences, most of them under ten words. The boy sat. The clock said two. A door opened and it was not the doctor. The words were small and common, the kind you learn by second grade. No sentence explained how the boy felt. The reader had to sit in the gaps between the sentences, and the gaps were where the fear lived. The paragraph did not run; it held its breath.

The second paragraph was built the way William Faulkner built his: one sentence that went on for six lines, clause piled on clause, the room and the boy's memories and the smell of the floor cleaner and the sound of his mother's voice on the phone an hour earlier all braided together without a period in sight. The words were bigger and more unusual. The sentence did not hold its breath; it could not stop talking. It was afraid too, but its fear was a flood instead of a held breath.

That is the whole point of this chapter. The first paragraph did not say 'short sentences feel tense.' The second did not say 'long sentences feel overwhelmed.' They just were those things, and every reader in the room felt it before anyone could name it. A writer's *style* is not decoration added at the end. It is the sum of the choices about which words and which sentence shapes to use, and those choices carry meaning as surely as the facts do.

By the end of the chapter you will be able to look at any paragraph and say precisely what it is doing: what level its words are pitched at, how its sentences are shaped, where it puts its weight, and how fast it moves. You will also be able to do it on purpose in your own writing, which is the difference between having a style and having one happen to you.

Section 1

Diction: The Words You Pick

48.1

Levels of Diction

Main ideaDiction is word choice, and every word sits at a level, from formal to casual, that tells the reader how to take the sentence.

Read these three sentences, all about the same event. ’The gentleman departed from the residence.’ ’The man left the house.’ ’The guy took off.’ Nothing about the facts changed. A male person went away from a building. What changed is the , the writer’s choice of words. Each version sits at a different level, and each level tells the reader something about who is speaking, to whom, and how seriously.

Writers usually talk about three levels. diction uses longer, often Latin-based words, no contractions and no slang: ’departed,’ ’residence,’ ’commence.’ It sounds like a legal document, a scholarly article or a ceremony. Standard (sometimes called middle) diction uses ordinary, correct English that most readers use every day: ’left,’ ’house,’ ’begin.’ Most published writing lives here. Informal or diction is the language of talk: ’took off,’ ’guy,’ ’kick things off.’ It includes slang and contractions and sounds like a person, not a page.

No level is better than the others. The question is fit. A eulogy in slang would feel disrespectful; a text to a friend in formal diction would feel like a joke. Skilled writers move between levels on purpose, and the move itself carries meaning. When a formal paragraph suddenly drops to ’and then it all fell apart,’ the drop lands hard because the reader feels the level change. When you analyze a passage, name the level first. Then ask why the writer chose it, and look for the moments where it shifts.

48.2

Connotation Inside a Sentence

Main ideaA word's connotation is the feeling it carries beyond its definition, and one swapped word can turn a whole sentence toward praise or blame.

’The senator was thrifty.’ ’The senator was cheap.’ ’The senator was stingy.’ Look up all three words and you will find nearly the same , the dictionary meaning: careful with money. Yet the first sentence is a compliment, the second a shrug, and the third an insult. The difference is , the cloud of feeling and association that travels with a word. Thrifty carries pictures of a wise grandmother. Stingy carries a picture of a closed fist.

You learned connotation as a word-level idea years ago. At this level you need to see how it works across a whole sentence, where several connotations add up. ’The crowd surged toward the stage’ and ’The mob swarmed toward the stage’ describe the same people moving the same way. But crowd is neutral and mob is dangerous; surged is energetic and swarmed is insect-like. Stack two loaded words and the sentence now takes a side without ever stating an opinion. That is why connotation matters so much in news writing, in political speech and in your own essays.

When you analyze a passage, do not just say ’the writer uses connotation.’ Everyone does. Instead, find the loaded words, name the direction they lean, and explain what the sentence would lose if a neutral word replaced them. Try it on this line written for the lesson: ’The old house squatted at the end of the lane.’ Replace squatted with stood. The house is the same shape, but stood is upright and calm, while squatted is low, heavy and a little ugly. The writer chose squatted so you would dislike the house before anyone walks in.

48.3

Register and Audience

Main ideaRegister is the level of language you choose for a particular audience and situation, and matching it is the first job of any writer.

A student writes to her principal: ’hey can u move my exam, thx.’ Nothing is misspelled on purpose, and the request is clear. It still fails, because it is pitched in the wrong . Register is the version of the language you select for a specific audience, purpose and situation. You already own several registers. You do not talk to a toddler, a coach, a judge and your best friend in the same way, and you did not have to be taught that. Writing asks you to make the same choice on paper, where you cannot see the listener’s face.

Register combines the diction levels from the first lesson with other choices: contractions or none, first person or not, sentence length, how much you explain, whether you joke. A lab report is in a high, impersonal register: ’The solution was heated to 80 degrees.’ A group chat is in a low, personal one: ’ok we’re heating it, this is taking forever.’ A college application essay lives in between: personal and warm but grammatically careful, with no slang. The most common failure in student writing is not bad grammar. It is a register mismatch, sounding like a text message in an essay or like a lawyer in a story.

To choose a register, ask three questions before you write. Who will read this, and what do they expect? What do I want them to do or feel? What would sound wrong here? Then hold the register steady, because a slip is loud. A cover letter that says ’I am highly motivated and, like, super organized’ loses the reader at ’like.’ When you read a professional writer, notice how consistent the register is, and notice the rare moments when it breaks on purpose to make you laugh or flinch.

Think it through

Syntax: The Shape of the Sentence

48.4

Sentence Length and Pace

Main ideaSyntax is sentence structure, and the simplest syntax choice, how long each sentence runs, sets the pace a reader feels.

Passage A, written for this lesson: ’The door was open. Nobody was home. She set the bag down and waited.’ Passage B: ’Because the door stood open onto an empty house that still smelled of the morning’s coffee, she set the bag on the counter and waited for a sound that did not come.’ Same door, same bag, same waiting. Passage A is three sentences totaling fourteen words. Passage B is one sentence of thirty-two. Read them aloud and you will feel the difference in your chest. A is tense and clipped. B is slow and full.

is the arrangement of words into phrases, clauses and sentences. Length is its most visible feature. Short sentences make the reader stop often, and each stop is a small beat of silence, so a string of short sentences feels urgent, blunt or stunned. Long sentences carry the reader across many ideas without a stop, so they feel thoughtful, flowing or, if they go on too long, exhausting. Neither is right in itself. A writer controls , the speed at which a reader moves, largely by varying length.

The strongest effect comes from contrast. A long sentence followed by a short one makes the short one hit like a door slamming. Read this: ’She had rehearsed the speech for a week, timed it, cut it, practiced it in the mirror and in the shower and on the bus, until every pause was planned. Then the microphone died.’ The second sentence is only four words, and it lands because the first was thirty. When you analyze a passage, count. Actual numbers, average length and the range from shortest to longest, make your claim about pace something a reader can check.

48.5

Word Order and Inversion

Main ideaEnglish expects subject, then verb, then object; when a writer inverts that order, the moved word grabs attention.

English is a word-order language. ’The dog bit the man’ and ’The man bit the dog’ use the same four words and mean opposite things, because the subject normally comes first, then the verb, then the object. Readers expect that order so strongly that when a sentence breaks it, they notice, even if they cannot say what they noticed. is the deliberate reversal of the usual order, and writers use it to throw weight onto a word that would otherwise sit unnoticed.

Compare: ’The letter came at last’ and ’At last the letter came.’ In the second, the writer has pulled ’at last’ to the front, and the relief in those two words now opens the sentence. Compare ’I have never seen such a mess’ with ’Never have I seen such a mess.’ The inverted version sounds older, more emphatic, almost theatrical, because the negative word leads and forces the verb ahead of the subject. Poets and orators love this move. Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address begins ’Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth,’ putting the time first, so the sentence opens like a door into history instead of starting flatly with ’Our fathers.’

Inversion is a spice, not a meal. One inverted sentence in a paragraph draws the eye; five in a row sound like a wizard in a movie. The other everyday form of reordering is simpler: moving a phrase to the front or the end. ’She signed the contract, reluctantly’ and ’Reluctantly, she signed the contract’ put the same word in the two positions readers remember most, the very end and the very beginning. When you analyze syntax, look for anything that is not in its expected place. Then ask what the writer gained by moving it.

48.6

Periodic and Loose Sentences

Main ideaA loose sentence gives its main point first and adds on; a periodic sentence holds the main point until the end, building suspense.

Two sentences, written for this lesson. First: ’She passed the exam, after three months of studying every night, two failed practice tests and a week of barely sleeping.’ Second: ’After three months of studying every night, two failed practice tests and a week of barely sleeping, she passed the exam.’ The words are the same. The first one tells you the outcome in four words and then trails the details behind it. The second makes you wait through all the details before you learn what happened.

The first is a (also called cumulative): the main clause comes early, and phrases and clauses accumulate after it. You could stop it at almost any comma and still have a complete thought. Loose sentences feel natural, relaxed and conversational, because that is how people talk. The second is a : the main clause is delayed until the end, so the sentence is not grammatically complete until its last words. Periodic sentences build suspense and land with force, because the reader has been holding all those details, waiting for the point.

Lincoln’s opening sentence at Gettysburg is loose: ’our fathers brought forth’ comes early, and the modifiers pile up after it. Many famous last lines are periodic, since the delay makes the ending ring. Neither shape is better. A paragraph of only periodic sentences would exhaust a reader, who would always be waiting; a paragraph of only loose ones can feel slack. When you analyze a passage, spot where the main clause sits. Early means loose, late means periodic, and a shift from one to the other usually marks a moment the writer wants you to feel.

From the source

48.7

Fragments on Purpose

Main ideaA sentence fragment is an error when it is an accident and a tool when it is a choice, and skilled writers use fragments for emphasis.

You have been told for years that a sentence fragment is wrong. In a lab report or a college application, it still is. But open almost any novel or magazine essay and you will find fragments doing deliberate work. Written for this lesson: ’He checked the lock twice, then the windows, then the lock again. Nothing. Just the wind.’ ’Nothing’ has no verb and no subject. ’Just the wind’ has no verb. Both are , and both are exactly right, because they sound like the man’s mind, checking and finding.

A fragment works when it is clearly a choice. Three signs tell the reader it is on purpose. It is short. It sits after a complete sentence that sets it up. And it carries a single strong beat: an answer, a correction, a punch. ’She said she would call. She didn’t. Not once.’ The fragment ’Not once’ hammers the point that the complete sentence already made. A fragment fails when it is long, wandering and unattached, like ’Which is the reason that I decided, after talking with my counselor about my options for the fall semester.’ That is not a beat. That is a sentence that lost its main clause on the way.

Two cautions. First, know your audience: fragments belong in narrative, personal essays, speeches and ads, not in formal academic or professional writing, where readers will assume you made a mistake. Second, ration them. One fragment in a paragraph is a drumbeat. Six is a drum solo, and readers stop hearing the beat. When you analyze a passage and find fragments, do not mark them as errors. Ask what each one emphasizes and why the writer chose a broken sentence over a whole one at that exact spot.

48.8

Where the Weight Falls

Main ideaReaders remember the end of a sentence most and the beginning next, so a writer controls emphasis by what goes in those two slots.

Read these two sentences. ’The bridge collapsed at 4:15 on a Tuesday afternoon.’ ’At 4:15 on a Tuesday afternoon, the bridge collapsed.’ Both are grammatical and both are clear. But in the first, the last thing in your ear is the day of the week. In the second, the last thing in your ear is the collapse. The second sentence is about a disaster; the first sounds almost like a schedule. The facts are identical. The emphasis moved because the words at the end moved.

Writers call this (some say end weight): the final position in a sentence is the position of greatest stress, and the opening position is second. The middle is where things go to be forgotten. This gives you a rule you can actually use. Put the word you most want remembered at the end of the sentence. Put the idea that connects to the previous sentence at the beginning. Bury the routine details, dates, minor names, qualifications, in the middle. ’The report, which ran to two hundred pages and took eleven months, recommended closing the plant’ sends the reader out on ’closing the plant,’ which is the news.

Punctuation shapes emphasis too. A colon points forward like a finger: whatever follows it is announced. A dash sets a phrase apart so it stands alone for a second. Parentheses whisper; whatever is inside them is marked as less important. Repetition builds weight the way a hammer does, one strike after another, which is why speeches repeat their key phrases. When you analyze a passage for emphasis, find the last word of each sentence and read those last words in a column. In good prose, that column is a summary of what the writer wanted you to keep.

Reading and Making a Style

48.9

Analyzing a Passage Precisely

Main ideaA precise style analysis names specific choices in diction and syntax, shows them in the text, and explains the effect each one creates.

Weak style analysis sounds like this: ’The author uses diction and syntax to create a mood.’ Every author does. That sentence could be stapled to any passage ever written, which means it says nothing. Strong analysis sounds like this: ’The passage relies on one- and two-syllable words and sentences averaging seven words, and the only long sentence, twenty-six words, arrives at the moment the narrator finally sees the letter, so the syntax slows exactly where the narrator’s attention does.’ The second version names a choice, measures it and ties it to a place in the text and an effect.

Use a routine. First, read the passage twice, once for meaning and once for sound, ideally aloud. Second, mark the diction: circle words that are unusually formal, unusually plain, loaded, technical or repeated. Third, mark the syntax: note sentence lengths, any fragments, any inversion, where main clauses sit, what each sentence ends on. Fourth, look for the shift, because almost every good passage changes somewhere, from long to short, from formal to blunt, from loose to periodic. Fifth, connect each choice to an effect and, above all, to the passage’s purpose. The question is never just ’what does the writer do’ but ’what does it do to the reader, and why would the writer want that?’

Write the analysis in the pattern of claim, evidence, effect. Claim: the writer’s sentences shorten as the danger grows. Evidence: the first paragraph averages nineteen words per sentence; the last averages six, ending in a two-word fragment. Effect: the reader’s breath shortens with the sentences, so the fear is felt rather than announced. Use the technical terms from this chapter, but only where they earn their place. ’Periodic’ is useful when it explains why an ending lands. It is noise when it is just a word you learned.

48.10

Imitating a Style

Main ideaImitation, writing your own content in another writer's sentence shapes, is the fastest way to feel a style from the inside.

Musicians learn by playing other people’s songs. Painters copy the masters in museums. Writers, for centuries, have done the same thing: taken a sentence they admire and written a new one in its exact shape, with their own subject. This is , and it is not cheating, because you keep the structure and change every word. It teaches you what analysis alone cannot, which is how a sentence feels to build.

Here is the method. Take a model sentence. Written for this lesson: ’After the storm, when the streets were still black with water and the power had not come back, the neighbors carried out chairs and sat in the dark together.’ Map it: introductory phrase, then a ’when’ clause with two parts joined by ’and,’ then the main clause with a compound verb and a final phrase. Now write your own sentence in the same slots. ’Before the exam, when the hallway was still loud with rumors and the doors had not opened, the students spread out notes and quizzed each other on the floor.’ Same architecture, new building.

Do this with sentences of different shapes: a periodic sentence, a loose one, a three-word sentence after a long one, a passage with a fragment. Then do it with a whole paragraph, keeping the pattern of lengths. You will notice things you never saw as a reader: how hard it is to hold a periodic sentence together, how a fragment needs a strong sentence in front of it. Afterward, the shapes are yours. You will find them showing up in your own drafts, in your own voice, which is exactly how every writer you admire learned to write.

48.11

Voice as a Set of Choices

Main ideaA writer's voice is not a mystery gift; it is the pattern of diction and syntax choices the writer makes over and over.

People talk about a writer’s as if it were something you are born with, like the sound of your speaking voice. It is not. Voice on the page is a pattern: the level of diction a writer returns to, the sentence lengths they favor, how often they use fragments, whether they like periodic suspense or loose ease, what they put at the ends of sentences, whether they joke. Read enough of one writer and you can recognize a paragraph without a name on it, the way you recognize a friend’s walk from across a parking lot. What you are recognizing is the sum of the choices.

This is good news, because it means voice can be worked on. Walt Whitman’s voice comes from long, listing lines, plain and physical nouns, and a first person that keeps addressing the reader directly: ’I celebrate myself, and sing myself, / And what I assume you shall assume.’ Emily Dickinson’s voice comes from short lines, dashes that interrupt, unexpected capital letters and abstract nouns treated like people: ’Because I could not stop for Death – / He kindly stopped for me –.’ Neither poet was mysterious. Each made a small set of choices with great consistency.

To find your own voice, stop looking for it and start choosing. Take a paragraph you wrote and ask the chapter’s questions of it. What level is your diction, and does it shift on purpose? How long are your sentences, and do they vary? Where does your weight fall? Then decide what you want more of. A writer who chooses short declaratives and plain nouns for a year will have a voice, and it will be theirs. Style is not the clothes a piece of writing wears. It is the way it moves, and you decide how it moves.

Three Hundred Words Become One Hundred Twenty

An editor sits down with a student's paragraph, a pencil and one rule: nothing leaves the page unless the idea it carried is still there when it is gone.

Devon was proud of the paragraph. It was the opening of his college essay, and it was about the summer he spent fixing bikes at a community shop on the West Side of Chicago. It ran to about three hundred words. Every sentence was grammatical. His English teacher, Mr. Reyes, read it twice, set it down, and said, 'Every idea in here is good. About half the words are not.' Then he uncapped a pencil and asked Devon to watch.

The first sentence said, 'In today's modern society, there are many people who believe that it is a true fact that working with your hands is a thing of the past.' Mr. Reyes drew a line through 'In today's modern society,' because today is modern and society is where people are. He cut 'there are many people who believe that' to 'many people believe.' He cut 'it is a true fact that,' because a fact is true and a belief is not a fact anyway. What remained was 'Many people believe working with your hands is a thing of the past.' Thirty-one words had become thirteen. Devon read both versions. The short one sounded more sure of itself.

They kept going. 'The reason I decided to volunteer was because' became 'I volunteered because.' 'Made an adjustment to' became 'adjusted.' 'At this point in time' became 'now.' A sentence that explained what the next sentence was going to say was cut entirely, because the next sentence said it. A sentence that repeated the first sentence in different words was cut too. Twice Mr. Reyes stopped and put a word back, because cutting it had changed the meaning. That, he said, was the test. Cut, then read it aloud, and if anything is missing, restore it.

When they finished, the paragraph was one hundred and twenty words. Devon counted the ideas in the long version and the short one: the same seven. But now the reader met them in a third of the time, and each one stood in its own sentence with air around it. The line Devon had liked best, about the first bike he fixed alone, had been buried in the middle of a forty-word sentence. Now it was a sentence by itself, at the end of the paragraph, where a reader would carry it into the next one.

This chapter is about that pencil. Cutting is not the opposite of good writing; it is most of it. You will learn to spot the five kinds of words that add nothing, to turn hidden verbs back into verbs, to make sentences vary and paragraphs cohere, to choose between a plain style and an ornate one on purpose, and to edit another person's paragraph without insulting them. At the end, you will build a short checklist you will actually use, because a checklist you use beats a rule you admire.

Cutting What Does Not Work

49.1

Redundancy and Filler

Main ideaRedundancy says a thing twice and filler says nothing at all; both can be cut without losing a single idea.

Written for this lesson: ’At this point in time, it is my personal opinion that the committee should make a decision about the proposal in a timely manner, due to the fact that the deadline is approaching quickly.’ That is thirty-four words. Here it is with everything that carries no meaning removed: ’The committee should decide on the proposal soon, because the deadline is near.’ Thirteen words. Read both aloud. Nothing is missing from the short one. The long one is not wrong, but it makes the reader wade.

The cuts fall into two families. is saying the same thing twice. ’Personal opinion’ is redundant, because every opinion is personal. So are ’true fact,’ ’past history,’ ’end result,’ ’free gift,’ ’advance planning’ and ’each and every.’ ’Approaching quickly’ hides a redundancy too, since ’near’ says it in one word. is a phrase that occupies space without meaning: ’at this point in time’ means now; ’due to the fact that’ means because; ’in a timely manner’ means soon; ’it is my opinion that’ means nothing, because the reader already knows whose opinion it is. Filler is what people write when they are warming up, and the warm-up can go.

There is a third, sneakier family: the sentence that announces what the next sentence will say. ’There are several reasons for this. The first reason is cost.’ The first sentence can go; the second makes it obvious. So can the sentence at the end of a paragraph that repeats the topic sentence in new words. When you edit, read each sentence and ask what it adds that the reader does not already have. If the answer is nothing, cut it, then read the paragraph again to be sure nothing broke.

49.2

Hidden Verbs and Weak Verbs

Main ideaA nominalization is a verb turned into a noun; turning it back into a verb makes the sentence shorter, clearer and more alive.

’The implementation of the plan by the staff resulted in an improvement in sales.’ Fourteen words, and something in it feels like fog. Here is the same fact: ’The staff implemented the plan, and sales improved.’ Eight words, and the fog is gone. What happened? In the first version the real actions, implement and improve, were frozen into nouns, implementation and improvement. A verb made into a noun is a , and it is the single most common reason that official, academic and business writing is hard to read.

You can spot nominalizations by their endings: -tion, -ment, -ance, -ence, -ity, -ness. Decision, assessment, performance, occurrence, ability, effectiveness. Not every such noun is a problem; ’the Constitution’ is a noun and should stay one. The problem is when the nominalization is hiding the sentence’s action. ’There was a discussion among the board members regarding the possibility of an expansion of the program.’ Nineteen words, three hidden verbs. ’The board discussed expanding the program.’ Six words. Notice what else vanished: the weak verb ’was,’ the empty opener ’there was,’ and the filler ’regarding the possibility of.’ Freeing the verb pulls the other junk out with it.

Weak verbs are the nominalization’s partners. ’Is,’ ’are,’ ’was,’ ’has,’ ’makes,’ ’does’ and ’gets’ are fine when nothing better exists, but they often stand in for a stronger verb that is hiding nearby. ’She is a runner’ hides ’she runs.’ ’He made a decision’ hides ’he decided.’ ’The report gives an explanation of’ hides ’the report explains.’ When you edit, find each sentence’s real action, ask who does it, and let that person or thing be the subject and the action be the verb. The sentence will shrink and the reader will see someone doing something, which is what sentences are for.

49.3

Clarity and Precision

Main ideaConcise writing is not only short; it is exact, choosing the one word that means what you mean instead of three that circle it.

Cutting words is half of concision. The other half is choosing the right ones. Written for this lesson: ’The weather was really bad, so the game was affected in a negative way.’ Short enough, but vague. Bad how? Affected how? ’Sleet canceled the game.’ Four words, and now the reader can see it. The vague version circled the meaning; the precise version landed on it. means picking the word that names exactly the thing, and it usually saves words, because one exact word replaces a cloud of approximate ones.

Vagueness has favorite hiding places. Intensifiers: ’very,’ ’really,’ ’extremely,’ ’so,’ ’incredibly.’ They promise force and deliver none; ’very tired’ is weaker than ’exhausted.’ General nouns: ’thing,’ ’stuff,’ ’aspect,’ ’factor,’ ’area,’ ’situation.’ Ask what the thing is and write that. Hedges: ’sort of,’ ’kind of,’ ’somewhat,’ ’basically,’ ’in some ways.’ A hedge is right when you truly are unsure and wrong when you are just being timid. And the all-purpose adjectives ’good,’ ’bad,’ ’interesting’ and ’important,’ which tell the reader you had a reaction but not what it was.

Precision also means saying who did what. ’Mistakes were made’ is a famous kind of sentence because it names no one. ’The mayor’s office lost the file’ names someone. Sometimes the actor is truly unknown or unimportant and the passive is right: ’The bridge was built in 1920.’ But when you catch yourself writing ’it was decided’ or ’errors occurred,’ ask who, and write it. Clarity is a kind of honesty. The reader should never have to guess what you saw, who did it, or how much you mean it.

Rhythm and Flow

49.4

Sentence Variety

Main ideaGood prose varies sentence length and opening so that the reader hears a rhythm instead of a metronome.

Read this, written for the lesson: ’The shop opens at eight. The owner arrives at seven. He unlocks the back door. He turns on the lights. He checks the register. He makes the coffee.’ Every sentence is four to six words. Every sentence begins with its subject. Every sentence has the same shape. By the fourth one, the reader has stopped hearing the words and started hearing the tick. This is what happens when concision goes too far: the sentences are clean and the paragraph is dead.

Now: ’The shop opens at eight, but the owner arrives at seven. Back door unlocked, lights on, he checks the register and makes the coffee. Then he waits.’ Same facts, three sentences instead of six, lengths of eleven, thirteen and three. One opens with the subject, one with a pair of participial phrases, one with ’then.’ The paragraph now has a , a pattern of long and short the ear can feel, and it ends on a short sentence that leaves the owner standing there. Variety is not decoration. It is how a paragraph keeps a reader awake.

There are three simple levers. Vary length: after a long sentence, try a short one. Vary the opening: begin some sentences with a phrase, a clause, an adverb or a participle, not always the subject. Vary the type: mix simple, compound and complex sentences, and use an occasional question or fragment where the register allows. Then read the paragraph aloud. Your ear catches sameness that your eye misses. If three sentences in a row begin the same way or run the same length, change one, unless you are repeating on purpose for emphasis, which is a different thing and sounds different.

Paragraph Coherence and Transitions

Main ideaA paragraph coheres when each sentence begins with something the reader already knows and ends with something new, and transitions mark how ideas connect.

Sentences can each be perfect and still not form a paragraph. Written for this lesson: ’The Chicago River once flowed into Lake Michigan. Drinking water came from the lake. Engineers reversed the river in 1900. Sewage had been reaching the water intake. The city built a canal to do it.’ Every fact is true and clear, but the reader has to keep jumping. Rearranged: ’The Chicago River once flowed into Lake Michigan, and the city drew its drinking water from that same lake. By the 1890s, sewage carried by the river was reaching the water intake. So in 1900, engineers reversed the river’s flow, building a canal that sent it away from the lake instead.’ Now each sentence picks up where the last one left off.

That is , and it comes from a simple principle: begin a sentence with old information, something the previous sentence gave the reader, and end it with new information. The lake appears at the end of sentence one and is picked up in sentence two. Sewage reaching the intake ends sentence two and explains the ’so’ that opens sentence three. Readers move through a paragraph like someone crossing a stream on stones. Each sentence’s opening should be a stone they are already standing on.

are the words that name the relationship between stones: ’so’ and ’therefore’ for cause, ’but’ and ’however’ for contrast, ’first’ and ’then’ for sequence, ’for example’ for illustration, ’in short’ for summary. Use them when the relationship is not obvious, and skip them when it is, because a paragraph with a transition on every sentence sounds like it is holding the reader’s hand too tightly. The best transition is often just the old-to-new link itself: repeat a key word, or use a pronoun that clearly points back. Test a paragraph by reading only the first few words of each sentence. If those words alone tell a rough story, the paragraph coheres.

Plain and Ornate

49.6

Two Styles, Both Legitimate

Main ideaThe plain style aims to be clear and unnoticed; the ornate style aims to be felt and admired; a skilled writer can do both and chooses on purpose.

This chapter has been arguing for cutting, and it means it. But it would be dishonest to pretend that short and plain is the only good style. Consider the opening of Poe’s ’The Fall of the House of Usher’: ’During the whole of a dull, dark, and soundless day in the autumn of the year, when the clouds hung oppressively low in the heavens, I had been passing alone, on horseback, through a singularly dreary tract of country.’ A concision editor would slash it: ’One gloomy autumn day I rode alone through dreary country.’ And the editor would be wrong, because the heaviness of the sentence is the point. The reader is meant to feel the low clouds pressing.

The uses common words, short to medium sentences, few modifiers and almost no figures of speech. It wants to be a clean window: you look through it at the subject and do not notice the glass. Instructions, news, science, law and most business writing want this, and so does much of the best fiction. The uses rich vocabulary, long and layered sentences, sound effects, repetition and imagery. It wants to be stained glass: the reader notices it and is moved by it. Sermons, some speeches, much poetry and certain fiction want this. Each style, done well, is hard. Each, done badly, has a name: plain done badly is flat, and ornate done badly is purple.

The mistake is not choosing one. The mistake is drifting into one without deciding. A student who writes an ornate lab report has not made a style choice; the register decided for them and they did not notice. A student who writes a flat, plain eulogy has missed a chance the occasion offered. Ask what the reader needs from this piece: information delivered fast, or an experience to feel? Then choose. And know that even the most ornate writers cut. Poe’s sentence has no filler in it. Every heavy word is pulling weight. Ornate is not the same as bloated.

49.7

Writing for Busy Readers

Main ideaMost real-world readers are in a hurry, so writing for them means front-loading the point, breaking the page into pieces, and cutting anything they would skip.

The person reading your email, your application, your report or your complaint to the landlord is not sitting by a fire with tea. They are on a phone, between two other tasks, with forty other things to read. Writing for this reader is a skill of its own, and it is the one you will use most in adult life. Its first rule is the reverse of a mystery novel: put the point first. Written for this lesson: ’I am writing to request an extension on the essay due Friday, because I was hospitalized Tuesday; I can submit it by Monday.’ The reader knows everything they need in one sentence and can decide.

The second rule is to design the page, not just the sentences. Short paragraphs, one idea each. A blank line between them. When there are several items, a list, so the eye can count them. A subject line or heading that states the point, not the topic: ’Request: essay extension to Monday’ beats ’Essay.’ Bold or a heading for the one thing the reader must not miss, used once, because if everything is bold, nothing is. Busy readers , and a well-designed page lets them skim and still get it right.

The third rule is respect for the reader’s time, which is what this whole chapter has been about. Cut the throat-clearing opening (’I hope this email finds you well’). Cut the backstory the reader does not need. Cut the apology for writing. Say what you want, why, and by when. Then stop. A busy reader who gets a short, clear message trusts the writer; one who has to dig for the point resents them a little, and remembers. In college and at work, the writer who is easy to read is the writer who gets answered.

Editing in Practice

49.8

Editing Someone Else's Paragraph

Main ideaEditing another writer's work means improving it without replacing their voice, and it starts with understanding what they were trying to do.

It is easier to see waste in someone else’s paragraph than in your own, which is why writers trade drafts. But editing another person’s work is a different job from editing your own. Their paragraph is not yours to rewrite into what you would have written. Your job is to help it become the best version of what they meant. Start by reading the whole thing once without a pencil and asking one question: what is this writer trying to do? If you cannot answer, that is your first note, and it is more useful than any comma.

Then edit in layers. First the big things: is the point clear, and is it where a reader will find it? Are the sentences in an order that coheres? Only then the sentence level: redundancy, filler, nominalizations, weak verbs, vague words, sameness of rhythm. Mark, do not rewrite. Cross out the filler and let them see what is left. Circle the nominalization and write ’verb?’ in the margin. For a sentence that could be cut, bracket it and write ’needed?’ The writer then makes the choice, keeps their voice, and learns the pattern, which is the point. A paragraph you rewrote teaches them nothing.

Finally, say what works, and be specific. ’The bike sentence at the end is the best line in here; everything before it should be trying to get there faster.’ Praise that names a real strength is not politeness; it tells the writer what to protect while they cut. When you receive edits, do the same in reverse. Do not defend the draft. Read each note, ask whether it serves what you meant, accept what does, and keep what the editor missed. The best editors and the best writers agree on one thing: the paragraph is not the ego. It is the paragraph.

A classmate's paragraph: 'In my personal opinion, there are a lot of reasons why the school should make a change to the start time. The first reason is that studies have shown that teenagers have a need for more sleep. It is a true fact that a later start would be beneficial to the health of students in many different ways.'

Mark, do not rewrite: list every redundancy, filler phrase and nominalization you would circle, and write one margin note that names what the writer is trying to do.

Why: Editing serves the writer's purpose, so you cannot begin until you know what the purpose is.

Why: A rewritten paragraph teaches nothing; a marked one lets the writer make the choice and see the pattern.

A Style Checklist You Will Use

Main ideaA short checklist run on every draft catches most style problems, and a checklist works only if it is short enough to actually run.

Every writer has read lists of a hundred rules and used none of them, because a hundred rules is a book, not a tool. A you will actually use fits on an index card and takes ten minutes per page. Here is one built from this unit, in the order that catches the most with the least work. One: is the point in the first sentence of the paragraph, or is it hiding at the end? Two: read only the first few words of each sentence; does the string cohere? Three: circle every word ending in -tion, -ment, -ance, -ity; can it become a verb? Four: cross out every ’very,’ ’really,’ ’thing,’ ’kind of’ and ’the fact that’; does the sentence survive?

Five: find every sentence over thirty words and every run of three sentences under eight; break or combine. Six: read the last word of each sentence; is the weight where you want it? Seven: read one paragraph aloud; does it sound like a person or a machine? That is seven checks. Written on a card, it is shorter than this paragraph. Run it on a real draft and you will find the same three or four problems every time, because every writer has habits. After a month, cross off the ones you have stopped making and add the one your editor keeps circling.

The checklist has one rule of its own: run it after the draft, never during. Cutting while you write freezes the writing, because the editor in your head is louder than the writer. Write the bad, long, filler-heavy draft freely. Then put on the editor’s hat, take out the card, and cut. Devon’s three hundred words were not a failure. They were the draft that had to exist before the hundred and twenty could. The pencil comes second. But it does come.

From the source
Strunk's Three Words
Omit needless words.
William Strunk Jr., The Elements of Style, 1918 (a section heading)

Strunk's rule is three words long and practices what it preaches. Which items on the seven-point checklist are really this one rule in different clothes, and which are about something else?

Why: A hundred rules is a book, not a tool; a card you actually run beats a rule you admire.

Why: Those endings mark verbs frozen into nouns, which the check asks you to free.

3. When should you run the checklist?

Why: Editing during drafting freezes the writing; the bad long draft has to exist before the good short one can.

1. 'The guy took off' and 'The gentleman departed' differ mainly in what?

Why: Both have the same structure and length; they differ in how formal or casual the words are.

Why: All share a denotation, but 'stingy' carries the picture of a closed fist.

3. What is the most likely problem with a college essay that reads like a text message?

Why: The language does not fit the audience and occasion, which is what register is about.

4. A writer follows a thirty-word sentence with a three-word sentence. What is the likely purpose?

Why: Contrast in length gives the short sentence the force of a slammed door.

5. 'Never have I seen such a mess' uses which technique?

Why: The negative leads and the verb precedes the subject, which is inversion for emphasis.

6. In which sentence is the main clause delayed until the end?

Why: The reader waits through the details before the point arrives; that is a periodic sentence.

7. Where does the lesson on emphasis say to bury routine details like dates?

Why: The end carries the most weight and the beginning the next; the middle is where things are forgotten.

8. Which sentence of analysis is precise rather than generic?

Why: It names a specific choice, measures it and ties it to an effect; the others could describe any passage.

9. What does imitation of a model sentence keep and what does it change?

Why: Imitation borrows the shape of a sentence and fills it with your own content.

10. 'The implementation of the plan resulted in an improvement in sales' can be improved by doing what?

Why: 'The staff implemented the plan, and sales improved' frees the hidden verbs and cuts the fog.

11. Which phrase is filler that should be cut?

Why: 'Due to the fact that' means 'because'; the others carry content.

12. A paragraph in which every sentence begins with old information and ends with new information is said to have what?

Why: Old-to-new linking is what lets a reader cross a paragraph without jumping.

13. Why would cutting Poe's long opening sentence in 'Usher' be a mistake?

Why: Ornate is not the same as bloated; every heavy word in the sentence is pulling weight.

14. What should the first sentence of an email to a busy reader do?

Why: Front-loading lets the reader know what is needed at once.

15. When editing a classmate's paragraph, what comes first?

Why: Editing serves the writer's purpose, so the purpose must be known before anything is marked.
