Twentieth-Century Crises

1914–1945

Between 1914 and 1945, Americans lived through two world wars, a wild decade of boom and change, and the worst economic crisis in the nation's history. A child born in 1910 could have seen soldiers sail to France, watched cars and radios fill American homes, stood in a Depression bread line and then worked in a war factory or fought overseas, all before turning 35.

These crises changed what Americans expected from their government. After World War I, many wanted the nation to stay out of the world's troubles. The Great Depression led millions to believe that the national government should help people in hard times. World War II pulled the United States into the center of world affairs, where it has remained ever since.

In every crisis, Americans argued about freedom and fairness. Who could speak freely in wartime? Who could immigrate? Who got help and who was left out? African Americans, women, immigrants, Mexican Americans, Native Americans and Japanese Americans all fought for their place in the nation, sometimes while serving it in war.

The big question

How did war, boom and depression change what Americans expected from their government and their nation's role in the world?

The Great War

1914–1920
Big questionWhy did the United States join a war across the ocean, and how did that war change life at home?
The story

Old Tactics Meet New Weapons

In 1914, soldiers marched off expecting a short war. They found a long line of ditches instead.

In August 1914, crowds in Paris, Berlin and London cheered as soldiers marched off to war. Many young men believed the fighting would be over in a few months. Their generals planned the war the old way. Soldiers would charge forward in great waves. Horse soldiers would ride through the gaps. Brave men would win quick victories.

That is not what happened. The armies had new weapons that changed everything. A single machine gun could fire hundreds of bullets a minute. Big guns called artillery could fire shells for miles. When soldiers charged across open ground, most never got close to the enemy. Within months, both sides stopped running and started digging.

By the end of 1914, two lines of trenches stretched across Belgium and France, from the North Sea to the border of Switzerland. This was called the Western Front. The trenches were deep ditches, lined with sandbags and wooden boards. Soldiers ate, slept and stood guard in them. Rain turned the bottoms to mud. Rats and lice were everywhere. Between the two lines lay a strip of torn-up land called no man's land.

Generals kept trying to break through. They ordered huge attacks, sometimes after days of shelling. Again and again, the attackers were cut down. In 1916, the Battle of the Somme lasted about five months. More than a million soldiers were killed or wounded. The front line moved only a few miles. Inventors raced to find an answer: poison gas, tanks, airplanes. Each new weapon made the war deadlier, but none ended it quickly.

Some moments showed that the soldiers were still human beings. On Christmas 1914, in a few places along the line, British and German soldiers climbed out of their trenches. They shook hands, traded small gifts and sang songs. The next day, the war went on. It would last almost four more years, and in 1917 it would pull in the United States.

A War Begins in Europe

21.1

Four Roots of War

Main ideaMilitarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism turned Europe into a place where one spark could start a huge war.

By 1914, the great powers of Europe did not trust one another. Four forces built up tension over many years. Historians often remember them as four roots of the war. Each one alone might not have caused a world war. Together, they made one far more likely.

The first was , the belief that a nation needs a large, strong military. Germany and Britain raced to build the biggest navy. Countries drew up detailed plans for war and kept millions of soldiers ready. The second was . Nations made deals to defend each other. France, Russia and Britain lined up on one side. Germany and Austria-Hungary lined up on the other.

The third root was , when a strong nation takes control of other lands. European countries competed for colonies in Africa and Asia. Each new colony caused jealousy and quarrels. The fourth root was , a strong pride in one’s own nation. Pride made leaders unwilling to back down. It also made some groups, like Serbs living inside Austria-Hungary, want their own country.

Together, these forces made Europe like a room full of gunpowder. Alliances meant that a fight between two countries could quickly pull in many more. Only a spark was needed. In the summer of 1914, that spark came in a city in southeastern Europe.

21.2

The Spark at Sarajevo

Main ideaThe killing of Austria-Hungary's Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914 set off a chain of war declarations across Europe.

On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand rode through the city of Sarajevo in an open car. He was next in line to rule Austria-Hungary. A young Serbian nationalist named Gavrilo Princip shot and killed the archduke and his wife, Sophie. Princip wanted lands with Serbian people to break free from Austria-Hungary.

Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and declared war on it. Then the alliances took over. Russia, a friend of Serbia, got its army ready. Germany, Austria-Hungary’s ally, declared war on Russia and then on France. When German troops marched through neutral Belgium to reach France, Britain declared war on Germany. All of this happened in about one week in early August.

Two sides formed. The included France, Britain and Russia. Italy joined them in 1915. The included Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria. Colonies around the world were pulled in too. People at the time called it the Great War. Later it became known as World War I.

21.3

A New Kind of War

Main ideaMachine guns, artillery, poison gas and other new weapons led to trench warfare and terrible losses with little gain.

On the Western Front, the war became a . That means neither side could win. Soldiers lived in for months at a time. Attacks across no man’s land usually failed, because defenders with machine guns could stop them.

Both sides tried new weapons to break the deadlock. In 1915, Germany used poison gas, and soon both sides did. Soldiers had to carry gas masks. Britain rolled out the first tanks in 1916. They were slow and often broke down, but they could crush barbed wire and cross trenches.

The war also moved into the sky and under the sea. At first, airplanes scouted enemy lines. Soon pilots fought each other in the air. At sea, German submarines, called U-boats, sank ships carrying supplies to Britain. Submarines would later help bring the United States into the war.

The cost was enormous. By the end, about 9 million soldiers had died, along with millions of civilians. A whole generation of young men in France, Britain and Germany was scarred. People asked how their leaders could let so many die for so little ground.

America Enters the War

21.4

Neutral in Name

Main ideaPresident Wilson wanted the United States to stay neutral, but trade, loans and old ties pulled Americans toward the Allies.

When the war began, President Woodrow Wilson said the United States would stay . Most Americans agreed. The war seemed far away. Many people did not want to send their sons to fight in Europe’s quarrels.

Yet Americans had strong feelings. About one in three Americans had been born abroad or had a parent who was. Many German Americans and Irish Americans were against Britain. Many other Americans felt close to Britain and France through language, history and democracy.

Money mattered too. American farms and factories sold huge amounts of food, steel and weapons to the Allies. Banks loaned the Allies billions of dollars. Britain’s navy blocked most trade with Germany, so the Central Powers got much less. Over time, America’s business was tied to an Allied victory.

British also shaped opinion. Britain cut Germany’s undersea telegraph cables, so most war news reached America through British sources. Stories of German cruelty in Belgium, some true and some exaggerated, turned many Americans against Germany.

21.5

Submarines and a Secret Telegram

Main ideaGerman submarine attacks and the Zimmermann Telegram turned many Americans against Germany.

Germany used U-boats to stop supplies from reaching Britain. On May 7, 1915, a U-boat sank the British passenger ship off the coast of Ireland. Nearly 1,200 people died, including 128 Americans. Americans were furious. Germany said the ship carried weapons, which was partly true, but most of the dead were ordinary travelers.

Wilson protested strongly. For a while, Germany promised to stop sinking passenger and merchant ships without warning. Wilson won reelection in 1916. Many voters liked that he had kept the country out of war.

Then, in early 1917, Germany took a gamble. It announced . U-boats would sink any ship near Britain, even American ships. German leaders hoped to starve Britain before America could make a difference.

At the same time, British spies decoded a secret message from Germany’s foreign minister, Arthur Zimmermann. He asked Mexico to join Germany if the United States entered the war. In return, Mexico could win back land it had lost to the United States. The message was printed in American newspapers in March 1917. Anger swept the country.

21.6

Safe for Democracy

Main ideaIn April 1917, Wilson asked Congress for war, saying the fight would help make the world safe for democracy.

In March 1917, U-boats sank several American ships. The same month, a revolution in Russia removed the czar, its all-powerful ruler. Now all the major Allies had some kind of elected government. That made it easier for Wilson to call the war a fight for .

On April 2, 1917, Wilson spoke to Congress. He asked lawmakers to declare war on Germany. He said America had no selfish goals. It wanted no land and no payment. It would fight for the rights of people everywhere.

On April 6, 1917, Congress voted for war. Most members agreed, but some voted no. Jeannette Rankin of Montana, the first woman elected to Congress, was one of them. She said she wanted to stand by her country but could not vote for war.

The United States had a small army. It would take many months to build a large one and ship it across the Atlantic. The Allies were exhausted. They needed American soldiers, food and money as fast as possible.

Americans in the Fight

21.7

Raising an Army

Main ideaThrough a draft, the United States built an army of millions and sent about 2 million soldiers to France.

To build an army fast, Congress passed the Selective Service Act in May 1917. This created a , a system that requires people to serve in the military. Millions of men signed up on registration day. Numbers were drawn to decide who would go.

The soldiers were called the American Expeditionary Force, or . General John J. Pershing led them. People nicknamed American soldiers “doughboys.” Soldiers came from farms, cities and immigrant neighborhoods. Some did not yet speak English well.

The army was , meaning Black and white soldiers served in separate units. More than 350,000 African Americans served. Many were given only labor jobs. But some Black units fought bravely. The 369th Infantry from New York, called the Harlem Hellfighters, fought alongside French troops. The 370th Infantry, first formed as the 8th Illinois National Guard, came from Chicago and had Black officers.

Native Americans served too, even though many were not yet U.S. citizens. Women served as nurses and as telephone operators in France. Their service helped change ideas about who belonged in the nation.

21.8

Over There

Main ideaFresh American troops helped the Allies stop Germany's last big attack and push forward until the armistice of November 11, 1918.

American troops arrived in large numbers in 1918, just in time. Russia had left the war after a second revolution. That let Germany move soldiers to the Western Front. In spring 1918, Germany launched huge attacks and pushed toward Paris.

American soldiers and Marines helped stop the Germans at Château-Thierry and Belleau Wood in June 1918. Then the Allies struck back. In the fall, more than a million Americans fought in the Meuse-Argonne Offensive in France. It was the largest battle in American history up to that time.

Germany was running out of soldiers, food and hope. Its people were hungry and angry. On November 11, 1918, at 11 a.m., an took effect. That is an agreement to stop fighting. Church bells rang across Europe and America.

About 116,000 Americans died in the war. More than half died from disease, not battle. Their sacrifice was smaller than that of France or Britain, but the fresh American troops had tipped the balance.

The Home Front and the Peace

21.9

Selling the War

Main ideaThe government raised money, shaped opinion and limited free speech to win support for the war.

War is expensive. The government sold , loans from ordinary people that would be paid back with interest. Movie stars held rallies. Children bought savings stamps. The government also raised taxes on high incomes.

To win hearts, Wilson created the Committee on Public Information, led by George Creel. It made posters, films and pamphlets. Thousands of volunteer speakers gave short talks in movie theaters. Some of this also spread fear and hatred of Germans.

German Americans faced suspicion and cruelty. Some schools stopped teaching German. Sauerkraut was renamed “liberty cabbage.” In Collinsville, Illinois, in 1918, a mob killed a German-born man named Robert Prager, who was falsely accused of disloyalty.

Congress passed the Espionage Act in 1917 and the Sedition Act in 1918. These laws made it a crime to interfere with the draft or, later, to criticize the government or the war in harsh ways. Labor leader Eugene V. Debs was sent to prison for a speech against the war. In 1920 he ran for president from his prison cell and won nearly a million votes.

21.10

New Jobs, New Journeys

Main ideaThe war opened factory jobs to women and to African Americans, starting the Great Migration from the South to Northern cities like Chicago.

With millions of men in uniform and immigration from Europe cut off, factories needed workers. Women took jobs in factories, offices and railroads. Many had never been paid for work outside the home before. Their war work helped win support for the , which gave women the right to vote in 1920.

African Americans in the South faced Jim Crow laws, low pay and violence. Now Northern factories were hiring. Hundreds of thousands of Black Southerners moved north. This movement became known as the . It continued for decades.

Chicago was a top destination. The Chicago Defender, a Black newspaper, urged Southerners to come north. Pullman porters on the trains carried copies south. The Illinois Central Railroad ran straight from Mississippi and Louisiana to Chicago. New arrivals found work in the stockyards, steel mills and rail yards.

The North was not free of prejudice. Black families faced unfair housing rules and hostility. In July 1919, a Black teenager named Eugene Williams drowned after being attacked at a Chicago beach. Days of rioting followed, and 38 people died. The violence showed how deep the racial divide ran.

21.11

The Deadly Flu of 1918

Main ideaA worldwide flu pandemic in 1918 and 1919 killed far more Americans than the war did.

In 1918, a new kind of , or flu, spread around the world. It moved fast in crowded army camps and on troop ships. Soldiers carried it from place to place. Unlike most flus, it was especially deadly for healthy young adults.

A is a disease that spreads across many countries. The 1918 flu reached almost every part of the world. It killed at least 50 million people worldwide. In the United States, about 675,000 people died.

Cities tried to slow it down. Many closed schools, theaters and churches. People were told to wear masks and avoid crowds. Nurses and doctors worked until they dropped. There were no vaccines or medicines that could cure it.

Because of the war, many governments kept news of the flu quiet at first. They did not want to hurt morale. Scientists today still study the 1918 pandemic to prepare for new diseases.

21.12

Winning the War, Losing the Peace

Main ideaWilson hoped for a fair peace and a League of Nations, but the Treaty of Versailles punished Germany and the U.S. Senate rejected the League.

In January 1918, Wilson listed his goals for peace, called the Fourteen Points. He wanted open agreements, free trade, fewer weapons and the right of peoples to choose their own governments. His last point called for a , a group where countries could settle disputes without war.

In 1919, leaders met near Paris. Britain and France wanted Germany to pay. The , signed on June 28, 1919, forced Germany to accept blame for the war. It had to pay huge sums called , give up land and shrink its army. New nations such as Poland and Czechoslovakia appeared on the map. The treaty did create the League of Nations.

Back home, the Senate had to approve the treaty. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and others worried the League could drag America into future wars without a vote of Congress. Wilson refused to compromise. He toured the country to win support, but he suffered a stroke in 1919.

The Senate never approved the treaty. The United States never joined the League. Many Germans felt the treaty was unfair and humiliating. That anger would help bring about an even bigger war twenty years later.

The Roaring Twenties

1919–1929
Big questionWere the 1920s a time of new freedom and fun, a time of fear and division, or both at once?
The story

A Trumpet on the South Side

In 1922, a young horn player from New Orleans stepped off a train in Chicago and helped change American music.

In the summer of 1922, a 21-year-old musician named Louis Armstrong rode a train north from New Orleans to Chicago. He carried his horn and a telegram. It came from his hero, Joe “King” Oliver, a famous bandleader. Oliver wanted Armstrong to join his Creole Jazz Band. Armstrong had grown up poor in New Orleans, and he had never lived anywhere else.

The Chicago he found was booming. Hundreds of thousands of African Americans had come north in the Great Migration. Many settled on the city's South Side, in a neighborhood that later became known as Bronzeville. It had Black-owned stores, churches, banks, theaters and the Chicago Defender newspaper. Newcomers from Mississippi, Louisiana and Alabama brought their food, their faith and their music with them.

That music was jazz. It grew out of the blues, ragtime, church songs and brass bands of the South. Its players often made up melodies on the spot. This is called improvising. At the Lincoln Gardens dance hall, Oliver and Armstrong played two horns together so tightly that listeners were amazed. Young white musicians came to listen and learn. Dancers packed the floor late into the night.

In 1923, Oliver's band made records, among the first ever made by a Black jazz band. Then, from 1925 to 1928, Armstrong recorded in Chicago with his own groups, the Hot Five and Hot Seven. His bold solos and warm, rough singing voice changed how people played and sang. Records and radio carried jazz into homes across the country. People began calling the whole decade the Jazz Age.

Life on the South Side was not easy. Unfair housing rules kept most Black families from moving to other neighborhoods, so homes were crowded and rents were high. Still, the South Side was a place of pride and creativity. From its clubs, a new American sound spread around the world.

22.1

A Return to Normal

Main ideaTired of war and reform, voters chose Republican presidents who promised calm, low taxes and help for business.

By 1920, many Americans were worn out. They had lived through a world war, a deadly flu and bitter strikes. Warren G. Harding, a Republican senator from Ohio, promised a return to “normalcy.” He won the 1920 election easily.

Harding picked some honest, skilled advisers. But he also gave jobs to friends who used their power to get rich. In the worst , called Teapot Dome, Interior Secretary Albert Fall took bribes. In return, he let private companies drill on government oil land. Fall became the first cabinet member sent to prison. Harding died in 1923 before most of the scandal came out.

Vice President Calvin Coolidge became president. People called him “Silent Cal” because he spoke so little. Coolidge believed government should stay small and let business grow. Taxes were cut, especially for the wealthy. Coolidge won the 1924 election. In 1928, voters chose another Republican, Herbert Hoover, for president.

Supporters said these policies brought jobs and prosperity. Critics said they helped the rich far more than farmers and workers. Both views would matter when the good times ended. People still argue about how much these choices helped cause the troubles of the 1930s.

Closing the Door

Main ideaNew laws in the 1920s set quotas that sharply limited immigration, especially from southern and eastern Europe and Asia.

Before the war, about a million immigrants a year came to the United States. Many came from Italy, Poland, Russia and Greece. Some Americans feared these newcomers. They said immigrants took jobs or brought dangerous ideas. Prejudice against Catholics and Jews was also strong.

Congress acted. A 1921 law created , or limits, on how many people could come from each country. The Immigration Act of 1924 made the limits much tighter. Each nation’s quota was based on how many people from that country lived in the U.S. in 1890. That was before most southern and eastern Europeans had arrived, so their quotas were tiny.

The 1924 law also blocked almost all immigration from Asia. Japan was deeply offended, because it saw the law as an insult to its people. Chinese immigrants had already been shut out since the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882.

The quotas did not apply to countries in the Americas. Many Mexican workers came north to work on farms and railroads. In 1924, Congress also created the U.S. Border Patrol. The quota system stayed in place until 1965.

Only What You Could Carry

In 1942, the U.S. government forced about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from their homes, though most were American citizens.

On December 7, 1941, Japan attacked the U.S. Navy at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii. At that time, about 127,000 people of Japanese ancestry lived on the U.S. mainland, most of them on the West Coast. They were American citizens.

After Pearl Harbor, fear spread fast. Rumors said Japanese Americans might help Japan invade. There was no evidence of this, but old prejudice made the rumors easy to believe. On February 19, 1942, President Franklin Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. It let the military remove “any or all persons” from areas it chose. The order did not name any group, but it was used against Japanese Americans.

Army notices went up on telephone poles. Families had only days to get ready. They could bring only what they could carry. Many had to sell homes, farms, stores and fishing boats for a small part of what they were worth. Some lost everything. Families were given numbers and put on buses and trains, not knowing where they were going.

First they were held in temporary centers. Some were racetracks, where families slept in horse stalls. Then they were sent to ten camps in lonely places, such as Manzanar in the California desert and Heart Mountain in Wyoming. The camps had barbed wire fences and guard towers. Families lived in single rooms in long wooden barracks. Dust blew through the cracks. About two-thirds of the people held there were U.S. citizens.

People tried to make normal lives. They started schools, newspapers, gardens and baseball teams. Many older people used a Japanese saying, shikata ga nai: it cannot be helped. Thousands of young Nisei men joined the U.S. Army, even while their families stayed behind barbed wire. Others refused to be drafted until their families' rights were restored. When the camps closed in 1945 and 1946, many families had no homes to go back to. Thousands started over in cities like Chicago.

Fighting for Freedom Twice

Main ideaAfrican Americans, Native Americans and Mexican Americans served bravely while still facing discrimination at home.

More than a million African Americans served in World War II, in a military that was still segregated. A Black newspaper called for a campaign: victory over enemies abroad and victory over racism at home. In 1941, labor leader A. Philip Randolph planned a march on Washington to protest job discrimination. Roosevelt then signed an order banning discrimination in defense industries.

The were the first Black military pilots in U.S. history. They trained at Tuskegee, Alabama. Flying over Europe, they protected American bombers and earned a strong record.

Native Americans served in large numbers. About 400 Navajo Marines became . They used their own language to create a code for sending messages in the Pacific. The enemy never broke it. Code talkers from other nations, such as the Comanche, also served.

Hundreds of thousands of Mexican Americans served, and many earned high honors. At home, the Bracero Program brought Mexican workers to farms and railroads. But in 1943, in Los Angeles, groups of servicemen attacked young Mexican Americans in the Zoot Suit Riots. Chinese Americans also served, and in 1943 Congress repealed the Chinese Exclusion Act.

Executive Order 9066

Main ideaThe forced removal of Japanese Americans was later judged a grave injustice, and in 1988 the U.S. government formally apologized.

Under Executive Order 9066, about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were forced into camps. This is called , being locked up. No Japanese American was ever convicted of spying for Japan during the war. In Hawaii, where people of Japanese ancestry made up about a third of the population, there was no mass removal. German and Italian Americans were not removed as whole groups either.

Some people fought back in court. Fred Korematsu, a young welder in California, refused to leave his home. He was arrested. In 1944, in Korematsu v. United States, the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 that the removal was allowed because of wartime needs. Three justices disagreed, and one called it racism. The same day, in another case, the Court ruled that the government could not keep loyal citizens locked up. The camps began to close.

Thousands of Nisei men served in the U.S. Army. The 442nd Regimental Combat Team, made up mostly of Japanese Americans, fought in Italy and France. It became one of the most decorated units in American history.

Years later, Japanese Americans pushed for , making things right. In 1983, Korematsu’s conviction was overturned. In 1988, President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act. It apologized and paid $20,000 to each surviving person who had been held.

24.7

Turning the Tide

Main ideaThe Allies focused first on defeating Germany, winning key victories in the Atlantic, the Soviet Union, North Africa and Italy.

The Allies agreed to defeat Germany first, since it was the strongest Axis power. In the Atlantic, German U-boats sank hundreds of ships carrying supplies. Over time, the Allies used radar, air patrols and warship escorts to win this battle.

In 1941, Hitler had invaded the Soviet Union. At first the Germans swept forward. But in the winter of 1942 to 1943, the Soviet army trapped and defeated a huge German army at Stalingrad. It was a major . From then on, Germany was mostly retreating in the east. The Soviet people suffered enormous losses in the war.

In November 1942, American and British troops landed in North Africa. Americans lost their first big battle there, but they learned fast. By May 1943, the Axis armies in North Africa had surrendered.

Next, the Allies invaded Sicily and then Italy in 1943. Italian leaders removed Mussolini from power. But German troops fought on in Italy’s mountains. The Allies freed Rome in June 1944. Meanwhile, Allied bombers struck German factories, railroads and cities.

D-Day and Victory

Main ideaOn D-Day, June 6, 1944, the Allies invaded France, and less than a year later, Germany surrendered.

For years, the Soviet Union had asked Britain and the U.S. to open a second front in Western Europe. The Allies planned a huge invasion of Normandy, on the coast of France. General Dwight D. Eisenhower led it.

On June 6, 1944, called , about 156,000 Allied soldiers crossed the English Channel by ship and plane. Americans landed at beaches code-named Utah and Omaha. At Omaha Beach, the fighting was fierce, and many soldiers died. By nightfall, the Allies held the beaches. Soon they poured more troops into France.

In August 1944, Paris was freed. In December, Germany launched a surprise attack in Belgium. This was the Battle of the Bulge, the largest battle Americans fought in the war. The Allies held on and pushed into Germany from the west while Soviet troops pushed in from the east.

On April 12, 1945, President Roosevelt died. Vice President Harry S. Truman became president. On April 30, Hitler killed himself. Germany surrendered on May 8, 1945, called , for Victory in Europe. Crowds celebrated in cities across America.

The Holocaust

Main ideaNazi Germany and its collaborators murdered about six million Jews, along with millions of other people, in the Holocaust.

The was the planned, government-run persecution and murder of about six million Jewish men, women and children by Nazi Germany and its helpers, from 1933 to 1945. means cruel, unfair treatment of people because of who they are. The Nazis also persecuted and murdered millions of others, including Roma people, people with disabilities, Poles and Soviet prisoners of war. They imprisoned political opponents, gay men and Jehovah’s Witnesses.

It began with hateful words and unfair laws. Hitler spread , hatred of Jewish people. In 1935, new laws took away German Jews’ citizenship. On November 9 and 10, 1938, Nazis attacked Jewish homes, shops and synagogues across Germany and Austria. This is called Kristallnacht, the Night of Broken Glass. About 30,000 Jewish men were sent to prison camps.

During the war, the Nazis forced Jews into crowded, walled-off city areas called ghettos. Killing squads murdered Jews in Eastern Europe. Then the Nazis built death camps, such as Auschwitz-Birkenau and Treblinka, where people were killed on a huge scale. About one and a half million of the victims were children. Anne Frank, a Jewish girl who kept a diary while hiding in Amsterdam, died in a camp in 1945 at age 15. Jews resisted, as in the Warsaw Ghetto uprising of 1943. Some non-Jews risked their lives to hide them.

Before the war, the U.S. kept strict immigration quotas and let in relatively few Jewish refugees. In 1939, the ship St. Louis, carrying more than 900 Jewish refugees, was turned away by Cuba and not allowed into the U.S. In 1944, Roosevelt created the War Refugee Board, which helped save many thousands. In 1945, American soldiers freed prisoners at camps like Buchenwald and Dachau. Survivors’ stories, shared in places like the Illinois Holocaust Museum in Skokie, help the world remember.

24.10

Island by Island

Main ideaAfter early Japanese victories, the U.S. won at Midway and then fought island by island toward Japan.

After Pearl Harbor, Japan won victory after victory. It captured the Philippines, where U.S. and Filipino troops surrendered in April 1942. Japanese soldiers forced the prisoners on a brutal march, and thousands died. It became known as the Bataan Death March.

In June 1942, the U.S. Navy won a huge victory at the Battle of . American code breakers had learned Japan’s plans. U.S. planes sank four Japanese aircraft carriers. Japan’s navy never fully recovered. Midway was the turning point in the Pacific.

The Allies then used . Instead of attacking every Japanese-held island, they captured key ones and skipped others. Each captured island gave them airfields closer to Japan. The fighting was fierce. At Guadalcanal, Iwo Jima and Okinawa, Japanese soldiers fought almost to the last person.

Near the end of the war, some Japanese pilots crashed their planes on purpose into American ships. They were called kamikaze pilots. The high losses made American leaders fear that invading Japan itself would cost a great many lives.

The Atomic Bomb

Main ideaThe United States built atomic bombs in secret and dropped two on Japan in August 1945, and Japan surrendered soon after.

Scientists warned Roosevelt that Germany might build a powerful new weapon from splitting atoms. The U.S. started a secret program called the . On December 2, 1942, under the stands of a stadium at the University of Chicago, a team led by Enrico Fermi created the first controlled nuclear chain reaction. It proved an atomic bomb was possible.

In July 1945, scientists tested the first atomic bomb in the New Mexico desert. President Truman now had to decide whether to use it. Japan had refused to surrender. Military leaders expected an invasion of Japan to cost huge numbers of lives on both sides.

On August 6, 1945, a U.S. plane dropped an on the city of Hiroshima. On August 9, a second bomb destroyed much of Nagasaki. The Soviet Union also declared war on Japan. By the end of 1945, well over 100,000 people had died in the two cities, most of them civilians. Many survivors suffered from radiation sickness for years.

Japan announced its surrender on August 15, 1945. The formal surrender was signed on September 2. People still debate the decision. Supporters argue the bombs ended the war quickly and saved lives by avoiding an invasion. Critics argue Japan was close to defeat, and that killing so many civilians was wrong.

The Cost and the Peace

Main ideaWorld War II was the deadliest war in history, and it left the United States a world superpower that helped create the United Nations.

World War II was the deadliest war in human history. More than 60 million people died, most of them civilians. Cities across Europe and Asia lay in ruins. Millions of people had lost their homes. About 16 million Americans served in the military, and about 405,000 died.

After the war, the Allies put Nazi leaders on trial at Nuremberg, Germany, for and crimes against humanity. For the first time, leaders were held responsible before an international court for the murder of civilians. Japanese leaders were also tried.

Leaders wanted to stop another world war. In 1945, delegates from 50 nations met in San Francisco and signed a charter creating the . This time, unlike after World War I, the United States joined. The UN was set up to help settle disputes, protect human rights and help people in need.

The war changed the United States. It came out as a , a nation with enormous military and economic strength. So did the Soviet Union, and the two soon became rivals. At home, veterans returned to a booming economy. The service of women and people of color would fuel new demands for equal rights.
