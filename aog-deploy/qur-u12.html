Close Reading Meccan Surahs

Surahs Close Up

Until now this course has looked at the Qur'an from a distance: what the book is, how it is arranged, where it came from, what it teaches. This unit sits down with the text itself. You will read whole surahs, slowly, the way a commentator or a literature class reads: verse by verse, noticing the sound, the structure, the speaker, and what each line does. The surahs chosen are all ones that Muslim tradition dates to Mecca, before 622, when the community was small and the message was mostly about one God, judgment and care for the weak.

The first chapter reads five short surahs that Muslims hear every day in prayer: Al-Fatiha, the opening; Al-'Alaq, which tradition calls the first revelation; Ad-Duha and Al-'Asr; and Al-Ikhlas, four verses on the oneness of God. The second chapter reads two long narrative surahs, Yusuf and Maryam, and sets them beside Genesis and the Gospel of Luke. You will hear from classical commentators such as al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir, from Sufi poets, and from academic scholars, and you will compare public-domain translations.

By the end you will be able to read a surah as a unit of meaning, find where it turns, name the tools of Meccan style and Qur'anic storytelling, and explain how Muslim and academic readers ask different questions of the same text. You will practice saying "the Qur'an says" and "Muslims believe" and "al-Tabari reads this as," and you will write an argument from the text with the verses cited.

Big questionHow can a surah of three or seven lines carry a whole way of thinking, and how do its readers unpack it?
The story

Three Lines, One Lifetime

The shortest surahs are the first a Muslim child learns, and the ones a scholar never finishes reading.

A five-year-old in a Chicago apartment sits on the rug beside her grandmother. She is learning Surah Al-'Asr, the 103rd chapter of the Qur'an. It has three verses. Her grandmother says a line in Arabic, and the girl repeats it. By the end of the week she can say the whole surah from memory. She does not yet know what every word means. She knows it sounds like a promise, and that her grandmother's voice slows down on the last word.

Across the city, in a university library, a graduate student has the same surah open on his desk. Beside it are four commentaries. One is by al-Tabari, who died in 923. One is by Ibn Kathir, who died in 1373. One is a Persian Sufi reading. One was written in Cairo in the twentieth century. He has been reading these three verses for two months. Each commentator hears something the others did not.

Ibn Kathir reports that the early jurist al-Shafi'i said that if people reflected on this surah alone, it would be enough for them. Muslim tradition has treated the short Meccan surahs this way for fourteen centuries: as small doors into a very large house. They are also the surahs a Muslim hears most, because the daily prayers are built from them.

This chapter reads five of these surahs closely: Al-Fatiha, the opening; Al-'Alaq, which tradition calls the first revelation; Ad-Duha and Al-'Asr, one a consolation and one a warning; and Al-Ikhlas, four verses on the oneness of God. You will read them in Marmaduke Pickthall's 1930 translation, compare older translations, and listen for the sound of Meccan style. The girl on the rug and the student in the library are reading the same three lines. The question is what each of them hears.

Section 1

Al-Fatiha, the Opening

23.1

Seven Verses at the Door

Main ideaAl-Fatiha, the first surah, is a seven-verse prayer that Muslims read as the doorway to the whole Qur'an.

Open a printed Qur’an and the first page holds one short chapter. Its name is , "The Opening." It has seven verses, or . In Pickthall’s translation it begins, "Praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds." It ends with a request to be shown "the straight path." Every other surah in the book comes after it, including the very long second surah, Al-Baqarah.

The surah has a clear shape. The first three verses praise God: as Lord of the worlds, as merciful, as the owner of the Day of Judgment. The fourth verse turns to speaking directly: "Thee (alone) we worship; Thee (alone) we ask for help." The last verses ask for guidance and describe two paths, one favored and one gone astray. Commentators from al-Tabari onward point out that the speaker changes. God is described in the third person, then addressed in the second. Many readers see this as a move from praise to prayer.

Muslim tradition gives this surah other names. It is called Umm al-Kitab, "the Mother of the Book," and as-Sab’ al-Mathani, "the seven oft-repeated," a phrase from 15:87 (Surah Al-Hijr) that most classical commentators read as pointing to Al-Fatiha. Most scholars, Muslim and academic, count it among the Meccan surahs, the ones tradition dates before the migration to Medina in 622. In the tradition (commentary on the Qur’an), a long commentary on Al-Fatiha often stands at the front of the whole work, as if the reader must pass through this door first.

23.2

A Prayer Said Every Day

Main ideaMuslims recite Al-Fatiha in every unit of the daily prayer, so it is the most repeated text in Muslim life.

To understand why Al-Fatiha matters, watch a prayer rather than a page. The Muslim daily prayer, , is made of units called rak&#x27;ahs. In each unit the person stands, bows, prostrates and sits. While standing, they recite Al-Fatiha in Arabic, then usually another short passage. Sunni law schools and Shia jurists agree that Al-Fatiha is part of every unit, though they describe the details differently. Muslim tradition reports a saying of Muhammad that a prayer without the Opening is incomplete; that report is in the hadith collections of Bukhari and Muslim.

The five daily prayers contain seventeen required units in the common Sunni count. That means a Muslim who prays them all says Al-Fatiha at least seventeen times a day. Many also say it in extra prayers, at a graveside, when a contract is agreed, or to open a gathering. Because of this, Al-Fatiha is the Arabic text that Muslims worldwide know best, whether or not they speak Arabic. A child in Jakarta, a grandmother in Dakar and a student in Chicago say the same seven verses in the same language.

Scholars who study ritual note something about this. A text recited seventeen times a day is not read the way a novel is read. It becomes a frame for the day, like a doorway one walks through again and again. Sufi writers, who look for inner meanings, have described the prayer as a conversation: God is praised, and then God is asked. Some hadith reports describe God answering each verse in turn. Whether or not a reader shares that belief, the point for our study is plain: for Muslims, Al-Fatiha is prayer before it is text.

23.3

One Surah, Three Translators

Main ideaComparing Pickthall, Rodwell and Sale on Al-Fatiha shows how every translation is also an interpretation.

The Qur’an is in Arabic, and Muslims hold that only the Arabic is the Qur’an itself. Everything else is a rendering of its meaning. That is why Pickthall called his 1930 work The Meaning of the Glorious Koran, not The Koran. Reading two or three translations of the same verse side by side is one of the best tools a student has. The differences show you where the Arabic is rich, or hard, or contested.

Take the two names of God in verse 1 and verse 3: ar-Rahman and ar-Rahim. Both come from a root that means mercy or tender care. Pickthall gives "the Beneficent, the Merciful." George Sale, whose 1734 English version was the first made directly from Arabic, used "the most merciful" for both, joining them into one phrase. J. M. Rodwell, in 1861, wrote "the Compassionate, the Merciful." Commentators like al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir discussed what the two words add to each other; a common reading is that one names mercy as God’s nature and the other names mercy in action.

Now look at the last verse. Pickthall’s "those who earn Thine anger" and "those who go astray" are close to the Arabic and name no group. Some classical commentators, citing hadith reports, identified these two groups with Jews and Christians. Other classical and most modern commentators read the verse as describing any person who knows the right path and leaves it, or who wanders without knowledge. A translator who adds a footnote is choosing among these readings. When you compare translations, you are watching interpretation happen in the open.

Al-'Alaq: The First Words

23.4

A Command to Read

Main ideaMuslim tradition holds that 96:1-5 were the first verses revealed, in a cave near Mecca, beginning with the word "Read."

According to Muslim tradition, the Qur’an began with one word. The , the traditional biography of Muhammad written down by Ibn Ishaq around 760, tells that Muhammad, about forty years old, used to go alone to a cave on Mount Hira outside Mecca. There, tradition says, around the year 610, the angel Jibril (Gabriel) came to him and said, "Read." Muhammad answered that he could not read. The angel repeated the command, and then the first verses of Surah 96, Al-&#x27;Alaq, came to him. Muslims believe this was the start of , God’s message being sent down.

The five verses are short. In Pickthall’s translation: "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth, Createth man from a clot. Read: And thy Lord is the Most Bounteous, Who teacheth by the pen, Teacheth man that which he knew not." The Arabic word iqra’ can mean "read" or "recite," and translators choose between them. The surah’s name, Al-’Alaq, comes from the word Pickthall gives as "clot," a stage of the unborn child. In five lines the passage names God as creator, names the pen as a tool of teaching, and says that human knowledge is given.

Historians handle this story carefully. The account comes from Muslim sources written down more than a century after the event. There are no outside witnesses. What historians can say is that the tradition placing these verses first is old and widely shared, that the early Muslim community remembered the beginning this way, and that the verses fit the style of the earliest Meccan surahs. Whether the event happened as the sira tells it is a matter of faith, and this course describes the tradition without deciding it.

23.5

The Pen and the Rest of the Surah

Main ideaSurah 96 continues past its famous opening into a warning against a man who forbids prayer, showing the early Meccan pattern of praise, warning and worship.

Most people know only the first five verses of Al-’Alaq. The surah has nineteen. After the opening on creation and the pen, the tone changes. Verses 6 and 7 say that man rebels when he sees himself as self-sufficient. Then the surah describes a man who forbids a servant of God from praying, and asks whether that man knows that God sees. Commentators from al-Tabari onward connect this to a Meccan leader, Abu Jahl, who tradition says tried to stop Muhammad from praying at the Kaaba. The surah ends with a command to prostrate and draw near.

Reading the whole surah shows a pattern that repeats across the earliest Meccan chapters. First, God is named as creator and giver. Then the human being is described, often as forgetful or proud. Then comes a warning: God sees, and there is a reckoning. Last comes a turn to worship. Many academic scholars, including Angelika Neuwirth in Berlin, have studied this structure and argue that the Meccan surahs were shaped for recitation in early gatherings, with parts that answer each other.

Muslim tradition adds that the surah’s two halves may have come at different times: verses 1 to 5 in the cave, and the rest later, once opposition in Mecca had begun. That would explain the change of tone. Because the Qur’an is arranged by surah, not by date, a reader has to use such traditions, and the commentaries that report them, to imagine the order in which the pieces first came. The last verse of Al-’Alaq is one of the places where Muslims prostrate during recitation, a practice called the prostration of recitation.

Consolation and Warning

23.6

Ad-Duha: The Morning Hours

Main ideaSurah 93 opens with an oath and speaks comfort to a person who feels abandoned; tradition reads it as consolation to Muhammad after a pause in revelation.

Surah 93, , is eleven verses long. It opens with two oaths: "By the morning hours, And by the night when it is stillest." Then comes the sentence the surah is built around, in Pickthall’s words: "Thy Lord hath not forsaken thee nor doth He hate thee." The surah goes on to promise that what comes later will be better than what came before, and that the one addressed will be content. It is the voice of comfort to someone who feels left alone.

Muslim tradition gives this surah an occasion. Commentaries report that after the first revelations there was a pause, the , when nothing came for a time. Opponents in Mecca mocked Muhammad, saying his Lord had left him. Ad-Duha, tradition says, came as the answer. Al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir both record this, drawing on hadith reports. The report gives the surah a human setting: fear that a gift has been withdrawn, and a reply that it has not.

Academic readers note the surah’s craft. The oath by morning light and still night sets a mood before a word of comfort is spoken. Then three questions follow: did God not find you an orphan, wandering, poor, and shelter, guide and enrich you? Muslim tradition reads these as facts of Muhammad’s life: his father died before his birth, his mother when he was about six. Whether or not a reader accepts the occasion, the surah’s movement is clear: from night to morning, from feeling abandoned to being told one is held. Readers across centuries have used it in their own hard hours.

23.7

The Orphan and the Beggar

Main ideaAd-Duha ends by turning comfort into duty: the one who was sheltered must not oppress the orphan or turn away the beggar.

The second half of Ad-Duha changes direction. After three questions about being found and helped, three commands follow. In Pickthall’s translation: "Therefor the orphan oppress not, Therefor the beggar drive not away, Therefor of the bounty of thy Lord be thy discourse." The word "Therefor" is doing the work. Because you were an orphan and were sheltered, do not harm the orphan. Because you were poor and were provided for, do not drive off the one who asks. Because you were given, speak of what you were given.

This structure, memory turned into duty, appears across the Qur’an. Commentators note that it matches a pattern in the Hebrew Bible, where Israel is told to protect the stranger because they were strangers in Egypt. The Qur’an does not cite that text, but scholars of both scriptures have compared the two. In Meccan surahs, the and the poor come up again and again. Surah 107, for example, describes as a denier of religion the person who pushes the orphan away and does not urge feeding the poor.

Muslim ethical writers have built on these verses. Care for orphans became a legal and moral topic in Islamic law, with rules about guarding an orphan’s property until adulthood. Charity for those who ask, called when voluntary, is urged throughout the tradition. Modern Muslim readers in Chicago and elsewhere often cite Ad-Duha in talks about refugees and the homeless. The point for our reading is how a surah of comfort ends in a command, and how the two are joined by one small word.

23.8

Al-'Asr: Three Lines, a Whole Ethic

Main ideaSurah 103 says in three verses that every person is at a loss except those who believe, do good, and urge each other to truth and patience.

Surah 103, Al-&#x27;Asr, is one of the shortest chapters in the Qur’an. In Pickthall’s translation: "By the declining day, Lo! man is in a state of loss, Save those who believe and do good works, and exhort one another to truth and exhort one another to endurance." That is all of it. An oath, a claim about every human being, and one exception with four parts. The Arabic word al-’asr can mean time, an age, or the late afternoon; translators differ, and the surah’s name is usually left as Al-’Asr or given as "Time."

The claim is stark. Everyone is losing, the surah says, the way a merchant loses when a day passes and nothing is earned. Commentators from al-Tabari to the modern Egyptian scholar Muhammad Abduh (d. 1905) read the oath by time as the setup for this image: time runs out. The exception lists what the surah counts as gain. Two parts are personal: faith, and good works. Two are shared: urging one another toward truth, and urging one another toward , patience or endurance.

Ibn Kathir reports that the jurist al-Shafi’i (d. 820) said that if people reflected on this surah alone, it would suffice them. Muslim writers have treated the four parts as a complete map of a good life, one that cannot be lived alone because two of the four require other people. Academic readers add that the surah shows Meccan style at its most concentrated: a single oath, a single rhyme sound, and a sentence that turns on the word "save," or "except." A companion report says that some early Muslims would not part from each other until one had recited this surah to the other.

Section 4

Meccan Style and Al-Ikhlas

23.9

Oath, Rhyme and Sound

Main ideaMeccan surahs share a recognizable style: opening oaths, short rhymed verses, urgent warnings and vivid images of the Last Day.

By now you have read four short surahs, and you may have noticed that they sound alike. Three of them open with an oath: by the morning hours, by the still night, by the declining day. Their verses are short and end in rhyming sounds. In Arabic, Ad-Duha’s verses end in a long "a" sound, and Al-’Asr’s in "r." This rhymed prose is called saj&#x27;. It is not poetry in the Arabic sense, which needs a fixed meter, but it is built for the ear.

Scholars, Muslim and academic, describe a set of Meccan features. The surahs are short. They speak of one God, of creation as a sign, of the Day of Judgment, and of justice for the weak: the orphan, the poor, the enslaved. They address a single listener, often Muhammad himself, with "thee." They use vivid pictures: the sun folded up, the mountains moving, scales that weigh deeds. The longer Medinan surahs, which tradition dates after 622, turn instead to law, community and other peoples of the Book, and their verses grow long.

The tradition itself sorted surahs into Meccan and Medinan, and the standard Egyptian edition of 1924 prints a heading on each surah saying which it is. It also lists a traditional order of revelation: in that order Surah 96 is first, Al-Fatiha fifth, Ad-Duha eleventh, Al-’Asr thirteenth and Al-Ikhlas twenty-second. Academic scholars such as Theodor Nöldeke (1860) proposed their own ordering using style, dividing the Meccan surahs into early, middle and late periods. The two systems mostly agree on which surahs are earliest. Either way, the short surahs we are reading are among them.

Al-Ikhlas: Saying One

Main ideaSurah 112 states tawhid, the oneness of God, in four verses that Muslims consider a summary of belief.

Surah 112 is called , a word meaning sincerity or purity. It has four verses. In Pickthall’s translation: "Say: He is Allah, the One! Allah, the eternally Besought of all! He begetteth not nor was begotten. And there is none comparable unto Him." The word "Say" at the start is a command to Muhammad, and the surah reads as the answer to a question. Tradition reports that Meccans asked him to describe his Lord’s lineage, and this came as the reply.

The surah is the Qur’an’s most compact statement of , the belief that God is one, without partner, parent or child. Each verse closes a door. God is one. God is the one all depend on and who depends on none; the Arabic word as-Samad is hard to translate, and Rodwell, Sale and Pickthall all render it differently. God has no children and no parent. Nothing is like God. Muslim theologians have read verse 3 as answering both the old Arabian belief in daughters of God and the Christian teaching that Jesus is God’s son. The surah does not name either; commentators supply the context.

A hadith in the collection of Bukhari reports Muhammad saying that this surah equals a third of the Qur’an. Commentators explain that the Qur’an’s content falls into three broad parts, belief, law and story, and that Al-Ikhlas covers the whole of the first. Because of this saying, and because it is short, Al-Ikhlas is recited constantly in daily prayer, often right after Al-Fatiha. Its four lines are among the first a Muslim child memorizes and, as our chapter’s story suggested, among the last a scholar stops thinking about.

23.11

The Surah as a Whole

Main ideaReading a surah as one unit of meaning, and knowing how it is used in prayer, changes how each verse is understood.

It is easy to read the Qur’an one verse at a time. Verses are numbered, quoted and memorized separately. But the five surahs in this chapter each work as a whole. Al-Fatiha moves from praise to petition. Al-’Alaq moves from creation to warning to worship. Ad-Duha moves from oath to comfort to command. Al-’Asr is one sentence. Al-Ikhlas is one answer. Take a verse out and the movement is lost. Muslim scholars have long spoken of , the arrangement or coherence of the text, and modern readers such as the Pakistani commentator Amin Ahsan Islahi (d. 1997) built whole commentaries on the claim that every surah has a central theme.

Academic scholars have come to a similar view from a different road. Angelika Neuwirth’s studies of the Meccan surahs treat each one as a composed unit with parts that balance, made for recitation aloud. She and others compare the surahs to the psalms and to the liturgy of the churches and synagogues of late antiquity. This does not settle any religious question. It gives students a way to read: find the surah’s turning point, notice what answers what, and hear the rhyme as a marker of where a section ends.

The last piece is use. These surahs are prayed. In salat, a Muslim recites Al-Fatiha and then usually a short surah; Al-Ikhlas, Al-’Asr and Ad-Duha are common choices. They are recited in , the rules of correct pronunciation that keep the sound of the Arabic. Some Muslims recite Al-Ikhlas three times before sleep. A reader who knows this understands why the shortest surahs are so often quoted: they are the ones that live in the mouth. A close reading that ignores the prayer misses half of what the text has meant.

Narrative Surahs
Big questionHow does the Qur'an tell a story, and what do its readers learn by setting Surah Yusuf and Surah Maryam beside Genesis and Luke?
The story

The Best of Stories

One surah tells a single story from beginning to end, and the Qur'an gives it a title of its own.

A reader who opens the Qur'an at Surah 12 finds something unusual. Most surahs move among topics: a warning, a sign in nature, a piece of a prophet's story, a law. Surah 12 does not. It opens with three verses of frame and then tells one story, from a boy's dream to an old man's restored sight, across more than a hundred verses. Then it closes the frame. The story is that of Yusuf (Joseph), and the surah's third verse calls it, in Pickthall's words, "the best of narratives."

Muslim tradition gives the surah a setting. Some commentators say it came in Mecca during a hard year, around 619, after the deaths of Muhammad's wife Khadija and his uncle Abu Talib. In that reading, a story about a man betrayed by his family, sold, imprisoned and finally reunited was told to a listener who needed it. Historians cannot confirm the date. What they can see is the surah's craft: dreams that open and close the plot, a shirt that appears three times, and a speaker who steps in to say what the story means.

Surah 19 tells stories too, but differently. It is named for Maryam (Mary), the only woman named in the Qur'an, and it moves in scenes: an old priest praying quietly for a son, a young woman alone under a palm tree, a father and son arguing about idols. Between scenes a refrain returns: "make mention in the Scripture of" the next figure. Muslim tradition tells that when early Muslims fled to Abyssinia around 615, one of them, Ja'far, recited from this surah before the Christian king, and the king wept.

This chapter reads both surahs closely. You will follow the plot of Surah Yusuf and the scenes of Surah Maryam, learn how Qur'anic narrative works through allusion, repetition and direct speech, and set the texts beside Genesis 37 to 50 and Luke 1. Muslim commentators and academic scholars have both written on these surahs at length, and we will hear from each. The question the chapter keeps asking is simple: what does the Qur'an do with a story that its hearers already knew?

24.1

A Frame Around a Story

Main ideaSurah 12 opens and closes with verses that say why the story is being told, framing the narrative as a lesson.

Surah 12 begins, like many surahs, with letters: Alif Lam Ra. Then verse 2 says the book was sent down as an Arabic Qur’an so that its hearers might understand. Verse 3 makes a promise. Pickthall renders it: "We narrate unto thee (Muhammad) the best of narratives, in that We have inspired in thee this Qur’an, though aforetime thou wast of the heedless." Only after this does the story start. The speaker is God, in the plural "We" that the Qur’an often uses; the listener is Muhammad. The story of Yusuf is placed inside this address.

The word Pickthall translates as "narratives" is qasas, from a root that means to follow tracks or to recount. Commentators have asked what "best" means. Al-Tabari collects several views: that it is the best because it is the most complete story in the Qur’an, or because of its lessons about patience and forgiveness, or because of its ending, in which everyone is reconciled. Academic readers note that the itself tells you how to read: this is a story with a point, told to someone who did not know it before.

The surah closes the frame in its last verse, 111. It says, in outline, that in the stories of these people there is a lesson for those with understanding, and that the Qur’an is not an invented tale but a confirmation of what came before, an explanation, a guidance and a mercy. So the surah announces at its start that a story is coming, tells it, and then says what kind of thing a story is. Scholars call this a , and it is one of the clearest in the Qur’an. Everything between verse 3 and verse 111 is held inside it.

Beside Genesis 37 to 50

Main ideaRead beside Genesis, Surah 12 keeps the same plot but shortens, reorders and adds scenes to fit its own purpose.

The Joseph story fills fourteen chapters of , 37 to 50, and one surah of 111 verses. Set them side by side and the same plot appears: the favored son, the dream, the brothers, the pit, the sale, Egypt, the master’s wife, prison, dreams, the famine, the brothers’ visits, the cup, the reveal, the reunion. Scholars of both texts agree that the Qur’an assumes its hearers know the outline. That is why it can skip names, places and numbers. The surah is not retelling Genesis; it is speaking to people who have already heard the story from Jewish and Christian neighbors in Arabia.

The differences are where reading gets interesting. Genesis gives the coat, the brothers’ names, Potiphar, the cupbearer and the baker, Pharaoh, and long speeches by Judah. The Qur’an gives the torn shirt as proof, the banquet with the knives, the sermon in prison, Yusuf’s request for office, and Ya’qub’s blindness and cure. Genesis ends with Jacob’s blessings and Joseph’s bones carried out of Egypt. The Qur’an ends with Yusuf’s prayer and the closing frame. Some Qur’anic details, such as the wolf and the women’s banquet, also appear in Jewish , rabbinic retellings, and scholars debate how these traditions traveled.

How the two traditions read the story also differs. In Jewish reading, Joseph’s story is part of how Israel came to Egypt, setting up the Exodus. In Christian reading, Joseph has often been seen as a figure who suffers and saves his brothers. In Muslim reading, Yusuf is a prophet, the story is a proof of Muhammad’s message, since tradition says he did not know it before, and its lessons are patience, chastity, forgiveness and trust in God’s plan. This course does not rank these readings. It asks you to see how one story became three.

Section 3

Surah Maryam

24.7

Zakariya's Quiet Prayer

Main ideaSurah 19 opens with the old priest Zakariya praying in secret for a son and receiving Yahya, a scene shared with Luke 1.

Surah 19 begins with five Arabic letters, Kaf Ha Ya Ayn Sad, whose meaning commentators have never settled. Then it says: a mention of the mercy of your Lord to his servant (Zechariah). Zakariya calls on his Lord in a low voice (19:3). He says that his bones have grown weak and his head shines with gray hair, and that he has never been disappointed in prayer. He fears for what his relatives will do after him, and he asks for an heir. The answer comes: a son named (John), a name given to no one before (19:7).

Zakariya asks how, since his wife is barren and he is old. The reply is that it is easy for God. He asks for a sign, and is told he will not speak to people for three nights, though sound of body (19:10). He comes out to his people and signals them to praise God morning and evening. The scene then jumps to Yahya as a child, given wisdom, tenderness and purity, kind to his parents (19:12-14). A blessing closes the scene: peace on him the day he was born, the day he dies and the day he is raised alive (19:15). That blessing will return.

The Gospel of , chapter 1, tells the same story: an old priest, Zacharias, and his barren wife Elisabeth; an angel in the temple; a son to be named John; a sign of silence. There are differences of detail. In Luke the sign is muteness until the birth; in the Qur’an it is three nights. In Luke the angel is named Gabriel; in Surah 19 the speaker of the answer is not named. Muslims believe both accounts describe a real prophet, Yahya. Christians revere John the Baptist as the forerunner of Jesus. Academic scholars study how the scene moved between communities. What all agree on is that the Qur’an tells it briefly, in speech, and moves on.

In Luke the silence is a rebuke; in Surah 19 it is a sign he asked for.

24.8

Maryam and the Palm Tree

Main ideaSurah 19 tells of Maryam withdrawing east, receiving news of a son, giving birth alone under a palm tree, and the infant Isa speaking in her defense.

The surah turns with its refrain: make mention in the Scripture of (Mary). She withdraws from her family to a place facing east and screens herself off (19:16-17). God’s spirit is sent to her and appears as a well-made man. Maryam says, in outline, that she seeks refuge in the Merciful from him, if he fears God. He says he is only a messenger, sent to give her a pure son. She asks how, when no man has touched her. He answers that it is easy for God, and that the child will be a sign and a mercy (19:19-21). The Qur’an does not name the visitor here; Muslim tradition identifies him as Jibril (Gabriel).

Then comes a scene found in no Gospel. Maryam conceives and withdraws to a far place. Labor pains drive her to the trunk of a palm tree, and she cries that she wishes she had died before this and been forgotten (19:23). A voice from below tells her not to grieve: a stream is at her feet, and if she shakes the palm, fresh dates will fall. She is told to eat, drink and be comforted, and to vow silence if anyone comes (19:24-26). Academic scholars note that a palm tree and a birth also appear in some early Christian texts outside the New Testament, and they debate the links.

Maryam carries the child to her people. They are shocked and call her "sister of Aaron," a phrase commentators have explained in several ways; al-Tabari records the view that she was of Aaron’s line, and a hadith in the collection of Muslim explains that people were named after earlier prophets. Maryam points to the baby. Her people ask how they can speak to a child in the cradle. Then the infant (Jesus) speaks. In Pickthall’s words: "Lo! I am the slave of Allah. He hath given me the Scripture and hath appointed me a Prophet." He says he is blessed, commanded to pray and give alms, and kind to his mother, and the refrain returns: peace on me the day I was born, the day I die, and the day I am raised alive (19:30-33).

24.9

Ibrahim, Musa and the Shape of the Surah

Main ideaAfter Maryam, Surah 19 gives short scenes of Ibrahim, Musa, Ismail and Idris, tied together by a refrain, then turns to warning and judgment.

After the infant speaks, the surah steps back to comment: that is Isa, son of Maryam, a statement of truth about which they doubt; it is not for God to take a son (19:34-35). Then the refrain sounds again: make mention in the Scripture of (Abraham). In a scene of direct speech, Ibrahim gently asks his father why he worships what cannot hear or see, and his father threatens to stone him. Ibrahim answers with a greeting of peace and leaves (19:41-48). The Qur’an calls his father Azar elsewhere; the Hebrew Bible calls Abraham’s father Terah, and scholars discuss the difference.

Musa (Moses) gets three verses: he was chosen, called from the right side of the mountain, and given his brother Harun (Aaron) as a prophet (19:51-53). Ismail (Ishmael) is remembered for keeping his promise and commanding his family to pray and give alms (19:54-55). , whom many commentators identify with the biblical Enoch, is raised to a high place (19:56-57). Then verse 58 gathers them all: these are the ones God favored among the prophets, and when the signs were recited to them they fell down prostrate and weeping. It is one of the verses at which Muslims prostrate during recitation.

The surah’s shape is now visible. It opens with two births, Yahya and Isa. It continues with a father and son, a prophet and his brother, and two more figures, each introduced by the same refrain. Then it turns to the generations who came after and neglected prayer, to paradise, to a warning against saying the Merciful has a son, and to the Day of Judgment. Academic scholars such as Angelika Neuwirth read Surah 19 as a carefully built Meccan composition, with its rhyme changing when the subject changes. Muslim commentators read it as a sequence of mercies, following the surah’s own first word after the letters: a mention of mercy.

Section 4

How Qur'anic Narrative Works

24.10

Allusion, Repetition and Speech

Main ideaQur'anic stories assume the audience knows the outline, repeat key phrases and objects, and give most of the action in direct speech inside a moral frame.

Having read two narrative surahs, you can now name the tools. The first is . The Qur’an rarely tells a story from scratch. It points to one its hearers know, from Arabian Jewish and Christian communities, and tells the part it needs. Names, places and dates drop away. The academic term for this is a referential style, and it explains why a reader who does not know Genesis or Luke can feel lost, while a reader who does feels the story being reshaped.

The second tool is . Surah 19 repeats "make mention in the Scripture of" and the blessing of peace at birth, death and rising. Surah 12 repeats the shirt, the dream and the phrase about God being the best of guardians. Repetition marks structure, the way rhyme does, and it builds meaning: when the shirt returns, the reader remembers where it has been. Other surahs retell the same story of Musa or Nuh (Noah) many times with different details, and commentators read each telling for what the surah around it needs.

The third tool is direct speech. Most of Surah 12 is dialogue: the brothers plotting, Ya’qub grieving, the governor’s wife accusing, Yusuf forgiving. Surah 19 gives us Zakariya’s prayer and Maryam’s cry in their own words. The narrator steps in briefly to move time or to comment. That commentary is the fourth tool: the . A Qur’anic story is never just told. It is told for a lesson, and the text says so, in the opening of Surah 12, in the comment after the infant speaks in Surah 19, and in the closing of both. Together these four tools give Qur’anic narrative its sound: fast, spoken, pointed, and aimed at the listener.

There is no "once, in Canaan," no description of the boy, no reason he tells the dream. It begins in the middle of a conversation, in the first person, with an image that will only make sense 96 verses later.

24.11

Muslim and Academic Readings

Main ideaCommentators, Sufi poets and academic scholars have all read these surahs, each asking different questions of the same text.

Muslim commentators read Surah Yusuf and Surah Maryam for guidance. Al-Tabari (d. 923) gathers reports from early Muslims on each verse and often lists more than one reading before choosing. Ibn Kathir (d. 1373) prefers to explain the Qur’an by the Qur’an and by hadith, and he warns against filling gaps with stories borrowed from Jewish and Christian sources, which he calls Isra&#x27;iliyyat. On the governor’s wife, for example, the commentators disagree about how far Yusuf was tempted, and al-Tabari records several views without settling it.

readers, who seek inner meanings, made Yusuf a symbol. The Persian poet Jami (d. 1492) wrote a long poem, Yusuf and Zulaykha, in which the woman’s love for Yusuf becomes an image of the soul’s longing for God. Rumi (d. 1273) returns to Yusuf again and again in his verse. These readings go far beyond the surah’s words, and their authors knew it; they read the story as a mirror, not a report. Shia commentators, too, have read Surah 19’s blessing on the prophets in light of the household of the Prophet.

Academic scholars ask different questions. When was the surah composed, and for whom? How does it relate to Genesis, Luke and the traditions of Arabian Jews and Christians? What does its structure show? Mustansir Mir analyzed Surah 12 as a plotted whole with a beginning, middle and end. Angelika Neuwirth places Surah 19 among the middle Meccan compositions. Some scholars look for sources; others study the surah as literature; others study how Muslims have used it. None of these questions settles whether the text is revealed. That is a matter of faith, and this course leaves it to each reader. What the study shows is how much one story can hold.
