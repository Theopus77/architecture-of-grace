How to Read a Sacred Text

Method

A Torah scroll is read with a silver pointer. A Qur'an is kept on a high shelf and recited in Arabic by people who speak many languages. A Sikh scripture rests under a canopy and is honored as a living Guru. Before this course studies any single religion, it asks a basic question: what makes a text sacred to a community, and how can someone from inside or outside that community read it carefully?

This unit gives you the tools. You will ask who gave or wrote a text, for whom, when and why. You will see how texts moved from memory and recitation to scrolls, codices, palm leaves and printed books, and how communities decided which books belong in their scripture. You will learn why every translation is also an interpretation, and how rabbis, church councils, Muslim scholars and gurus have guided their communities' readings.

You will also learn the rules of the academic study of religion in a public school: describe, attribute and do not rank. Chicago itself offers an example, from the World's Parliament of Religions of 1893 to the many houses of worship in the city today. By the end of the unit, you will be able to read a passage from any tradition, explain it in terms its adherents would recognize and compare two traditions fairly.

The big question

How can a careful reader understand a sacred text on its own terms, and compare traditions fairly without judging them?

The story

Three Books Behind the Glass

A class on a field trip stops at one museum case and finds three sacred texts, each treated in a different way.

The field trip has reached the last gallery of the afternoon, and most of the class is tired. Then Ms. Ortiz stops them in front of a long glass case with soft lights. Inside are three objects. On the left, a scroll of pale parchment is rolled around two wooden handles, open just enough to show neat columns of Hebrew letters. In the middle, a book lies open on a carved wooden stand. Its pages are covered in flowing Arabic script, edged with gold and blue. On the right sits a stack of long, narrow strips of dried palm leaf, tied together with a cord through two holes.

Jordan leans close to the palm leaves. The writing is tiny and even, scratched into the leaf and darkened with ink. The card says it is a Buddhist text copied in South Asia a few hundred years ago. Each leaf is about the size of a ruler. Jordan counts the holes and wonders how anyone kept the pages in order. Ms. Ortiz points out that the cord through the holes is the answer. Untie it, and the book falls apart into loose leaves.

Amira stands at the middle stand. She recognizes the Arabic before she reads the card. At home, her family's copy of the Qur'an is kept on a high shelf, wrapped in cloth, and her grandmother washes before she opens it. She tells the class that the book in the case is beautiful, but it is strange to see it behind glass, where no one can recite from it. A museum guard nods. He says visitors from many faiths often say the same thing about objects in this room.

Ben looks at the scroll. His cousin read from a Torah scroll at her bat mitzvah last spring. He remembers that no one touched the letters with their fingers. The reader followed the words with a small silver pointer shaped like a hand. The scroll in the case is old and worn, and the card says that when a scroll becomes too damaged to use, many communities bury it with care instead of throwing it away.

Ms. Ortiz asks the class what the three objects have in common. They are made of different materials. They are written in different languages. They come from different traditions. But each one was copied by hand with great effort, and each one was treated by a community as something more than an ordinary book. The question for this unit, she says, is what makes a text sacred, and how an outsider can read one with care.

Section 1

What Makes a Text Sacred

25.1

A Text a Community Treasures

Main ideaA text becomes sacred through the way a community receives it, treats it and lives by it, not only through what is written in it.

In a Sikh gurdwara, or house of worship, the Guru Granth Sahib rests on a raised platform under a canopy, covered with embroidered cloth. In many gurdwaras, at night it is carried to a separate room to rest, and in the morning it is brought back with prayers. Sikhs treat the book this way because they regard it as their living Guru, or teacher. For Sikhs, the words are not only about the Guru. They are the Guru’s presence among them.

Scholars of religion use the term for writing that a community sets apart as holy, authoritative or revealed. Another common term is , from a Latin word for writings. A sacred text is not defined by its age or its beauty alone. Many old, beautiful books are not sacred to anyone. What makes a text sacred is a relationship: a community reads it, recites it, argues over it and shapes its life by it.

You can often see that relationship in how people handle the object. Many Jews read a Torah scroll with a pointer so that hands do not touch the letters. Many Muslims perform ritual washing before touching a printed Qur’an in Arabic and keep it off the floor. Christians in many churches carry the Gospel book in procession and stand for its reading. These acts of , or deep respect, show outsiders what the text means to insiders.

This course studies sacred texts from the outside, as a scholar would. That means you will describe how each community treats its texts, and you will attribute each belief to the people who hold it. You do not have to share a belief to understand why it matters to someone else.

25.2

What Sacred Texts Do

Main ideaCommunities use sacred texts in worship, as guides for law and ethics, and as the story of who they are.

Five times a day, Muslims around the world perform salat, the ritual prayer. In every prayer they recite the opening chapter of the Qur’an, the Fatiha, in Arabic. A Muslim in Chicago and a Muslim in Jakarta say the same words in the same language. Here the text works as , the set words of public worship. It is spoken and heard, not only read silently.

Sacred texts also guide action. Jewish law, or halakha, draws on the commandments of the Torah as the rabbis interpreted them. Muslim scholars draw on the Qur’an and the example of the Prophet to describe right conduct. Many Christians turn to the Sermon on the Mount when they think about forgiveness. In each case the text is an , a source that people consult when they ask how to live.

Finally, sacred texts tell communities who they are. At the Passover Seder, Jewish families retell the Exodus as their own story. Christians read the Gospels as the story of the person at the center of their faith. Hindus hear the Ramayana told in plays and festivals. A text like this gives a people its , a shared memory that joins the living to the past.

Most sacred texts do all three jobs at once. A single psalm can be sung in worship, quoted in an argument about justice and remembered as part of a people’s history. When you meet a new text in this course, ask which of these jobs it does for the people who hold it sacred.

Section 2

Author, Audience, Purpose

25.3

Who Wrote It?

Main ideaTraditions often hold beliefs about who gave or wrote their texts, and scholars also study how texts were composed; a careful reader keeps both in view.

Open a Qur’an and you will not find an author’s name on the cover. Muslims believe the Qur’an is the word of God, revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Gabriel over about twenty-three years, from about 610 to 632 CE. In this view Muhammad is not the author but the one who received and recited it. The Arabic word qur’an itself means recitation. This belief about origin is called , a message believed to come from God.

Other traditions describe origins in other ways. Jewish and Christian tradition long held that Moses wrote the Torah. Many modern scholars instead see it as the work of several writers and editors over centuries, combined into one text. Hindus describe the Vedas as shruti, meaning what was heard. In this view, ancient sages heard eternal truths and did not invent them. Many texts are , with no author named at all.

Some texts do name their writers. Scholars across many backgrounds agree that Paul wrote at least seven of the New Testament letters that bear his name, including Romans and Galatians. The Analects of Confucius were gathered by his students and their followers after his death. So can mean one writer, a group of disciples, a long chain of editors or, for believers, a divine source.

In this course you will state both kinds of claims clearly and keep them apart. You might write, Muslims believe the Qur’an was revealed to Muhammad. You might write, many historians date this book to the sixth century BCE. Each sentence says who holds the view. Neither one asks you to decide which is true.

25.4

For Whom, When and Why?

Main ideaEvery text was first addressed to an audience at a certain time for a purpose, and knowing these helps a reader understand what it says.

The Gospel of Luke begins in an unusual way. Before telling any story, the writer explains himself. He says many others have written accounts, and he has decided to write an orderly one for a reader named Theophilus. He even says why: so that Theophilus may know the certainty of what he has been taught. In four verses the author names his and his .

Most texts are not so direct, but the same questions apply. The is the first group of readers or hearers. Paul’s letter to the Galatians was sent to specific churches in a region of what is now Turkey. The Qur’an often addresses the Prophet or the early Muslim community directly, and many verses respond to events in Mecca or Medina. Knowing the first audience explains why a text mentions certain people, places or problems.

The is what the author or community wanted the text to do: to teach, to warn, to comfort, to record a law or to shape worship. The date matters too. A psalm written for the Temple in Jerusalem reads differently when you know the Temple still stood. A prophecy spoken during a war reads differently when you know the war. Scholars call this setting the text’s .

None of this limits what a text means to believers today. Christians read Luke as speaking to them, not only to Theophilus. But a careful reader starts with the first audience and then asks how later communities have read the same words. A historian reading Luke asks the same questions a reader of Paul or the Qur’an would ask.

25.5

The Careful Reader's Questions

Main ideaAsking who, to whom, when and why turns a hard passage into one you can explain, even when some answers are uncertain.

Try the questions on a text from far away. The Dhammapada is a collection of short verses in the Pali language. Buddhists hold that the verses record teachings of the Buddha, who lived in northern India, probably in the fifth century BCE. Monks memorized and recited them for generations before they were written down. The audience was anyone seeking the path the Buddha taught, especially monks and nuns.

Now read its first verse in Max Müller’s 1881 English translation: All that we are is the result of what we have thought. With the questions in mind, you can see what the verse is doing. It is not a history lesson. It is a , meant to train the mind. Its purpose is practical: if thoughts shape a person, then guarding one’s thoughts is the first step on the path.

Sometimes the answers are uncertain. For many ancient texts, no one knows the exact date or writer. That is fine. A careful reader says what is known, what is traditional and what scholars debate. You might write: Buddhists attribute these verses to the Buddha; scholars date the collection’s written form to centuries after his life. That sentence is honest about , the facts that support a claim.

Use four questions for every text in this course. Who wrote or gave it, according to the tradition and to scholars? Who was its first audience? When and where did it come from? Why was it made, and why do people still read it? You will not always get full answers, but you will always read more carefully.

Section 3

From Voice to Page

25.6

Oral Tradition Before Writing

Main ideaMany sacred texts were memorized and recited for generations before anyone wrote them down, and recitation still matters today.

In parts of India today, young students still learn the Vedas the old way. A teacher chants a line, and the students repeat it, again and again, with exact rhythm and pitch. They learn to chant each hymn in several patterns that repeat and reverse the order of words, so that no syllable slips. For well over two thousand years, this method passed down the Vedic hymns mostly by voice. Scholars call this : knowledge handed down by speaking and memory.

Other traditions began the same way. Buddhist tradition holds that after the Buddha died, his followers met and recited his teachings together to fix them in memory. According to tradition, the Pali Canon was first written down in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE. Jewish tradition speaks of an Oral Torah given alongside the written one. It was passed from teacher to student and later recorded in the Mishnah, around 200 CE.

Memory still matters. Many Muslims memorize the entire Qur’an, more than six thousand verses in Arabic. A person who does so is honored with the title , meaning guardian or one who preserves. During the month of Ramadan, many mosques hold night prayers in which a hafiz recites the whole Qur’an over the month. , reading or chanting aloud from memory, is a central way many traditions keep their texts.

For readers, oral tradition is a reminder that many sacred texts were meant first to be heard. Repetition, rhythm and lists that seem strange on a page often make sense when you imagine them spoken aloud to a crowd. A psalm, a sutra or a surah may have been sung or chanted long before it was ever copied onto a page.

25.7

Scrolls, Codices and Palm Leaves

Main ideaThe form a text is written on, from scroll to codex to palm leaf to printed book, shapes how people read and use it.

In 1947, shepherds near the Dead Sea found clay jars in a cave, holding ancient scrolls. More finds followed in nearby caves near a site called Qumran. The Dead Sea Scrolls include the oldest known copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, some dating to the third or second century BCE. They are written on parchment, specially prepared animal skin, and on papyrus. They are : long sheets rolled up and read by unrolling.

Early Christians favored a different form, the , the ancestor of the modern book. A codex is made of sheets folded and sewn along one edge. It can hold more text than one scroll and lets a reader flip quickly between passages. Scholars note that surviving early Christian writings are mostly codices, while Jewish communities kept the scroll for the Torah read in worship, a custom that continues today.

In South and Southeast Asia, many texts were written on . Scribes cut and dried the leaves of certain palms, scratched letters into them with a metal stylus and rubbed in soot or ink. The leaves were stacked and tied with cord through holes, like the one in the museum story. Because leaves decay in a humid climate, texts had to be copied again and again. Many Buddhist and Hindu texts survive this way.

Printing changed things again. Johannes Gutenberg’s Bible, printed in Mainz around 1455, helped make identical copies common in Europe. Yet many communities still honor handwritten copies. A kosher Torah scroll must be written by hand by a trained scribe, and Qur’an calligraphy remains a treasured art.

25.8

Canon: Deciding What Is In

Main ideaA canon is a community's list of authoritative books, and different communities have drawn the list in different ways.

Pick up a Catholic Bible and a Protestant Bible and count the books. The Catholic Old Testament has 46 books. The Protestant one has 39. The difference comes from books such as Tobit, Judith and 1 and 2 Maccabees. Catholics and Orthodox Christians count them as scripture. Protestants call them the Apocrypha and usually leave them out or print them separately. The word names a community’s official list of sacred books.

Canon comes from a Greek word for a measuring rod. A canon measures which books count. Canons usually form slowly, as communities keep reading certain books in worship and stop reading others. For the Christian New Testament, an important moment came in 367 CE. That year Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, sent a letter listing the 27 books that make up the New Testament today.

The Jewish Tanakh contains the same writings as the Protestant Old Testament but counts and orders them differently, as 24 books. Other traditions draw their boundaries in other ways. The Pali Canon of Theravada Buddhism is huge, and Mahayana Buddhists add many other sutras. Hinduism has no single closed canon at all. Instead, Hindus speak of shruti, what was heard, and , what was remembered, a wider group including the epics.

When you compare traditions, ask what counts as scripture for this community, and who decided. The answer is often different from what an outsider expects. A Catholic, a Protestant and a Jewish reader may each hold a book called the Bible and still hold different lists. Asking about the canon helps you know which texts are being compared.

Kinds of Texts, Ways of Studying

25.9

Law, Story and Poetry

Main ideaSacred texts contain many genres, and each genre asks to be read in its own way.

Read three passages from the Hebrew Bible side by side. Exodus 20 gives commands: honor your father and mother, do not steal. Genesis 12 tells a story: God calls Abram to leave his country, and he goes. Psalm 23 sings: The LORD is my shepherd. All three are sacred to Jews and Christians, but they work differently. Scholars call these kinds of writing , a type of text with its own form and purpose.

tells people what to do or not do. It is often short, direct and specific, and it may include cases: if this happens, then do that. Readers of law ask who it applies to and how it has been interpreted. , or story, has characters, events and a plot. Readers of narrative ask what happens, why, and what the story shows about God, people or the world.

uses images, rhythm and repetition. Hebrew poetry often uses parallelism, saying one idea twice in slightly different words. A shepherd in a psalm is an image, not a job description. Readers of poetry ask what feelings and pictures the words create. Reading a poem as if it were a law, or a law as if it were a poem, leads to confusion.

Other traditions have their own genres. The Qur’an contains stories of earlier prophets, legal guidance and passages of intense rhythmic beauty. The Hindu epics are vast narratives. Knowing the genre of a passage is one of the first steps in reading it well.

25.10

Prophecy, Letter and Teaching

Main ideaProphecy speaks a message to a people, letters answer a community's questions and teachings instruct, and each shapes how a passage reads.

In the eighth century BCE, a herdsman named Amos went to the northern kingdom of Israel with a hard message. According to the book that bears his name, he condemned people who held religious festivals while cheating the poor. This is : a message that the prophet presents as coming from God, often a warning or call to change. In the Hebrew Bible, prophecy is less about predicting the future than about speaking to the present.

Some sacred texts are , written to real people about real problems. Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians answers questions the church in Corinth had sent him, about food, marriage and worship. A reader who knows it is a letter looks for the problem behind each answer. One side of a conversation is on the page, and the other side has to be pieced together.

Other texts are : sayings, dialogues and instructions from a teacher. The Analects record short sayings of Confucius and conversations with his students. The Bhagavad Gita is a dialogue in which Krishna teaches the warrior Arjuna. The Dhammapada gathers verses Buddhists attribute to the Buddha. Teachings are often brief, quotable and meant to be memorized and practiced.

Many books mix genres. The Gospels contain narrative, teaching, parables and prophecy. The Qur’an moves among warning, law, story and praise. Naming the genre of each passage, not just each book, keeps you from misreading it. A single chapter can shift from one genre to another in a few verses.

Every Translation Is a Choice

26.1

One Verse, Two English Versions

Main ideaBecause no two languages match word for word, every translator must choose, and each choice is a small act of interpretation.

The Hebrew verb in Exodus 20:13 does not match any single English word. The King James Version of 1611 renders it kill. The Jewish Publication Society translation of 1917 renders it murder. A carries a text from one language into another, but every language divides up meaning differently. So the translator must decide which English word comes closest to what the original means.

Translators also decide how closely to follow the original’s form. A keeps close to the original words and word order, even if the English sounds stiff. A meaning-based translation rewrites the sentence in natural English to carry the sense. Both approaches are honest. They simply value different things: one values the shape of the original, the other the ease of the reader.

Translators must also decide what their readers already know. Should a translation of the Qur’an keep the Arabic word Allah or write God? Should a Bible say LORD in capitals, following an old custom for the divine name, or use another form? Each decision reflects the translator’s community and purpose. That is why scholars say every translation is an , an explanation of what the text means.

For a student, the lesson is practical. When a verse matters to your argument, name the translation you are quoting. If you can, compare two versions. Where they differ, you have found a place where the original needed interpreting. Differences are not errors. They are clues to how each community reads.

Ways of Reading

26.4

The Plain Sense

Main ideaMany readers begin with the plain or literal sense of a text: what the words say in their ordinary meaning.

In the eleventh century, a rabbi in Troyes, France, wrote a line-by-line commentary on the Torah and the Talmud. His name was Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known as Rashi (1040–1105). Rashi aimed to explain what each verse plainly means. Printed Hebrew Bibles still place his notes beside the text, and Jewish students still learn Torah with Rashi at their side. A is a work that explains a text passage by passage.

Jewish tradition has a word for the plain meaning: . Peshat asks what the words say in their ordinary sense, in their setting. Jewish interpreters also speak of derash, the deeper lessons drawn out through study and storytelling, called midrash. Rabbis often said a verse can hold many meanings, but it does not lose its plain sense.

Christians, Muslims and others also value a : taking a text to mean what its words ordinarily say. Many Protestant Christians speak of the plain meaning of scripture. Muslim scholars distinguish the zahir, the apparent meaning of the Qur’an, from deeper meanings. In all these traditions, literal does not mean ignoring genre. Reading a poem literally means reading it as a poem.

People sometimes use literal loosely, to mean reading every sentence as a flat statement of fact. Scholars use it more carefully: the sense the author or community understood the words to have. That careful meaning is where most interpretation starts.

Main ideaContextual reading asks what a text meant in its own time and place, using history, language and comparison with other texts.

Context works in every tradition. Muslim scholars study the occasions of revelation, the events that surrounded particular verses of the Qur’an. Buddhist commentators explain the setting of each sutra. Asking what a text meant then helps a reader avoid assuming it must mean what it seems to mean now.

Main ideaJudaism and Christianity developed different bodies to interpret scripture: rabbis in study and debate, and church councils and traditions.

26.10

Describe, Attribute, Do Not Rank

Main ideaFair comparison describes what each tradition holds, attributes every belief to its holders and never ranks one tradition above another.

Read two sentences. First: Hindus worship many false gods. Second: Many Hindus worship Vishnu, Shiva or the goddess Devi, and many understand these deities as forms of one ultimate reality, Brahman. The first sentence judges and misdescribes. The second : it reports what adherents do and believe, in terms they would recognize. Description is the first rule of comparing religions.

The second rule is to : always say who holds a belief. According to the Qur’an, God is one. Buddhists teach that all things are impermanent. Catholics believe the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ. Attribution keeps your writing accurate and keeps you out of the argument. You are reporting beliefs, not claiming them true or false.

The third rule is not to rank. Words like primitive, advanced, superstitious or enlightened compared with others place traditions on a ladder. So do comparisons that measure every religion against one of them. Scholars try instead to use , the words a tradition uses for itself, explained plainly. Karma, halakha and tawhid are better than rough English stand-ins.

Comparison is still useful. You can say two traditions answer a question differently, or that a practice in one resembles a practice in another. The goal is to see each clearly, not to crown a winner. Differences can be described with care and without a verdict.

26.11

Chicago, 1893

Main ideaThe 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago was a landmark in bringing world traditions into conversation, with real limits scholars still discuss.

On September 11, 1893, a crowd of thousands filled a hall in Chicago, in the building on Michigan Avenue that now houses the Art Institute of Chicago. The World&#x27;s Parliament of Religions was opening, held alongside the World’s Columbian Exposition. For about two weeks, speakers from many traditions addressed audiences. It is often called the first formal gathering of its kind in the West.

A young Hindu monk from India, Swami , began his first address with the words Sisters and Brothers of America, and the audience rose to applaud. He spoke of many paths leading to the same goal. Anagarika Dharmapala of Sri Lanka and the Japanese Zen teacher Soyen Shaku spoke for Buddhism. Rabbis, Catholic and Protestant clergy, and speakers from other traditions also took part. For many Americans, it was their first encounter with these teachers.

Scholars also note the Parliament’s limits. Its organizers, led by the Presbyterian minister John Henry Barrows, were mostly Protestant Christians, and some framed Christianity as the fulfillment of other faiths. Some traditions, including Native American religions, had no voice of their own. Historians describe it as a that was real but uneven: many traditions present, not all as equals.

The Parliament’s influence lasted. Vivekananda and others taught in the United States in the years after. In 1993, a centennial Parliament of the World’s Religions met again in Chicago. The city’s many houses of worship today continue the conversation the 1893 gathering began.

Swami Vivekananda, opening address to the World's Parliament of Religions, Chicago, September 11, 1893 (paraphrased) (paraphrased)

A Toolkit for the Course

Main ideaSeven questions, drawn from this unit, will help you read any sacred text in this course carefully and fairly.

In the Analects, Confucius tells a student that real knowledge means knowing what you know and admitting what you do not. That is a good motto for a student of religion. Every chapter ahead will bring you a new text, a new practice or a new community. You will not become an expert in every tradition. But you can carry a , a set of questions that works on any text.

First, what kind of text is this: law, story, poetry, prophecy, letter or teaching? Second, who gave or wrote it, according to the tradition and to scholars? Third, who was its first audience, and what was its purpose? Fourth, which translation am I reading, and what might the original say? These questions come from Chapter 25 and the first part of this chapter.

Fifth, what is its historical context? Sixth, who interprets it in this community, and how do different groups inside the tradition read it? Seventh, how can I describe it in words the community would recognize, attributing every belief? Together these seven questions let you move from a first reading to a fair explanation.

Practice with any passage you have met so far: Psalm 23, Amos 5:24, the first verse of the Dhammapada. Each time, write one sentence for each question. The matters as much as any single answer, because it will carry you through every unit. Your answers will grow as you learn.

1. Which statement best explains what makes a text sacred?

Why: Scholars define a sacred text by its relationship to a community that treats it as holy or authoritative.

2. In Luke 1:3-4, the author names Theophilus and explains why he writes. Which two things does the passage reveal?

Why: Luke names his reader, Theophilus, and his purpose, that the reader may know the certainty of what he was taught.

3. What does the Hindu term shruti describe?

Why: Shruti means what was heard and refers to the Vedas; smriti, what was remembered, includes the epics.

4. Which practice is an example of oral tradition today?

Why: A hafiz has memorized the entire Qur'an, keeping the text through memory and recitation.

5. Why do Catholic and Protestant Old Testaments have different numbers of books?

Why: Catholics and Orthodox include books such as Tobit, Judith and Maccabees, which Protestants call the Apocrypha.

6. Psalm 23 says, The LORD is my shepherd. Which genre is this, and how should it be read?

Why: Psalm 23 is poetry, so the shepherd is an image of care, not a job description.

7. What did the Supreme Court allow in Abington School District v. Schempp (1963)?

Why: The Court struck down devotional readings but said objective study of religion is consistent with the First Amendment.

8. The KJV reads Thou shalt not kill; the 1917 JPS reads Thou shalt not murder. What does this show?

Why: The Hebrew verb has no exact English match, so each translation reflects an interpretive choice.

9. How did the Septuagint affect the reading of Isaiah 7:14?

Why: The Greek Septuagint rendered almah as parthenos, the form Matthew quotes, shaping Christian reading of the verse.

10. What is peshat in Jewish interpretation?

Why: Peshat is the plain meaning of a verse, as in the commentary of Rashi.

11. How did the rabbis of the Talmud interpret eye for eye in Exodus 21:24?

Why: The rabbis concluded that the verse requires monetary compensation for an injury.

12. Which group teaches sola scriptura, scripture alone, as the highest authority?

Why: Protestant reformers such as Luther taught sola scriptura; Catholics look to the magisterium alongside scripture.

13. What do Sikhs believe about the Guru Granth Sahib since 1708?

Why: Sikhs believe that Guru Gobind Singh ended the line of human Gurus and the scripture became the eternal Guru.

14. Which sentence follows the rules describe, attribute and do not rank?

Why: The first sentence attributes the teaching to Buddhists without judging or ranking.

15. What is one reason the 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago is remembered?

Why: The Parliament introduced many Americans to teachers such as Vivekananda and Dharmapala, though its pluralism was uneven.
