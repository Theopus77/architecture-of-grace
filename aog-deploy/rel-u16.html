The story

Eighteen Minutes Before Sunset

On a Friday in November, a Chicago family stops the week on purpose, and the house changes in a way you can hear.

It is 4:10 on a Friday afternoon in West Rogers Park, on Chicago's far North Side. Sunset will come at 4:28. In the Adler apartment the last twenty minutes are a scramble. A pot of chicken soup is moved to a warming tray. Two braided loaves of bread sit under a cloth. Noa, who is fifteen, is texting a friend about a group project while her younger brother hunts for his good shoes.

At 4:10 her mother calls from the dining room. Noa sends one last message, then puts her phone in the drawer of the hall table. Her brother's tablet goes in beside it. For the next twenty-five hours, until three stars appear on Saturday night, no one in the family will turn on a screen, drive a car, cook a meal or handle money. That is not a punishment. It is the point.

Her mother lights two candles, circles her hands over the flames, covers her eyes and says a blessing in Hebrew. The words thank God, who commanded the lighting of the Sabbath candles. When she uncovers her eyes, the Sabbath has begun. The apartment is suddenly quiet. Noa notices the sound of the radiator and the neighbor's television through the wall, sounds the week usually covers up.

Her father and brother walk four blocks to the synagogue for the short evening service. Noa and her mother set the table. When the men return, the family sings a song of welcome, her father raises a cup of wine and says a blessing over it, and everyone washes their hands and shares the bread. There is soup, roast chicken, a long dinner and no reason to hurry. A guest from her father's office, who is not Jewish, asks why the phones went in the drawer.

Noa's answer is the one her grandmother gave her. Six days a week, she says, we make things and buy things and fix things. On the seventh we stop, and we remember that the world does not depend on us. Across the city, her cousins in a Reform congregation in Hyde Park light the same candles, say the same blessing, and then drive to their temple. Same Sabbath, different customs. That range is part of what this chapter is about.

31.1

The Shema: Six Words

Main ideaThe Shema, a six-word line from Deuteronomy, states the core Jewish belief that God is one and is recited morning and night.

In a Jewish home a small case is often fixed to the doorpost, tilted slightly inward. It is called a , and inside is a tiny handwritten scroll. The first words on that scroll are the words a Jewish child learns to say before sleep. They are also the words many Jews hope to say at the moment of death. In Hebrew they begin Shema Yisrael, which means Hear, O Israel. Because of that first word, the whole prayer is called the .

The line comes from the Torah, in Deuteronomy 6:4. In the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation it reads: Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one. Six words in Hebrew carry the central claim of Judaism, which scholars call : there is one God, and only one. The verses that follow command Israel to love God with all its heart, soul and might, to teach these words to children, to bind them on the hand and forehead and to write them on the doorposts. The mezuzah and the small leather boxes called tefillin worn during weekday morning prayer come from a literal reading of those verses.

Observant Jews recite the Shema twice a day, in the morning and evening services, and again at bedtime. Many cover their eyes while saying the first line, to shut out distraction. Jewish tradition treats the Shema less as a prayer that asks for something and more as a declaration, a statement of who God is and to whom the speaker belongs. When historians want one sentence to sum up what Judaism teaches about God, this is usually the sentence they choose.

31.2

Covenant and Chosenness

Main ideaJews understand their relationship with God as a covenant, a binding two-way agreement made with Abraham and renewed at Sinai, that brings duties rather than privilege.

In the Book of Exodus, three months after leaving Egypt, the Israelites camp at the foot of Mount Sinai. Before the Ten Commandments are given, God tells Moses to make the people an offer. In the King James wording: if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto me above all people. The people answer that they will do all that the LORD has spoken. That exchange is the model for what Jewish tradition calls the , in Hebrew : a binding agreement with obligations on both sides.

The covenant did not start at Sinai. Genesis describes God making a covenant with Abraham, promising him descendants and a land, with circumcision as its sign. Later Jewish thought treats Sinai as the moment that covenant became the bond of a whole people, with the Torah as its terms. Israel promises to keep the commandments; God promises to be their God. Because the agreement is with a people and not only with individuals, Jews speak of being born into the covenant, and converts are described as joining it.

Out of this comes the idea often translated as . The phrase peculiar treasure in the old translation means a valued possession, not something odd. In Jewish teaching, being chosen means being chosen for a task: to receive the Torah and live by it. The prophet Amos even warns that being known by God brings greater accountability, not less. Jewish thinkers across the centuries have stressed that chosenness is not a claim to be better than other peoples. Many modern Jews, especially in the Reform and Reconstructionist movements, have debated or reworded the idea; Reconstructionist prayer books removed the language of chosenness altogether.

31.3

Mitzvot and Halakhah

Main ideaJewish tradition counts 613 commandments in the Torah, and halakhah is the body of law and custom that works out how to keep them.

Ask an observant Jew why the family does not cook on Saturday, or why there is a mezuzah on the door, and the answer is often a single word: it is a . The word literally means commandment. Jewish tradition holds that the Torah contains 613 mitzvot (the plural), a count first stated in the Talmud and later listed in detail by medieval scholars such as Maimonides. By the traditional count, 248 are positive commands (do this) and 365 are prohibitions (do not do this). Many concern the Temple in Jerusalem, which no longer stands, so far fewer than 613 can be practiced today.

The Torah’s commands are often brief. Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy, says Exodus, but it does not spell out what counts as work. Working that out is the job of , a word usually translated law but built on the Hebrew verb to walk: the way one walks, the path. Halakhah draws on the Torah, the discussions of the rabbis in the Talmud, later law codes and centuries of written questions and answers from rabbis called responsa. It covers prayer, food, business ethics, medical decisions and how to treat a stranger, not only ritual.

Jewish communities differ over how binding halakhah is. Orthodox Jews hold that it is divine law that can be interpreted but not set aside. Conservative Judaism treats halakhah as binding but historically developing, so its rabbis have ruled, for example, that driving to synagogue on the Sabbath may be permitted. Reform Judaism regards the ethical commandments as central and treats ritual laws as choices individuals make in an informed way. In everyday speech, though, mitzvah has a second, warmer meaning that every branch shares: a good deed. Visiting the sick, feeding the hungry and welcoming guests are each called a mitzvah.

The Sabbath

31.4

Welcoming Shabbat at Home

Main ideaThe Sabbath begins at home on Friday before sunset with candles, a blessing over wine and bread, and a festive meal.

The Jewish day runs from sunset to sunset, following Genesis, where each day of creation is counted as evening and then morning. So the seventh day, in Hebrew (Sabbath in English), begins on Friday evening. Custom fixes the start at eighteen minutes before sunset, which in Chicago means a little after 4:00 in December and after 8:00 in June. In traditional homes a woman of the household lights at least two candles and says a blessing. Once the candles are lit, the Sabbath has begun and its rules apply.

The Friday night meal is the center of the home celebration. It opens with , a blessing said over a cup of wine or grape juice that declares the day holy and recalls both creation and the exodus from Egypt. Then comes a blessing over two loaves of braided bread called . The two loaves remember the double portion of manna the Israelites gathered on Fridays in the wilderness, according to Exodus 16. In many families parents bless their children, and songs greet the Sabbath, which Jewish tradition pictures as a bride or a queen.

None of this requires a synagogue or a rabbi. The Sabbath table is a home ritual, led by family members, and that is one reason it has survived twenty centuries of exile and migration. The customs vary widely. Sephardi families from Spain, North Africa and the Middle East sing different melodies and serve different foods than Ashkenazi families from Central and Eastern Europe. Secular Israelis who never enter a synagogue may still gather for Friday dinner. The common thread is a meal set apart, at a set time, with words that mark it as different from the other six nights.

31.5

What Counts as Rest

Main ideaThe Torah forbids work on the Sabbath, and the rabbis defined work as thirty-nine categories of creative labor, which shapes what observant Jews do and avoid.

The fourth of the Ten Commandments reads, in the King James Version: Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days shalt thou labour, and do all thy work: But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work. The commandment appears twice in the Torah, with a different reason each time. Exodus 20 says God rested on the seventh day of creation. Deuteronomy 5 says Israel were slaves in Egypt and should remember it. Rest, in this reading, is both an imitation of God and a protection for workers.

But what is work? The Torah does not give a list. The Mishnah, edited around 200 CE, lists thirty-nine categories of forbidden labor, called . The Talmud explains that they are the kinds of work used to build the portable sanctuary in the wilderness, which the Torah describes right beside the Sabbath command. The list includes planting, baking, weaving, writing, building and kindling a fire. Later authorities extended the categories to new inventions: most Orthodox rabbis forbid turning on an electric light, many treating it as a form of kindling, which is why observant families set lights on timers before sunset.

This is why a Sabbath-observant Jew will carry a heavy book but not write a single word, or walk two miles to synagogue but not drive two blocks. The measure is not effort but creative change to the world. Jewish law also makes an exception that overrides nearly every Sabbath rule: , saving a life. A doctor may drive to the hospital and a person who is ill must eat. The Talmud puts it this way: the Sabbath was given to you, not you to the Sabbath. Reform and many Conservative Jews keep the day more selectively, choosing rest, family time and worship without the full list of prohibitions.

31.6

Synagogue, Torah and Havdalah

Main ideaSabbath worship centers on reading the Torah in the synagogue, and the day ends on Saturday night with the Havdalah ceremony.

On Saturday morning Jews gather in a , a word from Greek meaning a place of assembly. The building is not a temple; no sacrifices are offered. It is a house of prayer, study and community. At the front stands the ark, a cabinet holding the Torah scrolls, handwritten on parchment by a trained scribe. The main event of the morning is taking a scroll out, carrying it through the congregation and reading a portion aloud in Hebrew. The Torah is divided into weekly portions so that the whole of it is read over one year, ending and restarting on the holiday of Simchat Torah each fall.

Services follow a prayer book called the , from a Hebrew word for order. A rabbi, whose title means teacher, leads and gives a sermon, and a cantor may lead the singing, but any adult who knows the service can lead prayers. Practice varies by movement. In Orthodox synagogues men and women sit separately and the service is almost entirely in Hebrew. In Reform congregations, often called temples, families sit together, much is in English, and American Reform Judaism has ordained women as rabbis since 1972. Conservative synagogues fall between, mostly Hebrew with mixed seating and women fully included in most congregations.

The Sabbath does not end with sunset but with nightfall, when three stars are visible. A short home ceremony called , meaning separation, marks the change. A braided candle with several wicks is lit, wine is blessed, and a box of sweet spices is passed around and smelled, a comfort, tradition says, as the extra soul a person is given on the Sabbath departs. A final blessing praises God who separates holy from ordinary, light from dark, the seventh day from the six days of work. Then the candle is put out in the wine, the phones come out of the drawer, and the week begins.

31.7

A Calendar of Moon and Sun

Main ideaThe Jewish calendar follows the moon for months and the sun for years, adding a leap month seven times in nineteen years so holidays stay in their seasons.

Ask why Hanukkah fell in late November one year and late December another, and the answer is the Jewish calendar. Its months follow the moon. Each begins with the new moon and lasts 29 or 30 days. Twelve months therefore come to about 354 days, eleven short of the solar year. If nothing were done, Passover would drift backward through the seasons, as the months of the Islamic calendar do. But the Torah calls Passover a spring festival. So the calendar adds a thirteenth month, a second Adar, in seven years out of every nineteen. This makes it a calendar: moon for the months, sun for the years.

The rules were once decided by witnesses reporting the new moon to a court in Jerusalem, with messengers carrying the news. Communities far away could not be sure which day was correct, so they kept some holidays for two days to be safe. That is why Jews outside Israel traditionally keep eight days of Passover while Israel keeps seven. According to tradition, the sage Hillel II made a calculated calendar public in the fourth century CE, and it reached its present fixed form by about the tenth century. The year count begins from a traditional date for creation; the Jewish year that began in the fall of 2025 was 5786.

The months carry Babylonian names from the time of the exile: in the fall holds Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur and Sukkot; Kislev holds Hanukkah; Adar holds Purim; in the spring holds Passover. Every holiday, like the Sabbath, begins at sunset the evening before the date printed on a wall calendar. That is why a Jewish student may ask to leave school early on the day before a holiday. Knowing the calendar is the first step to understanding Jewish time.

31.8

The Days of Awe

Main ideaRosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur open the Jewish year with ten days of self-examination, repentance and a fast that Jewish tradition treats as the holiest day.

In early fall a curved ram’s horn called a is blown in synagogues around the world. Its raw, broken blasts open , the head of the year, on the first of Tishrei. The Torah names the day only as a day of blowing the horn. Later tradition made it the anniversary of creation and a day of judgment. In the imagery of the prayers, God opens a book and weighs each life. Families eat apples dipped in honey for a sweet year and round challah for the cycle of the year. On the afternoon of the first day many walk to a river or lake. There they symbolically cast away their sins by throwing bread crumbs on the water, a custom called tashlich.

The ten days that follow are called the Days of Awe, or the Ten Days of Repentance. The Hebrew word for repentance, , literally means return. Jewish teaching holds that Yom Kippur atones for wrongs against God, but wrongs against another person are forgiven only when that person is asked and forgives. So in these ten days people apologize, repay debts and make peace. The period ends on the tenth of Tishrei with , the Day of Atonement, which Leviticus 23 describes as a day to afflict your souls and do no work.

Yom Kippur is a full fast of about twenty-five hours: no food or drink from before sunset to after nightfall the next day, plus, by tradition, no bathing, leather shoes or perfume. The evening service opens with Kol Nidre, a legal formula sung to a haunting melody that cancels personal religious vows a person has made to God, not promises to other people. Much of the next day is spent in synagogue, confessing sins in the plural: we have sinned, we have betrayed. The final service ends with a long blast of the shofar. Even many Jews who rarely attend synagogue keep this day, and in Israel roads fall almost silent.

31.9

Sukkot, Hanukkah and Purim

Main ideaSukkot recalls the wilderness, Hanukkah the rededication of the Temple in 164 BCE, and Purim the rescue told in the Book of Esther.

Five days after Yom Kippur, small huts appear on porches and in backyards. This is , the Feast of Booths. The Torah connects it to the harvest and to the temporary shelters the Israelites lived in after leaving Egypt. Families eat, and some sleep, in a with a roof of branches thin enough to see the stars. Each day they hold together four plants named in Leviticus 23: a palm branch, myrtle, willow and a citron called an etrog. They wave them in every direction. Sukkot ends with Simchat Torah, when the year’s reading of the Torah finishes and starts over amid dancing with the scrolls.

, in Kislev, is the best-known Jewish holiday in America but a minor one in Jewish law. It remembers events of 164 BCE. Jewish fighters led by the Maccabees recaptured the Temple in Jerusalem from the Greek-ruled Seleucid kingdom, which had banned Jewish practice. They cleansed and rededicated it, and Hanukkah means dedication. The Talmud adds the story of a single day’s supply of pure oil that burned for eight days. So for eight nights Jews light a lamp with eight lights and a helper candle, adding one flame each night, eat foods fried in oil such as potato latkes and jelly doughnuts, and play with a spinning top called a dreidel. The lamp is placed in a window so that the miracle is publicized.

, in Adar, is the loudest day of the year. Its story is the Book of Esther, in which a Jewish queen in Persia and her cousin Mordecai foil a plot by the royal official Haman to destroy the Jews. Keeping Kosher

Main ideaKashrut, the Jewish dietary law, comes from the Torah's rules about which animals may be eaten and how, and Jews differ in how strictly they keep it.

Its rules begin in Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14. Land animals may be eaten only if they both chew the cud and have a split hoof, so cattle, sheep and goats are permitted but pigs, camels and rabbits are not. Fish must have fins and scales, which rules out shellfish. Birds of prey are forbidden; chicken, turkey and duck are allowed.

Permitted animals must be slaughtered in a specific way, called shechita. A trained person uses a very sharp knife in a single cut. Orthodox and many Conservative Jews keep kosher strictly. Some Jews avoid pork and shellfish but eat in ordinary restaurants. Reform Judaism leaves the choice to individuals, and a growing number of Jews of every kind speak of eco-kashrut, adding concerns about workers and the environment. Its example of the second is Korah, who in the Book of Numbers rebels against Moses to win power for himself. This value runs through Jewish learning. Main ideaLater scholars such as Rashi and Maimonides explained the Talmud and organized its law, and printing fixed the page students still use.

His comments define hard words, sometimes in Old French, and fill in steps the Talmud skips. Rashi’s grandsons and their students, known as the , meaning additions, went further. They raised questions and compared passages across the whole Talmud. Their comments became the two columns that frame the center of every page.

Others wanted a clear answer to what the law actually requires. (c. 1138 to 1204), born in Córdoba in Muslim Spain, completed the Mishneh Torah around 1180. It arranged all of Jewish law by subject in fourteen books. In the 1500s Joseph Karo, working in Safed in the land of Israel, wrote the , meaning set table, a practical guide based on Sephardi practice. Not every ruler welcomed the book. In Paris in 1242, after a public disputation put the Talmud on trial, officials burned thousands of handwritten volumes. Expulsions, Ghettos and the Shtetl

Main ideaIn medieval Christian Europe, Jews faced violence, false accusations and expulsions, and many moved east to Poland, where Jewish life grew and Hasidism began.

In 1096, bands on their way to the First Crusade attacked Jewish communities in the Rhineland towns of Speyer, Worms and Mainz. Many hundreds, and by some estimates thousands, were killed, and some chose death over forced baptism. Jewish chroniclers wrote down the names, and prayers remembering the dead are still said. Over the next centuries false charges led to more massacres. One was the , the invented claim that Jews killed Christian children for ritual use, first recorded in Norwich, England, in 1144. During the Black Death, around 1348, Jews were falsely blamed for poisoning wells.

Church and rulers also set legal limits. A church council in 1215 required Jews and Muslims to wear distinctive clothing. Jews were often barred from owning land or joining craft guilds, which pushed many into trade and moneylending. Then came , the forced removal of a whole community. England expelled its Jews in 1290 and France in 1306. In 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella ordered all Jews in Spain who would not convert to leave, and in 1497 Portugal forced most of its Jews to convert. Many Sephardi exiles found refuge in the Ottoman Empire, North Africa and later Amsterdam.

In 1516 the city of Venice required its Jews to live on a small island beside an old foundry, called a getto in the local dialect, whose gates were locked at night. The word spread across Europe for closed Jewish quarters. Meanwhile many Ashkenazi Jews moved east to Poland and Lithuania, where kings invited them and granted charters. By the 1700s this region held the largest Jewish population in the world, much of it in small market towns, each called a in Yiddish. There, in the mid-1700s, a teacher known as the Baal Shem Tov inspired , a movement stressing joyful prayer and heartfelt devotion, not scholarship alone.

Citizenship and the Movements

Main ideaAs European Jews gained citizenship in the 1700s and 1800s, they answered modern life differently, forming the Reform, Orthodox, Conservative and Reconstructionist movements.

In 1783 Moses Mendelssohn, a Jewish philosopher in Berlin, finished a translation of the Torah into German, printed in Hebrew letters. Jews could learn the language of their neighbors through their own sacred text. Mendelssohn became a symbol of the , the Jewish Enlightenment, which urged Jews to study science and European languages alongside Torah. At the same time came , the granting of equal citizenship. France was the first European country to grant it, in 1791, and others followed slowly. For the first time Jews could enter universities, professions and politics, and each had to decide what Jewish life should look like.

Reform Judaism began in Germany in the early 1800s. Its founders shortened services, added prayers and sermons in German and organ music, and taught that Jewish law should change with the times while its ethical core stayed. In the United States, Isaac Mayer Wise founded Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati in 1875 to train Reform rabbis. In response, leaders who held that the Torah and halakhah are unchanging divine law came to be called Orthodox. Samson Raphael Hirsch in Germany taught that faithful Jews could keep the whole law and also take part in modern culture.

Others sought a middle path. Conservative Judaism, rooted in the work of Zacharias Frankel in Germany, held that Jewish law is binding but has always developed through history. In America it grew around the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York, founded in 1886. In the twentieth century Rabbi Mordecai Kaplan described Judaism as an evolving religious civilization, and his followers founded Judaism. In Eastern Europe and later in America and Israel, Hasidic groups kept their own path under a rebbe. Each , or organized movement, differs on law, worship and the roles of women and men. Yet most Jews recognize one another as one people.

The Holocaust

Main ideaBetween 1933 and 1945 Nazi Germany and its collaborators carried out the Holocaust, the systematic murder of about six million Jews, which Jews also call the Shoah.

At the Illinois Holocaust Museum in Skokie, visitors pass a German railroad car of the kind used to deport Jews to killing centers. The event they described is called the , from a Greek word for a sacrifice burned whole. Many Jews prefer the Hebrew word , meaning catastrophe.

The Nazi Party took power in Germany in 1933 and quickly pushed Jews out of public life. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped German Jews of citizenship. On November 9 and 10, 1938, in attacks called , mobs burned synagogues and smashed Jewish shops across Germany and Austria. Main ideaModern Zionism led to the founding of Israel in 1948; today nearly half of the world's Jews live there and the rest in the diaspora, with a wide range of views.
