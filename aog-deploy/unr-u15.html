Sources

This course has met the Watchers, the giants, the gods of the nations and the council of heaven. Where do those ideas come from? This unit goes to the sources. It opens the ancient documents themselves: 1 Enoch and Jubilees, the Dead Sea Scrolls, Josephus and Philo, the tablets of Ugarit and the traditions of Mesopotamia. Then it listens to the researchers who have spent their careers reading them.

The first chapter reads the documents. You will see what 1 Enoch 6–16 says about the Watchers on Mount Hermon, how Jubilees retells the story with Mastema and his spirits, and what the broken Book of Giants adds. You will also learn Samuel Sandmel's warning against overstating parallels.

The story

The Plow That Opened a Library

In 1928 a farmer in northern Syria struck a stone, and a lost city began to speak again.

In the spring of 1928 a farmer was plowing a field near the Mediterranean coast of northern Syria, close to a bay called Minet el-Beida. His plow struck something hard. When he dug around it, he found a large stone slab. Under the slab was an old stone-built tomb, with pottery still inside. Local officials reported the find, and word reached archaeologists working for France, which governed Syria at the time.

The next year a French team led by Claude Schaeffer began digging at a mound nearby called Ras Shamra. Within weeks they found clay tablets covered in wedge-shaped marks. Some used the Akkadian script scholars already knew. Others used a script no one had seen before. It had only about thirty signs, which meant it was probably an alphabet.

By 1930 and 1931 several scholars had deciphered it. The language was close to Hebrew and was named Ugaritic, after the city. The mound was ancient Ugarit, a trading port destroyed around 1185 BCE. Its scribes had left letters, business records and long poems about gods. The poems told of a high god named El, who sat at the head of an assembly of gods called his sons.

Ugarit is only one of the libraries this chapter opens. Nineteen years after the plow struck stone, shepherds near the Dead Sea found the first of the Dead Sea Scrolls. Among them were Aramaic copies of the book of 1 Enoch, pieces of a lost Book of Giants, and a retelling of Genesis. Older still, Greek-speaking Jewish writers such as Philo and Josephus had written about the same stories.

This chapter reads those documents first, in their own words or in close paraphrase. It asks what each one says about the Watchers, the giants, the spirits and the council of heaven. Only after that does it turn to what researchers have made of them. The next chapter follows the researchers themselves.

The Books of Enoch

29.1

Five Books in One

Main idea1 Enoch is a collection of five Jewish writings from about 300 BCE to the first century CE, kept whole in Ge'ez and found in part among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Genesis 5:21–24 says only a little about Enoch. He was the seventh in the line from Adam, he walked with God, and he was not, for God took him. Jewish writers in the centuries before Jesus filled that silence. They wrote books in Enoch’s name about what he saw in heaven and what he learned about the world. Scholars call the collection .

1 Enoch has five main parts. The Book of the Watchers (chapters 1–36) tells of the angels who came down to earth. The Parables, or Similitudes (37–71), describe a heavenly figure called the Elect One and the Son of Man. The Astronomical Book (72–82) lays out a calendar of the sun and moon. The Dream Visions (83–90) retell history as animals in a dream. The Epistle of Enoch (91–108) gives teaching and warnings.

The whole book survives only in Ge&#x27;ez, the old church language of Ethiopia. It is part of the Bible of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches. In 1773 the Scottish traveler James Bruce brought Ge’ez copies to Europe. Parts also survive in Greek. Then, from 1947 on, Aramaic fragments were found in the caves at Qumran, showing that the book was being copied before the time of Jesus.

This course quotes 1 Enoch from the English translation by R. H. Charles, reissued in a small edition in 1917 and now in the public domain. Modern translations, such as the one by George Nickelsburg and James VanderKam (2004), are more exact but still under copyright, so they are described rather than quoted.

29.2

The Watchers on Mount Hermon

Main idea1 Enoch 6–11 tells how two hundred Watchers swore an oath on Mount Hermon, took human wives, fathered giants and taught forbidden skills, until God sent his angels to act.

1 Enoch 6 begins where Genesis 6:1–2 begins. People multiplied and beautiful daughters were born to them. Then the angels, the children of heaven, saw them and desired them. Their leader, whom Charles spells Semjaza, feared he alone would pay for the plan. So all of them swore an oath together. The text says there were two hundred, and they came down in the days of Jared on the summit of Mount .

Chapter 7 says the took wives and taught them charms and the cutting of roots and plants. The women bore great giants. The giants ate up all that people had grown, and then turned to violence against people and animals. Chapter 8 lists what one Watcher, , taught: how to make swords, knives, shields and armor, and how to make ornaments, bracelets and eye makeup. Other Watchers taught the signs of the stars and moon.

Chapters 9 and 10 turn to heaven. The archangels Michael, Uriel, Raphael and Gabriel look down and bring the cry of the earth before God. God sends a messenger to warn Noah of the flood. Raphael binds Azazel and places him in darkness. Gabriel sets the giants against one another. Michael binds Semjaza and his companions in the valleys of the earth until the day of judgment. Then the earth is cleansed and renewed.

Readers notice that this story is much longer than Genesis 6:1–4, which does not name the angels, count them, or mention Hermon. Many scholars also see two threads woven together here: one about Semjaza and the oath, and one about Azazel and his teaching. Chapter 30 looks at how researchers explain that.

29.3

Enoch's Errand and the Spirits

Main ideaIn 1 Enoch 12–16 Enoch carries a message of judgment to the Watchers, and God's answer says the spirits of the giants will remain on earth as evil spirits.

In 1 Enoch 12, Enoch is called a scribe of righteousness. Holy angels send him to tell the Watchers that they will have no peace. The frightened Watchers ask Enoch to write a petition for them and carry it to God. Enoch reads it by the waters of Dan, south-west of Hermon, and falls asleep. In a vision he is taken up to a house of crystal and fire, where the Great Glory sits on a throne (chapter 14).

The answer comes in chapter 15. God tells Enoch to say to the Watchers that they should pray for humans, not humans for them. They were holy, spiritual and living forever, yet they left heaven and took wives. Then comes a claim about the giants. Because the giants were born of spirits and flesh, their spirits will be called on the earth, and the earth will be their dwelling (15:8–10).

Chapter 16 adds that these spirits will go on troubling people, without being judged, until the day of the great judgment. This is one of the oldest Jewish explanations of where harmful spirits came from. It is a story about the origin of evil in the world, told with the language of angels and giants. It says nothing about any particular person being troubled by a spirit.

Researchers read this passage closely. Researchers such as Archie Wright and Loren Stuckenbruck have asked how it shaped later Jewish and Christian ideas of . Their readings are the subject of the next chapter. Here the first step is simply to know what chapter 15 says.

Jubilees and the Scrolls

29.4

Jubilees: Teachers Who Fell

Main ideaJubilees retells Genesis and says the Watchers were first sent down to teach justice, and only later sinned with human women.

The Book of retells Genesis and the start of Exodus as a revelation to Moses on Mount Sinai. It arranges history in jubilees, periods of forty-nine years. Most scholars date it to the middle of the second century BCE. Over a dozen Hebrew copies were found at Qumran, and the full text survives in Ge’ez. Like 1 Enoch, it is part of the Ethiopian Orthodox Bible. This course uses R. H. Charles’s translation (1917).

Jubilees 4:15 explains the name Jared. In his days the angels of the Lord, called the Watchers, came down to earth. The text says they were sent to teach humans and to do judgment and uprightness on the earth. That is a different beginning from 1 Enoch 6, where the Watchers come down already planning to take wives.

Jubilees 4:17–22 then praises Enoch. He was the first among humans to learn writing, knowledge and wisdom, and he wrote down the signs of heaven. He was with the angels for six jubilees of years. He testified against the Watchers, who sinned with the daughters of men. In Jubilees 5:1–11 the Watchers take wives, the giants are born, and wrongdoing spreads. God binds the Watchers in the depths of the earth and sends a sword among the giants, so that they destroy one another.

Putting the two books side by side shows how one story can be retold. Both describe a fall of heavenly beings and the birth of giants. But Jubilees stresses that the Watchers’ mission began well. Readers often note that this makes the fall a turning away from a good task, not a plan from the start.

29.5

Mastema Asks for a Tenth

Main ideaJubilees 10:1–14 tells how evil spirits troubled Noah's grandchildren, Noah prayed, and God bound nine parts of them, leaving one tenth under a leader called Mastema.

Jubilees 10 is set after the flood. The text says unclean began to lead Noah’s grandchildren astray. Noah prays to God to shut them away, so that they cannot rule over the spirits of the living. He reminds God that these spirits are the offspring of the Watchers. God tells the angels to bind all of them.

Then a new figure speaks. , the chief of the spirits, asks God to leave some of them with him. He says that without them he cannot carry out his work among humans, because human wickedness is great. God agrees to leave one tenth of the spirits with Mastema and to send nine parts to the place of judgment. The chapter ends with Noah writing down what the angels taught him and giving his book to his son Shem.

The name Mastema is related to a Hebrew word for hostility. In Jubilees he acts somewhat like the figure of Job 1–2: a being who tests and accuses, but only with God’s permission. Jubilees also gives him roles in later stories, such as testing Abraham. Readers see in this chapter an early attempt to explain why evil still exists after the flood, and why it has limits.

29.6

The Book of Giants

Main ideaFragments of an Aramaic Book of Giants, identified at Qumran by J. T. Milik and edited by Loren Stuckenbruck, tell the story from the giants' side.

Among the Qumran fragments, scholars found pieces of an Aramaic book that tells the Watchers story from the point of view of the giants. It is now called the . The fragments are small and broken. Even so, they show two giants, Ohyah and Hahyah, troubled by dreams. In one dream, gardeners water a garden of trees, and then water and fire destroy it. The giants send one of their number, Mahway, to ask Enoch what the dreams mean.

The fragments also name , the hero of the Mesopotamian epic, as one of the giants. That surprised scholars. It shows that Jewish writers in this period knew some Mesopotamian names and put them into their own story. A later version of the Book of Giants was used by the teacher Mani in the third century CE. Pieces of it were found in Central Asia, at Turfan, in the early 1900s.

J. T. Milik published Aramaic Enoch fragments in The Books of Enoch: Aramaic Fragments of Qumrân Cave 4 (1976). He identified the Qumran giants fragments with the book Mani’s followers used. Loren Stuckenbruck later edited all the fragments with a translation and commentary in The Book of Giants from Qumran (1997). Because the fragments are broken, both scholars stressed that the order of scenes is partly a reconstruction.

29.7

Two Scroll Echoes

Main ideaThe Damascus Document names the Watchers who fell as a warning, and the Genesis Apocryphon imagines Lamech fearing that his son Noah was a Watcher's child.

The was first found not at Qumran but in the storeroom of a Cairo synagogue, the Cairo Genizah. Solomon Schechter published it in 1910. Later, copies turned up in the Qumran caves. It gives rules and history for a Jewish community. In column 2 it warns its readers to walk in God’s ways, and gives examples of those who did not.

The first example is the Watchers of heaven. The document says they fell because they walked in the stubbornness of their hearts and did not keep God’s commandments. Their sons, it says, were as tall as cedars, with bodies like mountains, and they fell too. Here the Watchers story is not told at length. It is used in a short list of warnings, which shows the readers already knew it.

The , found in Cave 1 in 1947 and first published in 1956, retells Genesis in Aramaic. In one scene, Lamech sees his newborn son Noah and fears the child’s father was one of the Watchers or holy ones. His wife, Bitenosh, swears that the child is his. Lamech still goes to his father Methuselah, who travels to Enoch to ask. 1 Enoch 106–107 tells a similar story about Noah’s birth.

29.8

Josephus on Angels and Women

Main ideaJosephus, writing in Rome around 93 CE, retold Genesis 6 for Greek readers and said that many angels of God joined with women and fathered sons like the Greek giants.

was a Jewish priest and historian born in Jerusalem around 37 CE. After the Jewish war with Rome, he lived in Rome and wrote in Greek. His Jewish Antiquities, finished around 93 CE, retells Israel’s history for Greek and Roman readers. William Whiston’s English translation (1737) is in the public domain.

In Antiquities 1.73 (book 1, chapter 3 in Whiston), Josephus tells what happened after the generations of Seth. In Whiston’s words, many angels of God accompanied with women. Their sons, he says, were unjust and scorned what was good, trusting their own strength. He adds that the tradition says these men did things like the acts of those the Greeks call giants.

Josephus stands at an interesting point. He reads the sons of God of Genesis 6 as angels, as 1 Enoch and Jubilees do. But he does not tell the longer story of the Watchers, Hermon or the spirits. He also compares the sons to the giants of Greek stories, so his readers would understand. Scholars note that this comparison is Josephus’s own bridge between two cultures, not a claim that the stories are the same.

29.9

Philo, On the Giants

Main ideaPhilo of Alexandria read the angels of Genesis 6 as real souls in the air, but he also read the passage as an allegory about the soul and its choices.

was a Jewish thinker in Alexandria, Egypt, who lived from about 20 BCE to about 50 CE. He wrote in Greek and read the Bible in the Greek translation called the . He wrote a short treatise on Genesis 6:1–4 called On the Giants. C. D. Yonge’s English translation (1854–55) is in the public domain.

The Greek text Philo quoted reads angels of God in Genesis 6:2, not sons of God. The Septuagint also renders the Hebrew Nephilim of verse 4 with the Greek word gigantes, giants. Philo says that the beings other philosophers call daimones, Moses calls angels. They are souls that live in the air. Some serve God as messengers. Others, he says, are drawn down toward the body.

Philo then reads the passage as an , a story whose parts stand for ideas. In his reading, the giants stand for people who live only for bodily pleasure, the earth-born. Others are born of heaven, living by the mind, or born of God, like priests and prophets. So Philo holds two readings at once: angels exist, and the story also teaches about the soul. Many ancient readers of the Bible did the same.

29.10

The Council of El

Main ideaUgaritic poems from about 1400–1185 BCE picture El leading an assembly of the gods, and scholars compare that picture with Psalm 82 and Psalm 29.

Most tablets from date from about 1400 to 1185 BCE, centuries before most dates scholars give for the written books of the Hebrew Bible. In the poems, El is the aged king and father of the gods. The gods gather around him in an assembly. The tablets use phrases meaning the assembly of the gods and the circle of the sons of El. Scholars call such a gathering a . One poem also mentions the seventy sons of Athirat, El’s wife.

Psalm 82:1, in the King James Version, reads: God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods. The Hebrew for congregation of the mighty is adat el, which can be translated the council of El. Beware of Parallelomania

Main ideaSamuel Sandmel coined parallelomania in 1961 for overstating likenesses between texts, a warning that applies to every comparison in this chapter.

In December 1961 Samuel Sandmel, a Jewish scholar of the New Testament and early Judaism, gave the presidential address to the Society of Biblical Literature. It was published the next year under the title Parallelomania. He used the word to name a habit in scholarship: finding a likeness between two texts, overstating it, and then claiming one copied the other, as if the direction of borrowing were obvious.

Sandmel did not say comparison is useless. He said a likeness is only the start of the work. A shared image might come from a shared culture, from a common older source, or from chance. And the same phrase can mean something different in each text. This chapter has placed many texts side by side: Enoch and Jubilees, Genesis and Josephus, Ugarit and the Psalms, the apkallu and the Watchers. Each pairing needs that care.

The caution also guards against sensational claims. Some popular books say Mesopotamian carvings show ancient astronauts, or that giants built the pyramids. The texts say nothing of the kind. Researchers of many views reject such claims. Michael Heiser, for example, publicly challenged Zecharia Sitchin, arguing that Sitchin’s readings did not match the actual Sumerian and Akkadian words.

From Ugarit to the Psalms

Main ideaScholars such as Frank Moore Cross and E. Theodore Mullen studied the divine council decades before popular books made it widely known.

30.4

Main ideaJ. T. Milik's 1976 edition of the Aramaic Enoch fragments showed that most of 1 Enoch was being copied by about 200 BCE, though not the Parables.

30.9

Two Powers in Heaven

Main ideaAlan Segal (1977) studied rabbinic warnings against belief in two powers in heaven, and Heiser argued that the pattern behind that belief goes back to the Hebrew Bible.

Daniel 7:9–14 describes a throne scene. The Ancient of Days takes his seat, and one like a son of man comes with the clouds of heaven and receives everlasting rule. In Exodus 3 the angel of the LORD appears in the bush, and then God speaks from it. In Exodus 23:20–21 God says his name is in the angel he sends. Ancient readers asked how these figures relate to God.

In 1977 Alan Segal published Two Powers in Heaven: Early Rabbinic Reports about Christianity and Gnosticism. He studied rabbinic passages that warn against people who say there are two powers in heaven. Segal argued that the idea was older than the warnings. It grew from how some Jews read passages like Daniel 7, and the rabbis came to treat it as a serious error.

Heiser built on Segal in his dissertation (2004) and The Unseen Realm (2015). He argued that the Hebrew Bible itself has a visible and an invisible LORD figure, and that early Christians read Jesus into that pattern. Rabbinic Judaism reads these passages differently. The , Deuteronomy 6:4, declares that the LORD is one. The angel of the LORD is read as a created messenger speaking in God’s name.

30.10

A Framework and Its Critics

Main ideaHeiser joined Genesis 3, 6 and 11 into three rebellions and a Deuteronomy 32 worldview; readers find it clarifying or too tightly systematized.

Genesis 3 tells of the serpent in Eden. Genesis 6:1–4 tells of the sons of God and the daughters of men. Genesis 11 tells of Babel and the scattering of the nations. In the Bible these are separate stories. Heiser, in The Unseen Realm (2015), read them as three rebellions: in Eden, before the flood, and at Babel.

He tied the third to Deuteronomy 32:8–9, read with the Qumran scroll. In his view, after Babel God placed the nations under members of his council and kept Israel as his own. Psalm 82 then shows those rulers judged for ruling unjustly. Heiser called this the . He used it to read Daniel 10’s princes of Persia and Greece and Paul’s language of rulers and authorities.

Other readers raise questions. Genesis does not call Babel a rebellion in heaven, and Deuteronomy 32:8 does not say the sons of God rebelled. Psalm 82 does not say when their wrongdoing began. Some reviewers say the framework joins texts from different books and centuries into a tighter system than any one of them states. Supporters reply that it explains passages that otherwise seem scattered. Christian tradition has more often spoken of one fall of angels and one fall of humanity.

30.11

Jewish Readings Through Time

Main ideaJewish readers of Genesis 6 moved from angels in 1 Enoch, Jubilees and Philo to rulers and judges in the Targum, Genesis Rabbah and Rashi.

There is no single Jewish view of Genesis 6. Books written by Jews in the Second Temple period, such as 1 Enoch and Jubilees, read the sons of God as angels. So did Philo and Josephus in the first century CE. Some Dead Sea Scrolls, such as the Damascus Document, speak of the Watchers who fell.

Later rabbinic tradition mostly took another path. The Onkelos, an Aramaic translation of the Torah, renders sons of God in Genesis 6:2 as sons of the great ones, meaning nobles. The Genesis Rabbah reads it as sons of judges. Rashi, the great commentator of the eleventh century, explains them as sons of princes and judges. In this line, the wrong in Genesis 6 is the abuse of power by the mighty.

Jewish tradition still has a heavenly court. Genesis Rabbah, on Genesis 1:26, pictures God consulting the ministering angels before creating humans. What rabbinic Judaism rejects is a second being sharing God’s rule. Today Jewish communities read these passages in many ways. A fair study notes the variety and does not treat any one period as the whole of Jewish reading.

30.12

Claim, Evidence, Counter-Evidence

Main ideaA fair reader states each claim plainly, lists its evidence and its hardest counter-evidence, and sorts what is widely agreed from what is still debated.

Every interpretation makes a . The claim should fit in one sentence so it can be tested: The gods in Psalm 82 are members of a heavenly council. Or: The gods in Psalm 82 are human judges. Next comes : a verse, a word, a manuscript, a parallel. For the council reading, evidence includes adat el in verse 1, the Ugaritic parallels and die like men in verse 7.

Then comes , the facts hardest for the claim to explain. For the council reading, a reader might cite John 10:34–35, where Jesus speaks of those to whom the word of God came. For the human-judges reading, the council setting and die like men are the hardest facts. Stating the case against your own view is not weakness. It makes a conclusion trustworthy. Some teachers call this .

Last comes sorting. Widely agreed: the Bible has council scenes; the Qumran scroll reads sons of God in Deuteronomy 32:8; Jude 14–15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9; most of 1 Enoch is older than 150 BCE. Still debated: how monotheism developed, where demons come from, the three rebellions, the date of the Parables. Keeping the lists apart prevents a common mistake: treating a debated claim as settled, or a settled one as mere opinion.
