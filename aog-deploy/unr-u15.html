How Heiser Read the Bible

Method

Michael Heiser's books make big claims about the Bible's unseen world: a council of heavenly beings, gods placed over the nations, Watchers and giants. This unit steps back from those claims to ask how he reached them. What tools did he use? Which texts did he read, and in which languages? And what do other scholars say when they check his work?

The first chapter looks at his method. Heiser tried to read the Bible in its ancient setting, beside texts from its neighbors. Those neighbors include the clay tablets of Ugarit, found after a farmer's plow struck a tomb in 1928, and the Mesopotamian stories of the apkallu sages. You will see what those comparisons show. You will also see the cautions scholars attach to them, including Samuel Sandmel's warning against parallelomania.

The second chapter maps where Heiser's ideas stand. Some are widely shared, like the divine council and the Dead Sea Scroll reading of Deuteronomy 32:8. Others are debated, like his account of monotheism and his three rebellions. You will hear Jewish and Christian readings that differ from his. By the end you will be able to state an argument fairly, weigh its evidence and counter-evidence, and tell a settled claim from an open question.

The big question

How did Michael Heiser read the Bible, and how can a careful student test his readings against the texts and other scholars?

The story

The Plow That Opened a Library

In 1928 a farmer in northern Syria struck a stone, and a lost city began to speak again.

In the spring of 1928 a farmer was plowing a field near the Mediterranean coast of northern Syria, close to a bay called Minet el-Beida. His plow struck something hard. When he dug around it, he found a large stone slab. Under the slab was a stone-built tomb, very old, with pottery still inside. Local officials reported the find, and word reached archaeologists working for France, which governed Syria at the time.

The next year a French team led by Claude Schaeffer began digging at a mound nearby called Ras Shamra. Within weeks they found clay tablets covered in wedge-shaped marks. Some were written in the Akkadian script scholars already knew. Others used a script no one had seen before. It had only about thirty signs, which meant it was probably an alphabet.

Scholars worked on the new script quickly. By 1930 and 1931 several of them had deciphered it. The language was close to Hebrew and was named Ugaritic, after the city: the mound was ancient Ugarit, a trading port destroyed around 1185 BCE. Its scribes had written letters, business records, and long poems about gods.

Those poems named a high god called El, who sat at the head of an assembly of gods. They named El's sons, and a storm god named Baal who rode the clouds. Readers of the Hebrew Bible noticed at once that some of these words and pictures also appear in the Psalms. The Bible now had neighbors that could be read in their own words.

Decades later, Michael Heiser, a scholar of the Hebrew Bible and its related languages, made texts like these a central tool of his method. He argued that the Bible makes the most sense when read beside the world its first hearers lived in. This chapter looks at that method, its main tools, and the cautions that come with it.

Section 1

Reading in Context

29.1

The Ancient Context First

Main ideaHeiser's method was to read each biblical text in its ancient setting before asking what later traditions or modern readers made of it.

Open to Psalm 82. The first verse pictures God standing in a gathering and judging among beings the psalm calls gods. Many modern readers find the verse puzzling. It does not fit what they expect a psalm to say. Heiser said that this puzzle was exactly where careful reading should begin. He urged readers not to smooth the verse over, but to ask what it would have meant to an Israelite who heard it sung.

Heiser called this reading in . By context he meant the world of the writer and first audience: their language, their neighbors, their ideas about the heavens, and the other texts they knew. In The Unseen Realm (2015) he argued that many readers bring a filter to the Bible without noticing it. Some filters come from later church tradition. Others come from modern assumptions, such as the idea that ancient people must have thought about the world the way people do today.

Heiser did not say tradition is worthless. He said tradition should be tested against the text rather than read into it. This order of questions sits at the center of his method. First, what did the words mean in their own time? Only then, how have Jewish and Christian readers explained them since? Other scholars share this starting point. Most modern historical study of the Bible begins with the ancient setting.

Readers differ on how far to take this. Many Christians and Jews treat their long traditions of interpretation as trusted guides, not filters. They see the creeds, the rabbis or the church fathers as the community’s best reading of the text. Heiser’s approach is one voice in that conversation. It is the subject of this course, but it is not the only way people read.

29.2

The Naked Bible Idea

Main ideaHeiser used the name Naked Bible for his aim of reading Scripture stripped of later filters, and he named his long-running podcast after it.

From 2014 to 2023 Heiser hosted the Naked Bible Podcast. Episodes often worked slowly through one book of the Bible, sometimes a few verses at a time. The name described his goal. He wanted to read the Bible stripped of the layers readers had wrapped around it, so that its own words and its own ancient world could be seen clearly.

Take Job 1:6. It tells of a day when the sons of God came to present themselves before the LORD, and the satan came among them. Many readers picture the devil at a church meeting. Heiser asked readers to set that picture aside for a moment. In the Hebrew text, he pointed out, the word satan has the definite article, the satan, so it reads like a title meaning the adversary or accuser. The scene looks like a royal court, with members reporting to their king.

The phrase sons of God in Job is an example of what Heiser meant by letting the text speak. In Job 38:7 the sons of God shout for joy when the earth is founded, before humans exist. So, Heiser argued, in Job the phrase must name heavenly beings. Seeing this, he said, helps a reader notice the same phrase elsewhere, including Genesis 6:2.

A fair reader will notice a limit to the Naked Bible idea. No one reads with no filters at all. Every reader brings a language, a translation and questions. Heiser’s critics and friends alike have said so. The idea is best taken as a direction to work in, checking one’s own assumptions, rather than a claim that any reader can reach a perfectly bare text.

29.3

Languages as Tools

Main ideaHeiser was trained in Hebrew and related ancient languages, and he used them to notice words that translations can hide.

Heiser earned his PhD in Hebrew Bible and Semitic Studies at the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 2004. Semitic languages are a family that includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Akkadian, Ugaritic and Arabic. Knowing several of them lets a scholar compare how a word is used across neighboring peoples. His dissertation studied the divine council in Jewish writings from the Second Temple period, roughly 500 BCE to 70 CE.

Here is one reason languages matter. Psalm 29:1 in the King James Version begins, Give unto the LORD, O ye mighty. The Hebrew behind O ye mighty is bene elim, which can be read as sons of gods or heavenly beings. Some modern translations render it heavenly beings or divine beings. The English word mighty is not wrong, but it hides a connection that a Hebrew reader would hear at once.

A always involves choices. Translators must decide whether to keep a strange phrase or make it smoother. Heiser often told listeners to check more than one English translation. He also encouraged them to use reference tools that show the Hebrew or Greek word behind the English. Much of his career was spent at Logos, a company that makes Bible study software, working on such tools.

Languages do not settle every question. Two experts can read the same Hebrew word and disagree. What language study does is show the range of what a word could mean. It also shows which meanings are likely in a given setting. Heiser’s arguments often begin there, and so do his critics’ replies.

Section 2

The Library of Ugarit

29.4

A City Found by a Plow

Main ideaUgarit, a port city in Syria destroyed around 1185 BCE, left clay tablets in a Hebrew-like language that scholars use to understand the Bible's world.

Ugarit stood on the coast of what is now northern Syria, near the modern city of Latakia. It was a busy trading port during the Late Bronze Age. Ships and caravans linked it to Egypt, Cyprus, the Hittites and Mesopotamia. Around 1185 BCE the city was destroyed and never rebuilt. Its ruins became the mound called .

Excavations that began in 1929 uncovered palaces, temples and houses full of tablets. Scribes had written in several languages. The most important new one was , written in a wedge-shaped alphabet of about thirty letters pressed into clay. Most of the tablets date from roughly 1400 to 1185 BCE. That makes them centuries older than most scholars’ dates for the written books of the Hebrew Bible.

Ugaritic is a close relative of Hebrew. Many words are nearly the same, and poems in both languages use similar patterns, such as saying one thing twice in slightly different words. Because of this, the tablets became a major tool for Hebrew Bible scholars. They help explain rare Hebrew words and poetic habits.

Ugarit was not Israel, and its people were not Israelites. Scholars use it as a window onto the wider Canaanite world that Israel’s writers knew and often argued against. Heiser used it that way too. He said the tablets show the religious vocabulary of the region, which helps a reader notice when a biblical writer uses that vocabulary on purpose.

29.5

El and His Council

Main ideaUgaritic poems picture the high god El leading an assembly of gods called his sons, a picture Heiser compared to the Bible's divine council.

In the Ugaritic poems, is the aged, wise king of the gods. He is called father and creator, and he lives at a distant mountain where the gods come to meet him. The gods around him form an assembly. The tablets use phrases that mean the assembly of the gods and the assembly of the sons of El. Scholars call this kind of gathering a .

One poem refers to the seventy sons of Athirat, El’s wife. Heiser, like several scholars before him, noticed the number. Genesis 10 lists the nations after the flood, and Jewish tradition counts them as seventy. Deuteronomy 32:8, in its oldest known Hebrew copy, says God divided the nations according to the number of the sons of God. Heiser argued that a reader in Israel’s world would hear these ideas together.

Heiser did not say that Israel borrowed its beliefs from Ugarit. He argued that the Bible’s writers shared a vocabulary with their neighbors, like a family of languages sharing words. Then, he said, they used it to make their own claims. In the Hebrew Bible, the council belongs to Israel’s God, and its members are his creations.

Other scholars read the parallels differently. Mark S. Smith, for example, has argued that Israel’s early religion grew out of this Canaanite setting, and that El and Yahweh were once thought of as separate gods before being joined. Chapter 30 looks at that debate. For now, the point is shared ground: most scholars agree the council idea appears in both Ugarit and the Bible.

29.6

Psalm 82 Beside Ugarit

Main ideaHeiser read Psalm 82 as Israel's God judging the members of his council, while other readers take the gods there to be human judges or Israel at Sinai.

Psalm 82:1 in Hebrew says God stands in the adat el, which can be translated the council of El or the divine council. The phrase closely matches Ugaritic phrases for the assembly of the gods. The psalm goes on to accuse the gods of judging unjustly and failing the weak and the orphan. Then God pronounces a sentence: though they are gods and sons of the Most High, they will die like humans.

Heiser argued that the gods in Psalm 82 are real members of a heavenly council. In his reading, God had placed them over the nations, and they ruled badly. This, he said, ties Psalm 82 to Deuteronomy 32:8. He made this one of the anchor texts of The Unseen Realm (2015). He pointed out that the setting is a council like the one known from Ugarit, and that the sentence to die like humans makes the most sense if they are not human.

Other readings have a long history. Some Jewish and Christian interpreters read the gods as human judges, since judges act in God’s name. When Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6 in John 10:34, some readers take him to mean human leaders. An old rabbinic reading applies the psalm to Israel at Mount Sinai. Many modern scholars, including some who disagree with Heiser elsewhere, share his view that the psalm describes divine beings.

Setting Psalm 82 beside Ugarit does not decide the question alone. It shows that the phrase council of El had a known meaning in the region. Readers still must weigh the rest of the psalm, John 10, and how their own tradition has read it. A parallel opens the question; it does not close it.

29.7

Psalm 29 and the Storm

Main ideaMany scholars think Psalm 29 uses the language of Canaanite storm poems, and Heiser saw such borrowings as claims that Israel's God held the titles given to Baal.

Psalm 29 describes a great storm. The voice of the LORD is over the waters. It thunders, breaks the cedars of Lebanon, and shakes the wilderness. The psalm begins by calling on the bene elim, the heavenly beings, to give glory to the LORD. It ends with the LORD enthroned over the flood as king.

Since the discovery of Ugarit, many scholars have noticed how much Psalm 29 sounds like poems about , the Canaanite storm god. At Ugarit, Baal thunders, rules from his mountain, defeats the Sea, and is proclaimed king. Some scholars think Psalm 29 was adapted from an older Canaanite hymn. Others think it was written in Israel using the region’s familiar storm language.

Heiser read passages like this as polemic. A is writing that argues against a rival view. In his books he pointed to Baal’s title rider of the clouds in the Ugaritic poems. Then he noted that the Hebrew Bible gives Israel’s God similar imagery, as in Psalm 68:4 and Psalm 104:3. The message, he argued, was that the LORD, not Baal, is the true ruler of the storm and the sea.

Readers who do not accept every part of Heiser’s work often agree on this point. The idea that biblical writers answered Canaanite religion with its own imagery is common in scholarship. Where people differ is how much the imagery was borrowed, adapted or simply shared, and what that says about Israel’s early beliefs.

Sages, Watchers and a Hebrew Word

29.8

The Apkallu of Mesopotamia

Main ideaHeiser, building on Amar Annus, argued that Genesis 6 and 1 Enoch turn Mesopotamia's honored sages, the apkallu, into rebels who spread harmful knowledge.

In Mesopotamian tradition the were seven wise beings who lived before the great flood. They were said to have brought people the arts of civilization, such as writing, building and crafts. Assyrian palace carvings from the 800s BCE show figures often identified as apkallu, some dressed in a fish-shaped cloak and some with a bird’s head. A Babylonian priest named Berossus, writing in Greek around 290 BCE, told of a sage called Oannes who came from the sea to teach humans.

In Mesopotamia these sages were heroes. Kings liked to link their wisdom to them. Some texts describe later sages, after the flood, as only partly apkallu, mixed with human descent. In 2010 the scholar Amar Annus published an article comparing these traditions with the of 1 Enoch.

Heiser drew on Annus’s work in Reversing Hermon (2017). He argued that Genesis 6:1–4 and 1 Enoch retell the Mesopotamian story in reverse. In 1 Enoch 6–8, heavenly Watchers come down, have children with human women, and teach forbidden knowledge such as weapon-making. The sages Mesopotamia honored become, in the Jewish telling, the source of trouble. Heiser saw this as polemic against Babylon’s pride.

Not every scholar agrees on the details. Some think the links are clear; others think the traditions grew separately from a shared stock of flood-era stories. Checking the argument means reading the actual texts on both sides, in good translations, not only summaries of them.

29.9

Elohim: A Word About Where

Main ideaHeiser argued that the Hebrew word elohim names beings whose home is the spirit world, not a list of qualities that only the God of Israel has.

The Hebrew word is usually translated God when it refers to the God of Israel. But the Hebrew Bible uses the same word for other beings too. Psalm 82 uses it for the members of the council. Deuteronomy 32:17 uses it for the shedim, spirit beings to whom Israel wrongly sacrificed. In 1 Samuel 28:13, a woman at Endor uses the word for the spirit of the dead prophet Samuel.

If elohim meant all-powerful, eternal creator, these uses would make little sense. Heiser concluded that the word says where a being belongs, not what it is like. He called it a word about place of residence. An elohim, in his reading, is a being whose proper home is the unseen world. The God of Israel, the council, lesser spirits and the dead could all be described with the word in that sense.

This idea shaped much of his work. It lets a reader take the plural uses of elohim seriously without concluding that the Bible teaches many equal gods. The word marks a realm, and inside that realm there are very different kinds of beings, from the God of Israel to the spirits of the dead.

Some scholars welcome this as a helpful description of how the word is used. Others think it is too neat, and that elohim also carried ideas of power and rank, not only place. Many traditional readers explain the plural uses differently, as the word applied loosely to judges, idols or false gods. Knowing these options lets a student test each against the passages.

29.10

Unique Among the Elohim

Main ideaHeiser said the God of Israel is an elohim, but no other elohim is the God of Israel, because only he is uncreated and the creator of the rest.

If many beings can be called elohim, what makes the God of Israel different? Heiser answered with a phrase he used in The Unseen Realm (2015). He called Israel’s God . Every member of the council is an elohim, but only one elohim is Yahweh. In Heiser’s reading, Yahweh stands alone because he is uncreated, all-powerful and the maker of every other being.

Heiser pointed to verses that compare the LORD to other heavenly beings. Psalm 89:6–7 asks who in the skies can be compared with the LORD, and who among the heavenly beings is like him. Nehemiah 9:6 says the host of heaven worships him. Exodus 15:11 asks who is like the LORD among the gods. To Heiser, these verses only make sense if the other beings exist. Yet in each one the LORD has no equal.

This is why Heiser was uneasy with the word as it is often used today. He argued that if monotheism means only that no other spiritual beings exist, the Hebrew Bible does not fit it. If it means that only one being is God in the full sense, then he believed the Hebrew Bible fits it well.

Other scholars use other labels. Some say henotheism, the worship of one god without denying others. Some say monolatry, the worship of one god alone. Many Jewish and Christian readers simply say monotheism, meaning one God and created angels. Heiser’s point was about describing the texts carefully, whatever label is used.

Comparing With Care

29.11

What Comparison Can Show

Main ideaComparing the Bible with neighboring texts can show shared words and pictures, and it can also show where the Bible says something different.

Comparison works like holding two maps side by side. When scholars set Psalm 82 beside Ugaritic council scenes, they learn that council of El was a known phrase. When they set Genesis 6 beside the apkallu, they notice that similar ideas are valued very differently. Both kinds of results matter.

Heiser stressed the differences as much as the likenesses. At Ugarit, El is the old king and Baal is a younger god who fights to become king. In the Hebrew Bible, Heiser noted, both roles belong to one God. Israel’s God is the ancient king of the council and also the one who rides the clouds and rules the sea. The same pictures are used, but the claim is new.

Comparison also helps with words. A rare Hebrew word may appear only once or twice in the Bible, which makes its meaning hard to pin down. If the same word appears many times in Ugaritic or Akkadian texts, the context there can suggest a meaning. Dictionaries of biblical Hebrew now use such evidence.

Comparison does not tell a reader whether a text is true. It tells how a text fits its world. That is why Heiser’s method can be studied and discussed by readers of any faith or of none. Each reader can then decide what to make of the results, drawing on their own tradition or none.

29.12

Beware of Parallelomania

Main ideaSamuel Sandmel coined parallelomania in 1961 for overdoing likenesses between texts and assuming one must have borrowed from the other.

In December 1961 Samuel Sandmel, a Jewish scholar of the New Testament and early Judaism, gave the presidential address to the Society of Biblical Literature. It was published the next year. He gave it the title Parallelomania. He used the word to name a habit he saw in scholarship. Scholars would find a likeness between two texts, overstate it, and then claim one text copied from the other, as if the direction of borrowing were obvious.

Sandmel did not say comparison is useless. He said a likeness is only the start of the work. A shared phrase might come from a shared culture, from a common older source, or from chance. And a phrase can mean something different in each text. A careful reader studies each text in its own setting before deciding what a likeness means.

The same caution applies to claims about Heiser’s subjects. Some popular books say Mesopotamian carvings of apkallu show ancient astronauts, or that giants built the pyramids. Heiser rejected such claims. He publicly challenged Zecharia Sitchin, whose books claimed Sumerian texts described visitors from another planet. Heiser argued that Sitchin’s readings did not match the actual Sumerian and Akkadian words.

Readers can apply Sandmel’s test to Heiser’s own comparisons too. Is the likeness real in the original languages? Is the direction of influence shown, or only assumed? Asking these questions is not an attack on a scholar. It is how every serious argument in the field is checked.

Where Many Scholars Agree

30.1

The Council in Scholarship

Main ideaThe idea that the Hebrew Bible pictures a divine council was well established in scholarship before Heiser, in works such as E. Theodore Mullen's 1980 study.

Heiser did not invent the divine council. Scholars had discussed it for decades before The Unseen Realm (2015). Frank Moore Cross wrote about it in Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic (1973). E. Theodore Mullen devoted a whole book to it: The Assembly of the Gods: The Divine Council in Canaanite and Early Hebrew Literature (1980). Mark S. Smith discussed it in The Early History of God (1990) and later works.

These scholars pointed to council scenes across the Hebrew Bible. In 1 Kings 22:19–22 the prophet Micaiah describes the LORD on his throne with the host of heaven around him, discussing a plan. Job 1–2 opens in a heavenly court. Isaiah 6 shows the prophet in God’s throne room asking, whom shall I send, and who will go for us? And Psalm 82 names the council outright.

So when Heiser wrote about the council, he was building on a broad base. Most scholars accept that the texts use council imagery. What they debate is how to explain it. Some, like Smith, see it as a leftover from Israel’s Canaanite past that faded over time. Heiser saw it as a steady part of the Bible’s worldview from start to finish.

Heiser’s own contribution was to trace the idea across the whole Bible for a wide audience. He wrote his dissertation on the council in Second Temple texts (2004), then wrote popular books. He linked the council to many other topics: Eden, Genesis 6, the nations, and the New Testament.

Deuteronomy 32:8 in the Scrolls

Main ideaA Dead Sea Scroll reads sons of God in Deuteronomy 32:8 where the later Masoretic Text reads sons of Israel, and most text critics prefer the scroll's reading.

Deuteronomy 32 is the Song of Moses. Verse 8 describes the Most High dividing the nations and setting their borders. The , the standard Hebrew text used in most Bibles, ends the verse with according to the number of the sons of Israel. That is the reading behind the King James Version.

In the caves near Qumran, found from 1947 onward, scholars recovered a copy of Deuteronomy known as 4QDeut j. It is centuries older than the oldest complete Masoretic manuscripts. At verse 8 it reads sons of God, bene elohim. The , the ancient Greek translation, has a reading that points the same way; most of its copies say angels of God. Text critics compare such witnesses to decide which reading is most likely original.

Most text critics now prefer sons of God. Several modern translations, such as the English Standard Version and the New Revised Standard Version, follow it. Heiser argued for this reading in a 2001 article in the journal Bibliotheca Sacra. He made it a key to his whole approach. In his reading, the verse says God placed the nations under members of his council, while Israel remained his own (verse 9).

Text critics call the study of manuscript differences . The word criticism here does not mean finding fault with the Bible. It means judging carefully between readings. Many scholars who disagree with Heiser’s larger framework still accept the scroll’s reading of this verse.

30.3

Why a Scribe Might Change a Word

Main ideaText critics reason that a scribe was more likely to change sons of God to sons of Israel than the reverse, while some defend the Masoretic reading with the number seventy.

When manuscripts disagree, text critics ask which reading best explains how the others came about. One rule of thumb is that the harder reading is often older. A scribe copying a puzzling phrase might smooth it into something easier. Scribes rarely made an easy phrase harder.

Apply that rule to Deuteronomy 32:8. Sons of God, meaning heavenly beings over the nations, could trouble later readers worried about other gods. Sons of Israel is easier to accept. So most text critics judge that a scribe changed God to Israel, not the other way around. Heiser made this case in his 2001 article and in The Unseen Realm (2015).

The Masoretic reading has defenders and its own tradition of explanation. Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5 count seventy people of Jacob’s family who went down to Egypt. Jewish tradition counts seventy nations in Genesis 10. The medieval Jewish commentator Rashi linked the two, so that the nations match the number of Israel’s household. That reading gives sons of Israel a clear meaning.

Heiser answered that the number seventy fits the sons of God reading just as well, since Ugarit spoke of seventy sons of El’s wife Athirat. Here a student can see an argument at work. Both sides use the same number, and each explains it in a different way.

Where Scholars Debate

30.4

Monotheism: Two Accounts

Main ideaMark S. Smith describes Israel's monotheism as developing over time, while Heiser argued the Hebrew Bible consistently presents one unique God among other heavenly beings.

Mark S. Smith is a leading scholar of Ugarit and the Hebrew Bible. In The Early History of God (1990) and The Origins of Biblical Monotheism (2001), he argues that Israel’s religion developed over centuries. In his view, early Israel shared much with its Canaanite neighbors. Over time, the worship of Yahweh absorbed features of other gods and pushed others aside. Clear statements of appear, he argues, in texts from around the time of the Babylonian exile, such as Isaiah 40–55.

Heiser disagreed with parts of this story. He argued that the canonical Hebrew Bible, as it stands, presents one consistent picture: one species-unique God with a council of lesser elohim. He wrote about this in a 2008 article in the Bulletin for Biblical Research. There he asked whether terms like monotheism, polytheism, monolatry and henotheism describe the texts well.

Both scholars read verses such as Isaiah 45:5, where God says there is no God beside him. Smith and many others read such verses as denying that other gods exist. Heiser read them as statements of incomparability: no other being is like Yahweh. He pointed out that the same books still mention other heavenly beings, which, he argued, shows the writers were not denying their existence.

This debate is ongoing. It involves how to date biblical texts, how to read Ugarit, and what ancient people meant by a god. Smith works mainly with a historical account of how ideas changed. Heiser worked mainly with the final form of the canon. A student can learn from both by noticing which question each is asking.

Elyon and Yahweh in Deuteronomy 32

Main ideaSome scholars read Deuteronomy 32:8–9 as the high god Elyon giving Israel to Yahweh, while Heiser argued Elyon and Yahweh are the same God in the poem.

Deuteronomy 32:8 names the Most High, in Hebrew , as the one who divides the nations. Verse 9 says that the LORD’s portion is his people, and Jacob his inheritance. The question is whether these two verses speak of one God or two. Scholars who know the Hebrew well have answered both ways.

Some scholars, including Mark S. Smith, have read the verses against the Ugaritic picture of El and his sons. In that reading, Elyon is the high god. Yahweh is one of his sons, who receives Israel as his share, just as other sons receive other nations. If so, the verse preserves an older stage of belief in which Yahweh and El were distinct.

Heiser argued against this in his dissertation and articles. He pointed out that the same poem calls Yahweh the father who created and formed Israel (verse 6). He also noted that it says Yahweh alone led Israel with no foreign god (verse 12). And elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, Elyon and Yahweh are used as names of one God. For Heiser, the poem shows one God who places the nations under others and keeps Israel for himself.

Both readings take the Hebrew seriously and both use Ugarit. They differ in how they weigh the rest of the poem and the rest of the canon. This is a good example of why reading a whole passage, not one verse, matters. A single verse can seem to point one way until its neighbors are read too.

Two Powers in Heaven

Main ideaAlan Segal showed that early rabbis condemned a belief in two powers in heaven; Heiser argued its roots lay in Hebrew Bible texts about a second Yahweh figure.

In 1977 Alan Segal published Two Powers in Heaven: Early Rabbinic Reports about Christianity and Gnosticism. Segal studied passages in rabbinic literature that warn against people who say there are two powers in heaven. The rabbis treated this as a serious error. Segal argued that the idea was older than the warnings. He also argued that it grew from how some Jews read certain biblical passages, and that the rabbis came to reject it as heresy.

Which passages? One is Daniel 7:9–14. There the Ancient of Days sits on a throne, and one like a son of man comes with the clouds of heaven and receives everlasting rule. Another is the Angel of the LORD, who in some stories speaks as God, as in Exodus 3. In Exodus 23:20–21 God says his name is in the angel he sends.

Heiser built on Segal. In his dissertation (2004) and in The Unseen Realm (2015), he argued that the Hebrew Bible itself contains a pattern of two Yahweh figures, one unseen and one visible. He argued that early Christians read Jesus into that pattern. Heiser suggested that this Christian reading helps explain why the rabbis came to treat two powers as heresy.

Jewish tradition reads these passages differently. The Shema, Deuteronomy 6:4, declares that the LORD is one, and rabbinic Judaism understands God’s oneness as excluding any second divine person. The Angel of the LORD is read as a created messenger speaking for God. Scholars such as Daniel Boyarin have also discussed the two-powers debate, each with their own conclusions.

30.8

The Sethite View

Main ideaThe Sethite view reads the sons of God in Genesis 6 as the godly line of Seth, and it has long defenders, though Heiser argued the phrase names heavenly beings.

Genesis 6:2 says the sons of God saw that the daughters of humans were beautiful and took wives from among them. Who were the sons of God? The answers: descendants of Seth, Adam’s third son, who worshiped God. The daughters of humans, in this view, came from the line of Cain. The sin was the godly line marrying outside the faith.

This view has a long history. Julius Africanus, a Christian writer of the early 200s CE, held it. Augustine discussed it in The City of God (early 400s CE). Many Protestant commentators have held it since. Its defenders point to Genesis 4–5, where the lines of Cain and Seth are set side by side. They also cite Matthew 22:30, where Jesus says that in the resurrection people do not marry but are like the angels in heaven.

Heiser argued against the Sethite view in The Unseen Realm (2015) and Reversing Hermon (2017). He pointed out that the phrase sons of God in Job 1:6, 2:1 and 38:7 clearly names heavenly beings. He noted that Genesis never calls the line of Seth the sons of God. And he said Matthew 22:30 speaks of angels in heaven, not those who left heaven, as Jude 6 describes.

A third reading, found in some Jewish sources and some modern scholars, sees the sons of God as powerful rulers or kings. Each reading has passages it explains well and passages it struggles with. A fair student lists both for each, and notes which tradition or scholar holds each reading before comparing them.

The Three Rebellions

Main ideaHeiser organized the early chapters of Genesis around three rebellions in the unseen realm, a framework some readers find helpful and others think goes beyond the texts.

There, Heiser argued, God set the nations under members of his council (Deuteronomy 32:8), and those beings later ruled unjustly (Psalm 82).

Heiser used this framework to explain many later passages. He linked the gods of the nations to the idea that each nation had a heavenly ruler, as in Daniel 10. He also connected the framework to his reading of what the New Testament says about Jesus and the powers. For many readers, the framework gives a clear map of passages that had seemed scattered.

Other readers raise questions. Genesis never uses the word rebellion for Babel’s heavenly side, and Deuteronomy 32:8 does not say the sons of God rebelled then. Psalm 82 does not say when their wrongdoing began. Critics say the framework joins texts from different books and times into a tighter system than any one of them states.

Christian tradition has usually spoken of one fall in heaven and one fall of humanity in Eden. Heiser’s framework adds two more events. Whether a reader finds it convincing depends on how they weigh links between books. It also depends on how much they allow later texts, such as 1 Enoch, to shape the reading of earlier ones.

30.10

Jewish Readings

Main ideaJewish tradition includes more than one reading of these passages: earlier texts like 1 Enoch saw angels in Genesis 6, while rabbinic sources mostly read powerful men.

It is easy to speak of the Jewish view as if there were only one. There is not. Books such as 1 Enoch and Jubilees, written by Jews in the centuries before the rabbis, read the sons of God in Genesis 6 as angels. The Jewish writer Philo of Alexandria, in the first century CE, also discussed them as angels in his treatise On the Giants.

Later rabbinic tradition mostly took another path. The Onkelos, an Aramaic translation, renders sons of God in Genesis 6:2 as sons of the great ones, meaning nobles or rulers. The Genesis Rabbah reads it as sons of judges. Rashi, in the 1000s, explained them as sons of princes and judges. In this line of reading, the sin of Genesis 6 was the abuse of power by the mighty.

Jewish readers also have their own explanations of the council passages. Genesis Rabbah, on Genesis 1:26, has God consulting the angels before creating humans. So the idea of a heavenly court is not foreign to Jewish tradition. What rabbinic Judaism rejects is any reading that would give a second being a share in God’s divine rule.

Heiser drew heavily on Second Temple Jewish texts such as 1 Enoch and the Dead Sea Scrolls. His work shows how one strand of ancient Jewish reading shaped early Christians. A fair study notes that Jewish tradition as a whole developed many readings, and that today’s Jewish communities read these passages in their own ways.

Weighing an Argument

30.11

Claim, Evidence, Counter-Evidence

Main ideaTo weigh any reading fairly, name its claim, list its evidence, and state the strongest evidence against it.

Every interpretation makes a . Heiser’s claim about Psalm 82 is that the gods are divine beings in God’s council. A claim should be stated plainly, in one sentence, so that it can be tested. A human-judges reading makes a different claim about the same verses. Both can be checked the same way.

Next comes . For Heiser’s claim, the evidence includes the phrase council of El, the Ugaritic parallels, and the sentence you will die like humans in verse 7. It also includes the link to Deuteronomy 32:8 in the Dead Sea Scroll reading. Good evidence is specific: a verse, a word, a manuscript.

Then comes , the facts that are hardest for the claim. For Heiser’s reading, a reader might point to John 10:34–35, where Jesus refers to those to whom the word of God came. Some take that to mean humans. For the human-judges reading, the hardest facts are the council setting and the phrase die like humans. Stating the counter-evidence against your own view is not weakness. It is what makes a conclusion trustworthy.

Scholars add one more step: they ask how confident the evidence allows them to be. Some claims, like the Qumran reading of Deuteronomy 32:8, have wide support. Others, like the three rebellions, are debated. Saying which is which is part of reading an argument honestly.

30.12

Reading a Scholar Fairly

Main ideaReading fairly means stating a scholar's view in its strongest form, checking the sources, and separating wide agreement from open debate.

Heiser has many readers who admire him and some who disagree with him strongly. A fair student takes neither side too quickly. The first step is to state his view in a way he would recognize. A weak summary is easy to knock down. Some teachers call the fair approach : building the strongest version of a view before judging it.

The second step is checking sources. When Heiser says a Dead Sea Scroll reads sons of God, a student can look up 4QDeut j in published editions and modern translations. When he cites a Ugaritic phrase, the student can find it in a scholarly translation of the Ugaritic texts. The same goes for his critics. A claim is only as good as the source behind it.

The third step is sorting. From this unit, a student could list what is widely shared: the divine council imagery, the Ugaritic parallels, the Qumran reading of Deuteronomy 32:8. Then a second list of what is debated: how monotheism developed, the three rebellions, how much weight to give 1 Enoch, the Sethite view. Keeping those lists separate prevents a common mistake, which is treating a debated claim as settled or a settled one as mere opinion.

Finally, a fair reader stays respectful of everyone in the conversation. Heiser, Smith, Segal, Jewish commentators and Christian ones all read closely and carefully. Readers of any faith or none can study their arguments and reach their own conclusions.
