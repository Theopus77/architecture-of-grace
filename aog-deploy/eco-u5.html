Markets, Prices and Money

Markets

Every day, people buy and sell things. A family buys milk. A kid sells lemonade. A farmer sells corn. Each time, someone has to decide on a price. Why is a cup of lemonade 50 cents one day and 75 cents the next? This unit helps you find out.

You will also follow money on its trip. You will see why people stopped trading apples for shoes and started using coins and bills. You will visit a bank, see what it does with your savings, and learn how interest makes money grow.

By the end, you will be able to explain why prices go up and down. You will be able to figure out simple interest. And you will be able to make a budget for your own money, with a plan to spend, save and share.

The big question

How do buyers, sellers and banks work together to set prices and put money to work?

Prices
Big questionWhy does the price of the same cup of lemonade go up on some days and down on others?
The story

A Price War on Elm Street

Two lemonade stands, one hot Saturday, and a lesson about prices.

It was the hottest Saturday of June on Elm Street. Jada set up her lemonade stand at nine in the morning. Her sign said 50 cents a cup. By ten o'clock, a line of thirsty people stretched past the mailbox.

Jada sold cups as fast as she could pour them. Her pitcher ran low. People still waited in line. So Jada made a new sign. Now a cup cost 75 cents. A few people left, but most still paid. They were very thirsty.

Then Leo, who lived across the street, saw the long line. He had lemons and cups too. By noon he opened his own stand. His sign said 60 cents a cup. Half of Jada's line walked across the street to Leo.

Jada looked at her pitcher and then at Leo's sign. She changed her price to 55 cents and added a free ice cube. Leo added a paper straw. By the end of the day, both stands had sold out. The buyers got good deals, and Jada and Leo each earned money.

Buyers and Sellers

9.1

What Is a Market?

Main ideaA market is any place where buyers and sellers meet to trade goods or services.

Picture a farmers market on a Saturday morning. Farmers bring tomatoes, corn and honey. Families walk from table to table. The farmers are sellers. The families are buyers. When they trade money for food, a is working.

A is a person who pays for a good or service. A is a person who offers a good or service for money. A market does not need a building. A lemonade stand is a market. So is a website where people buy shoes.

Every market has two sides. Buyers want to get things for a low price. Sellers want to get a high price. The price they agree on is where the trade happens. In this chapter, you will learn what pushes that price up or down.

Main ideaDemand is how much people want to buy at each price; when the price goes down, people buy more.

Leo sells cookies at school. At $2 a cookie, only 10 kids buy one. At $1, 25 kids buy one. At 50 cents, 40 kids buy one. As the price went down, more kids wanted to buy. That is demand at work.

is how much of something people want to buy at each price. There is a pattern. When the price goes up, people buy less. When the price goes down, people buy more. This pattern is called the .

Why does this happen? When a cookie costs less, it is easier to afford. Some kids who said no at $2 say yes at $1. A common mistake is to think wanting is enough. Demand means wanting something and being able and willing to pay for it.

is how much of something sellers will offer at each price. Supply has its own pattern. When the price goes up, sellers offer more. When the price goes down, sellers offer less. This is called the .

Section 2

Why Prices Go Up and Down

9.4

When Many Want and Few Are for Sale

Main ideaWhen many people want something and only a few are for sale, the price goes up.

A new toy comes out before a holiday. A store gets 20 of them. But 100 families want one. There are not enough toys for everyone. This is called a . More people want to buy than there are items to sell.

In a shortage, buyers compete for the few items. Some will pay more to be sure they get one. Sellers notice the long lines. So the price goes up. As the price rises, some buyers decide it costs too much. The line gets shorter.

The price keeps rising until the number people want matches the number for sale. So the rule is simple. High demand and low supply push the price up. This is why concert tickets for a famous singer can cost a lot.

9.5

When There Is Too Much

Main ideaWhen there is more for sale than people want, the price goes down.

A farm stand picks 200 pumpkins in October. At $8 each, only 80 people buy one. When November comes, 120 pumpkins are left. There are more pumpkins for sale than people want. This is called a .

In a surplus, sellers have a problem. Pumpkins will rot if nobody buys them. So the farmer lowers the price. The sign now says $3 each. At the lower price, more people decide to buy. Some buy two pumpkins to make pie.

The price keeps falling until the pumpkins sell. So this is the other half of the rule. Low demand and high supply push the price down. That is why stores hold sales on winter coats in the spring.

9.6

A Hot Day and a Cold Day

Main ideaWhen something changes how much people want, like the weather, the price can change too.

Sam runs a lemonade stand. On a hot Saturday, 60 people want lemonade at 50 cents a cup. Sam has only 40 cups. That is a shortage, so Sam raises his price to 75 cents. Now about 40 people want a cup, and he sells out.

On a cold, rainy Sunday, only 15 people want lemonade at 50 cents. Sam still has 40 cups. That is a surplus. So Sam lowers the price to 25 cents. More people stop to buy. The weather changed the demand, and the price followed.

Many things can change demand. The weather, a holiday, a new fad or more people in town can all change how much people want. When demand goes up, the price tends to rise. When demand goes down, the price tends to fall.

Competition and Price Signals

9.7

More Sellers, Better Deals

Main ideaCompetition among sellers leads to lower prices, better quality and better service for buyers.

Jada was the only lemonade stand on Elm Street. She could charge 75 cents a cup. Then Leo opened a stand across the street for 60 cents. Now buyers had a choice. Jada lowered her price to 55 cents and added ice.

When sellers try to win the same buyers, that is . Each seller wants buyers to pick them. So they lower prices, make better products or give friendlier service. The buyers win. They get more for their money.

Competition also pushes sellers to try new ideas. A bakery might add a new flavor. A shop might stay open later. When there is only one seller, that seller has less reason to improve. Buyers have nowhere else to go.

9.8

A Price Is a Signal

Main ideaA price is a signal that tells buyers and sellers what to do.

A traffic light sends a signal. Green means go. Red means stop. Prices send signals too. When the price of strawberries is high, it tells farmers, grow more strawberries. It tells buyers, maybe buy blueberries this week.

A high price is a that something is scarce. Sellers see a chance to earn more, so they make more. Buyers see a reason to buy less or find a . A substitute is something you can use instead, like apple juice instead of orange juice.

A low price sends the opposite signal. It tells sellers to make less and tells buyers it is a good time to buy. Nobody has to give orders. Millions of people read prices and make their own choices. Together, those choices move goods where people want them.

9.9

Where the Price Settles

Main ideaThe market price settles where the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer.

Put buyers and sellers together. At $2 a muffin, kids want 10 but Jada bakes 35. That is a surplus, so the price falls. At 50 cents, kids want 40 but Jada bakes only 10. That is a shortage, so the price rises.

At $1, kids want 20 muffins and Jada bakes 20. Nothing is left over, and nobody is left out. This is the . Economists call it the price. It is where the amount people want equals the amount for sale.

The market price can move. If a hot day or a new fad raises demand, the price rises to a new level. If more sellers join, supply rises and the price falls. Prices keep adjusting as buyers and sellers change their plans.

Money, Banks and Interest

Money
Big questionWhat does money do for us, and what happens to it when we put it in a bank?
The story

Ana's Fifty Dollars

Ana puts her birthday money in the bank, and the bank puts it to work.

Ana turned ten, and her grandmother gave her a card with $50 inside. Ana wanted a new art set. But the art set cost $70. She did not have enough yet. "Put it in the bank," her grandmother said. "Let it grow while you wait."

At the bank, a teller named Mr. Ruiz helped Ana open a savings account. Ana handed over her $50. Mr. Ruiz typed it into the computer and gave her a paper slip. It showed $50 in her account. Ana frowned. "Where does my money go now?"

Mr. Ruiz smiled. "Some of it stays here, safe. Some of it we lend to people who need it. A family down the street borrowed money to fix their roof. A baker borrowed money to buy a new oven. They will pay it back, plus a little extra."

"And you get a little extra too," he said. "That is called interest. The bank pays you for letting it use your money." Ana thought about it. Her $50 was helping fix a roof and bake bread. And it would still be hers when she needed it.

That is the big problem with barter. It is hard to find the right match. It is also hard to split things. How many apples is half a cow worth? solves these problems. Everyone accepts it, so Maria can sell apples for money and use the money to buy shoes.

10.2

Three Jobs of Money

Main ideaMoney does three jobs: it helps us trade, it measures what things are worth, and it lets us save.

Think about $5 in your pocket. First, you can trade it for a sandwich. Money used to buy and sell is a . Medium means "in the middle." Money sits in the middle of every trade.

Second, money measures value. A book costs $10 and a game costs $30. You can see right away that the game is worth three times as much as the book. Money is a , like a ruler measures length.

Third, money lets you save for later. Apples rot, but a $5 bill still works next month. Money is a . You can keep it now and spend it later. Ana saved her $50 because she could not yet buy the $70 art set.

10.4

Keeping Money Safe

Main ideaA bank keeps people's deposits safe, and the government insures bank deposits up to $250,000.

Keeping $500 under a mattress is risky. It could be lost, stolen or burned in a fire. A bank is safer. Money you put into a bank is called a . The bank keeps a record of every dollar you put in and take out.

When you put money in, the bank adds it to your account. When you take money out, it is called a . If Ana deposits $50 and later withdraws $20, her account shows $30. The record helps her keep track.

Bank deposits are also protected. A U.S. government agency called the FDIC insures deposits at member banks up to $250,000. That means if the bank ever failed, the government would make sure savers got their money back, up to that amount.

Main ideaBanks lend some of the money people deposit to people and businesses who need to borrow.

Ana’s bank has many savers. Together they deposit $10,000 this month. Not everyone will take their money out at once. So the bank keeps some cash ready and lends the rest. A baker borrows $3,000 to buy a new oven.

So a bank is a meeting place for savers and borrowers. Savers have money they do not need right now. Borrowers need money now for a house, a car, a business or school. The bank connects them. Money that would sit still gets put to work.

is money paid for the use of money. The bank pays savers interest because it gets to use their money for loans. The rate is written as a . A higher rate means more money for the saver.

Why: The bank pays savers for letting it use their money to lend to borrowers.
Section 3

Borrowing and Budgeting

10.7

Paying to Borrow

Main ideaWhen you borrow money, you pay it back plus interest, so borrowing costs more than paying cash.

Carlos wants a $200 bike now. He does not have the money. His uncle agrees to lend him $200 at 10% interest for one year. This is an example rate. 10% of $200 is $20. So Carlos must pay back $200 + $20 = $220.

Interest works both ways. Savers earn interest. Borrowers pay interest. The first amount borrowed is called the . For Carlos, the principal is $200. The interest is the extra $20. Borrowing let him ride now, but the bike really cost $220.

Banks charge borrowers a higher rate than they pay savers. The difference helps the bank pay its workers and stay open. A smart borrower asks two questions. Can I pay this back? Is having it now worth the extra cost?

Think it through
Now or Later?

Carlos can borrow $200 now and pay back $220 in a year (example rate of 10%). Or he can save $20 a month from his allowance and chores and buy the bike with cash in 10 months.

What does Carlos gain by borrowing? What does he give up? Which would you choose, and why?

Words to know
principal
the first amount of money saved or borrowed, before interest
interest
money paid for the use of money; savers earn it and borrowers pay it
Check yourself

1. Lila borrows $300 at 10% simple interest for one year (example rate). How much must she pay back?

Why: 10% of $300 is $30. $300 + $30 = $330.

2. Carlos borrows $200 and pays back $220. What is the principal?

Why: The principal is the first amount borrowed, which is $200. The $20 is interest.

3. Who pays interest?

Why: Borrowers pay interest for using someone else's money. Savers earn it.
10.8

Income, Spending and Saving

Main ideaIncome is money you get; you can spend it, save it or share it.

Ana gets $10 a week for doing chores. Money that comes in is called . For grown-ups, income is often pay from a job. For kids, it might be an allowance, gift money or money from a lemonade stand.

Once Ana has income, she has choices. She can spend it now on a snack or a comic book. She can save it for the art set. She can share it by giving to a food pantry. Every dollar can go only one way.

So each choice has a cost. If Ana spends $4 on candy, she has $4 less to save. That $4 of saving is what she gave up. When money is limited, deciding where it goes is the most important money skill of all.

Think it through
Ana's Choices

Ana has $10 this week. A comic book costs $4. Her class is collecting money for the food pantry. She is also saving for a $70 art set and already has $50.

How would you split Ana's $10? What does she give up with each choice?

Words to know
income
money a person gets, such as pay from a job or an allowance
saving
keeping some income to use later instead of spending it now
Check yourself

1. Which of these is income for a kid?

Why: Income is money that comes in. Money earned from raking leaves is income.

2. Ana has $10. She spends $4 on candy. How much can she still save?

Why: $10 - $4 = $6 left to save or share.

3. Ana needs $20 more for her art set. She saves $5 a week. How many weeks until she has enough?

Why: $20 divided by $5 a week = 4 weeks.
10.9

Making a Budget

Main ideaA budget is a plan for how to use your income: how much to spend, save and share.

A is a plan for your money. You write down your income. Then you decide how much goes to spending, saving and sharing. Ana makes a budget for her $10 allowance. She plans $5 to spend, $3 to save and $2 to share.

Check the math: $5 + $3 + $2 = $10. A good budget uses every dollar of income and not one dollar more. If Ana planned $6 to spend, $3 to save and $2 to share, that adds to $11. She would be $1 short.

A budget helps you reach a . A goal is something you are saving for. At $3 a week, Ana’s goal of $20 more for the art set would take about 7 weeks. If she saves $5 a week instead, she reaches it in 4 weeks. Budgets help you see trade-offs.

Read the numbers
Ana's Weekly Budget

Does Ana's budget add up to her $10 income? What would you change to reach her goal faster?

Words to know
budget
a plan for how to spend, save and share your income
goal
something you plan and save for
Check yourself

1. Ana plans $5 to spend, $3 to save and $2 to share. What is the total?

Why: $5 + $3 + $2 = $10, which matches her income.

2. Leo's income is $12 a week. He plans $6 to spend and $4 to save. How much is left to share?

Why: $6 + $4 = $10. $12 - $10 = $2 left to share.

3. Why does a budget help you reach a goal?

Why: A budget is a plan. It shows how much you save and how soon you reach your goal.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. The price of bananas goes down. What will shoppers most likely do?

Why: The law of demand: when the price goes down, people buy more.

2. Bakers can now sell a pie for $15 instead of $10. What will bakers most likely do?

Why: The law of supply: when the price goes up, sellers offer more.

3. A store has 5 bikes and 25 people want one. What is this?

Why: More people want bikes than there are for sale. That is a shortage.

4. After a shortage, what usually happens to the price?

Why: Buyers compete for the few items, so the price goes up.

5. A shop has 300 leftover beach towels in October. What will it most likely do?

Why: This is a surplus. Sellers lower the price so more people buy.

6. A second bakery opens next to the first. What will likely happen to bread prices?

Why: Competition pushes sellers to offer lower prices or better quality.

7. At 30 cents, kids want 50 pencils and the store has 50. What is 30 cents?

Why: The amount wanted equals the amount for sale. That is the market price, or equilibrium.

8. Gas prices jump. Some families ride bikes instead of driving. What is the high price doing?

Why: A high price is a signal. It tells buyers to use less or find a substitute.

9. Tom has fish and wants bread. The baker wants eggs. Why is barter hard here?

Why: Barter only works when each person wants what the other has.

10. You keep $10 in a drawer to spend next month. Which job of money is this?

Why: Saving money to use later is money's job as a store of value.

11. Eva deposits $60, withdraws $15, then deposits $20. How much is in her account?

Why: $60 - $15 = $45. Then $45 + $20 = $65.

12. Why does a bank pay interest to savers?

Why: The bank pays savers for the use of their money, which it lends to borrowers.

13. Max saves $400 at 5% simple interest a year (example rate). How much interest does he earn in one year?

Why: 5% of $400: 400 x 5 / 100 = $20.

14. Rosa borrows $100 at 10% simple interest for one year (example rate). How much does she pay back?

Why: 10% of $100 is $10. $100 + $10 = $110.

15. Dev earns $20 a week. He plans $10 to spend and $6 to save. How much is left to share?

Why: $10 + $6 = $16. $20 - $16 = $4 left to share.
