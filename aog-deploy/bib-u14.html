Close Reading the Torah

Torah Close Up

The Torah, the first five books of the Bible, is read aloud in synagogues every week, studied in churches, cited in courtrooms and quoted on monuments. Most people know a few of its lines. Few have read it slowly. This unit slows down. You will read the opening of Genesis with a pen in hand, counting names and repeated phrases, and you will read the laws of Exodus, Leviticus and Deuteronomy beside the laws and treaties of Israel's neighbors.

Two questions run through the unit. First, what is actually on the page: the order of events, the names for God, the shape of a chapter, the words a law uses. Second, how careful readers have explained what is there. Jewish tradition, Christian tradition and academic scholarship each have their own answers, and they often begin from the same observation. You will learn the names of those answers, from midrash to the documentary hypothesis, and what evidence each one uses.

By the end you will be able to read a passage of the Torah closely, using four tools: repetition, keyword, structure and gap. You will be able to describe the two creation accounts, the Eden story and its readers, the flood and Babel, the covenant as a treaty, the Ten Commandments in three numberings, the goring ox in two codes, the Shema, the blessings and curses, and the Thirteen Attributes. And you will be able to say, for any reading, whose reading it is.

The story

Two Colors of Ink

Two students take the first three chapters of Genesis and underline every name for God. The pattern they find is one that readers have argued about for more than a century.

Maya and Jordan sit at a library table in Rogers Park with two copies of Genesis, one blue pen and one red pen. Their teacher gave them one job: read Genesis 1 through 3 and underline every name for God. Blue for the word "God" by itself. Red for "LORD God," printed with LORD in small capital letters. They think it will take ten minutes.

Genesis 1 goes fast. "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." Blue. "And God said, Let there be light." Blue. By the end of the seventh day, Maya has underlined the word God about thirty-five times, and there is not one red mark on the page. The chapter is orderly, almost like a list. Each day begins, God speaks, the thing appears, God sees that it is good, and evening and morning close the day.

Then Jordan reaches Genesis 2:4, and the pen changes hands. "In the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens." Red. From here to the end of chapter 3, nearly every line is red. And the story itself feels different. There is no seven-day list. A man is formed from dust before any plants grow. A garden is planted. Animals are made after the man, and a woman is formed last of all. The order in chapter 1 was plants, animals, then people together.

Maya flips back and forth. "It's like two different writers," she says. Jordan is not so sure. "Or one writer who wanted two views of the same event. A wide shot and then a close-up." They have, without knowing it, just restated a debate that has run since the 1700s. Jewish and Christian readers noticed the two names long ago and explained them one way. Modern scholars noticed the same names and explained them another way.

This chapter hands you the same two pens. You will read Genesis 1 and 2 side by side, learn the names of the different explanations, put Genesis beside a Babylonian creation poem, and then follow the text through Eden, Cain and Abel, the flood and the tower of Babel. The goal is not to decide who is right. The goal is to see what is on the page, and to understand why careful readers see it differently.

Reading Genesis 1 and 2

27.1

The Shape of Genesis 1

Main ideaGenesis 1:1–2:3 is built on repetition: a seven-day frame, a set formula for each day, and a refrain that God saw it was good.

Genesis 1:1–2:3 is one of the most patterned passages in the Bible. Read it aloud and the shows itself. Each day follows the same steps. God speaks ("And God said"). The thing comes to be ("and it was so"). God names or blesses it. God sees that it is good. Then a closing line: "And the evening and the morning were the first day." The same repeats six times, and the seventh day breaks the pattern by having no work at all.

Readers have long noticed a second pattern inside the first. Days one to three make spaces: light and dark, sky and sea, dry land with plants. Days four to six fill those spaces in the same order: sun, moon and stars for the light; birds and fish for sky and sea; land animals and people for the land. Many scholars call this a "framework" reading. Jewish and Christian commentators from the Middle Ages had already observed that the days come in matched pairs.

The chapter’s key words repeat too. The word for God here is the Hebrew , which appears about thirty-five times in these thirty-four verses. "And God said" comes ten times. "Good" comes seven times, ending with "very good." Repetition in an ancient text is rarely an accident. It is a way of stressing what matters, and it is the first tool in a close reader’s kit: count what repeats, then ask why.

27.2

The Garden Account

Main ideaGenesis 2:4–3:24 tells of creation again, in a different order and a different style, centered on a garden, a man, a woman and a command.

At Genesis 2:4 the text says, "These are the generations of the heavens and of the earth," and begins again. This time there is no count of days. The ground is dry and empty because no rain has fallen. Then the text says God formed a man from the dust of the ground and breathed life into him. Only afterward does God plant a in Eden and set the man in it, with one command: eat from every tree except the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

The order matters to close readers. In Genesis 1 the plants come on day three, the animals on days five and six, and people last, male and female together. In Genesis 2 the man comes first, then the plants, then the animals, which the man names, and last the woman, built from the man’s side. The style is different too. Genesis 1 is formal and repetitive. Genesis 2–3 is a with scenes, dialogue and suspense: a talking serpent, a fruit, a hiding couple, a question in the cool of the evening.

Chapter 3 ends the account with judgment and exile. The serpent is cursed, the woman is told of pain in childbirth, the man is told the ground will resist him, and both are sent out of the garden. Cherubim and a flaming sword guard the way back to the tree of life. Read as a piece of writing, Genesis 2–3 has a clear arc: a gift, a rule, a test, a choice, a loss. What it means is the work of the next section. What it says is the work of this one.

27.3

Two Names, Two Voices

Main ideaGenesis 1 calls God Elohim; Genesis 2–3 calls God YHWH Elohim, printed LORD God; readers explain that shift in very different ways.

Open the King James Version or the 1917 Jewish Publication Society Tanakh and you will see "God" in Genesis 1 and "LORD God" in Genesis 2–3, with LORD in small capitals. The small capitals are a signal. They mark the four Hebrew letters YHWH, called the . Jewish practice for many centuries has been not to pronounce this name, saying ("my Lord") instead. English translators followed that custom and printed LORD.

The shift in names is old news to the tradition. A rabbinic collection called Genesis Rabbah, compiled in the early centuries of the Common Era, reads Elohim as the name that shows God’s justice and YHWH as the name that shows God’s mercy. On that reading, Genesis 1 shows the world made by justice, and Genesis 2 adds mercy so that the world can stand. Many medieval Jewish and Christian commentators followed this path: one author, two names, chosen on purpose.

Modern scholars starting in the 1700s took the same clue and drew a different map. A French physician, Jean Astruc, proposed in 1753 that Moses had used older written sources, one favoring Elohim and one favoring YHWH. Later scholars turned this into a theory of separate documents. The next section explains it. For now, notice the method. Both the rabbis and the scholars began by counting names, exactly as Maya and Jordan did with their two pens.

Who Wrote the Torah?

27.4

The Documentary Hypothesis

Main ideaMany scholars since the 1800s have explained the Torah as a weave of four written sources, labeled J, E, D and P, combined by later editors.

The is the name for a scholarly theory about how the Torah was written. Its classic form was set out by the German scholar Julius Wellhausen in his Prolegomena to the History of Israel (1878). Wellhausen argued that four separate written sources had been joined by editors over several centuries. He labeled them with letters: J, E, D and P.

J is the Yahwist (spelled with a J in German), the source that uses the name YHWH from the beginning and tells vivid stories like the garden of Eden. E is the Elohist, which prefers Elohim and is thought to come from the northern kingdom of Israel. D is the Deuteronomist, mostly the book of Deuteronomy with its speeches and its call to worship in one place. P is the Priestly source, with its interest in order, genealogies, dates and ritual; Genesis 1 is the standard example. Wellhausen dated P last, after the exile of the 500s BCE.

Scholars who use this model point to evidence like the two creation accounts, two flood stories that seem to be spliced together (two of every animal in Genesis 6:19, but seven pairs of clean animals in 7:2), and differences in vocabulary and style. They call the person or people who combined the sources the . The hypothesis is a theory, not a discovery of actual scrolls. No manuscript of J or P has ever been found. It stands or falls on how well it explains the text we have.

27.5

Tradition and the Critics

Main ideaJewish and Christian tradition names Moses as the Torah's author, and both religious readers and academic scholars have challenged the four-source model.

For most of history, Jewish and Christian readers held that Moses wrote the Torah, the five books from Genesis to Deuteronomy. Later books of the Bible refer to "the book of Moses" and "the law of Moses." Many Orthodox Jews and many Christians today hold to as a matter of faith. A few medieval commentators, such as Abraham ibn Ezra in the 1100s, hinted that some verses came from a later hand. They did not doubt the whole.

Critics of the documentary hypothesis come from more than one direction. The Italian Jewish scholar Umberto Cassuto argued in lectures in 1941 that the shift between Elohim and YHWH follows the meaning of each passage, not separate authors, much as Genesis Rabbah had said. In the 1970s academic scholars such as Rolf Rendtorff argued that the Torah grew from smaller blocks of tradition rather than four long documents. Others defend a model: one base text expanded over time.

Where does that leave a student? With a text and a set of competing maps. Most university scholars today still speak of P and D as recognizable layers, while J and E are debated more than they once were. Religious readers may accept, adapt or reject any of this. The classroom task is narrower: know what each reading claims, what evidence it uses, and what it cannot explain. That is what it means to read a , a collection a community has received as Scripture, with both respect and care.

27.6

Genesis Beside Babylon

Main ideaEnuma Elish, a Babylonian creation poem, shares images with Genesis 1 but tells a very different kind of story, and readers weigh the likeness in different ways.

In 1876 George Smith of the British Museum published tablets from the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. On them, in cuneiform, was a Babylonian poem now called , from its opening words, "When on high." Its date of composition is debated. Many scholars place it in the late second millennium BCE. That is earlier than most dates given for the writing of Genesis 1. Its discovery changed how people read the Bible’s first page.

The poem tells how the young god Marduk fights the sea goddess Tiamat, kills her, and splits her body to make the sky and the earth. He then has humans made from the blood of a defeated god so that they can do the gods’ work. Readers noticed likenesses to Genesis: a watery beginning, a dividing of waters, a sequence ending in humans, and a rest for the gods. Some scholars connect the Hebrew word for "the deep," , in Genesis 1:2 with the name Tiamat. Others say the words are simply related and no borrowing is needed.

The differences are as striking as the likenesses. Genesis 1 has no battle, no rival gods and no birth of God. God speaks and the world is. Humans are made "in the image of God," not as servants from a corpse. Many scholars therefore read Genesis 1 as a deliberate answer to stories like Enuma Elish, using the shared of the ancient Near East to say something new. Jewish and Christian readers have generally agreed that the contrast is the point, whatever they think about the source of the likeness.

Eden and Its Readers

27.7

Eden Read as Story

Main ideaRead as narrative, Genesis 3 is full of deliberate gaps, and noticing what the text does not say is a close-reading skill.

A story can be read for what it says and for what it leaves out. Genesis 3 leaves out a great deal. The is called the most subtle of the beasts, but the text never says it is evil, never explains why it talks, and never names it Satan. That identification comes later, in Jewish and Christian writings from the last centuries BCE onward. The fruit is never called an apple; that idea grows from a Latin pun between malum (evil) and malum (apple).

Notice too what the serpent says. It asks whether God really forbade every tree, and the woman corrects it. Then it says, "Ye shall not surely die," and promises that their eyes will be opened and they will be as gods, knowing good and evil. The text lets the reader weigh who is telling the truth. The couple do not die that day. Their eyes are opened. They also lose the garden. A close reader marks this as a , a place where the text expects you to think.

The book of Genesis itself never returns to Eden to explain it. The prophets rarely mention it. So the meaning of Genesis 3 has been supplied by its readers, and the next lesson shows how differently they have done so. Before the interpretation, it helps to fix the plain events: a command, a question, a bite, a covering of fig leaves, a hiding, a questioning, three judgments and an exit east of Eden. Everything else is reading.

27.8

Eden as Theology and Allegory

Main ideaJewish, Christian and Muslim readers draw different lessons from Eden, and some in every tradition have read it as allegory rather than as literal history.

Christians have often read Genesis 3 as "the Fall," a phrase that is not in Genesis. The reading rests largely on Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter 5, which says that sin entered the world through one man. Augustine of Hippo, around 400 CE, developed this into the doctrine of : every human inherits a damaged nature from Adam. Eastern Orthodox Christians speak instead of "ancestral sin," inheriting death and weakness but not guilt. These are differences within Christianity, and both sides cite the same chapter.

Jewish tradition, reading the same text, did not develop a doctrine of original sin. Rabbinic writings speak of a human "inclination" to evil and to good, both present in every person from the start. Each person answers for their own choices. Eden in Jewish reading is more often about exile and return, or about the cost of knowledge, than about inherited guilt. The Qur’an tells of Adam and his wife in a garden too. Muslims read that Adam repented and God forgave him. In Islam Adam is the first prophet, not the source of a fallen nature.

Some readers in every tradition have treated Eden as , a story whose figures stand for something else. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish writer of the first century CE, read the garden as a picture of the soul. The Christian teacher Origen, in the 200s, asked who could take literally a God who walks in a garden in the evening. Others insist on a plain, historical reading. John Milton’s Paradise Lost (1667) retold the story as an epic and shaped how English readers imagine it. A public classroom presents all of these as readings, and takes none as the verdict.

27.9

Cain and Abel

Main ideaGenesis 4 tells the first murder in a few spare verses, and its unexplained choices have made it a model text for reading gaps.

Genesis 4 moves fast. Eve bears Cain, then Abel. Abel keeps sheep; Cain works the ground. Each brings an , and the text says God had respect for Abel’s offering but not for Cain’s. It never says why. Readers have proposed reasons for two thousand years: Abel brought the firstborn and the fat, Cain brought merely "of the fruit"; or Cain’s heart was wrong; or the text is teaching that God’s choice is free. The gap is the point.

God speaks to Cain before the crime, telling him that sin crouches at the door like an animal, wanting him, but that he can master it. Cain talks with Abel in the field and kills him. Then comes one of the most quoted exchanges in the Bible. God asks where Abel is. Cain answers with a question of his own: "Am I my brother’s keeper?" The text says the blood cries from the ground, and Cain is sent to wander, but with a that protects him from being killed in turn.

Notice the structure. Genesis 3 had a command, a wrong, a question from God, an answer that shifts blame, a judgment and an exile east of Eden. Genesis 4 repeats every step. Many scholars read this as a deliberate echo, the story of the first family told twice at growing scale. Jewish tradition draws from the plural "bloods" in the Hebrew of 4:10 the teaching that to destroy one life is to destroy a whole world. Christian writers have read Abel as the first martyr. The verse itself remains a question with no answer given.

27.10

The Flood and Gilgamesh

Main ideaThe flood story in Genesis 6–9 has close parallels in Mesopotamian texts, and its own doubled details have fueled the source debate.

In 1872 George Smith stood before a London audience and read from a broken clay tablet. It told of a man warned by a god to build a boat, load it with animals, and survive a great flood, and of birds sent out to find dry land. The tablet was part of the Epic of , tablet XI, and the survivor’s name was Utnapishtim. An older Babylonian version, Atrahasis, has since been found too. The likeness to Noah’s story in Genesis 6–9 was immediately clear.

The story

A Treaty on the Wall

A peace treaty carved in stone more than three thousand years ago has a shape. A few centuries later, a covenant in the Torah reads as if it knew that shape.

In the 1200s BCE the two great powers of the eastern Mediterranean, Egypt under Ramesses II and the Hittite kingdom under Hattusili III, ended a long war with a written treaty. The Egyptians carved their copy on temple walls at Karnak and the Ramesseum. The Hittites pressed theirs into clay in their capital, Hattusa, in what is now Turkey. Scholars date the treaty to about 1259 BCE. A bronze replica of the Hittite tablet hangs today at the United Nations in New York.

When the clay tablets from Hattusa were dug up in the early 1900s and read, scholars noticed that Hittite treaties followed a set pattern. First a preamble naming the great king. Then a history of what the king had done for the lesser party. Then the terms: loyalty, no alliances with enemies, help in war. Then instructions to keep a copy in a temple and read it aloud. Then a list of gods as witnesses. Last, blessings for keeping the treaty and curses for breaking it.

In 1954 an American scholar named George Mendenhall laid the Hittite pattern beside the book of Exodus and the book of Deuteronomy. The fit was striking. "I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt" reads like a preamble and a history in one line. The Ten Commandments read like terms. Deuteronomy tells Israel to keep the law beside the ark and read it aloud every seven years, calls heaven and earth as witnesses, and ends with blessings and curses.

Not everyone agreed on the details. Some scholars said the closer match was to Assyrian treaties from the 600s BCE, which would point to a later date for Deuteronomy. Jewish and Christian readers noted that a text can borrow a form and still mean something new: a treaty between a king and a vassal became, in the Torah, a bond between God and a people. The University of Chicago has been compiling a dictionary of the Hittite language since the 1970s, so some of the tools for this debate were built a train ride from this classroom.

This chapter follows the covenant from Sinai to the plains of Moab. You will read the Ten Commandments closely and learn why three traditions number them differently. You will set the Torah's law about a goring ox beside the same law in Hammurabi's code. You will read Leviticus 19, where holiness means how you treat your neighbor, and Deuteronomy's speeches with their blessings and curses. And you will read Exodus 34, where the text describes God's own character after the golden calf.

Main ideaHittite treaties of the 1300s and 1200s BCE follow a six-part form, and scholars have used that form to read the covenant texts of the Torah.

A clause orders the treaty stored in a temple and read aloud at set times. Gods are called as witnesses. Blessings and curses close the text: prosperity for the faithful, ruin for the traitor. Main ideaThe Ten Commandments appear twice in the Torah, in Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5, and the Hebrew text calls them the ten words.

Three Ways to Count

Main ideaJews, Catholics and Lutherans, and most other Christians number the Ten Commandments differently, because the text itself does not number them.

Exodus 20:2–17 has no numbers in it. Everyone agrees there are ten words, because the text says so in Exodus 34:28. Where the ten begin and end is a matter of . Three main systems exist, and each is old. The differences do not change the text. They change what a child memorizes, what hangs on a wall, and how the first and last commandments are read.

In the Jewish numbering, "I am the LORD thy God" (20:2) is the first word, and "no other gods" together with "no graven image" (20:3–6) is the second. The Catholic and Lutheran numbering follows Augustine. It treats 20:2–6 as one commandment against other gods and images. Sabbath, Two Reasons

Main ideaThe sabbath command is grounded in creation in Exodus 20 and in the exodus from Egypt in Deuteronomy 5, and readers draw on both.

The command is the longest of the ten in both versions, and the two versions explain it differently. Exodus 20:8–11 says to remember the seventh day because in six days God made heaven and earth and rested on the seventh. Deuteronomy 5:12–15 says to keep the seventh day. It adds that servants must rest too, "that thy manservant and thy maidservant may rest as well as thou." The reason given is that Israel was a servant in Egypt and God brought them out.

Close readers notice that each reason fits its book. Exodus 20 looks back to Genesis 1, the orderly creation with rest on the seventh day. Deuteronomy looks back to the exodus and forward to a society where the person who works for you also rests. One reason is about the shape of the world; the other is about between people. Jewish liturgy holds both together: the Friday-night blessing over wine calls the sabbath a memorial of creation and a memorial of the going out from Egypt.

Readers of every kind have drawn on this doubling. Rabbinic law developed detailed sabbath practice from the command to do no "work," defining thirty-nine categories of labor. Most Christians moved their weekly day of worship to Sunday, the day of the resurrection in the Gospels, while some, such as Seventh-day Adventists, keep the seventh day. Scholars read the two reasons as a window on how the two books think. For a public classroom, the point is what the texts say and how communities have lived them, not which practice is right.

The Goring Ox and Hammurabi

Main ideaThe Torah's law about an ox that gores closely matches laws in the Code of Hammurabi, and the differences show what each collection cared about.

In 1901 and 1902 a French expedition digging at Susa, in Iran, found a black stone pillar over two meters tall. Carved on it were about 282 laws of , king of Babylon, who reigned until about 1750 BCE. The stone had been carried to Susa as war booty centuries later. It now stands in the Louvre in Paris. The Code of Hammurabi is older than any date scholars give for the writing of Exodus, and it contains a law about a .

Hammurabi’s laws 250 to 252 say, in short, three things. If an ox gores a man in the street and kills him, no claim can be made. If the ox was known to gore and its owner did not blunt its horns or tie it up, and it kills a free man, the owner pays half a mina of silver. If the victim is a slave, the owner pays one third of a mina. Exodus 21:28–32 says three things too. If an ox gores a man or woman to death, the ox is stoned and its flesh not eaten, but the owner is clear. If the ox was known to gore and the owner was warned, the ox is stoned and the owner is liable to death, unless a ransom is set. If the victim is a servant, the owner pays thirty shekels of silver.

The overlap is too close to be chance, and scholars debate whether Exodus knew Hammurabi’s text directly or drew on a shared legal tradition of the ancient Near East. The differences are the interesting part. Exodus stones the ox, which suggests the animal has shed human life and is unclean. Exodus makes the negligent owner liable for a life, not silver. Exodus names women as victims alongside men. Jewish and Christian readers have pointed to these as the Torah’s distinct stress on the worth of a human life. Historians read them as data about two societies’ values.

Holiness as Ethics

Main ideaLeviticus 19 ties holiness to how people treat one another, from leaving crops for the poor to loving the neighbor and the stranger.

Leviticus is often skipped as a book of ritual, but chapter 19 opens with a command that turns ritual outward: "Ye shall be holy: for I the LORD your God am holy." What follows is a list that mixes worship and daily conduct. Honor parents. Keep the sabbath. Leave the corners of the field and the fallen grapes for the poor and the stranger. Do not steal, lie or swear falsely. Pay a worker the same day. Do not curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind. Judge fairly, favoring neither the poor nor the mighty.

Scholars call Leviticus 17–26 the . August Klostermann gave it that name in 1877, because the refrain "I am the LORD" and the demand to be holy run through it. In chapter 19 is not only about being set apart for God; it is about the neighbor. Verse 18 commands, "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself." Verse 34 extends it. In the Gospels, Jesus pairs it with the Shema of Deuteronomy 6 as the two greatest commandments. Readers differ on who counts as the neighbor and how far verse 34 extends the circle; the text itself puts the stranger inside it. Historians note that the practice of leaving for the poor appears again in the book of Ruth.

Deuteronomy and the Character of God

28.9

The Speeches and the Shema

Main ideaDeuteronomy is framed as Moses' farewell speeches, and its call to hear, the Shema, became the central daily prayer of Judaism.

The name comes from the Greek for "second law," because the book retells the law given at Sinai. Its Hebrew name, Devarim, means "words," from its first line: these are the words Moses spoke. The book is set in Moab, east of the Jordan, in the last weeks of Moses’ life. It is built as speeches: a first speech recalling the journey (chapters 1–4), a long second speech giving the law (5–26), and closing words with blessings, curses, a song and a blessing, ending with Moses’ death in chapter 34.

The heart of the second speech is Deuteronomy 6:4–9, called the from its first word, "Hear." "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD: And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." The verses go on: teach these words to your children, speak of them at home and on the road, bind them on your hand and between your eyes, write them on your doorposts. Jewish practice takes those lines as instructions. The Shema is said morning and evening. It is written in worn in prayer and placed in the mezuzah on the doorpost.

Christians know the verse from the Gospels, where Jesus cites it as the first commandment. Scholars have long connected Deuteronomy to the reform of King Josiah, described in 2 Kings 22–23, when a "book of the law" was found in the temple around 622 BCE; the German scholar W. M. L. de Wette made that link in 1805. Jewish and Christian tradition holds the book to be Moses’ own words, with the account of his death added. Whatever its date, Deuteronomy’s voice is unmistakable: warm, urgent, repeating "today" and "remember" to a people about to cross into a land.

28.10

Blessings and Curses

Main ideaDeuteronomy ends its law with blessings for keeping the covenant and curses for breaking it, and asks the people to choose.

A Hittite treaty ended with blessings and curses, and so does Deuteronomy’s law. Chapter 27 has the tribes stand on two mountains, Gerizim and Ebal, while the Levites call out curses and the people answer "Amen." Chapter 28 gives the : rain, harvests, healthy herds, victory. Then it gives the at far greater length: drought, disease, defeat, siege and exile, scattering among the nations. The imbalance is itself a clue to the book’s mood. It speaks to a people it expects to fail and hopes to call back.

Chapter 30 turns from threat to hope. Even after exile, the text says, if the people return to God, God will gather them again. Then comes a line that sums the book up: I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing; therefore choose life. Readers have heard in that verse a claim that the covenant is a , not only a command. Jewish tradition reads the passage each year on the sabbath before Rosh Hashanah, the new year, as a call to return.

Scholars note how closely chapter 28’s curses match the curses in Assyrian treaties of the 600s BCE, and use the likeness to date the chapter. Christian readers have paired the blessings and curses with Paul’s argument in Galatians 3 about "the curse of the law" and debated what that means. Jewish readers have generally read them as covenant consequences within an ongoing bond, never its end. All three see the same fact on the page: the covenant has stakes, and the text names them.

28.11

The Golden Calf and the Thirteen Attributes

Main ideaAfter the golden calf, Exodus 34:6–7 has God describe God's own character, a passage Jewish liturgy calls the Thirteen Attributes and readers of every tradition return to.

While Moses is on the mountain in Exodus 32, the people ask Aaron for gods to go before them. He makes a , and the people say, "These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt." The scene is the first breaking of the covenant, only weeks after Sinai. Moses shatters the tablets and the calf is ground to powder. The text describes both anger and pleading. God speaks of destroying the people, and Moses argues, reminding God of the promise to Abraham. The text says God relents.

In chapter 34 Moses cuts new tablets and asks to see God’s glory. What he receives is not a sight but a self-description. The text says the LORD passed by and proclaimed: "The LORD, The LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth, Keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, and that will by no means clear the guilty; visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children" to the third and fourth generation. Mercy for thousands, judgment to the fourth generation: the ratio has struck readers for centuries.

Jewish tradition calls this passage the of mercy. It is recited on fast days and on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement. The recitation often stops before the words about not clearing the guilty. The passage echoes through the Hebrew Bible: Jonah quotes it to complain that God is too merciful, and Psalm 103 sings it. Christian readers have paired the verse with John 1:14, "full of grace and truth," which many hear as an echo of "goodness and truth." Scholars read Exodus 32–34 as the Torah’s central meditation on how a covenant survives its own breaking. The answer the text gives is a character described as slow to anger.

28.12

How Readers Read the Law

Main ideaJews, Christians and academic scholars each read the law of the Torah in a different frame, and knowing the frame explains the reading.

For Jewish tradition the law of the Torah is , commandments, and keeping them is a way of living in the covenant. A rabbinic count, recorded in the Talmud, gives 613 commandments in the Torah, and medieval scholars such as Maimonides listed them. Around the written Torah grew the Oral Torah, the Mishnah and Talmud, which work out how each law is kept: what counts as work on the sabbath, how "eye for eye" becomes payment, who is a neighbor. The law is read as a living conversation, not a closed code.

Christians read the same books as Scripture, and have differed among themselves about the law. In Matthew 5:17 Jesus says he has come not to destroy the law but to fulfill it. Paul, in Galatians 3, calls the law a "schoolmaster" that leads to Christ. From these and other texts, most Christian traditions hold that the moral commands, such as the ten words, remain binding, while the ritual and civil laws belonged to ancient Israel. Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants draw the line in somewhat different places, and Christian ethics keeps returning to Leviticus 19 and Deuteronomy.

Academic scholars read the law as ancient and history. They compare the Covenant Code to Hammurabi, the treaty form to the Hittites, the curses to Assyria, and ask when each layer was written and for whom. They notice that the Torah’s law is embedded in story, which no other ancient code does. None of these frames cancels the others. A student in a public school is asked to be able to describe all three, to say which is speaking, and to read the text closely enough to see why each finds what it finds.
