Sugyot in Paraphrase I: Time and Damages

Halakhah

The Talmud is full of stories and big ideas, but much of it is about daily life. This unit reads famous passages in paraphrase. You will see the Talmud ask what time it is, how Shabbat works, how to light Hanukkah candles, and who pays when an ox falls into a pit.

Two threads run through every passage. The first is argument: a question, a proof, an objection and an answer. The second is mercy: each ruling protects someone, such as a worker, a finder, a neighbor, or a person who might be shamed. Watch for both as you read.

By the end, you will be able to explain what a sugya is and follow its moves. You will be able to name the tractate and page of a famous passage. You will be able to say what a ruling protects and how different Jewish readers have read it.

The big question

How does the Talmud turn questions about time and harm into lessons about living with other people?

The story

The First Page Asks the Time

Two study partners open the very first page of the Talmud and find a question about the evening sky.

It is a Tuesday evening in a living room on the North Side of Chicago. Two study partners, a retired nurse and a high school junior, have a large book open between them. It is the first volume of the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Berakhot. They are part of a Daf Yomi group, people around the world who read one page a day. Today is page 2a, the very first page. The junior leans in. "So what does it start with? Creation? God?" The nurse smiles. "It starts with a question about the time."

The Mishnah's first line asks: from when may a person recite the evening Shema? The Shema is the short passage from Deuteronomy that Jewish tradition recites twice a day. The Mishnah answers with a picture, not a clock. From the hour the priests come in to eat their holy food. Then it gives three views on how late is too late. Rabbi Eliezer says the end of the first watch of the night. The Sages say midnight. Rabban Gamliel says until dawn.

The junior frowns. "Why not just say sunset?" The nurse points to the next lines. "That is what the Gemara asks, more or less." The Gemara, the commentary that surrounds the Mishnah on the page, begins with its own puzzle. Where does this teacher stand? Why does he start with the evening instead of the morning? And who said there was a duty to recite the Shema at all? The answer comes from a verse: you shall speak these words when you lie down and when you rise up.

An hour later they have read two paragraphs. They have met priests, stars, a poor man eating bread with salt, and King David's harp, which tradition says woke him at midnight. The junior closes the book. "That is a lot for one question." The nurse nods. "That is the whole Talmud. One question, argued with care. Tomorrow we do page 2b."

Talk about itWhy might a book about law and life begin with a question about time instead of a question about belief?
Section 1

The Talmud's First Question

19.1

From When Do We Recite?

Main ideaThe Mishnah opens with a question about the earliest time to say the evening Shema, and answers it with a picture of priests eating.

The Talmud is a long work, but it begins with one short line. The of Berakhot 1:1 asks: from when do we recite the in the evening? The Shema is a set of Bible verses, beginning with Deuteronomy 6:4, that Jewish tradition recites each morning and evening. Berakhot means "blessings." It is the first tractate, or book, of the Talmud, and it is about prayer.

The Mishnah does not answer with a clock time. It says: from the hour the priests enter to eat their . Terumah was a portion of the harvest given to priests in ancient Israel. A priest who had become ritually unclean had to wait until nightfall to eat it. So "when the priests enter to eat" is a way of saying "when the stars come out." The rule is told through a scene people knew.

Then the Mishnah asks how late a person may recite. It gives three answers. Rabbi Eliezer says until the end of the first watch of the night. The Sages say until midnight. Rabban Gamliel says until dawn. The Mishnah keeps all three views on the page. That is a habit the whole Talmud follows: it records disagreement instead of erasing it.

From the source
The Mishnah's First Line
From when may the Shema be recited in the evening? From the hour the priests enter to eat their terumah, until the end of the first watch; these are the words of Rabbi Eliezer. The Sages say: until midnight. Rabban Gamliel says: until the dawn comes up.
Mishnah Berakhot 1:1, paraphrased from the Hebrew; compare Rodkinson's translation (1896–1903) (paraphrased)

19.2

Where Does the Teacher Stand?

Main ideaThe Gemara's first move is to ask what the Mishnah assumes, and it finds the answer in a verse from Deuteronomy.

The is the discussion that surrounds the Mishnah on the Talmud page. It was built up by teachers called over about three centuries. On Berakhot 2a the Gemara begins with a sharp question. The Mishnah asked "from when." But it never said that a person must recite the Shema at all. Where does this teacher stand? In other words, what is he assuming?

The Gemara answers: the teacher stands on a verse. Deuteronomy 6:7 says to speak these words "when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." Rabbinic tradition reads that as the evening and morning Shema. So the Mishnah does not need to say there is a duty. It takes the verse as known and asks the next question, the time.

Then the Gemara asks a second question. Why start with the evening and not the morning? One answer points back to the verse, which puts lying down first. Another answer points to the story of creation. Genesis 1:5 says "there was evening and there was morning, one day." In Jewish tradition the day begins at nightfall. The Talmud’s first page is already doing what it will do everywhere: asking why, and proving from a text.

19.3

Three Ways to See Night

Main ideaThe Gemara compares several teachings that mark nightfall by priests, by stars and by a poor man's supper, and asks whether they agree.

How do you know when night has come? The Gemara on Berakhot 2a–2b collects several answers from early teachings called baraitot. A baraita is a teaching from the age of the Mishnah that was not put into the Mishnah itself. One baraita marks nightfall by the priests going in to eat terumah. Another marks it by the stars coming out. Another marks it by the hour a poor man sits down to his bread with salt.

The Gemara treats these like witnesses in a case. Do they say the same thing, or do they clash? If the poor man eats before the priests, then the two markers give different times. The Gemara tests each pairing. It brings a verse from Nehemiah 4 about workers who labored from dawn until the stars came out, to show that "stars" means night in the Bible too.

Notice what the Gemara is doing. It is not settling for one answer. It is lining up sources, checking one against another and asking which can stand together. This move, question, proof, objection, answer, is called a , a unit of Talmudic argument. The first sugya of the Talmud is about a sky turning dark and a poor man’s supper.

Think it through
Three Markers, One Moment

Marker A: the priests, who waited all day, go in to eat their terumah. Marker B: three stars can be seen in the sky. Marker C: a poor man, home from work, sits down to bread and salt. The Gemara asks whether A, B and C happen at the same time.

Which marker would be easiest for an ordinary person to check in a village with no clocks? Why might the Gemara want more than one?

Section 2

Shabbat and Its Thirty-Nine Labors

19.4

Forty Less One

Main ideaThe Mishnah lists thirty-nine main categories of work that Jewish law forbids on Shabbat, from sowing to carrying.

The Bible says to rest on the seventh day, , but it does not give a full list of what "work" means. Tractate Shabbat, the Talmud’s book about the day of rest, fills that gap. Mishnah Shabbat 7:2 lists the main categories of forbidden labor. It calls them "forty less one," that is, thirty-nine.

The list reads like a tour of ancient life. It begins with the steps of making bread: sowing, plowing, reaping, binding sheaves, threshing, winnowing, sorting, grinding, sifting, kneading, baking. Then come the steps of making cloth: shearing wool, washing it, combing it, dyeing, spinning, weaving and more. Then come working with hides, writing and erasing, building and tearing down, lighting and putting out a fire, and one last item: carrying from one domain to another.

Each of the thirty-nine is called an , a "father" or main category. Related actions are its "children." Grinding wheat is a father; the Talmud asks what else counts as grinding. Jewish communities differ on how the list applies to modern life. Orthodox practice treats the list as binding and works out cases like electricity. Reform Judaism leaves much of this to personal choice. Conservative Judaism has ruled on some cases, such as driving to synagogue, in its own way.

19.5

Why the Tabernacle?

Main ideaThe Talmud explains the thirty-nine labors as the kinds of work used to build the tabernacle in the wilderness.

Where does the list of thirty-nine come from? The Bible does not print it. The Gemara in Shabbat 49b gives an answer from the age of the amoraim. Rabbi Chanina bar Chama teaches that the categories match the labors used to build the , the portable sanctuary the Israelites carried through the wilderness in the book of Exodus. Sowing and reaping grew plants for dyes. Shearing, spinning and weaving made the curtains. Writing marked the boards.

Why connect the two? In Exodus 35, Moses tells the people about Shabbat and then, in the very next lines, about building the tabernacle. Rabbinic readers took that order as a hint. The work that was holy enough to build the sanctuary is the work that stops on the holy day. Another teacher in the Gemara counts the word "labor" in the Torah and finds it thirty-nine times. The Talmud keeps both explanations.

Historians describe this list as the rabbis’ way of turning a broad command into something a person could actually keep. Jewish readers across the centuries have called the tabernacle link a beautiful idea: rest mirrors the building of a holy place. Either way, the lesson for a student of the Talmud is the method. A number appears in the Mishnah; the Gemara asks where it came from; the answer is found in the Torah’s own arrangement.

19.6

Carrying and the Eruv

Main ideaCarrying between a private and a public space is one of the thirty-nine labors, and the eruv is the rabbis' way of making a shared space count as one home.

The very first Mishnah of tractate Shabbat is about carrying, not cooking or lighting. It pictures a poor man standing in the street and a homeowner standing inside his door. If one reaches in and takes an object from the other’s hand, who has "carried"? The Mishnah works through the cases. Carrying from a private , like a house, to a public one, like a street, is the thirty-ninth labor.

This rule creates a problem for a family. Can a parent carry a baby to a neighbor’s home on Shabbat? Can someone bring food to a courtyard shared by many houses? The rabbis of the Mishnah developed the . The word means "mixing" or "joining." Households in a shared courtyard put a little food in one place before Shabbat. That act joins them into a single household for the day, so they may carry within the shared space. A whole tractate, Eruvin, is about this.

Today a community eruv is often a thin wire or string on poles that marks the edge of an area, with the food set aside in one place. Many Orthodox communities keep one; Chicago has eruvin in several neighborhoods. Some Jewish readers see the eruv as a clever act of mercy that keeps the law and keeps families together. Others, inside and outside Judaism, see it as a legal fiction. The Talmud itself shows the rabbis arguing over its limits in detail.

Section 3

Hanukkah on Shabbat 21b

19.7

What Is Hanukkah?

Main ideaThe Talmud tells the story of one jar of oil that burned eight days, while the books of Maccabees tell of the same festival without the oil.

Hanukkah is not in the Hebrew Bible. So the Gemara in Shabbat 21b, in the middle of a discussion about oil and wicks, stops and asks: what is ? Its answer is a baraita. In the days of the Greeks, the Greeks entered the sanctuary and made all its oil unfit. When the , the priestly family who led the revolt, won and entered the Temple, they searched and found one small jar of oil still sealed by the High Priest. It held enough for one day. A miracle happened, the baraita says, and they lit from it for eight days. The next year those days were fixed as a festival of praise and thanks.

Historians date the events to about 164 BCE, when Judah Maccabee’s forces rededicated the Temple in Jerusalem. The word Hanukkah means "dedication." The books of 1 and 2 Maccabees, written closer to the events, describe an eight-day celebration of the rededication. They do not mention the jar of oil. The oil story appears in rabbinic sources.

Readers handle the gap in different ways. Traditional Jewish readers accept the Talmud’s account as history preserved by the rabbis. Many academic readers see the oil story as a later teaching that shifted the holiday’s meaning from a military victory to a quiet miracle. The Talmud itself is not very interested in the battles. It asks what Hanukkah is and answers with a lamp.

A miracle happened, and they lit from it for eight days. The next year they fixed these days as a festival with praise and thanksgiving.

19.8

Hillel and Shammai on the Lights

Main ideaThe House of Shammai lights eight candles the first night and fewer each night; the House of Hillel starts with one and adds, because holiness should grow.

The same page, Shabbat 21b, records a famous disagreement. The basic duty, the Gemara says, is one lamp for each household each night. Those who do more light one for each person. And those who do the most? Here the two great schools of the first century BCE and first century CE part ways. The says: on the first night light eight, and go down one each night. The says: on the first night light one, and go up one each night.

The Gemara gives reasons for both. For Shammai’s school, the number matches the days still to come; it is also like the bulls offered on the festival of Sukkot, which decrease each day. For Hillel’s school, the number matches the days already passed, and there is a rule: in matters of holiness we go up, not down.

Notice how the Talmud treats the losing side. It does not call the House of Shammai wrong. It explains their logic with care and keeps it on the page. Jewish tradition holds that in most disputes the law follows Hillel’s school. But a student who reads only Hillel’s view has read half the page.

19.9

We Light Like Hillel

Main ideaJewish practice follows the House of Hillel and places the lamp where passersby can see it, because the point is to make the miracle known.

Jewish communities today light the way the House of Hillel taught: one flame the first night, eight on the last. Adding the helper candle, the , which lights the others, a family uses forty-four candles over the holiday. Ashkenazi and Sephardi customs differ on small points, such as whether each person in a home lights a separate lamp. The rule to increase each night is shared.

Shabbat 21b also asks where and when to light. The lamp is placed at the door facing the street, or, for someone upstairs, in a window. The Gemara gives the reason in two words: , making the miracle known. The lighting is a public act, meant to be seen. The time is from sunset until the last foot leaves the market, that is, while people are still passing by.

Then the Gemara adds a note of mercy. In a time of danger, when it was not safe to show a Jewish lamp outside, a person may set it on the table indoors, and that is enough. The law bends to protect the life of the one keeping it. Many Chicago homes and public squares light large menorahs each December; the practice on the street goes back to this page.

Section 4

Time, Mercy and Method

19.10

The Sons Who Came Home Late

Main ideaThe Mishnah's first page shows a strict rule, midnight, made not to punish but to keep people from putting things off.

Mishnah Berakhot 1:1 ends with a small story. Rabban Gamliel’s sons came home from a wedding feast after midnight. They had not yet recited the evening Shema. They asked their father whether it was too late. He told them: if the dawn has not come up, you must recite. His own view was that the duty lasts until dawn.

Then he explained the Sages, who had said midnight. They did not mean that the duty ends at midnight, he says. They set the earlier time to keep a person far from wrongdoing. If people think they have until dawn, they may fall asleep and miss it. Midnight is a fence built around the real limit.

This idea of a around the law appears across the Talmud. A rule is set earlier or stricter than the Torah requires, so that people do not slip. Readers see two things in Rabban Gamliel’s answer. One is the argument: he explains a rival view rather than mocking it. The other is mercy: he tells tired young men who came home late that they have not failed. The first page of the Talmud holds both.

19.11

Time Is the First Subject

Main ideaThe Talmud opens with time because Jewish law, as the rabbis shaped it, turns ordinary hours into a pattern of duties and rest.

Why does a work about all of Jewish life open with clocks and calendars? Look at the order. The first tractate, Berakhot, asks when to pray. The second order of the Mishnah, called Moed or "appointed times," is entirely about Shabbat and the holidays. Hanukkah’s rules appear inside Shabbat, because the question was about oil that may be used for lamps on Shabbat.

Time in the Talmud is measured by the world, not by machines. Nightfall is when stars appear. The day starts at evening, following Genesis 1:5. Hours are twelfths of daylight, so a winter hour is shorter than a summer hour. The Shema, Shabbat and Hanukkah each take a slice of ordinary time and give it a shape.

Different readers explain this differently. Many religious readers say time is the first subject because holiness in Judaism lives in when, not only in where; after the Temple was destroyed, sacred time replaced sacred space. Academic readers point out that the Mishnah’s editors simply began with daily practice, the Shema and blessings, before moving to the farm and the festivals. Both readings agree on the fact: the Talmud begins by asking what time it is.

A study class in Chicago spends a whole evening on one open hole in the road.

Four Fathers of Damage

20.1

The Mishnah's Four Fathers

Main ideaThe Mishnah sorts harm into four main kinds, the ox, the pit, the grazing animal and the fire, and asks who is responsible.

Tractate Bava Kamma, whose name means "the first gate," opens with a list. The Mishnah says there are four , four "fathers" or main kinds of damage. They are the ox, the pit, the grazing animal and the fire. Each one comes from a law in the Torah, mostly in Exodus 21 and 22.

Why call them fathers? In the Talmud’s language, a father is a main category. Smaller cases that work the same way are its "children." An ox that gores is a father. A goat that butts is like a child of that father. The list helps a judge sort a new case by asking which old case it looks like.

The Gemara then asks what each word really means. The Mishnah’s third item is the maveh. Rav, a teacher in Babylonia around 220 CE, says it means the animal’s tooth, eating in someone’s field. His colleague Shmuel says it means a person who causes harm. The Gemara keeps both views and tests each one. That is how a often starts: with the meaning of one word.

There are four main kinds of damage: the ox, the pit, the grazing animal and the fire. They are not all alike. What they share is that each one tends to cause harm, and the one who is responsible for it must pay from the best of his land.

20.2

The Pit in the Street

Main ideaThe Torah makes the person who digs and leaves open a pit pay for an animal that falls in, and the rabbis ask what counts as a pit.

Exodus 21:33 describes a man who opens or digs a pit and does not cover it. An ox or a donkey falls in. The Torah says the owner of the pit must pay. The rabbis read this as a lesson about hazards. A danger you create and leave for others is your responsibility.

The Mishnah and Gemara then press on the details. How deep must a pit be to kill an animal? The Mishnah gives ten handbreadths. What if a person digs in the public street, not on their own land? What if they covered it, but badly? What about a stone or a bucket left in the road? The Gemara treats these as children of the pit.

The pit is different from the ox. An ox moves and chooses. A pit just waits. Yet both can hurt a stranger who did nothing wrong. Readers have seen the pit laws as a way of protecting anyone who walks in a shared space. A city sidewalk left with an open hole is a modern pit.

20.3

Fire That Spreads

Main ideaFire is a father of damage because it spreads beyond the one who lit it, so the one who lit it must be careful where it goes.

Exodus 22:5 imagines a fire that catches in thorns and spreads to a neighbor’s grain. The Torah says the one who started the fire must pay. The Mishnah lists fire as the fourth father of damage.

Fire is a strange kind of harm. You light it in one place. Wind carries it to another. The Gemara asks whether fire is like your arrows, harm you send out yourself, or like your property, something you own that gets loose. Rabbi Yochanan and Resh Lakish take different sides. Each view changes who pays for what.

The Mishnah also asks how far a fire may be from a wall or a field. It asks what happens if a person gave a burning coal to a child who could not be careful. In that case, the Mishnah says, the one who handed it over is exempt under human law but liable under the laws of Heaven. Readers see a lesson here. Some duties go beyond what a court can enforce.

The Ox That Gored

20.4

The Innocent Ox and the Warned Ox

Main ideaAn ox with no history of goring pays half the damage, while an ox known to gore pays full damage, because its owner was warned.

Exodus 21:28–36 describes an ox that gores. The Torah treats two cases differently. In one, the ox has never gored before. In the other, the ox has gored before and the owner was warned, but did not guard it.

The Mishnah gives these two cases names. An ox with no record is , which means "innocent" or "simple." An ox that has gored three times, with the owner warned, becomes mu&#x27;ad, "warned" or "forewarned." For damage to other animals, a tam ox’s owner pays half the damage, and only from the ox’s own value. A mu’ad ox’s owner pays in full.

Why the difference? The owner of a tam ox could not easily know. The owner of a mu’ad ox had been told and did nothing. The rule weighs what the owner could have known. It protects the victim, and it also is fair to an owner who was surprised. Many readers compare it to modern rules about a dog known to bite.

20.5

An Eye for an Eye

Main ideaThe Talmud reads "an eye for an eye" as a rule about paying the value of the injury, not about harming the one who injured.

Exodus 21:24 says, "eye for eye, tooth for tooth." Read alone, it sounds like a rule of revenge. The Mishnah and the Gemara read it another way. They hold that it means the one who injures must pay money for the value of what was lost.

The Gemara on Bava Kamma 83b–84a argues for this reading. It asks: what if a blind man blinds someone? What if a person with weak eyes blinds a person with strong ones? Taking an eye back could never be equal. It also points to a verse that says a court may not take money in place of a life for a murderer, and argues that for injuries money may be taken. Several rabbis bring several proofs.

Rabbinic tradition holds that the money reading was how the verse was always meant. Many modern historians say the reading grew over time. Either way, Jewish law as the Talmud develops it did not take eyes. It measured the harm and paid for it. Readers have seen mercy in that move, for both the victim and the one at fault.

20.6

Five Payments for an Injury

Main ideaThe Mishnah says a person who injures another pays for five things: the damage, the pain, the healing, lost work and the shame.

Mishnah Bava Kamma 8:1 lists what a person who injures another must pay. There are five parts. The damage is the lasting loss. The pain is the suffering. The healing is the doctor’s cost. The lost work is the time the person could not earn. The last is , the shame or embarrassment.

The list is careful. The Mishnah does not stop at the body. It counts the hours of work lost and the feeling of being shamed. A person hurt in front of others may carry that hurt longer than a bruise. The Mishnah even says the payment for shame depends on who shamed and who was shamed.

This is where the Talmud’s argument and its mercy meet. The rules ask a court to look at the whole person. Readers today compare the list to modern injury law, which also pays for medical bills, lost wages and pain. The Talmud’s list adds shame, and treats a person’s dignity as something that can be harmed.

20.7

Two Hold a Cloak

Main ideaWhen two people both grab a found cloak and each claims it, the Mishnah has each swear and they divide it.

Tractate Bava Metzia, "the middle gate," opens with a famous case. Two people hold one cloak. One says, "I found it. It is all mine." The other says the same. No one saw who picked it up first. What should a court do?

The Mishnah on Bava Metzia 2a answers: each one swears an oath that they own no less than half, and they split it. Then it changes the case. What if one says "It is all mine" and the other says "Half is mine"? The second person already agrees that half belongs to the first. Only the other half is in dispute. So the first gets three quarters and the second one quarter.

The Gemara spends many pages on this mishnah. Why an oath? Why not let whoever is stronger keep it? The rabbis worry that a person might grab what is not theirs. The oath asks each person to face the truth. The split protects both from losing everything when no one can know for certain.

Lost Objects and Giving Up Hope

Main ideaThe Torah tells a finder to return lost things, and the rabbis ask when an owner has given up hope, which the Talmud calls ye'ush.

Deuteronomy 22:1–3 tells a person who finds a neighbor’s lost animal or garment to return it. It ends with a strong line: you may not look away. The rabbis read this as a , a commandment, called returning lost objects.

The Mishnah on Bava Metzia 2:1 lists things a finder may keep, such as scattered fruit or coins with no mark. Why? The owner cannot identify them and has surely given up hope. The Talmud calls giving up hope ye&#x27;ush, "despair." Once an owner despairs, the object may belong to the finder. If an item has a mark, like a knot or a name, the finder must announce it.

The Gemara asks a hard question. What if the owner does not know yet that the thing is lost? Abaye and Rava, teachers in Babylonia around 330 CE, disagree. Most of the Gemara’s long discussion follows from their debate. The rules try to protect the owner’s hope while being fair to an honest finder.

20.9

Wronging With Words

Main ideaThe Mishnah teaches that hurting someone with words is a real wrong, which it calls ona'at devarim.

Leviticus 25:17 says, in plain words, do not wrong one another. The Mishnah reads it as a rule about speech. It calls this ona&#x27;at devarim, "wronging with words." A person can be cheated with money. A person can also be hurt with a sentence.

Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:10 gives examples. Do not remind a person who has changed their ways about what they used to do. Do not remind a convert’s child of the deeds of their ancestors. Do not ask a seller the price of something if you have no plan to buy it. Each example protects someone from shame or false hope.

The Gemara on Bava Metzia 58b adds a sharp teaching. Shaming someone in public is compared to shedding blood, because the face goes pale. The rabbis say that wronging with words is in some ways worse than cheating with money. Money can be paid back. Words can not be taken back in the same way.

Love Your Neighbor

Main ideaRabbi Akiva called "love your neighbor as yourself" a great principle of the Torah, and other rabbis offered other great principles.

His colleague Ben Azzai named a different verse: the one saying that humans are made in the likeness of God.

These teachings link the damage laws to something larger. The ox, the pit, the lost cloak and the cruel word are all about neighbors. Readers have long seen the laws of Bava Kamma and Bava Metzia as the details of this principle. Different Jewish movements read the principle in different ways, but many point to it.

Main ideaThe Talmud tells of a judge who told a rich man to pay poor workers even after they broke his barrels, beyond what the strict law required.

Leviticus 19:13 says a worker’s wages must not stay with the employer overnight. The rabbis read this as a rule to pay workers on time. A worker who lives day to day needs the money now. The Mishnah and Gemara on Bava Metzia work out when and how wages must be paid.

Bava Metzia 83a tells a story. Some porters broke a barrel of wine while carrying it for Rabbah bar bar Hanan. He took their cloaks to cover his loss. They went to Rav. Rav told him to give back their cloaks. The porters said they were poor, had worked all day and were hungry. Rav told him to pay their wages too.

Rabbah asked, "Is that the law?" Rav answered, "Yes," and quoted Proverbs 2:20 about walking in the way of good people. Readers call this going , beyond the line of the law. The story shows a judge weighing the poor worker’s need, not only the strict rules.

Unit test

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. What is the first question of the Talmud, on Berakhot 2a?

Why: Mishnah Berakhot 1:1 opens the Talmud by asking about the time of the evening Shema.

2. How many main categories of Shabbat work does the Mishnah list?

Why: Mishnah Shabbat 7:2 lists forty less one, 39 categories of work (melakhot).

3. Where does rabbinic tradition find the categories of Shabbat work?

Why: The Gemara links the melakhot to the work done to build the tabernacle.

4. On Shabbat 21b, whose way of lighting Hanukkah candles does the law follow?

Why: The Gemara reports Beit Hillel adds a light each night, and practice follows Hillel.

5. What is an eruv for, in rabbinic law?

Why: An eruv joins spaces so that carrying within it is permitted on Shabbat.

6. Which is one of the four fathers of damage?

Why: Mishnah Bava Kamma 1:1 lists the ox, the pit, the grazing animal and the fire.

7. Why does the owner of a tam ox pay only half?

Why: Tam means innocent: the owner had no warning.

8. How does the Talmud read "eye for an eye" in Bava Kamma 83b–84a?

Why: The Gemara brings several proofs that the verse means monetary compensation.

9. What does the case of the two who hold one cloak teach?

Why: Bava Metzia 2a has each swear, then divides the cloak.

10. What is a siman?

Why: A finder must announce an object with a siman so the owner can claim it.

11. What does ona'at devarim protect?

Why: Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:10 forbids wronging a person with words.

12. What did Hillel say was the whole Torah, on Shabbat 31a?

Why: Hillel summed up the Torah this way, then told the man to go and learn.

13. In the porters' story on Bava Metzia 83a, what does Rav quote?

Why: Rav cites Proverbs 2:20 when telling Rabbah bar bar Hanan to pay the workers.

14. What is a sugya?

Why: A sugya is one unit of argument in the Gemara.

15. What are the names of the three "Gates" (Bava Kamma, Bava Metzia, Bava Batra) based on?

Why: Bava Kamma, Bava Metzia and Bava Batra mean the first, middle and last gate.

Five questions from earlier units

0 / 5

1. (Unit 9) Whom does tradition call "the end of instruction"?

Why: Bava Metzia 86a says this of Rav Ashi and Ravina.

2. (Unit 8) Why does Mishnah Eduyot 1:5 record a minority view?

Why: Eduyot 1:5 says a lone view is kept so a later court may rely on it.

3. (Unit 9) What is an amora?

Why: Amoraim taught c. 220–500 CE; their discussions became the Gemara.

4. (Unit 8) What did Hillel prove in Pesachim 66a using a gezerah shavah?

Why: Hillel linked "in its appointed season" in two laws to show the Passover offering overrides the Sabbath.

5. (Unit 9) Which rabbi, the Jerusalem Talmud says, hailed Bar Kokhba as a king-messiah?

Why: The Jerusalem Talmud, Ta'anit 4, tells that Rabbi Akiva said this, and another rabbi disagreed.

Write it

What does the Talmud think a person owes to others? Choose two or three passages from this unit (for example Bava Kamma 1:1, Bava Metzia 2a, Bava Metzia 58b or 83a, Shabbat 21b). Explain what each says and how its readers have read it.

  • Name each passage by tractate and page, or Mishnah chapter and number.
  • Say who is speaking: the Mishnah, the Gemara, or a named rabbi.
  • Show the argument: the question, the proof and the answer.
  • Say what or whom the ruling protects.
  • Describe how readers read it; do not judge the tradition.
