Social Class in Europe

Europe

For hundreds of years, most Europeans were born into a rank they could not easily leave. A serf's child became a serf. A noble's child became a noble. Then plague, towns, revolutions and machines began to shake that order loose. In this unit you will follow those changes from the medieval manor to the industrial city.

You will meet knights swearing oaths, barons facing down a king, and French commoners swearing an oath on a tennis court. You will stand inside a Manchester cotton mill and read the words of Adam Smith, Karl Marx and their critics. Each group saw fairness in its own way, and you will hear from all of them.

By the end, you will be able to explain how social class worked in Europe, name the events that changed it, and compare big ideas about work and wealth. You will use evidence to make your own claim, and you will treat people who disagree with care.

The big question

What decides a person's place in society, and how have people worked to change it?

Feudalism and the Three Estates

Medieval Europe
Big questionHow did a society built on fixed ranks slowly change, and what finally broke it apart?
The story

The Oath on the Tennis Court

In June 1789, locked out of their meeting hall, hundreds of French commoners made a promise that shook a kingdom.

On the morning of June 20, 1789, a crowd of men in plain black coats walked through the rain at Versailles, outside Paris. They were deputies of the Third Estate, the group that spoke for ordinary French people. They came to their meeting hall and found the doors shut. Soldiers stood guard. Workmen said the room was being made ready for a royal ceremony.

The deputies had reason to worry. King Louis XVI had called the Estates-General, an old assembly of the three ranks of French society. It had not met since 1614. The clergy and the nobles each voted as one group. So did the commoners. Two groups could outvote the third every time, even though the commoners were almost the whole nation.

Someone remembered a big indoor court nearby, where nobles played an early form of tennis. The deputies crowded inside. There were no seats worth mentioning, only bare walls and a wooden floor. Their leader, an astronomer named Jean-Sylvain Bailly, stood on a table. One by one, the deputies swore not to separate until France had a written constitution.

Only one man refused to sign. Everyone else raised a hand or signed the paper. A few days later, some clergy and nobles joined them. Within weeks, Paris rose up and the old order began to fall. The system of three estates had lasted for centuries. On a tennis court, it began to end.

Land, Loyalty and Lords

15.1

Oaths, Lords and Vassals

Main ideaFeudalism was a system where land was given in return for loyalty and military service.

Picture a knight kneeling before a great lord in a stone hall around the year 1100. The knight puts his hands between the lord’s hands. He promises to be the lord’s man and to fight for him. The lord promises to protect him. Then the lord hands him a clod of earth or a stick, a sign that the knight now holds a piece of land.

This bond is what historians call . The lord gave land to a follower. The follower who received it was a . The land itself was a . In return, the vassal owed loyalty, advice and usually about forty days of fighting each year. The promise of loyalty was called fealty, and it was sworn on holy objects.

Feudalism grew in western Europe after the empire of Charlemagne broke apart in the 800s. Kings were weak and raiders were strong. Local lords with armed followers kept order where they could. The system was never exactly the same everywhere. Many historians today say ’feudalism’ is a tidy label for a messy set of local customs.

15.2

Life on the Manor

Main ideaMost medieval people were peasants who farmed a lord's manor, and many were serfs tied to the land.

Imagine a village in England around 1250. There is a stone manor house, a church, a mill, and cottages of wood and mud. Around them stretch long strips of plowed field. The lord of the village owns all of it. Historians call this unit of land and people a .

Most families on a manor were peasants, and many were . A serf was not a slave. He usually could not be sold away from his family, and he had his own strip of land to farm. But he could not leave the manor without the lord’s permission. He owed the lord work on the lord’s own fields, often a few days a week, and paid fees to use the lord’s mill and oven.

Serfs lived hard lives, but they were not helpless. Villages had customs that even a lord was expected to respect. Peasants took disputes to the manor court. Over time, many serfs bought their freedom or ran away to towns. Life was different from place to place, and some peasants were free farmers from the start.

15.3

The Church at the Center

Main ideaThe Catholic Church shaped daily life, owned great amounts of land, and collected a tithe from almost everyone.

In a medieval village, the tallest building was usually the church. Its bell marked the hours of work and prayer. Babies were baptized there, couples married there, and the dead were buried beside it. For most western Europeans, the Catholic Church gave life its meaning and its calendar of feasts and holy days.

The Church was also rich and powerful. Bishops and abbots controlled huge estates, often with their own serfs. Almost everyone paid a , one tenth of their harvest or income, to support the Church. Monasteries copied books, ran schools and cared for the sick and the poor.

People inside the Church often argued about its wealth. Francis of Assisi, in the early 1200s, gave up his family’s riches and asked his followers to live in poverty. Critics then and later said some church leaders lived like nobles. Supporters pointed to the hospitals, schools and charity the Church provided.

Section 2

Ranks, Plague and Change

15.4

Those Who Pray, Fight and Work

Main ideaMedieval writers described society as three orders, or estates: clergy, nobles and commoners.

Around the year 1000, a few church writers described society with a simple picture. Some people pray. Some people fight. Some people work. Each group was supposed to serve the others, like parts of one body. This idea became the three .

The First Estate was the clergy, who prayed for everyone’s souls. The Second Estate was the , families who held land and fought as knights. The Third Estate was everyone else: peasants, craftsmen, merchants and later lawyers and bankers. The first two estates were small but held special rights, such as freedom from many taxes.

The picture was an ideal, not a photograph. A rich merchant and a poor serf were both in the Third Estate, yet their lives were very different. A younger son of a noble might become a bishop. Still, the idea of three orders lasted for centuries. In France it shaped law and taxes right up to 1789.

15.5

The Black Death

Main ideaThe plague of 1347–51 killed a huge share of Europe's people and left the survivors in a changed world.

In 1347, ships from the Black Sea docked in Sicily. Soon people in the port were falling sick with fever and painful swellings. Within four years, the disease had swept across nearly all of Europe. People later called it the .

Today scientists know the cause was a germ called Yersinia pestis, spread by fleas on rats and, some researchers think, from person to person. People at the time did not know about germs. Some blamed bad air. Some saw punishment from God. In many towns, angry crowds blamed Jewish neighbors and murdered them, a terrible crime against innocent people.

Historians disagree on exact numbers. Most say that between about one third and one half of Europe’s people died. Some villages were emptied. Whole families vanished. Survivors faced a world with far fewer hands to plant crops, weave cloth and build walls.

15.6

Workers After the Plague

Main ideaWith so many dead, surviving workers asked for higher pay, and lords tried hard to stop them.

After the plague, a lord in England might find half his fields empty. The peasants who were left knew their work was now precious. They asked for higher wages. If one lord said no, they could move to a manor that said yes. This is a : too few workers for the work that needs doing.

Lords and kings pushed back. In 1351 the English Parliament passed the Statute of Labourers. It tried to hold wages at the level they had been before the plague. Many workers ignored it, and many lords broke it quietly to keep their fields farmed.

Anger grew. In 1381, peasants in England rose up in what is called the Peasants’ Revolt. Led by Wat Tyler and a priest named John Ball, they marched on London. The revolt was crushed and Tyler was killed. Yet over the next century, serfdom slowly faded in England, as lords found it easier to rent land or pay workers.

Limits on Kings, Freedom in Towns

15.7

Magna Carta

Main ideaIn 1215 English barons forced King John to accept Magna Carta, which said even a king must follow the law.

In June 1215, King John of England rode to a meadow called Runnymede, beside the River Thames. Waiting for him were barons, powerful nobles who were furious. John had lost wars in France and squeezed them for money again and again. They had taken up arms, and London had opened its gates to them.

At Runnymede the king agreed to a long list of promises. The document became known as , Latin for ’Great Charter’. One famous clause said no free man could be jailed or have his property taken except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land. The idea that the ruler is bound by law is called the .

Magna Carta was mainly a deal between a king and nobles. Most of its promises did not help serfs. John quickly got the Pope to cancel it, and war followed. But later kings reissued it, and over centuries people used it to argue that no ruler is above the law. Its ideas later influenced the United States Constitution.

15.8

Towns, Guilds and Merchants

Main ideaGrowing towns gave merchants and craftsmen a new kind of life outside the manor, organized by guilds.

By the 1200s, towns like Florence, Paris, Bruges and London were bustling. Walls ringed markets full of cloth, spices, wool and wine. Many towns won charters from kings or lords that let them govern themselves. A serf who ran to a town and lived there a year and a day could often claim his freedom. A German saying held that ’town air makes you free’.

Inside towns, craftsmen organized . A guild was an association of people in one trade, such as bakers or weavers. It set prices, standards and training. A boy began as an , living with a master for years. Next he became a journeyman who worked for wages. If he made a fine ’masterpiece’, he could become a master himself.

Merchants grew rich, and some became richer than nobles. Yet they still belonged to the Third Estate. Guilds protected members but also kept outsiders out, and women’s work was often limited, though some widows ran their husbands’ shops. Towns showed that a person could rise through work and trade, not only through birth.

Section 4

1789: The End of the Estates

15.9

A Kingdom in Crisis

Main ideaBy 1789 France was deep in debt, bread was expensive, and the Third Estate paid most of the taxes.

In the spring of 1789, a loaf of bread in Paris could cost more than half a worker’s daily pay. A bad harvest the year before had pushed prices up. At the same time, the French government was nearly broke. It had spent heavily on wars, including helping the Americans win independence from Britain.

The burden fell unevenly. Nobles and clergy were free from many taxes. Peasants paid taxes to the king, dues to lords and tithes to the Church. Educated members of the Third Estate, such as lawyers and doctors, resented being treated as lesser people. Many had read writers of the Enlightenment, who argued that people have natural rights.

To raise money, Louis XVI called the Estates-General for May 1789. A priest named Emmanuel Sieyès wrote a famous pamphlet asking, ’What is the Third Estate?’ His answer was that it was everything, yet it had been treated as nothing. The pamphlet spread quickly and gave commoners a new sense of their own importance.

15.10

The Old Order Falls

Main ideaIn 1789 the National Assembly ended noble privileges and declared that people are born free and equal in rights.

After the Tennis Court Oath, the Third Estate called itself the . It claimed to speak for the whole nation. On July 14, 1789, crowds in Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison. In the countryside, frightened and angry peasants attacked lords’ manor houses and burned the records of their dues.

On the night of August 4, 1789, members of the Assembly stood up one after another and gave up old privileges. Nobles’ special rights, church tithes and many feudal dues were ended. Legally, the three estates were gone. Every French man was now a citizen under one law.

Weeks later, on August 26, the Assembly approved the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. Its first article said men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Critics noted it did not give women equal rights. The writer Olympe de Gouges answered in 1791 with a declaration of the rights of woman.

15.11

Hope and Terror

Main ideaThe Revolution ended legal ranks, but it also turned violent, and people still argue about its meaning.

The Revolution did not stop in 1789. In 1792 France became a republic, and in January 1793 Louis XVI was executed. War broke out with neighboring kingdoms. A group led by Maximilien Robespierre took control and began what is called the . Suspected enemies were tried quickly and sent to the guillotine.

Historians commonly count about 17,000 official death sentences during the Terror, and many more people died in prisons and in fighting inside France. Robespierre himself was executed in 1794. Years later, in 1799, the general Napoleon Bonaparte seized power.

People have argued about the Revolution ever since. Supporters say it ended feudal privilege and spread the ideas of equal citizens and rights. The British writer Edmund Burke warned in 1790 that tearing down old customs all at once would lead to chaos and tyranny. Many historians say both sides point to something real.

Factories, Workers and the Middle Class

Industrial Europe
Big questionWhen machines changed how people worked, who gained, who paid the cost, and how did societies respond?
The story

The Piecer

In a Manchester cotton mill in the 1830s, a ten-year-old girl spends her day chasing broken threads.

The mill bell rings at half past five in the morning. Mary is ten years old. She pulls on her shawl and hurries through the dark streets of Manchester with hundreds of other workers. The air smells of coal smoke. Inside the mill, the noise of the spinning machines is so loud that people talk with their hands.

Mary's job is called piecing. The spinning machine, called a mule, rolls forward and back, twisting cotton into thread. When a thread snaps, Mary must reach in and twist the ends together before the machine rolls back. She is small, so she fits where adults cannot. She walks back and forth all day, and she must keep her fingers clear of the moving parts.

The workday lasts about twelve hours, with short breaks for meals. The overseer watches for anyone who slows down or dozes off. Cotton dust floats in the warm, damp air and settles in her hair and lungs. Mary earns only a few shillings a week, but her family needs every penny to pay rent and buy bread.

Mary is not one real girl. She stands for many thousands of children who worked in British mills in these years. In 1832 some of them told their stories to a committee of Parliament. What they said helped change the law, and it began a long debate about work, wealth and fairness that still goes on.

The Machine Age Begins

16.1

Why Britain Went First

Main ideaThe Industrial Revolution began in Britain from about the 1760s, helped by coal, capital, trade and new inventions.

In 1700, almost all cloth in Britain was spun and woven by hand, often in family cottages. By 1850, much of it came from huge buildings full of machines. This great shift from hand tools to machines and factories is called the . It began in Britain from about the 1760s.

Historians point to several reasons Britain went first. It had lots of coal and iron close together. It had merchants and banks with money to invest, called . Its colonies and trade gave it raw cotton and markets for its goods. It also had inventors and tinkerers who kept improving machines.

One key invention was a better steam engine. In the 1760s and 1770s, James Watt improved earlier engines so they wasted far less fuel. Steam engines could pump water from mines and later run machines anywhere, not just beside rivers. Historians still debate how much each reason mattered, and some stress that raw cotton often came from enslaved labor in the Americas.

The Factory System

Main ideaFactories brought workers and machines together under one roof, run by the clock.

In 1771, Richard Arkwright opened a cotton-spinning mill at Cromford in Derbyshire. Its machines were driven by a water wheel. Hundreds of workers, many of them children, tended them in shifts. Arkwright is often called a founder of the .

In the old way, a weaver worked at home and set her own hours. In a , the owner owned the machines and the building. Workers came at a fixed time and left at a fixed time. Bells and clocks ruled the day. A worker who came late could lose pay.

Factories could make cloth far faster and cheaper than hand workers. Prices of cotton goods fell, and ordinary people could afford more clothes. But many hand weavers lost their living. Some, called Luddites, smashed machines in the 1810s in protest. The government sent soldiers, and machine-breaking was made a crime punishable by death.

16.3

Crowded Cities

Main ideaFactories drew millions of people into fast-growing cities that were often crowded and unhealthy.

Manchester was a modest market town in the mid-1700s. By 1851 it held about 300,000. People poured in from farms, and from Ireland, to find work in the mills. The growth of cities is called .

Builders threw up rows of cheap houses, packed tightly together. Many families lived in one room or a damp cellar. There were few sewers and little clean water. Diseases like cholera and typhus spread quickly. In the poorest districts, many children did not live to the age of five.

City life also brought new chances. There were shops, newspapers, chapels, and later libraries and schools. Reformers such as Edwin Chadwick, in a report of 1842, showed that dirty water and poor drains caused disease. Slowly, cities built sewers and water systems that saved many lives.

Section 2

New Classes, New Lives

16.4

Children at Work

Main ideaChildren worked long hours in mills and mines, and their testimony helped change public opinion.

Children had always worked on farms and in family shops. What changed in the factory age was the scale and the pace. In mills, children tied threads and crawled under machines to sweep. In coal mines, some children sat alone in the dark, opening and closing air doors for coal carts.

In 1832, a committee of Parliament led by Michael Sadler heard from mill workers. Many said they had started work at eight or younger. They told of twelve- to fourteen-hour days, beatings for being slow and injuries from machines. Critics then said Sadler chose the worst cases, and historians still discuss how typical they were.

Many parents sent children to work because families needed the wages to survive. Some mill owners argued that work kept children out of trouble. Others, like the reformer Lord Ashley (later the Earl of Shaftesbury), argued that children needed schooling and rest. This debate shaped laws on for the rest of the century.

16.5

The Working Class

Main ideaFactory workers who earned wages and owned no property became a new, large working class.

In a medieval village, a serf was tied to a lord. In an industrial city, a worker was tied to a wage. People who lived by selling their labor for wages, and owned little else, came to be called the . By the mid-1800s they were the largest group in Britain’s cities.

Work was often unsteady. When orders fell, mills cut hours or closed, and workers could be laid off with no help. There was no sick pay and no pension. Many families pooled wages from parents and children. Friendly societies, which members paid into each week, helped cover funerals and illness.

Workers built their own culture too. They founded chapels, reading rooms, choirs and cooperative shops. In 1844 a group of workers in Rochdale opened a cooperative store owned by its customers. Many workers also joined the Chartist movement of the 1830s and 1840s, which asked for the vote for all men.

16.6

The Rising Middle Class

Main ideaFactory owners, merchants and professionals formed a growing middle class with new wealth and influence.

Picture a brick house on a quiet street at the edge of Manchester, far from the smoke. A mill owner lives here with his family and a servant or two. His children go to school. On Sunday the family attends chapel, and in the evening they read novels by the fire. This family belongs to the .

The middle class included factory owners, merchants, bankers, doctors, lawyers, clerks and shopkeepers. They were not nobles, and most did not do manual work. Many valued hard work, saving, education and respectability. They saw themselves as people who had earned their place.

The middle class gained political power as well. The Reform Act of 1832 gave the vote to many middle-class men in Britain, though not to most workers. Some middle-class people gave to charity and pushed for reform. Critics said many ignored the poverty that made their wealth possible.

Section 3

Workers Organize, Laws Change

16.7

Trade Unions

Main ideaWorkers formed trade unions to bargain together for better pay and conditions, despite early bans.

In 1834, six farm workers from the village of Tolpuddle in Dorset, England, formed a society. They wanted to resist wage cuts. They swore a secret oath together. For that oath, they were arrested and sentenced to be shipped to Australia. Crowds protested, and after about two years they were pardoned. They became known as the Tolpuddle Martyrs.

A is a group of workers who join together to bargain with employers. One worker alone has little power. Many workers together can ask for better pay, shorter hours or safer conditions. If talks fail, they may go on , meaning they stop work together.

British law banned unions under the Combination Acts of 1799 and 1800, but these were repealed in 1824. Unions slowly grew and became legal and strong later in the century. Supporters said unions gave workers a fair voice. Critics, including many employers, said unions raised costs and limited a person’s freedom to work on their own terms.

16.8

The Factory Acts

Main ideaBritain passed Factory Acts that limited children's hours and sent inspectors to enforce the rules.

After the Sadler Committee, Parliament acted. The Factory Act of 1833 said textile mills could not hire children under nine. Children aged nine to thirteen could work no more than nine hours a day. It also created paid to visit mills and enforce the law. Earlier laws had failed partly because no one checked.

More laws followed. The Mines Act of 1842 banned women, girls and boys under ten from working underground. The Factory Act of 1847, known as the Ten Hours Act, limited women and young people in textile mills to ten hours a day. Over time, rules spread to more industries.

The Factory Acts were debated hard. Supporters said the state must protect those who could not protect themselves. Opponents said the laws interfered with free contracts, raised costs and could hurt families who needed wages. The acts are an early example of : government rules for how businesses operate.

Section 4

Big Ideas About Work and Wealth

16.9

Marx and Engels

Main ideaKarl Marx and Friedrich Engels argued in 1848 that history is a struggle between classes and that workers would overthrow capitalism.

Friedrich Engels was the son of a German factory owner. In the 1840s he worked at his family’s mill business in Manchester. He walked the city’s poorest streets and wrote a book in 1845 about the lives of English workers. He became close friends with Karl Marx, a German philosopher and journalist.

In 1848 the two published The Communist Manifesto. They argued that all history is a story of , fights between those who own and those who work. Under , they said, owners of factories profit from workers’ labor. They predicted that workers would rise up and create a society with no private ownership of factories, which they called communism.

Supporters over the years have said Marx and Engels saw real suffering and real inequality clearly. Their ideas inspired labor parties and movements for workers’ rights. The Manifesto ends with a call: ’Working men of all countries, unite!’

16.10

Marx's Critics and the Record

Main ideaCritics say Marx's predictions failed and that states built in his name caused mass suffering; supporters defend parts of his analysis.

Marx predicted that workers’ revolutions would come first in the most industrial countries, like Britain and Germany. That did not happen. Instead, in Britain and elsewhere, workers’ wages rose over time, and many workers won the vote and joined unions and parties that worked through elections.

In the 1900s, governments that called themselves communist took power, starting with Russia in 1917. They seized private property and ruled as one-party states. Under Joseph Stalin, millions were sent to the Gulag, a system of forced labor camps. In China, the Great Leap Forward of 1958–62 led to a famine in which tens of millions died. In Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge killed or starved over a million people in the 1970s.

Critics, such as the economist Friedrich Hayek, argued that giving the state control over the economy leads to loss of freedom. Some defenders of Marx reply that these states twisted his ideas. Others value his critique of inequality but reject revolution. This remains a real debate, and you can weigh the evidence yourself.

16.11

Adam Smith and Free Markets

Main ideaAdam Smith argued in 1776 that free markets and the division of labor create wealth, while also warning about their costs.

Long before Marx, a Scottish professor named Adam Smith visited a small pin factory. He saw that when ten workers each did one step, like drawing wire or sharpening points, together they made tens of thousands of pins a day. One worker alone might make only a few. Splitting a job into small steps is called the .

In 1776, Smith published The Wealth of Nations. He argued that when people are free to trade, each person seeking their own gain can end up helping others. A baker bakes good bread to earn a living, and the town gets bread. Smith called this guiding force an ’invisible hand’. His ideas became the basis for free-market economics.

Smith was not only a cheerleader for business. He warned that merchants often plot to raise prices. He also worried that doing one tiny task all day could dull workers’ minds, and he supported public schooling for them. Supporters credit free markets with lifting billions out of poverty. Critics say markets alone leave too many behind.

It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.
Adam Smith, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Book I, 1776

16.12

Bismarck's Welfare State

Main ideaIn the 1880s Germany under Otto von Bismarck created the first national social insurance for workers.

Otto von Bismarck was the powerful chancellor, or chief minister, of Germany. He was a conservative who disliked socialism. Yet in the 1880s he made Germany the first country to give workers national insurance. Health insurance came in 1883, accident insurance in 1884, and old-age and disability pensions in 1889.

This was a new idea: the government would help protect workers against sickness, injury and old age. Workers, employers and the state all paid in. A system where the government provides this kind of protection is called a . The insurance itself is called .

Bismarck’s reasons were mixed. He hoped to win workers’ loyalty away from the socialists, whom he also tried to ban. Supporters of welfare states say they reduce hardship and make life more secure. Critics warn that they can be costly and can weaken self-reliance. Many European countries later built their own versions.
