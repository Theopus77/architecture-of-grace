Unit 1 · Identity

The Mask and the Mirror. The Identity Audit. The Digital Backpack. This unit practiced telling the version of you that performs from the version of you that is real — and noticing what the gap costs.

Part A · Choose the answer

1 The Mask is —
B. The Mask is the performed self — what I show, perform, edit, hide. The Mirror holds the real opinions, actual struggles, and real values. Everyone carries both.
2 “The gap between the Mask and the Mirror is where the ______ lives.”
A. The wider the gap between the performed self and the real one, the more it costs to carry — that is the anchor chart’s exact sentence.
3 The two columns of the Identity Audit are —
C. The Identity Audit is two private columns — who I am when performing, who I am when not. Never collected. Never shared. The privacy is the point.
4 “The internet is an ______, not a moment.”
A. The Digital Backpack chart: every post is still there, searchable and permanent. A digital Mask becomes an archive.
5 Jordan keeps the funny bit going even when it exhausts him. In this curriculum, the exhaustion is —
D. “Being funny IS one of your real selves. It is also one of your most costly performances. Both can be true. The exhaustion is information.”
6 Which of these is NOT one of the three digital-backpack rules?
B. The three rules are: charge outside the bedroom, the 24-hour rule before emotional posts, and one trusted adult as witness. Posting right away is the opposite of the 24-hour rule.
7 In Room 36, masking is treated as —
A. The room’s promise: “You can be how you ARE.” Masking is made visible as a cost the curriculum tracks — never a virtue, never an expectation.

Unit 2 · Self-Compassion

The Internal Critic and its three tactics. The Comparison Trap. The Friend Test. The Compassionate Witness. This unit practiced catching the Critic in the act and answering it honestly and fairly.

Part A · Choose the answer

1 The Internal Critic’s three tactics are —
C. Catastrophizing (one bad moment = forever). Globalizing (one mistake = always). Personalizing (it must be about me).
2 “One bad moment = forever” is the tactic called —
D. Catastrophizing stretches one bad moment into a permanent future. Naming the tactic is how you catch it.
3 “One mistake = always” is the tactic called —
B. Globalizing turns one mistake into a rule about who you are. One data point is not a pattern.
4 “It must be about me” is the tactic called —
A. Personalizing makes you the cause of things that mostly are not about you at all.
5 The comparison between their highlight reel and your blooper reel is —
D. Everyone has both reels. Most people show only one. You are watching everyone else’s trailer and comparing it to your own deleted scenes.
6 The Friend Test asks you to write down —
C. Left column: to my best friend — kind, fair, honest, without punishment. Right column: to myself. The test: would you say that to your best friend?
7 The Compassionate Witness is —
A. The Witness is not a cheerleader — it is honest AND fair. The Critic is loud, not true; the Witness is quiet and accurate.
8 “The Critic is loud, not ______.”
B. Volume is not evidence. The whole unit turns on that one sentence.
Checked: 0 / 8 · Right on the first try: 0

Part B · In your own words

1 Write one sentence your Critic actually says to you. Under it, write what the Compassionate Witness — honest AND fair — would say about the same moment.
2 Which of the three tactics does your Critic use most: catastrophizing, globalizing, or personalizing? Give one real example of it in action, and name the tactic in your answer.
3 Run the Friend Test on something you said to yourself this week. Left: what you would say to your best friend in that moment. Right: what you actually said to yourself. What is the difference?
4 One “deleted scene” of yours that you keep comparing to other people’s trailers. What would change if you compared trailer to trailer — or stopped comparing at all?

Unit 3 · Forgiveness

Impact vs. Intent. Forgiveness vs. Reconciliation. The Letter of Release. This unit practiced repair that names what landed, and release that does not require trust.

Part A · Choose the answer

1 When something you did lands badly, the repair conversation is about —
D. “Impact is what is addressed in repair. Intent matters for understanding but does not reduce accountability.” The rule cuts both ways.
2 Forgiveness, in this curriculum, is —
B. Forgiveness is internal — mine to give, requiring nothing of the other. That is what makes it different from reconciliation.
3 Reconciliation is different from forgiveness because reconciliation requires —
A. Reconciliation is external: it takes BOTH people, changed behavior over time, and safety. Forgiveness never requires trust.
4 Amara’s grandmother taught: forgiveness is not the same as —
C. “Forgiveness, but the kind that does not include trust.” You can release someone and still not hand them access.
5 The Letter of Release is —
A. Four sections, never sent. “Release is not what they earned. Release is what you stop paying.”
6 The section of the Letter of Release that most curricula leave out — and the one that makes it work — is —
D. Section four: what I am keeping. You are not erasing the experience — you are extracting what to take with you and putting down what you do not.
7 “Release is what you stop ______.”
B. Not the same as okay. Not reconciliation. Not what they earned. Release ends the ongoing cost to YOU.
Checked: 0 / 7 · Right on the first try: 0

Part B · In your own words

1 Describe a time your intent was good but your impact landed badly. What was the repair about — and what would it have sounded like to address impact first?
2 In your own words: what does forgiveness require of the other person? What does reconciliation require? Why does the difference protect you?
3 Think of something you have released — or want to release. Without writing the story itself, write only section four: what you are KEEPING from that experience.
4 “Release is what you stop paying.” What does carrying an old hurt cost — attention, sleep, trust, energy? Name the cost you would most like to stop paying.

Unit 4 · Grace

The Pause. The Three-Strike Rule. The Micro-Moment Menu. Difficult vs. Unsafe. This unit practiced grace as a chosen act with a real cost — and grace that never means access for unsafe people.

Part A · Choose the answer

1 The Pause asks you, before deciding what someone’s behavior meant, to —
A. “Maybe they didn’t see. Maybe they had a bad morning. Maybe it is not about me.” Three maybes before the verdict — then decide if you even have enough information.
2 Charitable interpretation is for —
C. For repeated, escalating harm it is not charitable to override accurate pattern-recognition. That is the guardrail, said plainly.
3 The Three-Strike Rule says that the THIRD time the same person does the same kind of hurt —
D. First time: charitable interpretation. Second: name the pattern privately. Third: the pattern is real. You are not failing to be charitable — you are pattern-matching. That is data, not bitterness.
4 A person can be DIFFICULT (annoying, immature, exhausting) and still receive grace. For a person who is UNSAFE, grace looks like —
B. Grace ≠ access. “You do not owe access to people who have shown you they are unsafe. EVER.”
5 Which of these is grace under cost?
A. Speaking up in the moment, when speaking up costs you, is grace. Silent disapproval and after-the-fact sympathy skip the cost — and the cost is the proof.
6 Grace across distance is —
D. “The gap is grace. Friendship across distance is maintained by what you decide to keep extending.”
7 At this age, grace is detected by —
C. Grace at this age is not a feeling. It is a choice you make when the easier choice is in your hand. The cost is the proof.
Checked: 0 / 7 · Right on the first try: 0

Part B · In your own words

1 Pick a real moment when someone’s behavior stung. Write the three maybes — three explanations that are not about you. Then answer honestly: do you have enough information for a verdict?
2 Choose your three acts from the Micro-Moment Menu (or write your own three small acts of grace with a real cost). When will you do the first one?
3 Difficult or unsafe? Think of one hard person in your life (initials only). Which list do they belong on, using the chart’s definitions — and what does grace look like at that distance?
4 Where are you extending grace across distance right now — showing up for someone who has not had much to give back lately? What are you deciding to keep extending?

Year-End · Integration

The four pillars, carried out the door. Room 36 learned: I AM. I CAN BE KIND TO MYSELF. I CAN FORGIVE. I CAN GIVE GRACE.

Part A · Choose the answer

1 The four pillars of Room 36, in adolescent voice, are —
A. I AM (not the Mask) · I CAN BE KIND TO MYSELF (catch the Critic) · I CAN FORGIVE (release, with limits) · I CAN GIVE GRACE (from strength, not from fear).
2 “I CAN FORGIVE” in the Room 36 charter comes with a reminder —
B. Forgiveness is release — and it lives beside the limits you learned: forgiveness ≠ trust, grace ≠ access, difficult ≠ unsafe.
3 “I CAN GIVE GRACE” is given —
D. Grace from fear is appeasement. Grace from strength is a chosen gift — which is why it counts.
4 “The framework is ______. They are taking it with them.”
B. The closing line of the Room 36 curriculum. The four pillars, the vocabulary, the practices — they leave the room with you.
