Compare Without Ranking

Argument

Martial arts can be sorted into families: grappling, striking, armed as sport, flowing practice, diaspora and modern mix. Real bodies have drawn such lists, from the Japanese Budo Association in 1977 to UNESCO's heritage list. In this unit you will read those lists as records made by people, in given years, for their own purposes.

You will meet arts from Iceland, France, Japan, China, Trinidad, Israel and Switzerland. Glima went to the 1912 Stockholm Olympics as a demonstration. Kalinda survived a colonial law in 1884. Aikido chose to hold no contests, while fencing has been at every modern Olympic Games.

In the second chapter you will compare two sword sports, fencing and kendo, by origin, room, nation and code. You will use dated charters, federation records and rule books. By the end, you can build a comparison that explains differences with evidence and does not rank one art above another.

The story

Six Objects on a Shelf

Each object on the shelf points to a different room and a different set of records.

A museum storage shelf holds six objects with paper tags. There is a leather belt harness from Iceland and a pair of laced French boxing shoes. There is a bamboo practice sword from Japan and a printed booklet of a simplified taijiquan routine from Beijing, dated 1956.

The last two objects are a polished stick from Trinidad and a training manual in Hebrew from Israel. A curator must file them. One tag asks for a family name: grappling, striking, armed as sport, flowing practice, diaspora, or modern mix.

The curator notices that the tags do not rank the objects. A grappling harness is not higher than a stick. Each category only groups things that share a trait, such as a weapon, a contest, or a history of travel. Some objects could fit under two tags at once.

Scholars of martial arts face the same filing problem. Japanese federations, American authors and UNESCO have each drawn up lists and families. Every list was made by a real body, in a real year, for its own purpose. Reading who made a list is the first step in using it well.

31.1

Nine Arts, One Association

Main ideaThe Japanese Budo Association, founded in 1977, groups nine modern budo arts, so its list is a record of one body's choices.

In 1977 nine Japanese federations joined with the Nippon Budokan to form the . The nine arts were judo, kendo, kyudo, sumo, karatedo, aikido, shorinji kempo, naginata and jukendo. Kyudo is Japanese archery. Naginata uses a long curved blade on a pole, and jukendo uses a wooden rifle with a blunt bayonet.

The Nippon Budokan is the hall in Tokyo built for judo at the 1964 Olympic Games. It became the home of the new association. In 1987 the association issued the , a short document of principles. The charter describes budo as a way of building character, respect and self-discipline, in the words of its authors.

The list is a , a group drawn around shared traits. Its borders were set by people. Many Japanese arts older than 1868 are not on it, and neither is kickboxing. The list tells a historian which modern federations worked together in Japan after 1977. It does not say those nine arts are better than others.

31.2

Old Schools and New Budo

Main ideaJapanese historians often separate koryu, schools founded before 1868, from gendai budo, arts shaped in modern Japan, but the line is a tool.

In Japan the word means old school or old stream. It usually names a martial tradition founded before the Meiji Restoration of 1868. Many koryu passed teaching through one family or one licensed line. They kept written scrolls that listed the school’s founder and its later heads.

The word means modern budo. It names arts organized after 1868, such as judo from 1882, kendo’s national rules, aikido and karatedo. Gendai budo usually has open membership, printed rules and national federations. Many of these arts also hold public contests with referees.

The American author Donn F. Draeger wrote three books on this split: Classical Bujutsu and Classical Budo in 1973 and Modern Bujutsu and Budo in 1974. He used 1868 as a working border. Later historians noted that many koryu kept changing after 1868 and that modern arts borrowed from old ones. The border helps sort records; it does not settle which art is more genuine.

A Heritage List as a Map

Main ideaUNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage has named several martial practices since 2010, each nominated by a state for its own reasons.

UNESCO adopted a Convention for the Safeguarding of the in 2003. Intangible heritage means living practices such as songs, crafts, festivals and games, not buildings. A state nominates a practice, and a UNESCO committee decides whether to inscribe it on a list.

Turkey’s Kırkpınar oil wrestling festival and Iran’s Pahlevani and zoorkhanei rituals were inscribed in 2010. South Korea’s taekkyeon followed in 2011, Brazil’s capoeira circle in 2014, and Korean ssireum in 2018. That ssireum listing was the first joint nomination by North and South Korea. Taijiquan from China joined in 2020.

The list mixes wrestling, ritual exercise, music and flowing routines. What joins them is a , a formal request by a government. Governments choose what to nominate for reasons of pride, tourism and protection. A historian reads the list as a record of states naming their heritage, not as a ranking of arts.

Glima in Stockholm

Main ideaIcelandic glima, a belt wrestling tradition, was shown to the world as a demonstration at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics.

is the traditional wrestling of Iceland. Wrestlers wear a leather belt harness around the waist and thighs, and they hold it during a bout. Icelandic sagas written down in the 1200s and 1300s describe men wrestling at gatherings. Glima is often named as Iceland’s national sport.

At the 1912 Olympic Games in Stockholm, Icelandic wrestlers performed glima as a . A demonstration event is shown at the Games but awards no official Olympic medals. Iceland was then still under the Danish crown. Showing glima in Stockholm let Icelanders present a sport of their own to a world audience.

Glima belongs to the grappling family, the arts where contestants seize and control each other rather than strike. The family is very old and very wide. It also holds Greek wrestling, Japanese sumo, Mongolian bökh and Swiss schwingen. Each kept its own clothing, ring and rules, so the family name groups them without merging them.

Savate in Paris

Main ideaSavate, or boxe française, grew in Paris in the 1800s when French teachers joined foot fighting with English boxing into a rule-bound sport.

In the early 1800s, was a street fighting style in Paris and the port of Marseille. Its name comes from an old French word for a worn shoe. Michel Casseux, a Paris teacher, opened a training room for it in the 1820s. Police and polite society often saw savate as the fighting of the poor.

Charles Lecour, a pupil of Casseux, studied English boxing in the 1830s. He joined English punching with French kicking and taught the blend in his salle, or training hall. The result came to be called , French boxing. Wealthy Parisians began to attend Lecour’s salle.

Late in the century, Joseph Charlemont and his son Charles ran a well-known Paris salle and wrote books on the sport. Their work gave boxe française printed rules and a teaching system. Savate belongs to the striking family, which also holds boxing, Muay Thai and karate. Like those arts, it moved from street or village use to a salle with rules.

Kickboxing Gets a Name

Main ideaThe word kickboxing was coined in Japan in the mid-1960s for a new ring sport drawn from Muay Thai, karate and boxing.

In the early 1960s, Thai boxers and Japanese karate fighters met in bouts in Japan and Thailand. The Japanese promoter Noguchi Osamu watched these meetings. In the mid-1960s he organized a new ring sport and gave it the English name . Japanese television soon broadcast kickboxing cards, and the sport grew popular in Japan.

Kickboxing was a , a sport built from parts of several older arts. It kept the gloves and rounds of Western boxing. It borrowed scoring and kicks from Muay Thai and fighters from karate schools. Its rule books were new, and so were its title belts and weight classes.

In 1993 a Japanese karate leader, Ishii Kazuyoshi, founded the K-1 promotion in Japan. K-1 held kickboxing tournaments with fighters from many countries. Kickboxing shows how a modern mix grows: promoters, broadcasters and rule writers join pieces of older arts into a new . The sport’s history can be read in its founding years and its companies.

31.7

Bamboo and Armor

Main ideaIn the 1700s, Japanese sword schools adopted bamboo swords and padded armor, which later let kendo become a contest sport.

Early Japanese sword schools trained mostly with wooden swords in set, paired routines. In the 1700s, teachers in the Jikishinkage-ryū and Ittō-ryū lines are credited with spreading protective armor and bamboo swords. The is a practice sword made of four bamboo staves bound with leather. The armor, called , covers the head, body and hands.

The new gear allowed full-speed contests with less risk of injury. By the early 1800s, large sword training halls in Edo, today’s Tokyo, held such contests. After 1868 the sword lost its place in daily life, and the contests became public shows called gekiken. Around 1920 the Dai Nippon Butoku Kai adopted the name kendo, the way of the sword.

Kendo belongs to the armed-as-sport family. In these arts, a weapon has been made safe enough to score with. Olympic fencing, sport arnis and kendo all share this trait. In each, a change in equipment came before a change in rules, and the rules then shaped how the sport was judged.

31.8

Taijiquan in a Beijing Park

Main ideaTaijiquan spread from Chen Village to Beijing in the 1800s, and a 1956 simplified routine made it a mass health practice in China.

Yang Luchan, who lived from 1799 to 1872, studied taijiquan with the Chen family in Henan Province. He later taught in Beijing, the capital of the Qing dynasty. His students included men of the Manchu banner households. The Yang family line he began became one of the most practiced forms of taijiquan in the world.

In 1956 China’s national sports commission published a taijiquan routine of 24 movements. It was drawn from Yang-style forms and was shorter than older family routines. The goal was a practice that millions of people could learn for health. Group practice in parks and squares at dawn became a familiar sight in Chinese cities.

Taijiquan is often placed in a flowing family: done slowly, often without a partner or opponent. Many practitioners describe it as exercise, health care and moving meditation. Contest events also exist, such as judged taijiquan routines in modern wushu. So the same art can be sorted into more than one family, depending on the room.

Aikido Without Contests

Main ideaUeshiba Morihei's aikido, named in 1942, was organized by the Aikikai largely without competitions, while a later branch added them.

Ueshiba Morihei, who lived from 1883 to 1969, studied Daitō-ryū jujutsu under Takeda Sōkaku from 1915. In 1931 he opened a training hall in Tokyo called the Kobukan. The name came into official use in 1942. After the war, in 1948, Japan’s government approved the as a foundation to run the art.

The Aikikai chose not to hold contests. Its members practice in pairs and test for ranks before examiners instead of winners and losers. Ueshiba described aikido in spiritual and ethical terms, as a way of harmony. Those are his words and beliefs, and many Aikikai schools teach them as part of the art.

Not every aikido teacher agreed. Tomiki Kenji, a judo and aikido teacher, developed an aikido branch that includes competition. So aikido itself has more than one room. A family label such as flowing practice fits one branch well and the other less well.

Kalinda in Trinidad

Main ideaKalinda stick fighting in Trinidad grew among Africans and their descendants, and colonial laws in the 1880s tried to restrict it.

is a stick-fighting tradition of Trinidad in the Caribbean. It grew among enslaved Africans and their descendants on the island. Fighters, called , meet in a ring while drummers and singers perform. The songs, called kalinda songs, are part of the event, not an extra.

Britain ended slavery in its colonies by law in 1834, with full freedom in 1838. Freed people in Trinidad made Carnival their own, including the night festival of Canboulay. In 1881 police in Port of Spain tried to stop the Canboulay procession, and fighting broke out. In 1884 the colonial government passed a law that restricted drumming and the gatherings of stick-fighting bands.

Kalinda survived in villages and at Carnival. Trinidad and Tobago became independent in 1962, and stick-fighting contests still appear in its Carnival season. Kalinda belongs to the family: arts carried or rebuilt by people scattered from a homeland. Afro-Brazilian capoeira, with its music and ring, shows a similar history of law and survival.

Krav Maga in Israel

Main ideaImi Lichtenfeld built krav maga, a modern mixed self-defense system, for Jewish defense groups and then the Israel Defense Forces.

Imi Lichtenfeld, who lived from 1910 to 1998, grew up in Bratislava, then in Czechoslovakia. He competed in boxing, wrestling and gymnastics. In the 1930s he helped organize Jewish young people to defend their neighborhoods against antisemitic gangs. He left Europe in 1940 and reached Mandatory Palestine, then under British rule, in the early 1940s.

There he trained fighters of the Haganah, a Jewish defense force. After Israel became a state in 1948, he became chief instructor of physical fitness and for the Israel Defense Forces. The Hebrew name means contact combat. He retired from the army in 1964 and then opened civilian schools.

Krav maga is a : a system built in the twentieth century from boxing, wrestling and other sources, with no contest code. Its main room was a military one, not a stadium. Sambo in the Soviet Union and kickboxing in Japan are other modern mixes. Each was shaped by a specific institution, in a specific decade, for a specific purpose.

Two Rule Books, One Page Each

Two rule books open to the page that says who may step forward and begin.

On a library table lie two rule books. One is the rule book of the International Fencing Federation, known by its French initials, FIE. The other is a set of kendo match regulations from the International Kendo Federation. A student has opened each to the page that names who may compete.

Both sports use a blade that cannot cut. Both began in rooms where people once trained for real sword fights. Yet one became part of every modern Olympic Games, and the other has never been on the Olympic program.

32.1

Masters of Arms in Paris

Main ideaFencing masters in Paris received a royal charter in 1567, and their salles turned sword training into a taught, regulated craft.

In 1567 King Charles IX of France granted letters patent to the fencing masters of Paris. are an open royal document that grants a right or a status. The charter let the masters govern their own trade, test new teachers and control who could teach. A master of arms had to pass through years as a pupil and assistant.

Training took place in a salle d&#x27;armes, a hall of arms. In the 1600s and 1700s, gentlemen carried a light thrusting sword called the smallsword. French masters developed a lighter training weapon, the foil, with a blunted tip. They also wrote conventions about which fencer had the right to score first.

Those conventions later became the rules of foil fencing. The salle was both a school and a social club for men of the upper classes. Women and working people were mostly shut out of it for centuries. The French words used in the salle, such as en garde, stayed in fencing everywhere.

Sword Halls in Edo

Main ideaIn the 1800s, large sword halls in Edo taught thousands of students with bamboo swords, creating the rooms that later became kendo.

Edo, now Tokyo, was the seat of the Tokugawa shoguns from 1603 to 1868. By the early 1800s the city had many sword , training halls tied to a named school. Three became famous as the great halls of Edo. They belonged to the Hokushin Ittō-ryū, the Shintō Munen-ryū and the Kyōshin Meichi-ryū.

Chiba Shūsaku founded the Hokushin Ittō-ryū and opened his Genbukan hall in Edo in the 1820s. His hall trained with shinai and armor and drew many students. Students were mostly from samurai families, though some commoners also trained. Young men from many domains came to the capital to study at these halls.

A dojo of this kind had a named school, or ryū, a founder, and licenses that recorded a student’s progress. That makes it similar to the Paris salle, with its guild and masters. Both were private teaching rooms, and both ruled who could teach. Neither was yet a national sport body.

32.3

When Swords Left Daily Life

Main ideaJapan banned wearing swords in public in 1876, while France allowed dueling far longer, and both changes pushed sword arts toward sport.

In 1876 the Meiji government issued the , an order that banned most people from wearing swords in public. The samurai class had already lost its legal privileges. Sword teachers lost pupils and income. Some staged public gekiken shows with paid tickets to earn a living.

In France, the sword stayed in public life longer through the , a private fight arranged to settle a question of honor. Duels were illegal in theory but often tolerated. The épée, a heavier thrusting sword used in duels, became a sport weapon in the late 1800s. The last widely reported duel in France took place in 1967, between two politicians, Gaston Defferre and René Ribière.

In both countries the real sword faded from daily life over the same broad period. Sword training then needed a new reason to exist. In Japan the new reason was national education and budo. In France it was sport, clubs and international contests, including the Olympic Games from 1896.

32.4

The FIE, Paris 1913

Main ideaThe International Fencing Federation was founded in Paris in 1913, and French remains the language of fencing's commands.

Fencing was on the program of the first modern Olympic Games at Athens in 1896. It is one of only a few sports held at every modern Games. At first each country and each tournament used its own rules. Disputes over judging were common, because a touch was hard to see and judges often disagreed.

In 1913 national federations met in Paris and founded the Fédération Internationale d&#x27;Escrime, the FIE. An is a body that writes one set of rules for a sport across countries. The FIE took over the rules for foil, épée and sabre. Today its headquarters are in Lausanne, Switzerland, the city that also hosts the International Olympic Committee.

The FIE rules give referee commands in French. A referee says en garde, then prêts, then allez to begin, and halte to stop. A fencer in Tokyo or Chicago hears the same French words. Language in a rule book keeps a record of where a sport’s code was first written.

Kendo Federations, 1952 and 1970

Main ideaKendo gained a national federation in 1952 and an international one in 1970, which held the first World Kendo Championships that year.

The All Japan Kendo Federation was founded in 1952, after the Allied occupation of Japan ended. It wrote national rules and ran grading examinations. In the United States, Japanese American clubs had practiced kendo since before the Second World War. Clubs also grew in Brazil, Korea, Taiwan and Europe.

In 1970 national and regional kendo federations founded the International Kendo Federation, known as the . That same year the FIK held the first World Kendo Championships in Tokyo. The world championships have been held every three years since then, in cities from São Paulo to Paris.

Kendo is not an Olympic sport. Many kendo leaders have argued that Olympic entry would push the art toward winning and away from its educational aims. That argument is a choice about the room kendo wants. Fencing made the opposite choice in 1896 and stayed with it.

32.6

Wire Versus Flags

Main ideaFencing adopted electric scoring for épée at the 1936 Olympics, while kendo still relies on three referees with flags.

At the 1936 Olympic Games in Berlin, épée bouts were scored with for the first time. A wire connected each fencer’s weapon to a box that lit a lamp when a touch landed. Foil followed at the 1956 Games in Melbourne. Sabre was the last weapon to go electric, in the late 1980s.

Kendo took a different path. A kendo match has three , or referees, each holding a red flag and a white flag. A point, called ippon, counts when at least two referees raise their flags for the same competitor. The referees judge not only contact but also form, spirit and posture, following the federation’s rules.

The two systems record different ideas of what a point is. Fencing’s machine answers whether a touch landed, and a referee applies the right-of-way conventions in foil and sabre. Kendo’s panel asks whether a strike met the federation’s full standard. Neither system is more modern in a ranking sense; each fits its own code.

32.7

A Licence to Enter

Main ideaTo enter FIE competitions, a fencer must hold an FIE licence issued through a national federation, a paper gate that ties each fencer to a nation.

The FIE requires every fencer in its official events to hold an FIE . A licence is a paper or digital permit that shows a person may take part. Fencers get it through their national federation, which the FIE recognizes. Without one, a fencer cannot start in a World Cup or world championship bout.

This rule ties each fencer to a nation. In the United States, the national body began as the Amateur Fencers League of America in 1891. It is now called USA Fencing. National bodies choose teams, and those teams represent their countries at the Olympic Games.

The licence gives the FIE a list of who fenced, where and for which nation. That list is a historian’s record. It shows when new countries joined, when fencers changed nations, and when events opened. It also shows the gate: only those accepted by a national body may begin.

32.8

A Grade Exam to Rise

Main ideaIn kendo, the national federation ranks practitioners through dan examinations up to eighth dan, which very few candidates pass in any year.

The All Japan Kendo Federation runs examinations. A dan is a numbered black-belt-level grade; kendo uses the word even though kendo gear has no colored belt. Grades by examination run from first dan to eighth dan. Each higher exam requires a set number of years at the grade below.

The eighth dan examination is known in Japan as one of the hardest tests in any martial art. Candidates must have held seventh dan for many years. Very few pass in any year, and many try again and again. Panels of senior examiners judge the candidates’ matches.

The grade system decides who may judge, teach and examine others, not who may enter a first match. A beginner can join a kendo club and compete in local events. In fencing, results and national rankings open doors; in kendo, examined grades carry much of the authority. Each sport answers the question of standing in its own way.

Wheelchair Fencing, Rome 1960

Main ideaWheelchair fencing was part of the first Paralympic Games in Rome in 1960, opening fencing's starting line to athletes with disabilities.

In 1948 the doctor Ludwig Guttmann held a sports competition for injured military veterans at Stoke Mandeville Hospital in England. Many of them used wheelchairs after spinal injuries in the Second World War. Guttmann believed sport helped patients recover strength and confidence. His games grew each year.

In 1960 the event was held in Rome after the Olympic Games, and it is now counted as the first . was on its program. The wheelchairs are fixed to a frame on the floor, so fencers face each other at a set distance. Fencers use the same three weapons as in Olympic fencing.

Wheelchair fencing has been held at every Summer Paralympics since 1960. The rule of the fixed frame answered a practical question about who may start: it let athletes who use wheelchairs compete on equal terms. The event shows how a code can be rewritten to open a door, rather than keep one shut.

32.11

Comparing Without a Winner

Main ideaA strong comparison sets dated facts about origin, room, nation and code side by side and explains differences without ranking the arts.

Historians of sport use to see what is shared and what is particular. For fencing and kendo, the origin question has dated answers: the Paris charter of 1567 and the Edo halls of the early 1800s. The room question has answers too: the salle and the dojo. Each can be checked in records.

The nation question asks which states shaped each art. France gave fencing its words and its world body in 1913. Japan gave kendo its name, its national federation in 1952 and a place in school physical education. The code question asks who writes the rules: the FIE for fencing, the FIK and national federations for kendo.

A comparison becomes a when it treats one set of answers as the standard. Calling kendo less modern because it uses flags, or fencing less serious because it uses a machine, adds a judgment the records do not support. The dated evidence shows two institutions that made different choices, each with its own reasons.
