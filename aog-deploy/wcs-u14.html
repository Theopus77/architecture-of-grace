Class and Culture Today

Society Today

Every person starts life in a particular place, a particular family and a particular language. Some start with more money, some with less. Some grow up speaking the language of their grandparents, and some grow up between two languages. This unit looks at the world today and asks how much those starting points shape a life, and how people move beyond them.

In the first chapter you will learn how economists measure gaps between rich and poor, and how easily children move up or down compared with their parents. You will compare Norway, Brazil, Britain, India and China. In the second chapter you will follow music, food and people across borders. You will study how countries welcome newcomers and how communities keep languages alive. Then you will walk through four Chicago neighborhoods.

By the end, you will be able to read a Gini score and a mobility chart and explain what they show. You will be able to describe the main views on inequality and migration fairly. You will also be able to use evidence to argue your own answer to the unit's big question.

The big question

How much do birth, class and culture shape who we become, and how do people and societies change them?

The story

Two Babies, One Birthday

A student follows two newborns, one in Oslo and one in São Paulo, and asks what their chances really are.

Mariana is in tenth grade, and her project starts with a simple idea. Picture two babies born on the same morning. One is born in a hospital in Oslo, the capital of Norway. The other is born in a hospital in São Paulo, the largest city in Brazil. Both are healthy. Both have parents who love them. Mariana wants to know what the numbers say about the lives ahead of them.

She starts with money. Norway is one of the most equal countries in the world, measured by how income is shared. Brazil is one of the most unequal. In São Paulo, glass towers with rooftop helicopter pads stand a short drive from crowded hillside neighborhoods called favelas. In Oslo, the gap between a bus driver's pay and a lawyer's pay is smaller than in most countries.

Next she looks at schools and health. Both babies can go to free public schools. But in Brazil, many families who can afford it pay for private schools, and the public schools vary a great deal. In Norway, almost every child goes to the same kind of public school. Mariana notices that the Oslo baby's future seems to depend less on which family she was born into.

Then Mariana finds a surprise. Some economists argue that countries with bigger gaps between rich and poor also have less movement between generations. In those places, children tend to stay near where their parents started. Other economists question how strong that link is. Mariana realizes her project is really about one question: how much of a life is set at birth, and how much can a person change?

Measuring the Gap

27.1

Income Versus Wealth

Main ideaIncome is money coming in over time, while wealth is what a person owns minus what they owe, and wealth is usually far more unequal.

Think about two neighbors. Ana earns $80,000 a year as a nurse, but she rents her apartment and still owes money on student loans. Ben earns $40,000 a year at a part-time job, but he inherited a paid-off house and some savings from his grandparents. Who is better off? The answer depends on whether you look at income or wealth.

is the money that comes in over a period of time, such as wages, salary, rent from property, or interest from savings. is a snapshot of everything a person owns, such as a home, savings and stocks, minus everything they owe. Economists call wealth . A person can have a high income but little wealth, or a small income but a lot of wealth.

The difference matters because wealth is usually shared much less evenly than income. In the United States, the Federal Reserve estimates that the richest tenth of households hold about two-thirds of all household wealth. The bottom half of households hold only a few percent. Wealth can also be passed down to children, so it helps explain why advantages can last across generations.

Wealth also acts as a cushion. A family with savings can survive a lost job or a medical bill. A family with no savings may have to borrow at high interest. Wealth can pay for college, a first home, or starting a business. That is why many researchers who study opportunity look closely at wealth, not just at paychecks.

27.2

The Gini Coefficient

Main ideaThe Gini coefficient is one number, from 0 to 1, that sums up how evenly income is shared in a country.

Imagine a class of 30 students sharing 30 pizzas. If every student gets one pizza, the sharing is perfectly equal. If one student gets all 30 and everyone else gets nothing, the sharing is perfectly unequal. Real countries fall somewhere in between. Economists wanted one simple number to show where a country falls on that scale.

The most common tool is the , named after the Italian statistician Corrado Gini, who described it in 1912. It runs from 0 to 1. A score of 0 means everyone has the same income. A score of 1 means one person has all of it. Many reports multiply the score by 100, so a Gini of 0.40 is often written as 40. Higher means more , or a bigger gap.

The World Bank publishes Gini estimates for most countries. Nordic countries such as Norway, Denmark and Finland usually score in the mid to high 20s. The United States scores a little above 40. Latin America is often described as one of the most unequal regions, with Brazil and Colombia scoring above 50. South Africa has one of the highest scores measured anywhere, above 60.

A single number has limits. Two countries can have the same Gini but very different lives at the bottom. The Gini also says nothing about why a gap exists. Some measures use income before taxes, and others use income after taxes and government benefits. So careful readers always ask: which income, which year, and measured by whom?

27.3

Poverty Lines and Living Standards

Main ideaPoverty can be measured against a fixed line, like the World Bank's line for extreme poverty, or against what is normal in one country.

A family in rural Malawi and a family in Chicago may both be called poor. Yet their lives are very different. The Malawi family may lack clean water and electricity. The Chicago family may have both but struggle to pay rent. To compare them, researchers need clear rules about what counts as poverty.

One rule is , which uses a fixed line that does not change from country to country. The World Bank sets an international line for . In 2022 it set that line at $2.15 per person per day, adjusted for prices in each country. In 2025 it raised the line to $3.00, using newer price data. Below that line, people struggle to afford enough food, shelter and basic care.

Another rule is . Here, a person is poor if they have much less than most people in their own country. Many European countries count a household as poor if its income is below 60 percent of the middle income. Under this rule, a family in Norway could count as poor even though it lives far above the World Bank line.

Each measure answers a different question. Absolute lines ask whether people can meet basic needs. Relative lines ask whether people can take part in normal life where they live, like joining a school trip. Arguments about poverty often come down to which measure a speaker is using, so it helps to ask.

Section 2

Moving Up or Staying Put

27.4

What Social Mobility Means

Main ideaSocial mobility is how easily people move up or down the ladder of income and status, especially compared with their parents.

Picture a ladder with ten rungs. Everyone in a country stands on one rung, from the lowest incomes to the highest. Now picture the children of each person. Do the children end up on the same rung as their parents? Or do some climb higher and some slip lower? That movement is what means.

Researchers study two kinds. compares children with their parents, such as a farmworker’s daughter who becomes an engineer. Movement within one person’s lifetime is called intra-generational mobility, like a cashier who becomes a store manager. Mobility can also be or relative. Absolute mobility asks whether children earn more than their parents did at the same age. Relative mobility asks whether children change rungs compared with everyone else.

The economist Raj Chetty and his team studied tax records to measure absolute mobility in the United States. They found that about 90 percent of Americans born in 1940 grew up to earn more than their parents. For those born in 1980, it was about 50 percent. They found the main reason was that growth was shared less evenly. Slower growth also played a part.

High mobility is often seen as a sign of fairness. It suggests that talent and effort, not just family, shape where people end up. But people disagree about how much mobility is realistic and what helps it most. Some point to schools and neighborhoods. Others point to families, work and a growing economy.

27.5

The Great Gatsby Curve

Main ideaEconomists describe a pattern called the Great Gatsby curve: countries with more inequality tend to have less mobility between generations.

In the novel The Great Gatsby, published in 1925, a poor young man remakes himself as a rich one. But he never fully breaks into the old-money world he admires. In 2012 the economist Alan Krueger, then an adviser to the U.S. president, borrowed the book’s title for a graph. The graph used research by the Canadian economist Miles Corak.

The graph places countries on two measures. One is income inequality, often measured by the Gini coefficient. The other is how closely children’s earnings follow their parents’ earnings. Economists call that second measure . When they drew the dots, a pattern appeared. Countries like Denmark and Norway had low inequality and low persistence. The United States and the United Kingdom had higher inequality and higher persistence.

This pattern is called the . It is a , meaning two things rise and fall together. A correlation does not prove that one causes the other. Supporters argue that big gaps let rich families buy advantages, such as better schools and tutors, which keeps children near their parents’ rung.

Critics raise questions. Some say the countries differ in many ways, such as size, history and population, so the curve may mix up causes. Others say the data for some countries are weak. Still others argue that growth and strong families matter more than gaps. Economists still debate how much the curve tells us about cause.

27.6

Education as a Ladder

Main ideaEducation is one of the strongest ladders for moving up, but unequal schools can also pass advantages down.

In 1862 President Abraham Lincoln signed the Morrill Act. It gave federal land to states to build public colleges. The University of Illinois grew from that law. For millions of students since then, a public college has been a step up. Education is often called a ladder because it can lift people whose parents never had the chance.

The numbers show why. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports that workers with a bachelor’s degree earn much more, on average, than workers with only a high school diploma. They are also less likely to be out of work. Economists call the extra pay for a degree the . Skills and training are sometimes called , because they work like an investment that pays off later.

But the ladder is not equal for everyone. In the United States, schools are partly paid for by local property taxes. Richer areas can often spend more per student. Families with more money can pay for tutors, test preparation and summer programs. So schooling can lift some students up while helping others stay ahead.

Countries try different fixes. Finland aims to give every school similar resources and trains all teachers to a high level. South Korea has intense exams, and many families pay for private after-school academies called hagwons. People argue about which approach helps most. Many agree that early childhood learning and strong teachers make a real difference.

27.7

Class in Britain

Main ideaIn the United Kingdom, class has long been signaled by accent and schooling, and a small group from private schools still holds many top jobs.

In Britain, people have often been able to guess each other’s class within a few words. For generations, an accent called was linked to the upper and upper-middle class and to the BBC. Regional accents from Liverpool, Glasgow or East London were sometimes looked down on. Many working-class Britons are proud of their accents, and today the BBC hires presenters with many different ones.

Schools are another signal. In Britain, the oldest private schools, such as Eton and Harrow, are confusingly called public schools. Fees are high. The Sutton Trust, a charity that studies mobility, reports that only about 7 percent of British pupils attend private schools. Yet its 2019 study found that people from private schools held a much larger share of top jobs, such as senior judges.

The word means a small group at the top of a society. Critics argue that Britain’s elite reproduces itself, with the same schools feeding the same universities, Oxford and Cambridge, and then the same jobs. Supporters of private schools reply that parents have a right to choose, and that the schools often give scholarships and share facilities.

Britain has also changed a great deal. After World War II, free secondary school and later new universities opened doors. Many prime ministers since then went to state schools. The debate today is about how fast the doors are opening, and what would open them wider.

27.8

India's Reservations Debate

Main ideaIndia reserves places in colleges, government jobs and legislatures for groups that faced caste discrimination, and people still debate how the system should work.

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar was born in 1891 into a caste that others treated as untouchable. As a boy, he was made to sit apart at school. He went on to earn doctorates in New York and London. After independence he led the committee that wrote India’s Constitution, adopted in 1950. It banned untouchability and made room for the policy known as .

Reservations set aside a share of seats in public colleges, government jobs and legislatures. At first they covered , the official name for groups once treated as untouchable, and Scheduled Tribes. In 1990 the government extended reservations to groups called Other Backward Classes, following the Mandal Commission report. In 1992 India’s Supreme Court ruled that reservations should generally not go above 50 percent.

Supporters say reservations have opened doors for millions. They point to Dalit doctors, officers and members of Parliament whose families were once shut out. Dalit, meaning broken or oppressed, is a name many members of these communities chose for themselves. Supporters say caste bias still exists, so the help is still needed.

Critics raise other points. Some say benefits go mostly to better-off families within each group. Others argue that jobs should go by merit or by family income, not by caste. In 2019 India added a 10 percent reservation for poorer families outside the older groups. The debate continues in courts, colleges and elections.

27.9

China's Great Climb

Main ideaAfter reforms began in 1978, China's economy grew fast and hundreds of millions of people left extreme poverty, while new gaps also opened.

In 1978, most people in China were farmers, and most were very poor. That year, a group of farmers in the village of Xiaogang secretly divided their collective land. Each family agreed to farm its own plot and keep what it grew above a quota. Their harvest jumped. Soon the government under Deng Xiaoping allowed this system across the country.

These changes were called . Farmers could sell extra crops at market prices. In 1980 China created , such as Shenzhen, where foreign companies could build factories. Shenzhen grew from a small town into one of the world’s great cities. The Communist Party kept political control, but the economy became much more open to markets.

The results were huge. The World Bank estimates that close to 800 million people in China left extreme poverty from 1978 to about 2020. That is roughly three-quarters of the global drop in extreme poverty over those years. Millions moved from villages to cities to work in factories, building roads and shipping goods around the world.

The climb also brought new gaps. China’s Gini rose, and coastal cities pulled ahead of inland villages. The system, a household registration rule, often kept migrant workers from getting the same schools and health care as city-born residents. Some credit markets for the growth. Others credit the state’s planning. Both sides study China closely.

Why Gaps Exist

27.10

Explanations From Left and Right

Main ideaPeople on the left and right often explain inequality differently, and each side points to real evidence.

Two students watch the same news story about a closed factory in a small town. One says the company and the government let those workers down. The other says the town needs new businesses and workers need new skills. Both care about the workers. They just start from different ideas about what causes gaps and what fixes them.

Thinkers on the political often stress causes. These are forces bigger than one person, such as unequal schools, discrimination, weak unions or tax rules that favor the rich. The philosopher John Rawls argued in 1971 that a fair society allows inequalities only if they help the worst-off. The left often supports higher taxes on the rich, stronger unions and public programs.

Thinkers on the political often stress freedom, markets and personal responsibility. They argue that growth lifts most people, even if some rise faster. They point out that high taxes can weaken the reasons to work, save and invent. The economist Milton Friedman argued that putting equality ahead of freedom would harm both. The right often supports lower taxes, school choice and strong families.

Many people mix these views. Most agree that both personal choices and outside forces shape lives. The debate is about how much weight each deserves, and what a government should do. Good arguments on either side use evidence, like the data in this chapter.

27.11

Tools Countries Use

Main ideaCountries use taxes, public services and growth policies to shape inequality and mobility, and each tool has supporters and critics.

Norway taxes its citizens heavily and pays for free college, long paid parental leave and public health care. Singapore keeps many taxes low but requires workers to save part of every paycheck and builds public housing where most citizens live. Both countries are rich. They took different paths to share the gains.

One common tool is , moving money from richer to poorer through taxes and benefits. A takes a higher percentage from higher incomes. In many European countries, taxes and benefits cut the Gini a great deal compared with income before taxes. Supporters say this protects families and gives children a fairer start. Critics say high taxes can slow growth and reduce the reward for effort.

Other tools focus on opportunity rather than outcomes. These include early childhood programs, job training and scholarships. Some focus on growth, such as lowering barriers to starting a business. Supporters on the right often argue that a growing economy creates more jobs and more chances to climb.

No country uses only one tool. Most mix taxes, public services and markets in different amounts. Voters in each country argue about the right mix. The best choice depends on what people value most, such as equality, freedom, security or growth, and on what the evidence shows works.

Why People Migrate

Main ideaPeople migrate because of push factors that drive them away and pull factors that draw them to a new place.

In 1990 a young nurse left Manila, in the Philippines, for a hospital job in New York. Her pay in the United States was many times higher, and she sent part of it home each month. Researchers sort causes into two kinds. drive people away, such as war, poverty, disasters or persecution. draw them in, such as jobs, safety, schools or family already living there. Most people move for a mix of both. The largest sources of migrants today include India, Mexico, Russia and China.

A is a person forced to flee their country because of war or persecution. International law, including a 1951 United Nations convention, gives refugees the right not to be sent back to danger. Other migrants move by choice to work or study. Many send money home. These payments, called , are a major source of income for countries like Mexico, the Philippines and India.

Migration changes both places. Home countries can lose skilled workers but gain money and new ideas. New countries gain workers, businesses and culture. They also face questions about jobs, housing and how newcomers fit in. Those questions are at the center of the debates in the next lesson.

Assimilation, Multiculturalism, Integration

Main ideaCountries choose different policies for newcomers, from assimilation to multiculturalism to integration, and each has supporters and critics.

In 1908 a play called The Melting Pot opened in the United States. Its writer, Israel Zangwill, pictured immigrants blending into one new American people. That idea is called : newcomers adopt the language and customs of their new country and become part of the mainstream. Many American public schools once taught this goal directly.

Canada chose another path. In 1971 it became one of the first countries to adopt as official policy. The idea is that groups can keep their languages and traditions while being full citizens. Supporters often compare Canada to a mosaic, where each piece keeps its color. France, by contrast, stresses one shared republican identity and keeps religion out of public schools. A 2004 law bans conspicuous religious symbols there.

Many countries now speak of , a middle path. Newcomers learn the language and laws and take part in work and civic life. They may keep their culture at home and in their communities. Integration is described as two-way: newcomers adapt, and the country also adapts to them.

Each approach has critics. Some say assimilation erases heritage. Others say multiculturalism can leave groups living apart. In 2010 German Chancellor Angela Merkel said her country’s attempt at multiculturalism had failed, and she called for more focus on learning German. Supporters of each model point to places where it has worked.

Debates About Migration

Main ideaPeople disagree about how much migration a country should allow and how, and each side raises real concerns.

In a town meeting, a factory owner says she cannot find enough workers and wants more visas. A retired machinist worries that newcomers will lower wages. A teacher says her classroom is richer for the students from many countries. A nurse points out that half the aides at her hospital were born abroad. Each speaker is describing something real.

Supporters of more migration often argue that newcomers fill jobs and start businesses. They say migrants pay taxes and help aging countries with too few young workers. People who want less or more controlled migration raise other concerns. These debates are about values as well as facts. How much should a country weigh the needs of newcomers against those of current residents? How fast can a community change and still hold together? Good arguments on any side use evidence and describe the other side fairly.
