The Real Illuminati and the Legend After

Illuminati

You have probably heard the word Illuminati in a video, a song or a joke. Online it is often used to mean a hidden group that secretly runs the world. Fewer people know that the Illuminati were a real, small society. A law professor founded it in Bavaria, in southern Germany, in 1776. The government banned it within ten years.

This unit follows two stories. The first is the real order: its founder, its code names, its quarrels, the laws against it and the police searches that exposed its papers. The second is the legend that grew after the order ended. Writers blamed it for the French Revolution. Ministers warned Americans about it. In the 1960s and 1970s, joke books and novels, one begun in Chicago, gave it new life.

Along the way you will practice skills historians use every day. You will read primary sources, spot bias and test a claim for corroboration. You will sort claims into documented, disputed and unsupported. By the end, you will be able to explain what the evidence shows about the Illuminati and why the legend lasts.

The big question

How can a real secret society that lasted less than a decade turn into a legend that people still believe more than two hundred years later?

Weishaupt's Order, 1776–1785

The Order
Big questionWhat do the Illuminati's own seized papers show about what the order was, and what it was not?
The story

A House Search in Landshut

In October 1786, Bavarian officers search a man's home and carry off the papers of a secret order.

It is October 1786, in Landshut, a town in Bavaria in southern Germany. Officers of the Elector, the ruler of Bavaria, arrive at the house of Franz Xaver von Zwack. Zwack had worked for the government as a councillor. Now the government suspects him of something. It believes he is a leader in a banned secret society.

The search lasts two days, the 11th and 12th of October. The officers go through desks, boxes and shelves. They find bundles of letters. They find rule books and lists of members who used strange names from ancient Greece and Rome. They find plans for how the group should grow. Everything is packed up and taken away.

The papers belong to the Order of the Illuminati. The name means the enlightened ones. A law professor named Adam Weishaupt had started it ten years earlier. For most of those years, only members knew its rules. Bavaria had already banned the order in 1784 and 1785. The search was meant to show the public what it had really been.

Then the government did something new. In 1787 it printed the seized papers as a book, by order of the Elector. Anyone could buy it and read the secret order's letters. Today, historians still read those printed papers. They are the best evidence we have about what the real Illuminati wanted and did.

Talk about itThe government printed a secret group's private papers to turn people against it. Is the enemy's printing of a group's papers good evidence about that group? What would you check?
Section 1

A Professor Starts an Order

23.1

Ingolstadt, May 1776

Main ideaAdam Weishaupt, a young professor of canon law at the University of Ingolstadt in Bavaria, founded the Order of the Illuminati on 1 May 1776.

Ingolstadt is a town on the Danube River in Bavaria. In the 1770s it had Bavaria’s main university. One of its teachers was Adam Weishaupt, born in 1748. He taught , the law of the Catholic Church. He was still in his twenties, young for a professor.

On 1 May 1776, Weishaupt met with a few of his students to start a club. It was a , a group that keeps its membership, rules or meetings hidden from outsiders. He called it the Order of the Illuminati. The name comes from a Latin word for people who have been given light, or understanding.

The date is a favorite of later rumors. 1776 is also the year of the American Declaration of Independence. But no document links the two events. Historians call it a coincidence of the calendar. The date itself comes from the order’s own papers, which the Bavarian government later seized and printed.

At the start the order was tiny. Its first members were students and young men at the start of their careers. Weishaupt planned to train them in his ideas and then help them into good jobs. He hoped that over time they would spread those ideas through Bavarian society.

23.2

Reason Against Control

Main ideaWeishaupt's order grew from Enlightenment ideas: trust in reason, and opposition to church control over what students could learn.

The 1700s in Europe are often called the age of the . Writers argued that people should use reason and evidence to understand the world. They questioned old authorities, including kings and church leaders. Many Enlightenment thinkers wanted schools, science and books to be freer.

For about two hundred years, members of the Jesuit order, a Catholic order of priests, had shaped teaching at Ingolstadt. In 1773 the pope dissolved the Jesuit order. Weishaupt had been educated at a Jesuit school himself. Yet he was never a Jesuit, and he opposed their influence at his university.

Weishaupt disliked , the control of what people are allowed to read, write or say. In Bavaria, church and state officials decided which books were allowed. Weishaupt wanted his students to read freely and think for themselves. He also wanted them to become better people, not just better scholars.

So why keep the order secret? Weishaupt believed open talk of these ideas could cost members their jobs. Critics saw it differently. To them, a hidden group that wanted to place its members in power looked like a plot. Both views appear in writing from the time, and students today can weigh them.

23.3

Grades and Code Names

Main ideaThe order ranked members in grades and gave them classical code names; Weishaupt himself was Spartacus.

Inside the order, members were ranked in . A grade is a level of membership. New members started at the lowest grade. Each one reported to a superior above him, who guided his reading and judged his progress. Only those higher up knew much about the order’s leaders and plans.

Members also used . A code name is a false name used to hide a person’s identity. Weishaupt took the name Spartacus. The real Spartacus was an enslaved gladiator who led a great revolt against Rome around 73 BCE. The name hinted that Weishaupt saw himself as a freer of minds.

Code names had a practical use. If a letter was lost or opened, outsiders would not know who wrote it. Later, one member, Baron Adolph Knigge, used the name Philo. Many other members chose names from ancient Greece and Rome. The lists linking code names to real names were among the papers police later found.

Grades, code names and ceremonies were common in secret societies of the time. Freemasons, for example, had grades and private rituals too. In the Illuminati, though, the system also served Weishaupt’s control. Members lower down often could not see who was steering the order.

Section 2

Growth and Quarrels

23.4

Knigge and the Lodges

Main ideaAfter Baron Adolph Knigge joined in 1780, the order grew by recruiting inside Masonic lodges.

For its first few years the order stayed small. That changed in 1780, when Baron Adolph Knigge joined. Knigge was a writer and a nobleman from northern Germany. He was also an active Freemason. He knew many men who liked ideas, ceremonies and quiet meetings.

is a brotherhood that grew out of the stonemasons’ guilds. By the 1700s its members met in , local branches with their own officers and rituals. Lodges were already networks of educated men across Europe. Knigge saw them as the best place to find new members for the Illuminati.

So the Illuminati began to inside Masonic lodges. To recruit means to bring in new members. Some Illuminati joined lodges, then invited chosen brothers into the order. Knigge also helped add higher grades and ceremonies that would appeal to Masons.

This is why the two groups are often mixed up. The Illuminati did not begin inside Freemasonry. Weishaupt founded it separately in 1776. But from about 1780 it grew through Masonic lodges. Most Freemasons of the time never joined it, and many lodges knew nothing about it.

23.5

How Big Was It?

Main ideaNo one knows the exact size of the order; historians' estimates range from a few hundred to a little over two thousand members.

How many Illuminati were there? This is a harder question than it looks. The order kept its lists hidden, and not every list survived. Historians build their counts from seized papers, letters and later archive finds. Each source catches only part of the membership.

So historians give an , a careful guess based on the evidence. Estimates run from a few hundred members to a little over two thousand. The number depends on who counts as a member. Some men joined and left within a year. Some are known only by a code name.

Famous names make the question harder. Some well-known German writers appear in lists that researchers have put together. How active they were is , meaning serious scholars disagree. Appearing on a list does not show that a person attended meetings or shared the order’s goals.

Compare this with later rumors that the order held thousands of powerful leaders across Europe. The records show something smaller. It was a network of students, officials, professors and some nobles, mostly in German-speaking lands. It was real, but it was never an army.

23.6

A Split at the Top

Main ideaKnigge quarreled with Weishaupt over control of the order and left it on 1 July 1784, then later told his side in print.

As the order grew, its two leaders clashed. Knigge had written ceremonies and brought in many members. He felt he deserved a real say. Weishaupt wanted to keep control of the order’s direction. Letters between the men grew angry and bitter. Neither man was willing to give way.

Knigge complained that Weishaupt acted like a , a ruler who holds power alone and harshly. He said Weishaupt demanded obedience from members who had joined to think freely. For a group that praised freedom of thought, this was an awkward charge. Weishaupt, for his part, felt Knigge was changing the order too much.

On 1 July 1784, Knigge formally left the order. His departure was a , a split inside a group. It came just days after Bavaria’s first ban on secret societies, issued on 22 June 1784. The order now faced trouble from outside and inside at the same time.

In 1788 Knigge published his own account of his time in the order. Its short title is Philo’s Final Answer, after his code name. A member’s own account is a useful . It gives an inside view, but it also defends the writer. Historians read it alongside the letters seized by police.

Section 3

Bavaria Bans the Order

23.7

The Edicts of 1784 and 1785

Main ideaBetween 1784 and 1787 the ruler of Bavaria issued a series of edicts that banned secret societies, first in general and then by name.

Bavaria was ruled by an , a German prince who had a vote in choosing the Holy Roman Emperor. In the 1780s the Elector was Karl Theodor. Rumors reached his court about a hidden order placing its members in offices. He and his advisers decided to act.

On 22 June 1784, the Elector issued an , an official order from a ruler that has the force of law. It banned all societies that the state had not approved. It did not name the Illuminati. But every secret group, including the order, now met at its own risk.

On 24 February 1785, members sent the Elector a , a formal written request to a ruler. They said the order had been misunderstood. It did not help. On 2 March 1785, a second edict named the Freemasons and the Illuminati directly. Another followed on 16 August 1785.

A fourth edict, of 16 August 1787, went further. It made recruiting for the order a crime that could be punished by death. By then the order had already broken apart. The harsh law shows how afraid the government had become of what the order might have been.

23.8

Weishaupt Leaves Bavaria

Main ideaWeishaupt lost his post in 1785, left Bavaria and lived quietly in Gotha until his death in 1830.

The bans reached Weishaupt quickly. In 1785 he lost his professor’s post at Ingolstadt. He left Bavaria to avoid arrest and lived the rest of his life in . Some of his followers lost their government jobs too. The order he had built over nine years fell apart within months.

Weishaupt found a , a powerful person who protects or supports someone. Duke Ernst II ruled the small German state of Saxe-Gotha. The duke had an interest in the order himself. He let Weishaupt settle in his city of Gotha, outside Bavarian law.

Weishaupt lived in Gotha for more than forty years. He wrote books defending himself and his ideas. He did not lead a hidden empire. He died there on 18 November 1830, at 82. He had outlived his order by more than forty years, and he never led it again.

This quiet ending matters for later rumors. Many modern stories say the Illuminati went underground and kept growing. Historians find no , written records from the time, that the Bavarian order continued after the late 1780s. Groups that use the name today were founded much later.

23.9

The Lightning Story

Main ideaA famous story says papers found on a man killed by lightning exposed the order, but the official record credits a house search instead.

Many books tell a dramatic story. In July 1785, a priest named Johann Jakob Lanz was traveling near Regensburg. He was a member of the order. Lightning struck and killed him. According to the story, secret papers were found sewn into his clothes, and they exposed the whole order.

The death by lightning is widely reported in older German histories. The papers are another matter. The government’s own 1787 books, its , do not credit Lanz. They say the papers came from a search of Zwack’s house in October 1786. The lightning story is a , a story handed down that may mix fact and invention.

Historians test a story like this by looking for . That means a second, independent source that agrees. The official books were printed by the men who seized the papers. If they had found papers on Lanz, they had every reason to say so. They named a house search instead.

Why did the lightning story spread? It is exciting. It makes it seem as if heaven itself exposed the plot. Good stories travel faster than careful records. A careful reader can enjoy a legend as a legend. The same reader can still say plainly what the evidence shows.

Section 4

The Papers Go Public

23.10

Landshut and Sandersdorf

Main ideaOfficers seized the order's papers in a search of Zwack's house on 11–12 October 1786 and in a second search at Sandersdorf castle in 1787.

Franz Xaver von Zwack was a former government councillor living in Landshut. He was one of Weishaupt’s closest partners in the order. On 11 and 12 October 1786, officers searched his house. They his papers, which means they took them by legal force.

The haul was large. It included the order’s rules, lists linking code names to real names, and letters from Weishaupt. Some papers showed plans to place members in good positions. Others showed members discussing books, morals and how to recruit. For the first time, outsiders could read the order’s inner workings.

In 1787 officers searched a second place. It was Sandersdorf castle, home of Baron Thomas von Bassus, another member. More papers were taken there. Together, the two searches gave the government a large , a collection of records kept together.

A search like this raises hard questions. The members had broken the Elector’s law. But the search also exposed private letters to the whole country. Historians are grateful the papers survived. They also remember that the papers reached us through force.

23.11

Printed by Order of the Elector

Main ideaIn 1787 the Bavarian government printed the seized papers for the public, a new way to turn people against a secret group.

Governments usually hide the evidence they seize. Bavaria did the opposite. In 1787, by order of the Elector, it turned the Landshut papers into a public , Some Original Writings of the Order of the Illuminati. A second volume, called the Supplement, printed papers from later searches.

The aim was to the order, to make people stop trusting or respecting it. Readers could see the members’ code names, the founder’s plans to place followers in office and quarrels among leaders. Some private letters also embarrassed Weishaupt personally. The government chose what to print and when.

This makes the books a form of . Propaganda is information spread on purpose to shape what people think, often for one side. The papers inside seem to be real. But the way they were chosen, framed and released served the Elector’s own goal.

The plan had an effect no one in Munich expected. The printed books spread across Europe. Writers far away read them and imagined an order much bigger than it had been. The government meant to end the Illuminati. Its books helped the Illuminati legend begin.

23.12

What the Papers Actually Show

Main ideaThe papers show a real, secretive reform club that wanted influence, not a plan it could carry out to take over the world.

What do the printed papers actually show? They show a secret order with ranks, code names and oaths of loyalty. They show a founder who wanted his members to gain influence in schools and government offices. They show plans to spread Enlightenment ideas and reduce church control of learning.

They also show limits. The members argued, quit and struggled to recruit. Much of their writing is about reading lists, moral self-improvement and running their own meetings. A large collection of the order’s papers survives today in a Berlin archive. Researchers who study it find many essays that read like homework.

People have drawn different from the same papers. Bavarian officials saw a plot against church and throne. Later admirers saw brave reformers under a harsh government. Both groups read with a , a leaning that shapes what a person notices and believes.

A fair reading holds both parts. The Illuminati were real, secretive and ambitious. They wanted influence and hid their plans. The papers do not show that they controlled any government, caused a revolution or survived the 1780s. The legend that followed is the subject of the next chapter.

24.1

The Rosicrucian Manifestos

Main ideaThree pamphlets printed in 1614–1616 described a secret Rosicrucian brotherhood that readers tried to join, but no such group ever answered.

In 1614 a short pamphlet appeared in Kassel, a German city. It was called the Fama Fraternitatis, the Fame of the Brotherhood. A second one, the Confessio, followed in Kassel in 1615. Together they are called the Rosicrucian . A manifesto is a public statement of a group’s beliefs or aims.

The pamphlets told of a man named Christian Rosenkreutz, or Rose-Cross. He was said to have traveled in the East and gathered hidden wisdom. The Fama says his followers found his tomb 120 years after his death, and his body had not decayed. The brotherhood, it said, would now reform learning and invite worthy men to join.

Across Europe, readers were excited. Some printed open letters asking to join the brotherhood. No documented answer ever came. Historians have found no member lists, meeting records or letters from the brotherhood itself. Most scholars conclude it existed on paper, as an idea.

The manifestos belong to writing, meaning writing about hidden knowledge meant for a small inner circle. They matter here because they set a pattern. A mysterious, invisible brotherhood with secret wisdom became a powerful idea in European imagination, long before the Illuminati.

24.4

A Shock That Needed an Explanation

Main ideaThe French Revolution shocked Europe, and some writers explained it as the work of secret societies rather than of many long-building causes.

Historians explain the Revolution through many causes. France was deep in debt. Bread prices rose and people went hungry. Many people resented the privileges of nobles and clergy. Enlightenment ideas about rights spread through books and clubs. The most famous club was the , a political club of radical revolutionaries.

Some writers wanted a simpler answer. They argued that a hidden group had planned it all. This kind of explanation is a , a belief that a secret group secretly causes major events. It can feel satisfying. It gives a clear villain for a confusing and frightening change.

The Illuminati were a tempting villain. Their papers had been printed in 1787, so writers could quote them. But the order had been banned in 1785 and broken up by about 1787. No document shows it directing anything in France. Modern historians reject the idea that it caused the Revolution.

Main ideaThe French priest Augustin Barruel published Memoirs in 1797–98 that blamed the Revolution on a plot of philosophers, Freemasons and Illuminati.

In his account, the plotters were anti-church philosophers, then some Freemasons, then the Illuminati. One volume focuses on the Illuminati. It quotes the Bavarian papers of 1787 at length. That is part of why the book seemed convincing. It pointed to real documents.

Robison's Proofs of a Conspiracy

Main ideaJohn Robison, a respected Edinburgh scientist, published Proofs of a Conspiracy in 1797, showing that expertise in one field does not guarantee sound history.

John Robison taught at the University of Edinburgh in Scotland. Natural philosophy was the old name for physics and related sciences. He was also secretary of the Royal Society of Edinburgh. Readers trusted him as a serious man of learning.

In 1797 he published a book with a very long title: Proofs of a Conspiracy against all the Religions and Governments of Europe, carried on in the secret meetings of Free Masons, Illuminati, and Reading Societies. It was printed in Edinburgh and London. American editions followed in 1798.

Robison wrote at almost the same time as Barruel, and both used the same Bavarian publications. Barruel said he saw Robison’s book late in his own work. Historians note that Robison used the Bavarian papers loosely. He stretched what they showed to fit a large plot across Europe.

Robison’s case teaches , the careful testing of a source before trusting it. A great scientist can still be a weak historian. Deep in one field does not carry over to every field. Readers must ask what evidence each claim rests on, no matter who makes it.

Main ideaThe 1798 scare was partly political, and Thomas Jefferson read the same evidence and judged Weishaupt a well-meaning reformer.

In 1798 American politics was bitterly divided. The , the party of President Adams, favored a strong central government and leaned toward Britain. Main ideaThe eye and pyramid on the one-dollar bill come from the Great Seal designs of 1776–1782 and have no documented link to the Illuminati.

In 1776 Congress asked a committee of Benjamin Franklin, John Adams and Thomas Jefferson to design a , the official stamp of the new nation. The seal’s reverse was put on the one-dollar bill in 1935. None of the designers is linked to the Bavarian order. The eye was a familiar symbol long before 1776. A symbol can be shared by many groups without meaning they are connected. Sharing a picture is not the same as sharing a plan.

Why: The Bavarian order ended before 1789, and no document shows it directing events in France.

4. What was happening between the United States and France when Morse preached in 1798?

Why: The Quasi-War was an undeclared naval conflict, and it made many Americans fearful.

5. What did Washington believe about American Masonic lodges in 1798?

Why: Washington said the ideas had spread in America, but he did not believe the lodges as bodies spread them.

6. How did Jefferson's reading of Barruel differ from Morse's alarm?

Why: In 1800 Jefferson judged Weishaupt a sincere lover of humanity who used secrecy under a harsh government.

7. Where does the eye above the pyramid on the dollar bill come from?

Why: The eye was proposed for the Great Seal in 1776, and the pyramid and eye were adopted in 1782.

8. What role did Illuminatus! (1975) play in the legend?

Why: Shea and Wilson wrote the trilogy as satire, but many readers treated its Illuminati as real.

Unit test

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Where did Adam Weishaupt found the Illuminati in 1776?

Why: Weishaupt founded the order at Ingolstadt, where he taught canon law.

2. Which idea was central to Weishaupt's order?

Why: The order grew from Enlightenment ideas about reason and freer learning.

3. What was Spartacus?

Why: Weishaupt used the code name Spartacus, after the leader of a revolt against Rome.

4. What role did Baron Adolph Knigge play?

Why: Knigge joined in 1780, recruited through lodges and left on 1 July 1784 after quarrels.

5. What did the Bavarian edicts of 1784 and 1785 do?

Why: The 1784 edict banned unapproved societies; the 1785 edict named Freemasons and Illuminati.

6. Why are the 1787 printed papers both valuable and biased?

Why: The papers seem genuine, but the government printed them as propaganda against the order.

7. Which story lacks corroboration from the official record?

Why: The 1787 books credit the house search, not papers found on Lanz.

8. Why was John Robison's book taken seriously in 1797?

Why: Robison taught natural philosophy at Edinburgh, so readers trusted him, even though his history was weak.

9. Which statement about Barruel and the French Revolution is most accurate?

Why: Barruel quoted the Bavarian papers, but they did not show the order directing events in France.

10. Why do historians call the 1798 scare partly partisan?

Why: Historians from Stauffer on note that Federalist clergy used the scare against the Democratic-Republicans.

11. How did Washington answer G. W. Snyder in 1798?

Why: Washington drew a distinction between ideas spreading and the lodges, as bodies, spreading them.

12. What does the pair of Morse's sermon and Jefferson's 1800 letter show?

Why: Both drew on the same kind of evidence, yet Morse saw a plot and Jefferson saw a reformer.

13. Which fact about the dollar-bill eye is documented?

Why: The eye was proposed to the first Great Seal committee in 1776 and adopted for the seal's reverse in 1782.

14. What made the Golden Dawn different from the Rosicrucian brotherhood of the manifestos?

Why: Records show the Golden Dawn's founders, temples and members, while no records show a Rosicrucian brotherhood.

15. Which claim about the Illuminati is unsupported?

Why: No documentary evidence shows the order continued after the 1780s, so the claim is unsupported.
