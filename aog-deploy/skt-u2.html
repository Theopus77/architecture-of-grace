Safe and Steady

Safety

Before a skater rolls, they get ready. They buckle a helmet. They strap on pads. They pick a safe, smooth place. A grown-up comes along.

Next, they learn to stand still on the board. They start on grass or carpet. They bend their knees and hold their arms out. They look ahead.

In this unit you will learn to check a helmet, fall small and find your front foot. You will see that balance grows with practice.

The big question

How do skaters get safe and steady before they ever roll?

When it happened
Step 1

Put on a helmet. Set it low, make a V with the straps, click the buckle.

Step 2

Strap on knee pads, elbow pads and wrist guards.

Step 3

Wear flat sneakers and tie the laces.

Step 4

Pick a smooth, dry, flat place far from cars, with a grown-up nearby.

Step 5

Practice falling small on grass: get low, arms bent, roll.

Step 6

Set the board on grass or carpet so it cannot roll.

Step 7

Front foot over the front bolts, back foot over the back bolts.

Step 8

Bend your knees, arms out, look ahead. Practice every day.

Helmet First

Safety
Big questionWhat do skaters do before they roll, so a fall is a small one?
The story

The Strap Check Line

No one rolls yet. First, every helmet gets checked by a friend.

It is skate day in the gym. Six boards rest on the floor. Nobody stands on them yet. Coach Rivera claps two times. The class lines up in pairs. Each child holds a helmet.

"Helmets on," says Coach. Maya puts hers on. It sits low on her forehead. Her partner, Jon, holds up two fingers. They fit between her eyebrows and the helmet. "Good," says Jon.

Next, Jon checks her strap. It is buckled. He slides one finger under it. The strap is snug, not tight. Then Maya checks Jon. His buckle hangs open. He clicks it shut.

Then come the pads. Knee pads. Elbow pads. Wrist guards. Everyone wears flat sneakers. Coach looks down the line. "Now we are ready to learn," she says.

Talk about itWhy do you think the class checks helmets before anyone stands on a board?
Section 1

Your Helmet

3.1

A Helmet Guards Your Head

Main ideaA helmet has soft foam inside that keeps a bump from hurting your head.

Your head holds your brain. Your brain helps you think, see and move. A fall can bump your head. A is a hard hat that guards it.

Look inside a helmet. You see thick foam. The outside is a hard shell. If you fall, the shell spreads out the bump. The foam squishes. It soaks up the hit, so your head does not get it all.

Skaters wear a helmet every time they ride. Put it on before you step on the board. A helmet that took a hard hit may be worn out. Ask a grown-up to check it.

Think it through
Egg in a Cup

Look at two eggs. One egg sits in a plain cup. One egg sits in a cup full of foam. Both cups tip over onto the rug. The foam hugs the second egg. It soaks up the bump.

Which egg is like your head inside a helmet? Why?

Words to know
helmet
a hard hat with soft foam inside that guards your head
foam
soft, squishy stuff inside a helmet that soaks up a bump
Check yourself

1. What does the foam inside a helmet do?

Why: The foam squishes when you fall. It soaks up the hit so your head does not get it all.

2. When should a skater wear a helmet?

Why: Skaters wear a helmet every time they ride, even on flat ground.

3. What should you do if your helmet took a hard hit?

Why: A helmet that took a hard hit may be worn out. A grown-up can check it.
3.2

Snug and Buckled

Main ideaA helmet works best when it sits low, fits snug and is buckled.

A helmet must fit. It should be . Snug means close, but not squeezing. Shake your head. A snug helmet does not wobble or slide.

First, set it low on your forehead. About two fingers fit between your eyebrows and the helmet. Next, the side straps make a V under each ear. Then click the under your chin.

Now open your mouth wide. You should feel the helmet pull down a bit. About one finger should fit under the chin strap. A helmet tipped back or left open cannot do its job.

Think it through
The Two-Finger Check

Look in a mirror. Put on your helmet. Lay two fingers flat above your eyebrows. They touch the helmet edge. Now find the straps by your ears. They make a V. Click the buckle. Slide one finger under the strap.

Try the two-finger check with a partner. Does the helmet sit low enough?

Words to know
snug
close and steady, but not squeezing
buckle
the clip under your chin that holds the helmet strap shut
Check yourself

1. Where should a helmet sit?

Why: A helmet sits low on the forehead, about two fingers above the eyebrows.

2. What does snug mean?

Why: Snug means close and steady but not squeezing. A snug helmet does not slide.

3. What do you do right after the side straps make a V?

Why: First the helmet sits low. Next the straps make a V. Then you click the buckle.
Section 2

Pads and Shoes

3.3

Pads and Wrist Guards

Main ideaKnee pads, elbow pads and wrist guards cover the parts that hit the ground first.

When people fall, knees, elbows and hands often touch down first. Pads cover those spots. A has a hard cap on the front. It lets your knee slide instead of scrape.

Elbow pads work the same way. A has a stiff piece along the palm. Wrists get hurt a lot in skating. Wrist guards help keep them safe.

Pads must fit too. Pull each strap so the pad stays put. A pad that slides down cannot cover your knee. Wear all your pads while you learn.

Words to know
knee pad
a cover with a hard cap that guards your knee
wrist guard
a glove-like brace with a stiff piece that guards your wrist
Check yourself

1. Which body parts often touch the ground first in a fall?

Why: Knees, elbows and hands often touch down first. Pads cover those spots.

2. What does the hard cap on a knee pad let you do?

Why: The hard cap lets your knee slide on the ground instead of scraping.

3. Why do skaters wear wrist guards?

Why: Wrist injuries are common in skating. Wrist guards help keep wrists safe.
3.4

Flat Shoes That Grip

Main ideaFlat sneakers with rubber soles help your feet stay on the board.

Look at the top of a skateboard. It feels like rough sandpaper. This is . Flat rubber soles hold onto it, so your feet do not slip.

Wear flat sneakers that cover your toes. Tie the laces. Loose laces can catch on the wheels. Sandals, flip-flops and boots are not good for skating.

A thin, flat sole lets you feel the board under your foot. You can tell where your feet are. That helps you stay steady.

Think it through
Two Shoes Side by Side

Look at two shoes. One is a flip-flop. It is loose and slippery. It can slide right off. The other is a flat sneaker. It has a rubber sole. The laces are tied tight.

Which shoe would hold onto grip tape? Point to it and tell why.

Words to know
grip tape
the rough, sandpaper-like top of a skateboard that keeps shoes from slipping
sole

Check yourself

1. What is grip tape?

Why: Grip tape is the rough, sandpaper-like top of a skateboard.

2. Which shoes are best for skating?

Why: Flat rubber soles grip the board and cover your toes.

3. Why tie your laces before you ride?

Why: Loose laces can get caught in the wheels. Tied laces stay out of the way.
Section 3

Where and Who

3.5

A Safe Place to Ride

Main ideaRide on smooth, dry, flat ground, far away from cars.

Small wheels stop fast on a pebble. A stick or a crack can stop them too. Then the rider tips forward. So pick ground that is , with no rocks or cracks.

Pick dry ground too. Wet ground is slippery. Pick flat ground for learning, so the board does not roll away on its own.

Stay far away from cars. Never ride in a street or a parking lot. Never ride down a driveway that goes into a street. A playground blacktop or a skatepark is a good place.

Think it through
Pick the Spot

Look at three places. Spot one is a busy street. Cars drive by. Spot two is a sidewalk with big cracks and puddles. Spot three is a dry, smooth blacktop behind the school. A fence keeps cars out.

Which spot is the safest place to learn? What makes the other two unsafe?

Words to know
smooth
flat and even, with no bumps, rocks or cracks
Check yourself

1. What can happen if a wheel hits a pebble?

Why: Small wheels stop fast on a pebble. Then the rider tips forward.

2. Which place is safe for learning?

Why: A dry, smooth, flat place away from cars is safe for learning.

3. Why is wet ground a bad place to ride?

Why: Wet ground is slippery, so shoes and wheels can slide.
3.6

A Grown-Up Nearby

Main ideaYoung skaters ride with a grown-up close by to help.

Doctors who care for children give advice about skating. They say children under 5 should not ride skateboards. Children 6 to 10 should have a grown-up watching.

A grown-up can check your helmet and pads. A grown-up can pick a safe spot. If you fall, a grown-up can help right away.

A grown-up can also hold your hand while you learn. That is called . Lots of skaters start this way.

Section 4

Falling Small

3.7

Get Low, Then Roll

Main ideaIf you start to fall, bend low and roll so the fall is short and soft.

Every skater falls. Falling is part of learning. You can learn to fall . A small fall is short and soft.

First, bend your knees and get low. Low means you are close to the ground. You have less far to go. Next, step off the board if you can. Then roll onto your side or slide on your knee pads.

Rolling spreads out the bump. Your back and side share it. No one part takes the whole hit.

Think it through
Falling Like a Ball

Look at a child on the soft grass. She pretends to wobble. She bends her knees. She tucks her chin. She curls up round like a ball. She rolls onto her side. She sits up and smiles.

On grass, with a grown-up, try bending low and rolling onto your side. Did you tuck your chin?

Words to know
fall small
to bend low and roll so a fall is short and soft
roll
to turn your body over on its side to spread out a bump
Check yourself

1. What is the first thing to do when you start to fall?

Why: First you bend your knees and get low, so you have less far to fall.

2. Why does rolling help?

Why: Rolling spreads the bump over your back and side, so no one part takes the whole hit.

3. Which one is a small fall?

Why: Getting low and sliding on knee pads makes the fall short and soft.
3.8

Bent Arms, Not Straight

Main ideaDo not catch yourself with straight, stiff arms; keep them bent and soft.

When people fall, they often throw their hands out. They lock their arms straight and stiff. Then the whole fall lands on the wrists. Wrists can get hurt this way.

Instead, keep your arms . Bent arms work like a spring. They give a little. Tuck them close and roll.

Practice on grass or a mat first. Get low. Keep your arms bent. Roll to your side. Practice makes this move happen by itself when you need it.

Think it through
Stiff Stick or Soft Spring

Look at two arms. One arm is straight and stiff, like a stick. When it hits the ground, it does not give. The other arm is bent, like a spring. When it hits, it squishes and gives a little.

Push gently on a wall with a straight arm, then a bent arm. Which one gives?

Words to know
bent
curved at the joint, not straight and stiff
Check yourself

1. What happens when you catch a fall with straight, stiff arms?

Why: Stiff, straight arms send the whole fall into the wrists.

2. How should your arms be when you fall?

Why: Bent arms give a little, like a spring. Tuck them close and roll.

3. Where should you first practice falling?

Why: Practice falling on soft grass or a mat first.

Balance Is Something You Practice

Balance
Big questionHow does your body learn to stay steady on a board?
The story

The Board That Stayed Still

On the classroom carpet, a skateboard waits. It will not roll. Everyone gets a turn.

Mr. Kim sets a skateboard on the reading rug. He gives it a push. The wheels barely turn. "The carpet holds it still," he says. "That makes it a good place to start."

Lena goes first. She wears her helmet. She puts her front foot on the board. Then her back foot. She wobbles. Mr. Kim says, "Bend your knees. Put your arms out." She does. The wobble gets smaller.

Next is Theo. He puts his other foot in front. "Is that wrong?" he asks. "No," says Mr. Kim. "Some people ride that way. Both ways are fine."

By the end of the week, everyone can stand still for a long time. Nobody got a new body. They just practiced, a few minutes every day.

Talk about itWhat helped Lena's wobble get smaller?
Section 1

Start on Something Soft

4.1

Grass or Carpet First

Main ideaStand on the board on grass or carpet first, so it cannot roll away.

On smooth ground, a board rolls with a tiny push. On grass or carpet, it stays put. The soft ground grabs the wheels. This grab is called .

So start on grass or carpet. Put on your helmet and pads first. Step on and just stand. You can learn how the board feels without rolling.

When standing feels easy, you are ready for more. Then a grown-up can help you roll slowly on smooth, flat ground.

Think it through
Push Test

Look at one board on a smooth floor. Give it a small push. It rolls far away. Now set the same board on the grass. Give it the same small push. It barely moves.

Where does the board stay still? Why is that a good place to start?

Words to know
friction
the rubbing grab between two things that slows or stops motion
Check yourself

1. Why start on grass or carpet?

Why: Soft ground grabs the wheels, so the board stays still while you learn.

2. What is friction?

Why: Friction is the rubbing grab between two things. It slows or stops motion.

3. What comes after standing feels easy?

Why: Next, a grown-up helps you roll slowly on smooth, flat ground.
4.2

Feet Over the Bolts

Main ideaPut your front foot over the front bolts and your back foot over the back bolts.

Look at the top of a board. Near each end you see four small . They hold the trucks on. The are the metal parts that hold the wheels.

Stand with your feet across the board, toes pointing to one side. First, put your front foot over the front bolts. Next, put your back foot over the back bolts.

Feet over the bolts sit right above the wheels. The board is steady there. Feet too close to the ends can tip it up.

Count the Bolts

How many bolts sit under your front foot?

Words to know
bolts
the small metal screws on top of the board that hold the trucks on
trucks
the metal parts under the board that hold the wheels
Check yourself

1. Where does your front foot go?

Why: Your front foot goes over the front bolts, right above the front wheels.

2. What do the bolts hold on?

Why: The bolts hold the trucks onto the board.

3. Which way do your feet point?

Why: You stand with your feet across the board and your toes pointing to one side.
4.3

Knees Like a Spring

Main ideaBent knees work like a spring and help you stay steady.

Stand up straight with stiff legs. Have a friend give a tiny nudge. You tip. Now bend your knees and try again. You stay up.

Bent knees work like a . A spring squishes and pops back. Your knees soak up wobbles the same way. Bending also brings your body lower, closer to the board.

On the board, bend your knees a little. Not a deep squat. Just soft, like you are ready to jump. Keep your back tall.

Think it through
Stiff or Springy

Look at a child on the board on the carpet. His legs are straight and stiff. He wobbles side to side. Now he bends his knees a little. His body drops lower. The wobble gets smaller.

Stand on the floor. Bend your knees a little. Does it feel steadier?

Words to know
spring
a coil that squishes and pops back
Check yourself

1. How do bent knees help?

Why: Bent knees squish and pop back like a spring, so they soak up wobbles.

2. How much should you bend your knees on the board?

Why: Bend a little, like you are ready to jump. Not a deep squat.

3. What happens with stiff, straight legs?

Why: Stiff legs cannot soak up a nudge, so you tip more easily.
Section 2

Arms and Eyes Help

4.4

Arms Out Wide

Main ideaHolding your arms out helps you catch your balance.

Watch someone walk on a curb. Their arms go out to the sides. Arms out make easier. Balance means staying steady without falling.

On the board, hold your arms out low and loose. If you tip toward your toes, your arms can lean back. If you tip toward your heels, they lean forward. They help pull you back to the middle.

Keep your arms soft, not stiff. Little moves are best. Big flapping can make the wobble bigger.

Think it through
The Tightrope Walker

Look at a tightrope walker on a low line at a circus. She holds her arms out wide. She tips a little to one side. Her arms move a little the other way. She stays up.

Stand on one foot with your arms down. Now try with arms out. Which is easier?

Words to know
balance
staying steady without falling over
Check yourself

1. What does balance mean?

Why: Balance means staying steady without falling over.

2. How should you hold your arms on the board?

Why: Arms out low and loose help you catch your balance with small moves.

3. Why are little arm moves better than big flapping?

Why: Big flapping can make a wobble bigger. Little moves help you get back to the middle.
4.5

Eyes Where You Go

Main ideaLook up and ahead to where you want to go, not down at your feet.

Your eyes help you balance. Your body tends to follow where your head points. If you stare at your feet, you lean forward. Then you can tip.

So keep your chin up. to where you want to go. Pick a spot, like a tree or a door. Keep your eyes on it.

It is fine to peek down to check your feet. Then bring your eyes back up. When you start to roll, looking ahead also helps you see what is coming.

Think it through
Find Your Spot

Look at a girl on the board on the grass. She stares down at her shoes. She leans and wobbles. Then she lifts her chin. She looks at the red door across the yard. She stands taller and steadier.

Stand on one foot and look down. Now look at a spot far away. Which is steadier?

Words to know
look ahead
to keep your eyes up on where you want to go
Check yourself

1. Where should your eyes be on the board?

Why: Keep your chin up and look ahead to where you want to go.

2. What can happen if you stare at your feet?

Why: Your body follows your head. Staring down makes you lean forward.

3. Is it okay to peek down at your feet?

Why: A quick peek is fine. Then bring your eyes back up to your spot.
Section 3

Your Way, Every Day

4.6

Regular and Goofy

Main ideaSome riders put the left foot in front and some the right; both are normal.

Skaters do not all stand the same way. Riding with the left foot in front is called . Riding with the right foot in front is called .

Goofy is just a name. It does not mean silly. Lots of great skaters ride goofy. Lots ride regular. Neither one is better.

To find yours, stand on the board on carpet. Try one foot in front. Then try the other foot in front. Pick the way that feels steady. The foot in front is your front foot.

Think it through
Two Riders on the Rug

Look at two friends on two boards. Ana stands with her left foot in front. She rides regular. Ben stands with his right foot in front. He rides goofy. Both bend their knees. Both hold their arms out. Both stand steady.

On carpet, try each foot in front. Which way feels steady for you?

Words to know
regular
riding with the left foot in front
goofy
riding with the right foot in front
Check yourself

1. What is regular?

Why: Regular means riding with the left foot in front.

2. Ben rides with his right foot in front. What is that called?

Why: Riding with the right foot in front is called goofy. It is a normal way to ride.

3. How do you find your front foot?

Why: Try both ways on carpet. The way that feels steady shows your front foot.
4.7

Practice Makes Steady

Main ideaBalance gets better each time you practice.

Nobody is born knowing how to skate. Your brain and body learn by doing. Each time you stand on the board, they learn a little more. This is called .

Short practice helps a lot. Stand on the board on carpet for a few minutes. Rest. Try again another day. Wobbles get smaller as you go.

Count how long you can stand still. Write it down each day. Watch the number grow. A wobble just means you are learning.

practice

2. What kind of practice helps a lot?

Why: Short practice with rest, repeated on different days, helps a lot.

3. What does a wobble mean?

Why: A wobble is part of learning. Wobbles get smaller with practice.
