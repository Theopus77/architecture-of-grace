Grammar, Speaking and Listening

Language and Speaking

Devon reads his beach paragraph aloud and runs out of air halfway through, because the whole thing is one sentence. Across the hall, a book club argues about an old tale until three people are talking at once and nobody hears anybody. Both rooms have the same problem. The words are there. The shape that lets people follow them is not.

This unit is about that shape. First, the grammar of a sentence: subjects and predicates, the kinds of words that fill them, how to join sentences and how to fix ones that are broken or never stop. Then, the grammar of a conversation: how to prepare, listen, build on someone's idea, disagree without being rude, and give a short talk that a room can follow.

By the end you will be able to take a run-on apart and put it back together, make your verbs agree, pick their over there, and tell the difference between talking at people and talking with them. None of this is about sounding fancy. It is about being followed.

The big question

How do the rules of sentences and the rules of talking help other people follow what you mean?

The steps
Day 1

You split a sentence into its two halves: who or what, and what happens.

Day 2

You sort the words in a sentence: nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions.

Day 3

You join two short sentences with a comma and a conjunction, then with because or when.

Day 4

You find the fragments and run-ons in a paragraph and fix each one a different way.

Day 5

You make subjects and verbs agree, keep one tense, and choose their, there or they're.

Day 6

You read a story, mark two spots, and bring one question to the circle.

Day 7

You say back what the last speaker said before you add your own idea.

Day 8

You plan a two-minute talk with an opening, three ordered points and an ending.

Day 9

You record your talk, count the ums, fix one thing and record it again.

Day 10

You give the talk to a real audience, at a pace they can follow, with one visual that helps.

The story

The Sentence That Would Not Stop

Devon reads his own paragraph aloud, and about halfway through he runs out of air.

Mr. Alvarez asks Devon to read his paragraph to the class. Devon stands up. He takes a breath and starts. "On Saturday we went to the beach and it was hot and my cousin brought a kite and the wind was strong and the kite went up and then the string broke and we ran after it and a dog got there first." Devon's face turns red. He has not stopped once. He has not taken a breath.

The class laughs, but not in a mean way. A girl named Priya says, "That is one sentence? I got lost after the kite." Devon looks at his paper. She is right. It is one sentence. It is a sentence with eight little sentences stuffed inside it, all tied together with the word and.

Mr. Alvarez writes the whole thing on the board. Then he hands Devon a marker. "Find the places where you could stop," he says. Devon reads it again, slowly. He puts a period after hot. He puts one after kite. He puts one after broke. Now the paragraph has four sentences. He reads it again. This time he breathes.

"Nothing changed in the story," Mr. Alvarez says. "The beach is still hot. The dog still wins. But now a reader can follow you." He taps the board. "That is what grammar is for. Not to trap you. To help people follow you."

Devon sits down. He is still a little red. But he writes something at the top of his paper: Stop and breathe. This chapter is about the parts of a sentence and how they fit, so that every reader can follow you all the way to the dog.

Talk about itDevon's story did not change when he added periods. So what did change, and why did it matter to the class?
Section 1

The Parts of a Sentence

20.1

Subject and Predicate

Main ideaEvery sentence has a subject (who or what) and a predicate (what happens), and it needs both.

Look at this sentence: The dog got there first. Ask, who is this about? The dog. That part is the . Now ask, what did the dog do? Got there first. That part is the . Every full sentence has both parts. Take one away and it falls apart.

The subject is the who or the what. It can be one word or several: Devon. My cousin’s red kite. The strong wind at the beach. The predicate is the rest. It holds the action or tells what the subject is. Devon laughed. My cousin’s red kite went up. The wind at the beach was strong.

Here is a quick test. Cover the subject with your hand. Does the rest still tell what happened? Cover the predicate. Does the rest still tell who? If either half is missing, you do not have a sentence yet. You have a piece of one.

Think it through
Two Halves on the Board

Mr. Alvarez draws a line down the middle of the board. On the left he writes: The little brown dog. My cousin. The kite string. On the right he writes: broke with a snap. ran across the sand. barked at the waves. He asks the class to match a left side to a right side to make a sentence that makes sense.

Which left side goes with which right side? Could any left side match more than one right side?

Words to know
subject
the part of a sentence that tells who or what the sentence is about
predicate
the part of a sentence that tells what the subject does or is
Check yourself

1. In the sentence 'The wind was strong,' what is the subject?

Why: The subject tells who or what the sentence is about. It is about the wind.

2. What does the predicate of a sentence do?

Why: The predicate holds the action or tells what the subject is. 'Got there first' is a predicate.

3. 'My cousin's red kite' has no predicate. What is it?

Why: It names a what, but nothing happens. Without a predicate, it is only a piece of a sentence.
20.2

Nouns and Pronouns

Main ideaNouns name people, places, things and ideas, and pronouns stand in for nouns so you do not repeat them.

Read this: Priya lost Priya’s pencil, so Priya asked Devon if Devon had seen Priya’s pencil. Ouch. Now read this: Priya lost her pencil, so she asked Devon if he had seen it. Much better. The words her, she, he and it took the place of the names. Those words are .

First, the names. A is a word that names a person, a place, a thing or an idea. Priya, beach, kite and courage are all nouns. Some nouns name one thing: dog. Some name more than one: dogs. A noun that names one special person or place gets a capital letter: Devon, Chicago, Lake Michigan.

A pronoun stands in for a noun. I, you, he, she, it, we and they are the common ones. Here is the rule that matters: the reader must know which noun the pronoun replaces. Priya asked Devon, and he said no. Who said no? Devon, because he is the closest boy. If two boys were there, you would need the name again.

20.3

Verbs, Adjectives and Adverbs

Main ideaVerbs show action or being, adjectives describe nouns, and adverbs describe verbs.

The kite went up. The kite is the noun. Went is the , the word that shows the action. Some verbs show a strong action: ran, snapped, barked. Some verbs show being, not doing: is, was, are. The sand was hot. Nothing moves, but was is still the verb.

Now add some color. The red kite went up. Red tells you more about the kite. A word that describes a noun is an . The strong wind. A tiny dog. Three cousins. Adjectives answer which one, what kind, or how many.

One more. The kite went up fast. Fast tells you how it went up. A word that describes a verb is an . Adverbs answer how, when or where. Many end in -ly: slowly, loudly, suddenly. The dog barked loudly. Loudly describes barked. Here is a trick: an adjective sticks to a noun, and an adverb sticks to a verb.

2. What does an adjective describe?

20.4

Prepositions Show Where and When

Main ideaA preposition is a small word like under, on or after that links a noun to the rest of the sentence.

Where is the dog? The dog is under the table. On the table. Beside the table. Behind the table. Each little word tells you where the dog is. Those little words are . They link a noun (the table) to the rest of the sentence.

Prepositions can tell where: in, on, under, over, beside, between, across. They can tell when: before, after, during, until. They can tell other things too: with, for, about, of. A preposition never stands alone. It always comes with a noun or pronoun after it: after lunch, with my cousin, about the kite.

The preposition plus its noun is called a . A phrase is a group of words that works together but is not a whole sentence. The string broke over the water. Over the water tells where. Take the phrase away and you still have a sentence: The string broke. Add it back and the reader can see the place.

Think it through

Which word changed each time? What would happen to the picture if you took that word out and just said 'The dog is the chair'?

Words to know
preposition
a small word like under, on, after or with that links a noun to the rest of the sentence
phrase

Check yourself

1. Which word is a preposition?

Why: 'Under' links a noun to the rest of the sentence and tells where. It is a preposition.

2. What always comes after a preposition?

Why: A preposition never stands alone. It needs a noun or pronoun: under the table, with him.

3. In 'We ate lunch after the race,' what does 'after the race' tell you?

Why: 'After' is a preposition that tells when. The phrase 'after the race' tells the time of lunch.
Section 2

Kinds of Sentences

20.5

Simple and Compound

Main ideaA simple sentence has one subject and one predicate; a compound sentence joins two with a comma and a joining word.

The wind blew. That is a . One subject, one predicate, one complete thought. The kite went up. That is another. Two short sentences in a row can sound choppy. So writers join them: The wind blew, and the kite went up. Now it is one .

Look at what holds the two halves together: a comma and the word and. The words and, but, or and so are joining words, also called . Each one means something different. And adds. But shows a change. Or gives a choice. So shows a result. The string broke, so the kite flew away.

Here is the rule for the comma. When you join two full sentences with a conjunction, put a comma before the conjunction. The dog got there first, but Devon did not care. Both halves could stand alone, so the comma goes in. If the second half cannot stand alone, no comma: Devon laughed and sat down.

Check yourself

1. Which one is a compound sentence?

Why: Two full sentences joined by a comma and 'and' make a compound sentence.

2. Which conjunction shows a result?

Why: 'So' shows that one thing caused another: The string broke, so the kite flew away.

3. Where does the comma go in a compound sentence?

Why: The comma goes right before the joining word: 'The dog got there first, but Devon did not care.'
20.6

Complex Sentences

Main ideaA complex sentence joins a full sentence to a part that cannot stand alone, using words like because, when or although.

Read this: When the string broke. Something is missing. You want to ask, what happened then? Now read: When the string broke, the kite flew away. There it is. The first part cannot stand alone. It leans on the second part. Together they make a .

The part that cannot stand alone is a . It depends on the rest. It starts with a word like because, when, if, although, after, before or since. The part that can stand alone is the main part: the kite flew away. A complex sentence has one of each.

The order can go either way. When the string broke, the kite flew away. Or: The kite flew away when the string broke. Notice the comma. If the dependent part comes first, put a comma after it. If it comes second, you usually need no comma. Complex sentences let you show why, when and what if.

Check yourself

1. Which is a dependent clause?

Why: 'Because the wind was strong' starts with because and cannot stand alone. It needs a main part.

2. When does a complex sentence need a comma?

Why: 'When the string broke, the kite flew away' needs the comma. 'The kite flew away when the string broke' does not.

3. What can a complex sentence show that a simple one cannot?

Why: Words like because and when connect two ideas and show why, when or what if.
Section 3

Fixing Broken Sentences

20.7

Fragments Are Missing a Part

Main ideaA fragment is a piece of a sentence that is missing a subject or a predicate, and you fix it by adding the missing part.

Read this: Ran after the kite. Who ran? You cannot tell. Now this: The kite over the water. What did the kite do? Nothing happens. Each of these is a . A fragment looks like a sentence. It starts with a capital and ends with a period. But a part is missing.

There are two ways a fragment usually breaks. Either the subject is gone: Ran after the kite. Or the predicate is gone: The kite over the water. Sometimes a dependent clause is left alone: Because the dog was faster. That is a fragment too. It still needs a main part.

The fix is simple. Ask what is missing and add it. Ran after the kite becomes We ran after the kite. The kite over the water becomes The kite hung over the water. Because the dog was faster becomes Because the dog was faster, it won. Read your sentence aloud. If it leaves you waiting, it is a fragment.

Think it through
The Note on the Fridge

A note on the fridge says: Went to the store. Back at five. Milk in the bag. Nobody in the house is confused. They know Mom wrote it, and they know what she means. But every line is a fragment. Now imagine the same lines in a school report about a trip to a store.

Why are fragments fine on a fridge note but not in a report? What would you add to each line to make it a full sentence?

Words to know
fragment
a piece of a sentence that is missing a subject, a predicate or a main part
complete thought
an idea that is finished on its own, with nothing left hanging
Check yourself

1. Which one is a fragment?

Why: 'Ran after the kite' has no subject. Nobody is named as running. It is a fragment.

2. How do you fix a fragment?

Why: A fragment is missing a subject, a predicate or a main part. Add the missing part and it becomes a sentence.

3. 'Because the dog was faster.' What is wrong?

Why: The clause starts with because and cannot stand alone. It needs a main part such as 'it won.'
20.8

Run-ons Never Stop

Main ideaA run-on jams two or more sentences together without the right stop, and you fix it with a period, a comma and a conjunction, or a joining word like because.

Devon’s beach paragraph was a . It had nine sentences tied together with and. A run-on is two or more full sentences that run into each other without the right stop. Here is a short one: The string broke the kite flew away. Two full sentences, no stop at all.

Some run-ons have a comma where a period should be. It was hot, we swam. That is called a comma splice, and it is still a run-on. A comma alone is too weak to hold two full sentences apart. It needs help.

You have three good fixes. One: use a period. The string broke. The kite flew away. Two: use a comma plus a conjunction. The string broke, so the kite flew away. Three: use a word like because or when. When the string broke, the kite flew away. Pick the fix that fits what you mean. Any of them lets the reader breathe.

Section 4

Making Verbs and Words Behave

20.9

Subject and Verb Agree

Main ideaA singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb, and they must match.

The dog barks. The dogs bark. Say them out loud. When the subject is one dog, the verb is barks. When there are two or more dogs, the verb is bark. The subject and the verb . They match in number. One dog, barks. Many dogs, bark.

It feels backward, and that trips people up. Adding -s to a noun makes it plural: dogs. Adding -s to a verb makes it singular: barks. So a plural subject usually takes a verb with no -s. The kids run. The kid runs. The waves crash. The wave crashes.

The tricky ones are is and are, was and were, has and have. She is here. They are here. He was late. We were late. It has a tail. They have tails. Watch out for words in between: The box of kites is heavy. The subject is box, not kites. Box is one thing, so the verb is is.

20.10

Past, Present, Future

Main ideaA verb's tense tells when something happens, and a piece of writing should stay in one tense unless the time really changes.

The kite flies. The kite flew. The kite will fly. Same kite, same action, three different times. The form of the verb tells you when. That is called . Present tense is now: flies. Past tense is before now: flew. Future tense is later: will fly.

Most verbs make the past by adding -ed: jump, jumped. walk, walked. But many common verbs change in other ways. These are , and you learn them by ear and by heart. Go, went. Run, ran. See, saw. Eat, ate. Break, broke. Nobody says goed or breaked.

Here is the rule that matters most in writing. Pick a tense and stay in it. Read this: We went to the beach and my cousin brings a kite. Went is past, brings is present. The reader stumbles. Fix it: We went to the beach and my cousin brought a kite. Change tense only when the time really changes.

20.11

Choosing the Right Word

Main ideaWords that sound the same are spelled differently for a reason, and the words you choose should fit where you are and who is listening.

Read this: There going to they’re house over their. Every word is said right, and every one is spelled wrong. Their, there and they’re sound the same. They are . Their shows owning: their house. There points to a place: over there. They’re is short for they are: they’re going. Fixed: They’re going to their house over there.

Other pairs trip people up too. Your and you’re. Its and it’s. To, too and two. The test is the same each time. Say the long form. If it’s can become it is, use the apostrophe. If not, use its. The dog wagged its tail. It’s a good dog.

Word choice also depends on where you are. With friends you might say, Yo, that kite was sick. In a class talk or a letter to the principal, you use : That kite flew very well. Formal English uses full sentences, no slang, and no text-speak. Neither way is bad. A good writer knows which one fits the room.

Conversations and Presentations

Speaking and Listening
Big questionHow do you talk so that people understand you, and listen so that you understand them?
The story

Was the Ending Fair?

A book club of five fourth graders takes on an old tale, and the argument gets loud before it gets good.

Ms. Nakamura's book club sits in a circle on the rug. This week they read a very old story by Hans Christian Andersen, first printed in 1837, about a little mermaid. In the tale, the mermaid trades her voice to a sea witch for a pair of human legs. She hopes the prince will love her. He marries someone else. She is given a chance to save herself by harming him, and she refuses. At dawn she turns into foam on the sea.

Jamal starts. "That ending was not fair. She gave up everything and got nothing." Sofia jumps in before he finishes. "No, it was fair, she chose it!" Then three people talk at once. Nobody can hear anybody. Ms. Nakamura holds up one hand and waits. The circle goes quiet.

"Let's try that again," she says. "One person at a time. And before you answer, say back what the last person said." Jamal goes first, slowly. Sofia says, "Jamal thinks the ending was unfair because she gave up so much. I see that. But I think choosing not to hurt the prince was her own choice, and that makes it hers." Jamal nods. "Okay. But could you read me the part where she chooses? I want to hear it again."

Sofia finds the page and reads it aloud. Marcus, who has not spoken yet, says, "What if the story is not about whether she wins? What if it is about what kind of person she is?" The circle gets quiet in a different way. Everyone is thinking.

By the end, nobody has changed their vote. Jamal still says unfair. Sofia still says fair. But each one can explain the other side now. Ms. Nakamura writes on the board: Listen. Say it back. Ask. Build. This chapter is about how to talk and listen so that a conversation gets better instead of just louder.

Talk about itThe book club did not agree at the end. So how do you know the second try was better than the first?
Section 1

Rules for a Good Discussion

21.1

Come Prepared

Main ideaA good discussion starts before it starts: read the text, mark what you noticed, and bring one thing to say.

Sofia came to book club with a sticky note on page 30. It marked the moment the mermaid throws the knife into the sea. When Jamal asked to hear that part, she found it in five seconds. That is what it looks like to . She did the work before the circle formed.

Preparing for a discussion means three things. Read the whole text, not just the first pages. Mark places that surprised you, confused you or made you feel something. Then write down one thing you want to say and one question you want to ask. That is enough. You do not need a speech.

Being prepared changes how you feel in the circle. You are not scrambling. You are not hoping nobody calls on you. You have something to give. And when someone says, Where does it say that?, you can put your finger on the page.

Think it through
Two Sticky Notes

Before book club, Marcus put two sticky notes in his book. One says: Page 12, she cannot talk anymore. Why did she agree to that? The other says: Page 30, I did not expect her to throw the knife. When the circle starts, he has a question and a surprise ready. Jamal has no notes and is flipping pages looking for the part he means.

Who is more likely to help the discussion move forward, and why? What would one sticky note have done for Jamal?

21.2

Listen and Build On

Main ideaListening means taking in what someone said and connecting your idea to theirs, not just waiting for your turn.

In the first try, Sofia talked before Jamal finished. In the second try, she said, Jamal thinks the ending was unfair because she gave up so much. Then she added her own idea. That is called building on what someone said. Her idea sat on top of his instead of knocking it over.

Real listening is not silent waiting. It is taking in the other person’s words so well that you could say them back. Try these starters: I agree with Jamal because. I want to add to what Sofia said. That connects to what Marcus asked. Each one shows you heard, and each one moves the talk forward.

One more rule: eyes on the speaker, not on your own page. When you look at the person talking, you catch more than words. You catch when they are unsure. You catch when they are excited. A discussion works when people are listening to each other, not performing at each other.

21.3

Ask and Disagree Politely

Main ideaQuestions and polite disagreement make a discussion stronger, as long as you talk about the idea and not the person.

Jamal did not give up his view. He asked Sofia to read the page again. That is a good question: it asks for , the proof in the text. Other good questions are: What do you mean by that? Where does it say so? Why do you think that? A question is not an attack. It is an invitation.

Disagreeing is allowed. Being rude is not. Compare these. You are wrong and that is dumb. Or: I see it differently, because the story says she chose to throw the knife. The second one disagrees with the idea and gives a reason. It does not call anyone a name. That is .

Here is a pattern you can use every time. Say what you heard. Say you see it differently. Give your reason from the text. Sofia did all three in one breath, and Jamal nodded. Nobody lost. A discussion where everyone agrees in one minute is usually a discussion where nobody thought very hard.

From the source
The Wind and the Sun
In an old fable, the North Wind and the Sun argue about who is stronger. They pick a traveler in a coat. The Wind blows as hard as it can, and the man only pulls his coat tighter. Then the Sun shines gently. The man grows warm and takes the coat off by himself. The Sun wins without force.
Aesop's fables, an ancient Greek tale, retold in our words (paraphrased)

How is the Wind like a rude argument? How is the Sun like a polite one? Which one changed the traveler's mind?

Words to know

proof, such as words from the text, that supports an idea

saying you see it differently and giving a reason, without attacking the person
Check yourself

1. Which is a polite way to disagree?

Why: It disagrees with the idea, gives a reason from the text, and does not attack the person.

2. What is evidence in a book discussion?

Why: Evidence is proof. In a book discussion, it is what the text actually says.

3. What does the fable of the Wind and the Sun suggest about arguments?

Why: The Wind blew hard and lost. The Sun was gentle and the man took his coat off himself.
Section 2

Listening Closely

21.4

Summarize What You Heard

Main ideaA summary of a speaker gives the main point and the biggest reasons in your own words, in a few sentences.

Jamal talked for a full minute about the ending. Then Sofia said, in one sentence, what he meant: Jamal thinks the ending was unfair because she gave up so much. That sentence is a . It is short. It is in Sofia’s words. And it keeps the main point and the biggest reason.

To summarize a speaker, listen for two things. First, the main point: what does this person want me to believe or know? Second, the reasons: what did they say to back it up? Leave out the small stuff, the jokes and the side trails. Keep the point and the reasons.

A summary should be much shorter than the talk. If someone speaks for a minute, your summary might be two sentences. Say it back to the speaker if you can: So you are saying that. If they nod, you got it. If they say no, not quite, you just learned something before you argued about the wrong thing.

So how is that fair? She did everything and got nothing.

21.5

Check What You Heard

Main ideaWhen you are not sure what a speaker meant, ask a clear question instead of guessing.

Marcus asked, What if the story is about what kind of person she is? Priya was not sure what he meant. She could have nodded and stayed lost. Instead she said, Do you mean the ending shows she is kind? Marcus said yes. Now Priya can answer him. That is : asking so that something fuzzy becomes clear.

Good clarifying questions are short and honest. Can you say that again? What do you mean by fair? Are you saying the ending was good, or that it made sense? Can you give an example? None of these are dumb. Every one of them helps the whole circle, because if you were lost, someone else was too.

Clarifying also works when the speaker is a teacher, a video or a guest. Write down the parts you did not catch. Then ask about them. A listener who asks one good question learns more than a listener who nods at everything. Nodding is not the same as understanding.

Think it through
The Nod

A guest comes to class to talk about her job as a nurse. She says, Most of my day is triage. Half the class nods. Nobody knows what triage means. Elena raises her hand. What does triage mean? The nurse smiles and explains: deciding who needs help first. Now the whole class understands the rest of her talk.

Why did so many students nod at a word they did not know? What did Elena's question do for everyone else in the room?

Words to know

example
one specific case that shows what a bigger idea means
Check yourself

1. What is a clarifying question?

Why: Clarifying means making something clear. You ask because you are not sure yet.

Section 3

Giving a Short Talk

21.6

Put It in Order

Main ideaA short talk needs a clear order: tell what it is about, give two or three parts in a sequence, and end on purpose.

Elena has to give a two-minute talk about her grandmother’s tamales. Her first try jumps around: the steaming, then the corn husks, then back to the filling, then her cousin’s birthday. Her class is lost. Her second try goes in : first, the filling. Second, the wrapping. Third, the steaming. Now they can follow.

A short talk has three parts. The opening tells the audience what the talk is about in one or two sentences. The middle gives two or three main points, one at a time, in an order that makes sense. The ending says the big idea one more time, or tells why it matters. Do not just stop. End.

Words that show the order help a listener stay with you. First, next, then, finally. Another reason is. The last thing is. These are signposts. A reader can look back at a page. A listener cannot rewind you. Signposts tell them where they are and what is coming.

From the source
Elena's Second Try
My grandmother makes tamales every December, and it takes all day. First, she cooks the filling in a big pot. Next, she spreads dough on corn husks and adds the filling. Then she rolls and ties each one. Finally, she steams them for an hour. When you smell them, you know it is almost Christmas.
written for this lesson

Words to know

Check yourself

1. What are the three parts of a short talk?

Why: The opening says what the talk is about, the middle gives the points in order, the ending closes on purpose.

2. Why are signpost words extra important in a talk?

Why: Words like first and finally tell a listener where they are, since they cannot reread what you said.

3. What was wrong with Elena's first try?

21.7

Details That Help

Main ideaA talk comes alive with a few specific details, facts and examples that support each main point.

Marcus gives a talk about Lake Michigan. First try: Lake Michigan is really big and cool. That is a claim with nothing under it. Second try: Lake Michigan is so big that from a Chicago beach you cannot see the other side. It touches four states. Now the audience has something to picture. Those are .

A detail is a specific fact, example or description that supports a main point. Big is not a detail. Cannot see the other side is. Each main point in your talk needs at least one detail under it. Pick the one that a listener could repeat to a friend later. That is the one that will stick.

Do not pile on. Three strong details beat ten weak ones. If a fact does not support your point, leave it out, even if you like it. And if you say a number or a name, be sure of it. A talk with one wrong fact makes the audience doubt the rest.

21.8

Pace and Volume

Main ideaSpeak slowly enough to be understood, loudly enough to reach the back, and pause where a period would go.

Priya knows her talk cold. She gets up and races through it in forty seconds. The class hears a blur. She sat down thinking she did fine. The words were right. The , the speed of her talking, was wrong. Nervous speakers almost always go too fast, because fast feels like it will be over sooner.

Slow down more than feels normal. What feels slow to you sounds normal to a listener. Pause at the end of each sentence, right where a period would go. Breathe there. A pause is not empty time. It is the moment your listener catches up.

Then think about , how loud you are. Do not yell. Aim your voice at the person in the back row and speak so they can hear every word. Lift your chin. Your voice goes where your face points. If your face points at the floor, your words go there too.

Think it through

Now say it slowly, with a tiny pause after grandmother and after tamales, looking at the far wall. Notice which one you would want to hear if you were sitting in the back.

Which version was easiest to understand? Why do nervous speakers pick the fast one anyway?

Why: Pace is speed. Priya's words were right, but her pace was too fast.

2. Where is a good place to pause in a talk?

Why: Pause where a period would go. That is when the listener catches up.

3. Why does lifting your chin help your volume?

21.9

Pictures and Objects

Main ideaA picture or object can show what words alone cannot, but it should help the point, not replace it.

Elena brings a corn husk to her tamale talk. She holds it up when she says, she spreads dough on corn husks. Suddenly the class understands what a husk is. Nobody needed a definition. That is a : something the audience can see that helps them understand.

A visual aid can be a real object, a photo, a drawing, a map or a simple . Marcus showed a map of Lake Michigan touching four states. Jamal drew a dinosaur next to a school bus. It showed the size. Each one did a job that words could not do as well.

Two rules. First, the visual should help the point, not decorate it. If you cannot say what it shows, leave it out. Second, do not hide behind it. Hold it up, let people look, say what it shows, then put it down and keep talking to the audience. The aid is a helper. You are still the talk.

Think it through

Option two: a cool dinosaur sticker with flames on it. Option three: a photo of himself at the museum with his thumb up. His main point is that some dinosaurs were as long as a bus.

Which visual actually helps the main point? What do the other two do instead?

visual aid
an object, picture, map or chart the audience can see that helps them understand a point

Check yourself

1. What is a visual aid?

Why: A husk, a map or a drawing is something the audience sees that helps them understand.

2. Why did Elena's corn husk work so well?

Why: Seeing the husk explained the word in one second. That is what a good visual does.

3. What should you do after holding up a visual?

Section 4

Getting Better

21.10

Record and Listen Back

Main ideaRecording yourself and listening back shows you what your audience hears, so you can fix it before it counts.

Priya records her talk on a tablet and plays it back. She is shocked. She says um eleven times. She rushes the ending. She says like every few seconds. None of that felt real while she was talking. Now she can hear it. A shows you what your audience hears, not what you meant.

Listen back for four things. Pace: are you racing? Volume: can you hear every word? Fillers: um, like, you know, so. And the ending: did you end on purpose or just stop? Write down one thing to fix. Just one. Record again. Then pick the next thing.

It feels strange to hear your own voice. Everybody hates it the first time. That feeling passes, and what is left is the fastest way to get better at speaking that there is. Athletes watch film. Singers listen to takes. Speakers listen back.

21.11

Audience and Purpose

Main ideaWho is listening and why you are talking should change your words, your details and your tone.

Elena gives her tamale talk twice. Once to her class. Once to a room of kindergartners. To her class she says, the dough is made from corn that has been soaked and ground. To the little kids she says, the dough is soft and yellow, like play dough you can eat. Same talk. Different . Different words.

The audience is who is listening. Before you speak, ask: What do they already know? What do they care about? Kindergartners do not know what ground corn is. Your class does. A room of parents wants something different again. Change your words and details to fit the people in front of you.

The is why you are talking. To teach how tamales are made? To make people laugh? To convince the principal to have a tamale day? Each purpose changes what you say first and what you leave out. A speaker who knows the audience and the purpose has already won half the room.

Think it through

Marcus has to talk about Lake Michigan three times. First, to first graders during buddy reading. Second, to his own class for a science grade. Third, to the school board, asking for a field trip to the lakefront. Same lake. Three rooms. In one room he needs simple words. In one he needs facts. In one he needs a reason they should say yes.

Match each room to what it needs. What one fact about the lake would you use for each audience?

audience
the people who are listening to you
purpose
the reason you are speaking: to teach, to entertain or to convince
