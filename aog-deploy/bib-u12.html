Poetry and Wisdom

Psalms and Wisdom

Most of the Bible tells a story or gives a law. This unit turns to the books that sing and the books that argue. The Psalms are 150 poems that have been sung in synagogues and churches for more than two thousand years. The wisdom books, Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs, ask plain questions: How should I live? Why do good people suffer? Does anything last? What is love?

You will read these books closely, in translation. You will learn how Hebrew poetry works, with its paired lines and its pictures from daily life. You will sort the psalms by type, meet an Egyptian text that resembles Proverbs, follow the argument in Job to the storm at its end, and see why Jewish and Christian readers have read a love poem as something more.

By the end you will be able to name the pattern in a Hebrew poem, tell a lament from a hymn, explain how different traditions have used these texts, and write a psalm-shaped poem of your own. Everything here is the study of a text and its readers. Nobody is asked to believe or disbelieve; everyone is asked to read carefully.

The story

One Psalm, Two Rooms

The same words are sung in Hebrew on Friday night and in English on Sunday morning, and each room hears them a little differently.

It is Friday evening on the North Side of Chicago. The sun is going down, and a synagogue is filling up. A cantor stands at the front and begins to sing in Hebrew. The words are old. They come from the Book of Psalms, the songbook of the Hebrew Bible. Some people sway. Some read along in a prayer book that prints the Hebrew on one side and English on the other.

The service that welcomes the Sabbath uses several psalms in a row. One of them, Psalm 95, opens with a call to sing. Another, Psalm 29, describes a storm rolling over the sea and the mountains. The people in the room have heard these poems every week of their lives. For many of them, the sound of the Hebrew matters as much as the meaning.

Two days later, a few miles away, a church choir stands up on Sunday morning. The organ plays a few bars. Then the choir sings in English: "The Lord is my shepherd." That is Psalm 23. Some people in the pews know it by heart. A few whisper the lines along with the choir. Others simply listen.

Both rooms are singing from the same book. Jewish tradition calls it Tehillim, which means "praises." Christians call it the Psalms, from a Greek word for a song played on a stringed instrument. The Hebrew words were written more than two thousand years ago. The English words came much later, through many translations.

In this chapter you will read some of these poems closely. You will see how they are built, what kinds there are, and why so many singers, from ancient priests to Chicago gospel choirs, have kept coming back to them.

A Book of Songs

23.1

One Hundred Fifty Poems

Main ideaThe Book of Psalms is a collection of 150 poems, arranged in five smaller books.

Open a Bible to the middle and you will probably land in the Book of . It is the longest book in the Bible by chapters. There are 150 psalms. Each one is a separate poem. Some are only a few lines long. Psalm 117 has two verses. Psalm 119 has 176 verses and is the longest chapter in the Bible.

The psalms are not in random order. Ancient editors arranged them in five smaller . Each book ends with a short line of praise called a . Jewish tradition has long noticed that five books of psalms match the five books of the Torah. Many scholars think the editors meant that match on purpose.

Where did the psalms come from? The text itself does not say when each one was written. Many scholars think the poems were composed over several centuries. Some may go back to the time of the kings, before 586 BCE. Others clearly speak from the time after the exile to Babylon. The collection as we have it was probably finished a few centuries before the Common Era.

In Jewish tradition the book is called Tehillim, "praises." That name fits many psalms but not all. Some psalms are full of complaint and sorrow. As you read, remember that this is an anthology, a gathered collection. It holds many voices, moods and moments from ancient Israel’s life.

23.2

Names at the Top

Main ideaMany psalms carry a heading naming David or another figure, and readers disagree about what those headings mean.

Most psalms begin with a short heading, often printed in smaller type. Scholars call it a . It may name a person, an instrument, a tune or an occasion. Seventy-three psalms carry the Hebrew words "le-David," which most English Bibles print as "A Psalm of David." Others name Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon or Moses.

What does "of David" mean? The Hebrew word le can mean "by," "for" or "belonging to." Jewish and Christian tradition long read it as "written by David," the shepherd who became king. The books of Samuel describe David as a musician who played the harp. Many scholars today think some headings were added later by editors. They read "of David" as "in David’s collection" or "in David’s style."

A few headings tell a story. Psalm 51’s heading connects it to the moment when the prophet Nathan confronted David about Bathsheba. Psalm 3 is linked to David fleeing from his son Absalom. These headings invite the reader to hear the poem as a scene from David’s life. Whether they record history or a later reading, they shape how the psalm is heard.

Some headings are musical notes we no longer fully understand. The word appears seventy-one times in the Psalms. It may have marked a pause or a change in the music. Nobody today is certain. When you meet these words, treat them as clues about how the psalms were once performed.

23.3

Kinds of Psalms

Main ideaScholars sort the psalms into types, such as praise, lament, thanksgiving, royal, wisdom and pilgrimage, by their shape and purpose.

Read a few psalms in a row and you will notice patterns. Some shout with joy. Some cry out in pain. In the early 1900s a German scholar named Hermann Gunkel sorted the psalms by their shape and their setting in life. His categories, with some changes, are still used today. Learning them helps you see what a psalm is doing.

A psalm, or hymn, calls people to praise God and gives reasons. Psalm 100 and Psalm 150 are examples. A is a cry for help. It names a trouble, asks for rescue and often ends with trust. Laments are the largest group. Psalm 13 begins, "How long?" A psalm looks back on a rescue and says thank you, as in Psalm 30.

Other types are tied to a setting. A psalm is about the king, such as Psalm 2 or Psalm 72. A psalm teaches, like a page from Proverbs. Psalm 1 and Psalm 37 fit here. Psalms 120 to 134 each carry the heading "A Song of Ascents." Many scholars think pilgrims sang them on the way up to Jerusalem for festivals.

The types are tools, not walls. Many psalms mix them. Psalm 22 begins as a lament and ends as praise. When you read a psalm, ask what it is doing: calling for praise, crying for help, saying thanks, teaching or walking a road. Then look for the turn, the moment where the mood shifts.

23.4

Psalm 23: The Shepherd

Main ideaPsalm 23 builds its comfort out of two pictures, a shepherd and a host, and the parallel lines that carry them.

Psalm 23 is the best-known poem in the Bible. It is only six verses long. It opens with a picture: "The LORD is my shepherd." In the ancient Near East, kings were often called shepherds of their people. Here the speaker makes the picture personal. The one who guides is my shepherd, and the speaker is one sheep in the flock.

The poem moves through a landscape. There are green pastures and still waters. Then comes a dark valley. The King James Version calls it "the valley of the shadow of death." The Jewish Publication Society Tanakh of 1917 uses the same phrase. Some modern translations render it "the darkest valley." The Hebrew word can mean deep darkness. Both readings are defensible.

Halfway through, the picture changes. The shepherd becomes a host. A table is set, the guest’s head is anointed with oil, the cup overflows. In the last verse the speaker hopes to dwell "in the house of the LORD." Jewish readers have often heard this as the Temple. Many Christian readers hear it as heaven. The text itself does not explain.

Notice the . Hebrew poetry rarely rhymes. Instead, it says a thing and then says it again in a new way. "He maketh me to lie down in green pastures: he leadeth me beside the still waters." The second line matches the first and adds a new image. This is the basic music of the psalms.

23.5

Psalm 1: Two Roads

Main ideaPsalm 1 opens the whole book by contrasting two ways of life, and its first word is translated differently in Jewish and Christian Bibles.

Psalm 1 stands at the door of the book. Many scholars think editors placed it there as an introduction. It is a psalm. It does not pray or praise. It teaches. It sets two kinds of people side by side: the one who delights in God’s , or teaching, and the wicked who do not.

The first Hebrew word is ashrei. The King James Version of 1611 translates it "Blessed." The Jewish Publication Society Tanakh of 1917 translates it "Happy." Both are fair. The Hebrew word describes a person whose life is going well, someone you might envy. That is why some translators avoid "blessed," which can sound like a blessing from above.

The psalm works by . The good person is like a tree planted by streams of water. Its leaf does not wither. The wicked are like chaff, the dry husks the wind blows away at harvest time. A tree is rooted and lasting. Chaff is light and gone in a moment. The two images carry the whole argument.

Because Psalm 1 comes first, some readers say it tells you how to read the rest. The psalms, on this view, are meant to be studied like Torah, not only sung. Others point out that most psalms are prayers, not lessons. Either way, the editors chose to start the songbook with a teaching.

The Jewish Publication Society Tanakh (1917) opens the same verse with "Happy is the man." Both translate the same Hebrew word. "Blessed" can suggest a gift from God. 23.6

Psalm 137: Songs in Exile

Main ideaPsalm 137 is a lament from the Babylonian exile that ends with a cry for revenge, and readers have long wrestled with that ending.

In 586 BCE the Babylonian army destroyed Jerusalem and its Temple. Many people of Judah were taken to Babylon. Psalm 137 speaks from that time. It begins: "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." is a name for Jerusalem and its Temple hill. The singers have hung up their harps.

The captors ask for a song. The psalm answers with a question: "How shall we sing the LORD’s song in a strange land?" Then the speaker makes a vow. If I forget Jerusalem, let my right hand forget its skill. Let my tongue stick to the roof of my mouth. The poem turns grief into a promise to remember.

The last verses are hard to read. The speaker calls down a curse on Babylon and on Edom, a neighbor that helped in Jerusalem’s fall. The final verse blesses the one who smashes Babylon’s infants against the rock. This is a cry for revenge from a people who saw their own city burned. The psalm does not soften it.

Readers have handled this ending in many ways. Some synagogues and churches leave the last verses out when the psalm is sung. Some readers say the poem is honest: it shows rage as a real human feeling brought before God. Others read it as a warning about where hatred leads. The chapter’s job is to see what the text says and to notice how readers respond.

23.7

Psalm 150: Every Instrument

Main ideaPsalm 150 closes the book with a burst of praise that names instrument after instrument.

The Book of Psalms ends with five psalms that each begin and end with , Hebrew for "praise the LORD." Psalm 150 is the last. It is short, only six verses. It gives no story, no complaint and no argument. It is pure call to praise. Many scholars see it as the closing doxology for the whole book.

The psalm is built on one command, "praise," repeated more than ten times. First it says where: in the sanctuary and in the heavens. Then it says why: for mighty acts and greatness. Then it says how, with a list of instruments. The trumpet, the harp and lyre, the tambourine and dance, strings and pipe, loud cymbals, clashing cymbals.

The instruments were the orchestra of the Temple in Jerusalem. The books of Chronicles describe Levite musicians playing cymbals, harps and lyres during worship. Psalm 150 reads like a roll call of that orchestra. The tambourine and dance may point to celebration outside the Temple, in the streets and villages.

The last verse widens the circle. "Let every thing that hath breath praise the LORD." Not only priests, not only Israel, but everything that breathes. Then one final Hallelujah. Many readers have noticed the shape: the book that began with one person studying Torah in Psalm 1 ends with all creation making noise.

23.8

In the Synagogue

Main ideaJewish worship has used the psalms daily, weekly and at festivals for well over two thousand years.

The psalms began as songs, and Jewish communities never stopped singing them. The is the Jewish house of prayer and study. There, psalms fill the daily service. A morning service includes a group of psalms of praise called Pesukei d’Zimra, "verses of song." Psalm 145 is recited three times a day in traditional practice.

Each day of the week has its own psalm. The Mishnah is a Jewish law code written down c. 200 CE. It records that the Levites sang a set psalm each day in the Temple. Psalm 24 on Sunday, Psalm 92 on the Sabbath, and so on. Many synagogues still say the psalm of the day. It is a living link to the Temple that fell in 70 CE.

On Friday evening the service that welcomes the Sabbath, Kabbalat Shabbat, uses Psalms 95 to 99 and Psalm 29. Jewish mystics in the town of Safed put this order together in the 1500s. At festivals such as Passover, Jews sing the . That is Psalms 113 to 118, a set of praise psalms. Christian readers note that the Gospels describe Jesus and his disciples singing a hymn after the Passover meal.

Psalms also mark hard times. Traditional Jewish mourners recite Psalm 49 or Psalm 16 in a house of mourning. Psalm 23 is often said at funerals. In many communities people gather to recite the whole book of Psalms for someone who is sick. For these readers the psalms are less a text to study than a voice to borrow.

23.9

In the Church

Main ideaChristians took the psalms into their worship from the beginning, chanting, translating and setting them to new music.

The first Christians were Jews who already knew the psalms. The New Testament quotes the Psalms more than any other book of the Hebrew Bible. Christian tradition reads many psalms as pointing to Jesus. Psalm 22, which begins "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?", is quoted in the Gospels at the crucifixion. Jewish readers read the same psalm as a lament of an individual or of Israel.

Monks in the Middle Ages sang the whole book every week. They used , a plain, unaccompanied melody in Latin. The chant is named for Pope Gregory I, who died in 604 CE, though the music was written down centuries later. In many Catholic and Orthodox churches, chanted psalms are still the backbone of daily prayer.

The Protestant Reformation of the 1500s changed the sound. Reformers in Geneva wanted everyone, not just monks, to sing. They turned the psalms into rhymed verses in French, the Genevan Psalter of 1562. English Puritans did the same. The first book printed in British North America was the Bay Psalm Book, in 1640 in Massachusetts.

Psalms also shaped larger music. Composers set psalm texts in Latin, German and English. Handel’s Messiah of 1742 draws on the Psalms among other books. Later, African American spirituals borrowed psalm images of rivers, exile and deliverance. Psalm 137’s "rivers of Babylon" became a reggae hit in the 1970s.

23.10

Gospel Music in Chicago

Main ideaChicago's Black churches turned psalm-shaped praise into gospel music in the 1930s.

In the 1920s and 1930s, hundreds of thousands of African Americans moved north to Chicago in the Great Migration. They brought their churches with them. In those churches a new music was born. It mixed the blues, spirituals and hymns. People called it .

Thomas A. Dorsey is often called the father of gospel music. He had been a blues pianist. After a personal tragedy in 1932, he wrote "Take My Hand, Precious Lord." He led the choir at Pilgrim Baptist Church on Chicago’s South Side for decades. Mahalia Jackson, who sang in Chicago churches, carried gospel to the world.

Gospel songs are not psalms. But they share the psalms’ shapes. A gospel song often begins in trouble and ends in trust, like a lament. Many are built on repeated praise, like Psalm 150. Singers use : a leader sings a line, and the choir answers. Some scholars compare this to the parallel lines of Hebrew poetry.

Chicago’s gospel story shows something about the psalms. Their forms travel. A shepherd in Judah, a monk in Latin, a farmer in Geneva and a choir on the South Side have all used the same moves: praise, cry, thanks, and a turn from one to the other.

23.11

How the Lines Work

Main ideaParallelism comes in several patterns, and spotting them is the first step to writing a psalm-shaped poem.

In 1753 an English bishop named Robert Lowth gave a set of lectures on Hebrew poetry. He gave its name and sorted it into kinds. His labels are still taught. The first kind is synonymous: the second line says the first line again in new words. "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork" (Psalm 19:1, KJV).

The second kind is : the second line says the opposite. Psalm 1 ends this way. The LORD knows the way of the righteous, but the way of the wicked will perish. Proverbs is full of this pattern. The third kind Lowth called synthetic: the second line simply carries the first forward, adding a step or a result.

Later scholars added a warning. The second line rarely just repeats. It usually sharpens, narrows or raises the stakes. "How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me?" (Psalm 13:1, KJV). Forgetting is bad. Hiding a face is worse. Reading the second line as "more," not "again," unlocks the poem.

Psalms also use , pictures drawn from daily life. A shepherd, a tree, chaff, a fortress, a cup, a storm. And some use an , where each line or section begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Psalm 119 has 22 sections of 8 verses, one for each letter. The pattern disappears in translation, but a good footnote will tell you it is there.

On the eighth day Job speaks, and curses the day he was born. Now the friends answer, and the trouble begins. Eliphaz says, gently at first, that the innocent do not perish. Bildad says God does not twist justice, so Job's children must have sinned. Zophar says Job is getting less than he deserves. Each speech says the same thing: suffering must be earned.

Job refuses that answer. He knows he did nothing to deserve this. For nearly thirty chapters the four men argue in poetry. The friends defend the rule that the good prosper and the wicked fall. Job demands to face God in court. At the end, a voice speaks from a storm. It does not answer the way anyone expected. This chapter follows that argument and the other books that join it.

Wisdom in the Ancient World

24.1

What Wisdom Writing Is

Main ideaWisdom literature is a genre found across the ancient Near East that asks how to live well, based on experience rather than law or prophecy.

Most of the Hebrew Bible tells Israel’s story or gives its laws or records its prophets. A few books do something else. They ask a plain human question: how should a person live? Scholars call these books . In the Hebrew Bible they are Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes. Many readers add the Song of Songs and some psalms.

Wisdom books have a distinct voice. They rarely mention Moses, the covenant at Sinai or the exodus from Egypt. They do not say "thus says the LORD" the way prophets do. Instead they observe. They watch ants, lazy people, kings, farmers and fools, and they draw lessons. Their authority comes from experience, not from a vision.

This kind of writing was not unique to Israel. Egypt produced instruction books for young officials for more than a thousand years. Mesopotamia had proverb collections and a poem about a righteous sufferer. In the Bible, 1 Kings 4 says Solomon’s wisdom was greater than that of "all the children of the east country" and of Egypt. The comparison itself shows that the writers knew of other nations’ wisdom.

In Jewish tradition Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes sit in the third part of the Tanakh, the or Writings. In Christian Bibles they sit together after the history books, along with Psalms and the Song of Songs. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles add two more wisdom books, Sirach and the Wisdom of Solomon. Protestant Bibles place those in the Apocrypha or leave them out.

Egypt's Amenemope

Main ideaAn Egyptian instruction book, the Instruction of Amenemope, closely resembles a section of Proverbs, and scholars debate how the two are related.

In 1888 the British Museum acquired an Egyptian papyrus. It held a text called the . An official named Amenemope writes advice to his son in thirty chapters. Scholars date the work to roughly the late second millennium BCE, perhaps c. 1100 BCE, though the copy is later. In 1923 the text was published, and readers of the Bible noticed something.

Proverbs 22:17 to 24:22 is a section that begins with a call to hear "the words of the wise." Its sayings often match Amenemope’s, in order and in image. Both warn against moving a boundary stone. Both say riches can fly away like a bird. Proverbs 22:20 even seems to mention "thirty" sayings, though the Hebrew word is hard to read. Most scholars think the Hebrew writer knew the Egyptian text, or a version of it.

Not everyone agrees on the direction. A few early scholars argued the Egyptian text borrowed from Hebrew. Most today think the borrowing went from Egypt into Proverbs, because Amenemope is older and the Egyptian genre is much older still. Some suggest both drew on a shared pool of sayings. The debate is about literary history, not about which text is true.

What does this mean for reading Proverbs? For many scholars it shows that Israel’s wisdom teachers were part of an international conversation. For many religious readers it shows that wisdom can be found among all peoples, a point Proverbs itself makes. Both readings can note the differences too. Proverbs speaks of the LORD, Israel’s God, where Amenemope speaks of the Egyptian gods.

Who Wrote Proverbs?

Main ideaProverbs names several sources in its headings, and tradition and scholarship read those names in different ways.

The first verse of the book says, "The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel." Jewish and Christian tradition long held that Solomon wrote the book. The book’s own headings name more than one source. Proverbs 10:1 begins a second Solomon collection. Proverbs 22:17 introduces "the words of the wise." Most scholars today read these headings as signs of a long editing process. Sayings from many centuries and sources, some from Egypt, were gathered under Solomon’s famous name. This is common in the ancient world, where a great figure’s name often stood over a whole genre. Traditional readers may accept Solomon as the author while granting later editing. The headings themselves leave room for both.

24.7

The Wager in Heaven

Main ideaJob's prose opening sets up a test that Job never learns about, and it raises the question the whole book argues.

The Book of is built like a sandwich. Chapters 1 and 2 and the end of chapter 42 are prose, a simple story. Everything in between is poetry. The story opens in Uz, a land the text does not locate. Job is not called an Israelite. Many scholars think the story is meant to feel like a folktale from anywhere.

The scene shifts to heaven. God praises Job’s goodness before a court of heavenly beings. One of them, called in Hebrew , "the adversary" or "the accuser," asks a question. Does Job fear God for nothing? Take away his rewards and he will curse you. God allows the test. Job loses his wealth, his children and his health. He does not curse God.

Readers should be careful with the name. In Job the adversary is a member of God’s court, a kind of prosecutor. He is not yet the devil of later Jewish and Christian tradition. Most scholars say that fuller picture developed after Job was written. Translations that print "Satan" as a name can hide this.

The wager creates a gap in knowledge. The reader knows why Job suffers. Job does not, and never finds out. His friends do not know either, though they think they do. That gap drives the book. Every speech that follows is an attempt to explain suffering by people who lack the one fact the reader has.

The friends are not villains. Their belief comes straight from Proverbs and Deuteronomy, where obedience brings blessing. Eliphaz begins kindly. He reminds Job how he once comforted others. He says he spoke of things he did not understand, and he repents "in dust and ashes" (Job 42:6, KJV). Readers have drawn very different lessons. Some say the speech means humans cannot understand God’s plan and should trust. Some say it shows a world that is vast and wild and not built around human justice. Some say Job is being silenced by power. Then comes the prose ending: God says the friends spoke wrongly and Job spoke rightly, and Job’s fortunes are restored. Readers argue about that ending too.

Ecclesiastes and the Song

24.10

Vanity of Vanities

Main ideaEcclesiastes questions whether anything lasts, and its key word hevel has been translated many ways.

The book opens with a voice called Qohelet in Hebrew. The word probably means one who gathers an assembly. The Greek translators rendered it , and the King James Version calls him "the Preacher." The speaker says he was king in Jerusalem, and tradition identified him with Solomon. Most scholars date the book much later, c. 300 BCE, partly because of its late Hebrew and its Persian loanwords.

The book’s key word is . It appears about thirty-eight times. Literally it means breath, vapor or mist. The King James Version translates it "vanity." Modern translators have tried "futility," "meaningless," "absurd" and "mere breath." Each choice changes the book’s mood. Vapor is real but cannot be held. That is Qohelet’s picture of human effort.

Qohelet watches the sun rise and set, rivers run to the sea, and generations come and go. Nothing changes. "There is no new thing under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 1:9, KJV). He tries wisdom, pleasure, work and wealth, and finds that death takes the wise and the foolish alike. This is a direct challenge to the confidence of Proverbs.

Yet Ecclesiastes is not simply gloomy. Again and again the speaker says: eat your bread with joy, enjoy your work, enjoy life with the one you love. The famous poem in chapter 3 says there is a time for everything. A time to be born and to die, to weep and to laugh. In Jewish tradition the book is read at the festival of Sukkot, a season of joy. The last verses tell the reader to fear God and keep the commandments. Many scholars think an editor added them.

24.11

The Song of Songs

Main ideaThe Song of Songs is love poetry between two voices, and Jewish and Christian traditions have long read it as an allegory as well.

The is eight chapters of love poetry. Two main voices speak, a woman and a man, with a chorus called the daughters of Jerusalem. They praise each other’s bodies with images from the land: gazelles, vineyards, doves, spices, a garden. The woman speaks more than the man. God is never clearly named in the book.

The title means "the best of songs." The first verse links it to Solomon, and tradition called him its author. Many scholars think it is a collection of love poems from different times, put together after the exile. Some compare it to Egyptian love songs. Its frank celebration of desire made some ancient readers ask why it belonged in scripture.

The answer, for both Jews and Christians, was , reading a story as a picture of something else. Rabbi Akiva, c. 100 CE, is quoted in the Mishnah defending the book strongly, calling it the holy of holies among the Writings. Jewish tradition read the Song as the love between God and Israel. It is read in synagogues at Passover, the festival of Israel’s freedom.

Christian readers from Origen in the third century to Bernard of Clairvaux in the twelfth read it as the love between Christ and the church, or Christ and the soul. Since the 1800s, many scholars and religious readers have returned to reading it as human love poetry. Some hold both readings at once. The text supports the plain reading; the traditions supply the second.

24.12

Three Books That Disagree

Main ideaProverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes give different answers to the same question, and the Bible keeps all three.

Put the three main wisdom books side by side and they argue. Proverbs says the world runs on order: work hard, act justly, fear the LORD, and things will go well. Job says: I did all that, and look at me. Ecclesiastes says: the wise and the foolish both die, so enjoy what you have while you have it.

Some readers try to smooth this out. They say Proverbs gives general rules, Job the exceptions and Ecclesiastes the limits. Others think the disagreement is the point. Jewish tradition preserved debates between rabbis without settling them, and some scholars see the same habit in these books. A that keeps disagreeing voices invites its readers to think.

The three books also share a lot. All three prize honesty over easy comfort. All three watch the world closely. All three, in their final forms, come back to the fear of God: Proverbs at its start, Job at its center in chapter 28, Ecclesiastes in its last verses. Whether that shared ending came from the authors or from editors is one of the debates.

For a student in a public school, the wisdom books offer a model of how a tradition thinks. They show that ancient readers asked the hard questions too. Why do good people suffer? Does anything last? What is love? The Bible’s answer was not one book but four, kept together and left in tension.
