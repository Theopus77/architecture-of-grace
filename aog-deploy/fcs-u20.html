Capstone: Plan, Cost, Produce

Capstone

You have spent years in this course learning one skill at a time: a twenty-second hand wash, a level cup of flour, a back stitch, a unit price, a food thermometer, a safe crib, a budget built from take-home pay. The capstone asks you to use all of them at once on a single project that you choose, plan, pay for, make, judge and present. It may be a meal for a group or a piece you sew, and it will be yours from the first line of the plan to the last sentence of the presentation.

The first chapter is the plan. You will write a specification that another person could follow, a cost sheet with every ingredient or material at its unit price, a timeline built backwards from the deadline with checkpoints, an equipment list, a safety page with the standard temperatures and rules, and a rubric that says ahead of time what a good result looks like. Every number on those pages is a number you can check.

The second chapter is the doing and the judging. You will run the timeline on production day, record temperatures, times, receipts and photos as you go, serve or finish the piece, and then score it honestly against your own spec and rubric. You will compare plan to actual, name what you would change and why, present the whole project to an audience, and see how the same cycle of plan, cost, produce and evaluate is the daily work of chefs, dietitians, patternmakers, child-care directors and family educators.

The big question

How do you plan, cost, produce and honestly evaluate a real project so that the result is safe, on budget, on time and worth showing?

The story

Twelve Chairs, Three Courses, One Notebook

A senior offers to cook a real dinner for twelve and discovers that the cooking is the easy part.

Marisol had cooked for her family for years, so when her grandmother's seventieth birthday came around, she said the words before she thought them through: "I'll make the dinner. Three courses. Twelve people." Her aunt raised an eyebrow and asked one question. "What are you serving?" Marisol realized she had no idea.

That night she opened a notebook and wrote the date at the top. Then she stared at the blank page. A soup, maybe. Chicken, because her grandmother liked it. Something sweet. But how much chicken feeds twelve? How many pots did she own? When did the soup need to start if dinner was at six? She did not know a single number yet.

Her FCS teacher had a phrase for this moment. "A wish is not a plan. A plan has numbers, tools and a clock." Marisol started with the guests. Twelve people, one with a shellfish allergy, two who did not eat pork, one small cousin who ate almost nothing. That list changed the menu before she had bought a thing.

By the end of the week the notebook had a menu with quantities, a cost sheet with every item and its price, a list of every pot and pan, and a timeline that started two days before the party. It also had a page called Safety with the food thermometer temperatures written in ink. The dinner was still ten days away, and Marisol already felt like she had done half the work.

She had. In this chapter you will do the same for a project of your own, a meal for a group or a piece of clothing you sew. You will write a specification, cost every part, plan every hour and decide ahead of time what a good result will look like.

Section 1

Choosing and Defining the Project

39.1

Pick a Project You Can Finish

Main ideaA good capstone project is one you can plan, afford, complete on time and evaluate against a clear standard.

Picture two students on the first day of the capstone. One wants to sew a lined wool coat with a zipper and welt pockets. The other wants to make a hemmed apron with two patch pockets. Both are real sewing projects, but only one can be finished in six class periods by a student who has made one drawstring bag. The first step of any project is choosing something that fits the time, the budget, the tools and your own skill.

Use four questions to test an idea. Can I finish it in the time I have? Can I pay for it with the budget I have? Do I have, or can I borrow, every tool it needs? Have I already done the hardest step at least once? If the answer to any question is no, shrink the idea until every answer is yes. A three-course dinner for twelve becomes a two-course dinner for eight. A coat becomes a vest.

Shrinking is not failing. A finished apron with straight seams and even pockets shows more skill than a half-done coat. Employers, judges and teachers all grade the result you deliver, not the one you imagined. Write your idea in one sentence, such as "I will cook a soup, a main dish and a dessert for twelve guests on May 9" or "I will sew a lined tote bag from a commercial pattern by May 9." Every later page of the plan grows from that sentence.

39.2

Writing a Specification

Main ideaA specification describes the finished product in measurable terms so anyone can tell whether the result meets it.

Imagine handing your plan to a friend who has never seen your project and asking them to make it while you are away. Every question they would have to ask you is a hole in your . A specification, or spec, is a written description of the finished product: what it is, who it is for, how big it is, what it must do and what it must not contain. Engineers, chefs, tailors and builders all write one before they start.

For a meal, the spec lists the number of guests, the courses, the dishes in each course, the portion size per person, the serving time, and any dietary limits. Example: "Serves 12. Course 1: tomato soup, 1 cup each. Course 2: roast chicken, 6 oz each, with rice and green beans. Course 3: lemon bars, 2 per person. Served at 6:00 p.m. No shellfish. No pork. One child portion at half size." Every line can be checked with a measuring cup, a scale or a clock.

For a sewn piece, the spec names the pattern number and view, the size, the fabric type and yardage, the color, the notions such as thread, zipper and interfacing, the finished measurements, and the seam finish. Example: "Tote bag, pattern view B, 14 by 16 inches finished, medium-weight cotton canvas, lined, 5/8-inch seams pressed open, straps 24 inches long, all seams zigzag finished."

Write in terms. "Enough chicken" is not measurable; "6 oz per person, 4.5 lb total" is. "A nice color" is not measurable; "navy blue canvas" is. When you evaluate the project at the end, you will hold the result against this spec line by line, so every line must be something you can see, weigh, measure or time.

39.3

Know Your Audience

Main ideaThe people you serve or sew for shape the spec: their number, ages, allergies, food rules, sizes and tastes come before your own preferences.

Marisol’s guest list changed her menu before she bought a single ingredient. A shellfish allergy meant no shrimp. Two guests who did not eat pork meant chicken, not ham. A small cousin meant a mild dish and a half portion. Your project exists for other people, so the second page of your plan is a plain list of who they are and what they need.

For a meal, gather: the exact head count, any allergies (the nine major allergens are milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame), religious or personal food rules, ages, and anything the host tells you about likes and dislikes. Ask directly and write the answers down. An allergy is a safety matter, not a preference, so an allergen must be kept out of the dish and off the shared cutting board, not just left off the plate.

For a sewn garment, gather the wearer’s body measurements taken with a tape over light clothing: bust or chest, waist, hips, back length and any other line the pattern envelope asks for. Compare them to the pattern’s size chart and choose the size by the measurements, not by the store size the person usually wears. Ask about fabric feel, color and how the piece will be washed. A shirt for someone who will machine wash it hot should not be cut from a dry-clean-only fabric.

Respect what people tell you. A family that eats no meat on certain days, a guest who fasts, a wearer who wants long sleeves for religious reasons: these are not obstacles to work around but the requirements the spec is built on. The best cooks and tailors are the ones who listen first.

Section 2

Costing the Project

39.4

Every Ingredient, Every Material

Main ideaA cost sheet lists every single item the project uses, with its unit price and the amount needed, so the total is real and not a guess.

Look at Marisol’s first cost estimate: "Chicken, rice, beans, soup stuff, dessert stuff: about $60." Now look at her second: twenty-three lines, each with an item, a package size, a package price, the amount she actually needs and the cost of that amount. The first number was a wish. The second was a . The difference was two hours in a grocery store aisle with her phone and a notebook.

Build the sheet from the recipes, not from memory. Open each recipe, scaled to your head count, and copy every ingredient down, including the ones that seem too small to matter: salt, oil, the parchment paper, the foil. For a sewing project, copy every line from the pattern envelope’s back: fabric, lining, interfacing, thread, zipper, buttons, and add the things the envelope does not list, such as a new machine needle or a marking pen.

Give the sheet five columns: item, package size and price, unit price, amount needed, and cost of the amount needed. Unit price is the package price divided by the package size. Example: a 5-lb bag of rice at $4.50 (example) is $0.90 per lb. If the recipe needs 3 lb, the cost line is 3 × $0.90 = $2.70. Do this for every line, then add the column. That sum is your ingredient or material cost.

Forgetting a line is the most common mistake, and the forgotten line is usually a cheap one that turns into a trip to the store on the day of the event. The second most common mistake is copying the package price into the cost column when you only use part of the package. You will still have to buy the whole package, so keep a sixth column for what you will actually spend at the register.

Check yourself

1. A 5-lb bag of rice costs $4.50 (example). The recipe needs 3 lb. What goes in the cost column?

Why: Unit price is $4.50 ÷ 5 = $0.90 per lb, and 3 lb × $0.90 = $2.70.

2. Where should the list of ingredients on a cost sheet come from?

Why: Copying from the scaled recipe catches every ingredient, including small ones like salt, oil and parchment that memory skips.

3. Why keep a separate column for the register total when a recipe uses only part of a package?

Why: The cost column shows what the project consumes; the register column shows the cash you must have, since stores sell whole packages.
39.5

Scaling Recipes to the Head Count

Main ideaTo scale a recipe, divide the servings you need by the servings it makes to get a factor, then multiply every ingredient by that factor and convert to sensible units.

Marisol’s lemon bar recipe makes 12 bars in an 8-by-8-inch pan. Her spec says 2 bars each for 12 people, which is 24 bars. The is 24 ÷ 12 = 2. Every ingredient is multiplied by 2: 1 cup of flour becomes 2 cups, ½ cup of butter becomes 1 cup, 3 eggs become 6, 2 Tbsp of lemon juice become 4 Tbsp, which is ¼ cup. Two 8-by-8 pans, or one 9-by-13, hold the doubled batch.

Factors are not always whole numbers. A soup recipe that serves 8 must serve 12, so the factor is 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5. If the soup calls for 4 cups of broth, the scaled amount is 6 cups, which is 1.5 quarts. If it calls for 1 Tbsp of oil, you need 1.5 Tbsp, which is 1 Tbsp plus 1.5 tsp, since 3 tsp make 1 Tbsp. Write the scaled amount in the unit you can actually measure.

Some things do not scale in a straight line. Cooking time rises for a larger pot but not by the factor; a doubled roast does not take twice as long, so you cook by internal temperature and a thermometer, never by the clock alone. Seasonings like salt and hot pepper are usually scaled by the factor and then tasted and adjusted. Pan size must be checked separately, because a doubled cake batter in the original pan overflows.

Compute every scaled line twice, once when you write it and once the next day, and mark the sheet with the check. A single arithmetic slip, such as writing 2 tsp when you meant 2 Tbsp, is a small mistake on paper and a ruined dish in the pan, and it is the kind of error a second look always catches.

Check yourself

1. A recipe serves 8 and you need 12 servings. What is the scaling factor?

Why: Servings needed ÷ servings the recipe makes = 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5.

2. A recipe calls for 2 Tbsp lemon juice and the factor is 2. Which is the scaled amount written in a measurable unit?

Why: 2 Tbsp × 2 = 4 Tbsp, and 4 Tbsp = ¼ cup.

3. You double a chicken recipe. How should you decide when the chicken is done?

Why: Cooking time does not scale by the factor. Poultry is done at a safe minimum internal temperature of 165 °F, checked with a thermometer.
39.6

Hidden Costs and the Budget Line

Main ideaBeyond ingredients or fabric, a real cost sheet includes supplies, equipment you must buy or rent, a waste allowance and a cushion, compared against the budget you were given.

A student sewing a tote bag priced the canvas, lining, thread and webbing and came to $18.40 (example). Then the machine needle broke on the first seam, the interfacing was not on the sheet, and the bag needed a magnetic snap the pattern had listed in tiny print. The real total was $27.10. Nothing on the second list was expensive, but together they were almost half again the first number. These are , and the cost sheet needs a section for them.

Add a block below the ingredient or material lines. For a meal: foil, parchment, storage containers for leftovers, dish soap, disposable plates or napkins if used, ice, and any equipment you must buy, rent or borrow such as a large stockpot or a second roasting pan. For a sewn piece: needles, marking tools, interfacing, fusible tape, pattern cost, and a pressing cloth. Price each one the same way, unit price times amount.

Then add two lines that are not things at all. The first is a waste allowance, because peeling, trimming and cutting on grain all lose material. Cooks often buy about 10 percent more produce than the recipe weight; sewers buy the envelope’s yardage, which already includes layout waste, but add fabric for a matched print or a cutting mistake. The second is a cushion, a small amount, often 5 to 10 percent of the total, held back for a price that rose or an item you forgot.

Finally, write the line: the money you actually have. Compare the total to it. If the total is higher, change the plan now, not at the register. Swap a store brand for a name brand, choose chicken thighs over breasts, replace fresh berries with frozen, drop the lining or pick a less costly fabric. The spec may change slightly; the budget does not.

Section 3

Time, Tools and Safety

39.7

Working Backwards From the Deadline

Main ideaBuild the timeline from the finish time backwards, giving each task its real duration, so that the longest and earliest jobs start first.

Dinner is at 6:00 p.m. A roast chicken takes about 1 hour 30 minutes in the oven plus 15 minutes of rest, so it goes in at 4:15. The oven must preheat, so it is turned on at 4:00. The chicken must be seasoned before that, at 3:45. Read those times again: they were found by starting at the end and stepping backwards. That is how every timeline for a project is built.

List every task the project needs, including the ones that are not cooking or sewing: shopping, washing produce, thawing, preheating, pressing fabric, cutting, resting, cooling, plating, cleanup. Next to each, write how long it really takes, timed from experience or from the recipe or pattern, not from hope. Then place the last task at the deadline and work backwards, task by task, until you reach the first thing you must do.

Thawing is the task most often placed wrong. A 4-to-5-lb chicken thaws in the refrigerator in about a day, so it moves from freezer to fridge two days before the dinner to be safe. It never thaws on the counter, because the outside sits in the danger zone of 40 to 140 °F while the inside is still frozen. Dessert that must cool and set, such as lemon bars, is baked the day before. Fabric is washed, dried and pressed the day before cutting.

Mark : a few moments where you stop and confirm that you are on schedule. "Day before, 8 p.m.: dessert baked and chilled, chicken thawed, all produce washed. Day of, 3:30: soup simmering, oven on at 4:00." A missed checkpoint tells you early enough to fix it, by simplifying a dish or asking for help, instead of at 5:55 with twelve people at the table.

Think it through
Put the Evening in Order

Dinner at 6:00. Tasks with real durations: rest the chicken 15 min; roast 90 min; preheat oven 15 min; season chicken 10 min; cook rice 25 min; steam green beans 8 min; reheat soup 15 min; set the table 10 min. Only one person is cooking.

Write the start time for each task working backwards from 6:00, and name which tasks can overlap while the chicken roasts.

Words to know
timeline
a schedule that lists every task with its start time, built backwards from the deadline

danger zone

Check yourself

1. A chicken needs 90 minutes to roast and 15 minutes to rest, and dinner is at 6:00. When does it go in the oven?

Why: Work backwards: 6:00 minus 15 minutes rest is 5:45, minus 90 minutes roasting is 4:15.

2. Why must a frozen chicken be moved to the refrigerator two days before the dinner?

Why: Refrigerator thawing keeps the whole bird at or below 40 °F. A counter lets the outside sit between 40 and 140 °F for hours.

3. What is the purpose of a checkpoint in the timeline?

Why: A checkpoint compares the plan to the clock while there is still time to simplify or get help.
39.8

The Materials and Equipment List

Main ideaList every tool and piece of equipment for every step, check that you have it or can borrow it, and stage it before you start.

Marisol’s soup recipe said "blend until smooth." She did not own a blender. She found this out at 3:40 p.m. on the day of the party, with a pot of hot chunky soup in front of her. A tool list would have caught it a week earlier. For each step in the recipe or pattern, write the tool that step needs, then walk through your kitchen or sewing room and check each one off.

Group the list by station. For a meal: prep (cutting boards, one for raw meat and one for produce, a chef’s knife, a paring knife, peeler, measuring cups and spoons, bowls), cooking (pots by size, roasting pan, sheet pans, tongs, spatula, whisk, food thermometer), serving (platters, ladle, serving spoons, twelve of every plate, bowl and glass), and storage (containers with lids, foil, labels). For sewing: cutting (shears, rotary cutter and mat, pins, weights), marking (chalk, tracing paper), sewing (machine, correct needle, bobbins, seam ripper), pressing (iron, board, pressing cloth), and finishing (hand needles, tape measure).

Count the ones that matter. Twelve guests need twelve bowls and twelve spoons, plus serving pieces. A three-course dinner may need more pots on the stove than you have burners, which changes the timeline, not just the list. If something is missing, decide now: borrow it, buy it and add it to the cost sheet, or change the method. Soup can be left chunky; a potato masher stands in for a blender.

The day before, stage the equipment: pull it out, wash it, and set it where it will be used. A thermometer that lives in a drawer gets forgotten; one sitting beside the stove gets used. This is for tools, the same idea as measuring ingredients before you cook, and it is the single habit that most separates a calm cook from a frantic one.

equipment list
every tool, pan, machine and serving piece a project needs, checked against what you have

Check yourself

1. Why should raw chicken and salad greens be cut on separate boards?

Why: Raw poultry juices carry bacteria that cooking would kill in the chicken but that would survive on uncooked greens.

2. A three-course dinner needs five pots at once and the stove has four burners. What does this change?

Why: Equipment limits shape the schedule. A dish cooked ahead is reheated to 165 °F when it is time to serve.

3. What does it mean to stage equipment?

Why: Staging is mise en place for tools: a thermometer beside the stove gets used, one in a drawer gets forgotten.
39.9

The Safety Plan

Main ideaA safety plan writes down the standard temperatures, times and rules for the project before the day, so that safety decisions are already made.

At 5:30 on the day of the dinner, with the soup boiling and the guests arriving, nobody remembers whether chicken is 160 or 165 °F. So the number is written on a page taped to the cabinet a week earlier. A is that page: every safety figure and rule the project depends on, decided in a calm moment and posted where the work happens.

For a meal, the page holds the standard figures. Safe minimum internal temperatures: poultry 165 °F; ground meats 160 °F; whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb and veal 145 °F with a 3-minute rest; fish 145 °F; leftovers reheated to 165 °F; eggs cooked until firm. Cold food is held at or below 40 °F and the freezer at 0 °F. The two-hour rule: perishable food that has sat out for more than two hours, or one hour above 90 °F, is discarded. Hands are washed for 20 seconds before starting, after touching raw meat, and after any interruption. Raw meat gets its own board. A lid and baking soda sit near the stove for a grease fire, and water never goes on one.

For a sewing project, the page covers the machine and the iron. Fingers stay clear of the needle; the foot is off the pedal while threading; pins are removed before the needle reaches them, never sewn over. The iron is set to the fabric’s setting, rests on its heel, is never left face down, and is unplugged when the session ends. Shears and rotary blades are closed or capped when set down and carried point down. Long hair is tied back around both the machine and the stove.

Add the allergen line from your audience list to the safety page in large letters, with the rule that the allergen does not enter the kitchen during the project. Add the emergency numbers and the location of the first-aid kit and fire extinguisher. Then sign and date the page. When you evaluate the project, this page is one of the things you will be graded on, and it is the one page where "almost right" is not a passing grade.

39.10

Writing the Rubric First

Main ideaA rubric written before the work names what a good result looks like at each level, so the finished project can be judged fairly against the spec.

Ask a friend to taste your soup and they will say "good." Ask them to score it on a and they will tell you the salt was right, the texture was slightly grainy and the portion was exactly one cup. A rubric is a table: the rows are the things that matter, and the columns describe what each row looks like at levels such as excellent, good, needs work and not met. Writing it before the project turns the spec into a scoring tool.

Choose the rows from the spec. For a meal, common rows are: served on time, portion size matches the spec, food safety figures met and recorded, taste and seasoning, texture and doneness, appearance and plating, cost within budget, cleanup complete. For a sewn piece: fits the spec’s measurements, seams straight and at 5/8 inch, seam finish as specified, pressing, topstitching even, notions attached securely, cost within budget, finished on time.

Describe each level in words that can be checked. For "served on time": excellent means every course within 5 minutes of the plan; good means within 15 minutes; needs work means one course more than 15 minutes late; not met means a course was not served. For "seams": excellent means every seam at 5/8 inch within 1/16 inch and no puckers; good means one seam off by 1/8 inch; and so on. Numbers make the rubric fair to you and to the judge.

Give each row a weight. Safety and the spec’s required items weigh most; appearance weighs less. Then, before you begin, score the plan itself: a plan with no thermometer check in the timeline cannot score excellent on safety. Fix the plan, not the rubric. On the day the project is evaluated, you and your teacher will each fill in the same table, and the conversation about where the scores differ is where the real learning happens.

Think it through

A draft rubric row for a tote bag reads: Seams. Excellent: really good seams. Good: pretty good seams. Needs work: seams are a little off. Not met: bad seams. The spec called for 5/8-inch seams, pressed open, zigzag finished.

40.1

Running the Timeline

Main ideaOn the production day you follow the timeline you wrote, check the clock at every checkpoint and adjust the plan, not the safety rules, when you fall behind.

It is the morning of the project. The timeline is taped beside the safety page. The first task on it is not cooking; it is a five-minute walk through the plan: read every line, confirm every ingredient is in the kitchen, every tool is staged, and the thermometer has a battery. Then wash your hands for 20 seconds, tie back your hair, and start the first task at the time written next to it, not earlier and not later.

Work from the paper, not from memory. Cross off each task as it finishes and write the actual time beside the planned one. At each , stop for thirty seconds and compare. If you are ahead, do not start the next hot task early; use the time to clean or to prep the next step’s ingredients. If you are behind, decide what to cut, and cut the least important thing. A garnish goes before a course; a second side dish goes before the main; nothing on the safety page ever goes.

Common day-of mistakes come from skipping the paper. A student who "knows the recipe" forgets the salt; one who starts the rice first because it is easy serves it dry; one who does not write the thermometer reading cannot prove the chicken was safe. Another common mistake is doing every task in series when the timeline planned overlaps: while the chicken roasts for 90 minutes, the beans are trimmed, the table is set and the soup is reheated. The oven does the work; you do the next task.

For a sewing project the same rules hold. The timeline says press, cut, mark, sew in order, and the checkpoints fall after cutting and after the first major seam. If the first seam is off, you find it at the checkpoint with a seam gauge and rip it while it is one seam, not after the lining is in. Keep the on the table, not in the box: using it early is a sign of a careful worker, not a failed one.

40.2

Documenting as You Go

Main ideaPhotos, times, temperatures and short notes taken during the work are the evidence you will need to evaluate the project and present it.

At the evaluation Marisol could say "the chicken reached 167 °F at 5:52" because she wrote it down at 5:52. She could show the mise en place on Thursday, the cost sheet with receipts stapled to it, the chicken going into the oven, and the plated soup. None of that could have been reconstructed the next day from memory. is recorded while it happens or not at all.

Keep three kinds of records. First, numbers: every thermometer reading with its time, the actual start and finish time of each task, the register total from each receipt, the finished measurements of a sewn piece. Second, photos: the staged tools, each major step, each finished component, the final plate or garment from the front and back, and any problem, such as a puckered seam or a scorched pan, exactly as it looked. Third, short notes: one line when something goes differently from the plan, written in the moment. "4:41 rice done too early, holding in oven."

Set up documentation before the work so it does not slow the work. A phone on a stand at the counter, a pencil tied to the timeline, a folder for receipts. Wash your hands after touching the phone and before touching food again, and keep the phone off the prep surface; it is one of the dirtiest objects in the room. Take the photo, then wash, then continue.

Photograph problems as carefully as successes. The judge will believe a report that shows the dry rice more than one that shows only the good plates, and the dry rice is the part you learn from. A sewing student who photographs the first crooked seam beside the ripped and resewn one has shown more skill than one who shows only the finished bag.

40.3

Serving and Finishing

Main ideaThe final steps of plating and serving, or pressing and finishing, decide how the work is received, and they follow rules of temperature, timing and care like every earlier step.

Twelve bowls of soup do not fill themselves. Marisol laid out twelve warmed bowls on the counter in a grid, ladled one level cup into each, wiped each rim with a clean towel, and carried them out three at a time. Hot food is served hot: above 140 °F, out of the danger zone, on warm plates. Cold food is served cold, at or below 40 °F, so the salad stayed in the refrigerator until the moment it went out. Serving is the last cooking step, and it has the same rules.

Plate in an order. The main item goes down first, in the same spot on every plate; sides go beside it; sauce goes over or under, never dripped across the rim; garnish goes on last and only if it is edible and belongs to the dish. Wipe the rim. Check every plate against the spec’s portion before it leaves the counter: a scale or a measuring cup at the plating station keeps six ounces from becoming four on the last plate. Serve everyone at a table at the same time, then clear the course before the next one comes.

For a sewn piece, finishing is pressing, trimming and checking. Every seam is pressed as sewn, then open or to one side as the pattern says. Threads are clipped. Notions are tested: the snap closes, the zipper runs, the buttons hold. The piece is measured against the spec’s finished measurements, and any marking chalk is brushed away. A garment is tried on the person it was made for, and the fit is noted for the evaluation.

Cleanup is part of finishing, not something after it. Leftovers go into shallow containers, labeled with the date, and into the refrigerator within two hours of cooking. The stove, boards and counters are washed and sanitized, the thermometer is wiped and put back, and the machine is covered and the iron unplugged. The rubric has a row for this, and it is the row most often lost by a project that was otherwise excellent.

40.4

Scoring Against the Spec

Main ideaEvaluation means putting the finished result beside the spec and the rubric line by line and recording what met, what did not and by how much.

Lay the spec on the left and your documentation on the right. Read the first line of the spec: "Serves 12." Did it? Twelve bowls, twelve plates, twelve desserts: met. Next line: "Soup, 1 cup each." The ladle was a one-cup ladle and each bowl got one level scoop: met. Next: "Chicken, 6 oz each." The scale at the plating station read between 5.5 and 6.5 oz: met within a reasonable range. Go line by line to the end, and write met, not met or partly met beside each.

Then open the rubric and score each row using the evidence, not your feelings. "Served on time" is scored from the actual times written on the timeline. "Safety" is scored from the recorded temperatures. "Cost within budget" is scored from the receipts against the budget line. "Texture and doneness" is scored from tasting notes and photos, and here you must be the harshest taster at the table: the rice was dry at the edges, and dry is a texture fault, so the row is needs work even though nobody complained.

For a sewn piece, score with tools. A seam gauge on every seam; a tape measure on the finished dimensions; a close look at the stitching on both sides under good light; the closure tested ten times. Compare to the rubric levels you wrote, which used numbers such as 5/8 inch within 1/16, so there is no argument about what excellent means.

Total the weighted score and write it down before you talk to anyone. Then compare with your teacher’s scoring of the same table. Where the two scores agree, you have learned to see your own work clearly. Where they differ, ask why, and look at the evidence together. Honest self-evaluation is the skill employers describe when they say someone "takes feedback well."

Cost and Time, Plan Against Actual

Main ideaCompare the planned cost sheet and timeline with what really happened, find every difference and name its cause.

Marisol’s cost sheet planned $85.00 and the receipts totaled $87.40 (example). Two dollars and forty cents over is small, but the useful question is not how much; it is where. She laid the receipts beside the sheet. Chicken: planned $2.49 per lb, paid $2.79, because the sale had ended; on 4.5 lb that was $1.35 more. Lemons: planned 6 at $0.50, bought 8 because two were soft, $1.00 more. Foil: not on the sheet at all, $0.05 short of her cushion covering it. The total told her she was over; the lines told her why.

Do the same with time. Beside each planned time on the timeline is the actual time you wrote on the day. Subtract. The soup went out 4 minutes late because the bowls were not warmed until 5:55. The chicken went out 16 minutes late because the rest ran long while the soup was cleared. The rice was done 79 minutes early because it was started first. Each has a cause, and the cause is what gets fixed next time.

Sort the causes into three kinds. Things outside your control, such as a sale that ended: you cannot prevent them, but you can plan for them with a cushion, which is exactly what the cushion is for. Things in the plan, such as starting the rice first: the plan was wrong and is rewritten. Things in the execution, such as forgetting to warm the bowls: the plan was right and was not followed, so the fix is a line on the timeline or a checkpoint that catches it.

Write the comparison as a short table with four columns: item, planned, actual, cause. Keep it to the lines that differed. Then write one sentence for money and one for time: "Over budget by $2.40 (example), mostly from the chicken price; covered by the cushion. Main course 16 minutes late, from an unplanned overlap between the rest and clearing." Those two sentences, backed by the table, are the core of the presentation.

40.6

What I Would Change

Main ideaThe most valuable page of the evaluation is a short, specific list of what you would do differently, each tied to a cause you found in the evidence.

"What would you change?" is the question every judge, employer and teacher asks, and the weak answer is "nothing" or "everything." The strong answer is three to five specific changes, each tied to a variance and a cause. Marisol’s list: cook the rice last and hold the chicken instead, because rice dries when held and chicken keeps in its resting period. Warm the bowls at the 5:30 checkpoint, written on the timeline. Buy chicken at the second store, $0.30 per lb cheaper. Put foil on every cost sheet. Add a two-person rule for clearing a course while the next one rests.

Each change has the same shape: the action, then the reason, then the evidence. Write it that way. "Start the rice at 5:25 instead of 3:20, because rice held for 79 minutes dried at the edges; see photo 14 and the texture row." A reader can check every part. A change without a reason is a guess; a change without evidence is an opinion.

Include what you would keep, too. The thermometer readings, the staged tools, the day-before dessert all worked, and a that names the parts of the plan that held up is more useful than one that only lists faults. Then look for the change that would have prevented the most points lost. On Marisol’s sheet that was the rice, which cost two points on a weight-2 row; the chicken price cost one point on the cost row. Fix the biggest one first.

Finally, write one sentence about the skill, not the dish: "I learned that holding time is part of the recipe." This is the sentence that transfers to the next project, to a job in a restaurant kitchen, to a garment shop, or to planning a family’s week. The chicken is eaten; the sentence stays.

Main ideaA project presentation walks the audience from the spec to the result to the changes in a set order, with evidence on every slide or page and the product itself in the room.

Careers in Food and Nutrition

Main ideaCulinary and dietetics careers use exactly the planning, costing, safety and evaluation skills of the capstone, at every level of education.

Everything Marisol did for twelve people, a restaurant kitchen does for two hundred a night. A writes the prep list and the timeline; a purchasing manager keeps the cost sheet; a line cook holds the sauté station and reads the thermometer; a kitchen manager posts the safety page and checks the walk-in cooler is at or below 40 °F. Culinary careers range from a first job as a prep cook, often with no degree required, to executive chef, caterer, food stylist, recipe developer or restaurant owner.

Food safety itself is a career. Health inspectors visit restaurants and school cafeterias, check holding temperatures and hand-washing sinks, and write reports. Quality-assurance technicians in food plants test products and records. Many kitchen jobs require a food handler certificate, and managers often hold a food protection manager certification; both cover the same danger zone, temperatures and cross-contamination rules you posted on your safety page.

Dietetics is the science side. A registered dietitian nutritionist plans menus for hospitals, nursing homes, schools and sports teams, counsels people with diabetes or food allergies, and manages large food-service operations; the credential requires a degree, supervised practice and an exam. Dietetic technicians and nutrition assistants work alongside them. School nutrition directors plan meals for thousands of students under federal rules while meeting a budget, which is a cost sheet with more zeros.

Whatever the title, the interview questions will sound familiar. Tell me about a time you planned something under a deadline. How do you handle being over budget? What did you do when something went wrong? Your capstone is the answer: the spec, the cost sheet, the recorded temperatures, the dry rice and what you changed. Keep the documentation; it is your first portfolio.

Careers in Apparel, Children and Families

Main ideaApparel, early childhood, family services and consumer affairs careers each grow from one strand of the FCS course and use its planning and evaluation habits.

The student who sewed the tote bag from a spec did in miniature what an apparel industry does at scale. A turns a designer’s sketch into the pattern pieces with grainlines and notches; a technical designer writes the spec sheet with every finished measurement and seam finish; a sample sewer makes the first one; a quality inspector measures it against the spec with a tape and a seam gauge. Related jobs include tailor, alterations specialist, costume technician for theater, textile buyer and merchandiser, and the sustainability roles now growing as companies try to cut textile waste.

Early childhood careers build on the child development strand. Preschool and child-care teachers plan a day around routines, choices and redirection; Head Start and early-intervention staff work with families of children with disabilities; a child-care center director manages safety plans, ratios, budgets and menus all at once. Most states require training hours and a background check for child-care work, and many roles require an associate or bachelor’s degree. The safe-sleep rules and choking-hazard lists from your course are posted in every infant room.

Family and community services and consumer affairs are the strand that helps households manage. Family support workers, case managers and Extension educators teach budgeting, meal planning and parenting in community settings; the University of Illinois Extension runs nutrition and family programs across the state. Consumer affairs roles include credit counselors, housing counselors, and staff at agencies that handle complaints and scams. Financial literacy teachers and FCS teachers themselves are in this group, and there is a shortage of them in many districts.

Look across all of these and notice what repeats: a plan written before the work, a cost sheet, a safety page, evidence gathered as you go, an honest evaluation and a clear report. That is the capstone, and it is also a description of nearly any job worth having. Choose a strand that interests you, find one person who works in it, and ask them the question you have learned to answer: what would you change?

Think it through
One Skill, Five Jobs

The capstone skill: write a measurable spec and then evaluate the result against it. Five workplaces: a garment factory's quality station; an infant room at a child-care center; a credit counseling office; a school cafeteria kitchen; a theater costume shop.

For each workplace, describe what the spec would contain and what tool or record would be used to check the result against it.

1. At a checkpoint you are 20 minutes behind. Which may never be cut to catch up?

Why: Nothing on the safety page is ever cut; garnishes and extras go first.

2. Which is evidence that a dish met the safety plan?

Why: Only a recorded thermometer reading proves the safe minimum internal temperature was reached.

3. Hot soup must wait 45 minutes before serving. What temperature must it be held above?

Why: Hot food is held above 140 °F, the top of the danger zone, until served.

4. A cost sheet planned $85 and receipts total $87.40 (example). What is the most useful next step?

Why: The lines, not the total, show the causes, and the causes are what get fixed next time.

5. The plan listed warming the bowls and the cook forgot. What kind of cause is this?

Why: The plan was right and was not followed; the fix is a checkpoint or a timeline line that catches it.

6. Which is the strongest "what I would change" statement?

Why: Action, reason and evidence together make a change a reader can check.

7. In what order should a project presentation run?

Why: The presentation follows the project's own order, from the spec everything is judged against to the finished product.

8. Which credential plans hospital menus and counsels patients on nutrition?

Why: The registered dietitian nutritionist credential requires a degree, supervised practice and an exam.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Which project statement is complete enough to plan from?

Why: A project statement names the product, the scope and the date.

2. A student's idea fails the budget test. What is the right move?

Why: Shrinking the scope keeps the project real; the budget is a fixed constraint.

3. Which spec line is measurable?

Why: Cups and servings can be checked with a measuring cup and a count.

4. A guest has a tree-nut allergy. Which plan is safe?

Why: An allergen is a safety hazard, and trace amounts from shared tools can cause a reaction.

5. A 3-lb bag of onions costs $2.40 (example). What is the unit price?

Why: $2.40 ÷ 3 lb = $0.80 per lb.

6. A recipe serves 6 and you need 9 servings. A line calls for 2 cups of broth. What is the scaled amount?

Why: The factor is 9 ÷ 6 = 1.5, and 2 cups × 1.5 = 3 cups.

7. Which item is a hidden cost on a meal's cost sheet?

Why: Small, necessary supplies are the lines most often forgotten.

8. Dinner is at 6:30. A dish bakes 50 minutes and rests 10. When does it go in?

Why: 6:30 minus 10 minutes rest is 6:20, minus 50 minutes baking is 5:30.

9. Why does a frozen roast move to the refrigerator days ahead instead of the counter on the day?

Why: Refrigerator thawing keeps the whole roast at or below 40 °F.

10. What is the safe minimum internal temperature for poultry?

Why: Poultry is safe at 165 °F; ground meats at 160 °F; whole cuts of beef and pork at 145 °F with a rest.

11. A grease fire starts in a pan on the stove. What does the safety page say?

Why: Water spreads burning grease; a lid or baking soda cuts off the air.

12. Which rubric level is written correctly?

Why: Rubric levels use numbers or tools so they can be checked the same way by anyone.

13. Which record proves the chicken was safe?

Why: Only an internal temperature reading is evidence of safety.

14. Dinner ended at 8:30; the chicken came out at 5:50. What happens to leftovers?

Why: The two-hour rule counts from when the food left the oven.

15. Which is the strongest reflection statement?

Why: Action, reason and evidence make a change that a reader can check.

Write it

Choose a project: a two-course meal for eight or a simple sewn piece. Write the specification, a cost sheet with at least eight line items at unit prices (labeled example), a timeline built backwards from the deadline with two checkpoints, and a safety page. Then explain why each part is there and what would go wrong without it.

  • Write every spec line so it can be checked with a tool: a cup, a scale, a tape measure, a clock.
  • Compute each cost line as unit price times amount, add a cushion, and compare the total to your budget.
  • Start the timeline at the deadline and step backwards; put thawing and cooling days ahead.
  • Copy the standard safety figures exactly: poultry 165 °F, ground meat 160 °F, hold hot above 140 °F, fridge at or below 40 °F.
  • Name the one thing most likely to go wrong and the checkpoint that would catch it.
