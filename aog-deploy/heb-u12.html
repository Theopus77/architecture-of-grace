Close Reading Genesis

Torah Close Up

Most people who know the Bible know Genesis from a distance: a garden, a snake, a flood, a coat of many colors. This unit reads it up close. You will open the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation to Genesis 1, 2, 3 and 22, and then to the long stories of Jacob and Joseph in chapters 25 to 50, and read them the way a careful reader reads anything: slowly, noticing what the words say, what they repeat and what they leave out.

You will not read alone. At the table will be the classical Jewish commentators Rashi, Ibn Ezra and Ramban, the rabbis of the midrash, and modern readers from a German philologist writing in exile to an American literary critic. You will meet the Babylonian creation poem that was dug out of the ground in the 1800s and the academic theory that sees several sources woven into the Torah. Each reader asks different questions, and this unit teaches you to say whose question you are asking.

By the end you will be able to describe the structure of a passage, show how a repeated word or a silence carries meaning, compare two commentators on one verse, and explain how Jewish and Christian traditions read the same chapter differently, all without deciding for anyone what the text must mean. That is close reading, and it is the skill this course builds on from here.

The story

Counting the Words Isaac Never Says

Two students take a highlighter to Genesis 22 and go looking for what Isaac was thinking.

Maya and Devin have a copy of Genesis 22 on the table, nineteen verses in the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation. The assignment is simple. Highlight every word that tells you what Isaac feels or thinks. They expect to be busy. A father takes his son up a mountain to offer him as a sacrifice. Surely the text says something about the boy.

Devin reads out loud. Abraham gets up early. Abraham saddles the donkey. Abraham cuts the wood. Abraham lifts up his eyes and sees the place. Maya's highlighter stays capped. Then Isaac speaks, once. "My father," he says, and asks where the lamb is. Abraham answers that God will see to the lamb. Then the text says they went on, both of them together.

Maya counts again. Isaac says twenty or so words in the whole chapter. The text never says he is afraid. It never says he is confused, or brave, or that he trusts his father. It does not say how old he is. It does not say what he does when he is untied. At the end of the story, Abraham goes back down to his servants. The verse does not mention Isaac at all.

Devin is frustrated. "The writer left out everything I want to know." Their teacher smiles, because that is the point of the lesson. Nearly three thousand years of readers have noticed the same silence. The rabbis of the midrash wrote conversations to fill it. Medieval commentators argued about Isaac's age. A German scholar in 1946 said this silence is what makes the Bible's style what it is.

This chapter reads three passages of Genesis this way: the seven days of creation, the garden of Eden, and the binding of Isaac. Each one is short. Each one has been read for a very long time by Jewish readers, by Christian readers, by historians and by people who simply love a good story. The goal is to see what the words say, what they leave out, and what different readers have done with the gaps.

Section 1

The Seven Days

23.1

A Pattern Built From Refrains

Main ideaGenesis 1:1–2:3 is built on a strict pattern of repeated phrases, and the pattern itself is part of the meaning.

The Tanakh opens with , the Hebrew word that gives the book of Genesis its Hebrew name. The first account of creation runs from Genesis 1:1 to 2:3. Read it slowly and the same phrases keep returning. "And God said." "And it was so." "And God saw that it was good." "And there was evening and there was morning." Each day closes with that last line, like a bell at the end of a class period. These repeated lines are called , and they give the passage the feel of a poem or a liturgy more than a report.

Readers have long noticed a shape inside the seven days. Days one to three create spaces: light and dark, sky and sea, dry land and plants. Days four to six fill those spaces: sun, moon and stars for the light; birds and fish for sky and sea; animals and humans for the land. Day three and day six each have two acts of creation. The seventh day is different. Nothing is made. God rests, and the day is blessed and made holy. Jewish tradition reads this as the root of , the weekly day of rest.

The name for God in this passage is Elohim, translated "God." It is used the same way from the first verse to the last. Creation happens through speech. God speaks, and the thing exists. There is no fight and no rival. Humans are made last, male and female together, and are said to be made in the image of God. The text does not explain what that image means. Rabbinic and later readers have offered many answers, and this course will meet several of them. What the passage itself gives is order, rhythm and a world declared good.

23.2

Three Commentators on Verse One

Main ideaRashi, Ibn Ezra and Ramban each read the first verse differently, and their disagreement is part of how Jewish tradition studies Torah.

Jewish study of the Torah has always happened with commentators on the page. The three most read medieval commentators are (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, France, 1040–1105), Abraham (Spain, c. 1089–1164) and (Rabbi Moshe ben Nachman, Spain, 1194–1270). In a traditional printed Bible their words surround the text. On Genesis 1:1 they do not agree, and generations of students have learned to read by watching them argue.

Rashi opens with a question he takes from an earlier rabbi. The Torah is a book of commandments, so why does it not begin with the first commandment given to Israel, in Exodus 12? Rashi answers that the Torah begins with creation so that Israel can say the whole earth belongs to God, who gives land to whom God chooses. Then Rashi turns to grammar. He reads the Hebrew bereshit as "in the beginning of," so the verse means "when God began to create," and he says the verse does not teach the order in which things were made.

Ibn Ezra, a grammarian, also reads bereshit as a phrase that leans on the next words, and he warns against reading too much into a single verse. Ramban pushes back. He argues that the verb bara, "created," is used only for making something from nothing, and that verse one teaches exactly that: God brought the raw material of the world into being from nothing at all. Ramban also says the deep meaning of creation cannot be learned from the verses alone. Three readers, one verse, three results. That is what close reading with the commentators looks like.

23.3

Enuma Elish Beside Genesis 1

Main ideaReading Genesis 1 next to the Babylonian creation epic shows what the two texts share and where Genesis makes different choices.

In the 1840s and 1850s, British excavators at Nineveh, in what is now northern Iraq, uncovered thousands of clay tablets from the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal. Among them were pieces of a Babylonian poem now called , after its first words, "When on high." George Smith of the British Museum published an English account of it in 1876. For the first time, readers of Genesis could set the biblical creation story beside an older neighbor written in , the wedge-shaped script of Mesopotamia.

Enuma Elish tells of a fight among gods. The young god Marduk kills the sea goddess Tiamat, splits her body, and makes the sky from one half and the earth from the other. Humans are made from the blood of a defeated god so that the gods can rest from labor. The poem ends with Marduk’s temple in Babylon and his fifty names. Scholars note some points of contact with Genesis 1: water and darkness at the start, a sky that holds back waters above, a sequence that ends in rest. Many also point out that the Hebrew word for the deep, tehom, sounds like the name Tiamat.

The differences are just as clear, and readers have drawn different lessons from them. In Genesis 1 there is one God, no battle and no rival. Creation happens by speech. The sun and moon, gods in Babylon, are called simply the greater and lesser lights. Humans are not slaves of the gods but are made in God’s image and told to rule over the earth. Some historians read Genesis 1 as a quiet argument with the Babylonian story, perhaps written or shaped during the Babylonian exile in the 500s BCE. Traditional Jewish readers, who hold the Torah to be given by God, generally see the contrast as the Torah’s teaching against the beliefs of Israel’s neighbors. Both readings start from the same comparison.

The Garden

23.4

A Second Way to Begin

Main ideaGenesis 2:4–3:24 tells creation again, with a different order, a different name for God and a different style.

At Genesis 2:4 the text starts over. "These are the generations of the heaven and of the earth," it says, and then tells a story that does not match the first account point for point. In Genesis 1 plants come on day three, animals on days five and six, and humans last. In Genesis 2 there is no rain and no plant yet when God forms the man from the dust of the ground. Then God plants a garden in , then forms the animals, and only then makes the woman from the man’s side. Close readers have noticed this for a very long time.

The name for God changes, too. Genesis 1 says Elohim, "God." From Genesis 2:4 the text says "the LORD God," pairing the four-letter name of God, which Jewish practice does not pronounce, with Elohim. The style changes as well. Genesis 1 is a list with refrains and no dialogue. Genesis 2 and 3 are a story with a garden, two named rivers among four, a talking serpent, questions and answers. God is described walking in the garden in the cool of the day. The first account looks up at the sky; the second kneels in the dirt.

Jewish readers across the centuries have handled the two accounts in more than one way. Rashi, following the rabbis, treats Genesis 2 as adding detail to Genesis 1 and reads the differences as answers to questions the first chapter leaves open. The Talmud in tractate Berakhot notes the two names of God and links them to two ways God relates to the world, justice and mercy. Modern academic scholars, whom the next lessons will meet, read the two accounts as coming from different sources. What every reader shares is the observation itself: the Torah gives creation twice, and the second telling is not the first.

23.5

The Serpent and the Question

Main ideaGenesis 3 turns on wordplay, dialogue and a question from God, and each of these has been read closely by Jewish readers.

Genesis 2 ends with the man and woman naked and unashamed. Genesis 3 begins with the , called the most "subtle" of the animals. In Hebrew the two verses sit side by side on a pun. The humans are arummim, naked; the serpent is arum, cunning. The sound is nearly the same. Readers who know Hebrew have long heard the link, and translators struggle to carry it into English. The pun tells the reader that the story of the garden is going to be about knowing and not knowing.

The serpent’s first words are a question about what God said. The woman answers, adding a rule the text did not record: not even to touch the tree. The serpent promises that eating will open their eyes and make them like God, knowing good and evil. They eat. Their eyes open, and what they know first is that they are naked. They sew fig leaves and hide. The text moves quickly, through short lines of dialogue, and never explains what the is. Readers have proposed moral judgment, adult awareness, or simply all knowledge; the text does not settle it.

Then comes one of the most discussed questions in the Torah. God calls to the man, "Where art thou?" Rashi, drawing on the midrash, says God of course knew where the man was. God opened with a question so as not to frighten him, giving him room to answer. The man’s answer shifts blame to the woman, and the woman’s to the serpent. The chapter ends with the consequences for each: the serpent on its belly, pain in childbirth, hard work for the ground, and the two sent out of Eden, with and a turning sword at the gate. The text does not use the word sin, and it does not use the word fall.

23.6

Eden in the Midrash and Beyond

Main ideaJewish and Christian traditions read the garden story differently, and midrash fills its gaps in its own way.

is the rabbinic way of reading that asks what a text leaves out and answers with story, law or teaching. The largest collection on Genesis is , compiled in the land of Israel around the 400s CE. On Eden it asks many questions. From what earth was the first human formed? One answer: from dust gathered from the whole world, so that no land could claim him. Why was the woman built from the side and not the head or the foot? One answer: so that she would be neither above nor beneath the man. These are readings, offered by named rabbis, and often several are placed side by side without choosing.

The midrash also reads the garden as a story about consequences rather than an inherited condition. In rabbinic thought, each person is born with two inclinations, the yetzer ha-tov and the yetzer ha-ra, a pull toward good and a pull toward selfishness, and each person chooses. The garden explains why humans die and work hard and why the world is not Eden. It does not, in the mainstream Jewish reading, make later humans guilty for what the first two did. The word Adam is also simply the Hebrew for human, and the rabbis play on that.

Christian tradition took a different road. The apostle Paul, in his letter to the Romans, read Adam as the one through whom sin and death entered the world. Augustine of Hippo, writing around 400 CE, developed this into the doctrine of , the teaching that all humans inherit a fallen state from Adam. Many Christian traditions hold this; some Christian readers read it differently. Jewish tradition as a whole did not adopt it. The same three chapters, read by two communities, became two different stories about what it means to be human. A careful reader can describe both without deciding between them.

23.7

The Documentary Hypothesis in Brief

Main ideaModern academic scholars explain the doubled stories of the Torah as woven from several sources, and Jewish readers have responded in several ways.

By the 1800s, scholars in European universities had gathered the puzzles that close readers had always seen: two creation accounts, two names for God, two versions of some stories, laws that repeat with changes. The German scholar Julius Wellhausen, in a book published in 1878, gave these puzzles a full theory. The Torah, he argued, was woven together from four written , later called J, E, D and P for the clues that mark them. J uses the four-letter name of God from the start; E prefers Elohim; D is most of Deuteronomy; P is the priestly material, with its lists, dates and laws of worship. This is the .

On the theory, Genesis 1:1–2:3, with its refrains and its Elohim, is P. Genesis 2:4–3:24, with the LORD God, the garden and the dialogue, is J. Scholars who hold the theory have argued for more than a century about dates, about whether E ever existed on its own and about how the sources were joined. Today many academic scholars keep the basic idea of several sources while rejecting Wellhausen’s details. Others prefer models of layers added over time. It remains a hypothesis, a proposed explanation, and it is taught as such in universities, including in Israel.

Jewish responses have ranged widely. Wellhausen’s own writing carried a low view of later Judaism, and in 1903 Solomon Schechter, a leading Jewish scholar in New York, called this kind of criticism "higher anti-Semitism." The Italian-Israeli scholar Umberto Cassuto argued in lectures in the 1940s that the Torah was a single work and the sources a mirage. Orthodox Judaism holds that the Torah was given to Moses by God and does not accept the hypothesis as an account of how the Torah came to be. Conservative and Reform Judaism generally teach it in their seminaries alongside traditional study. Some scholars, such as Richard Elliott Friedman, are both observant Jews and source critics. A student in a public school describes these positions; the class does not vote on them.

The Binding of Isaac

23.8

Reading the Journey Word by Word

Main ideaGenesis 22 is told in spare, exact words, and its repetitions carry the weight of the story.

Jewish tradition calls Genesis 22 the , the Binding, from the verb used when Abraham binds Isaac. The chapter opens by telling the reader something Abraham does not know: God is testing him. Then God calls, and Abraham answers with one Hebrew word, , "here am I." He will say it three times in the chapter, once to God, once to Isaac, once to the angel. The command is built up in steps: your son, your only son, whom you love, Isaac. Rashi, following the Talmud, reads it as a conversation, with Abraham answering each phrase, "I have two sons," "each is the only son of his mother," "I love them both," until the name closes every door.

The text says nothing about the night that follows. Abraham rises early, saddles the donkey, takes two servants and Isaac, cuts the wood and goes. Three days pass in a single verse. On the third day Abraham lifts his eyes and sees the place from afar. He tells the servants to wait, and says that he and the lad will worship and return. Abraham puts the wood on Isaac. He carries the fire and the knife himself. Then comes a line the chapter will repeat: "and they went both of them together."

Isaac speaks once. "My father." Abraham answers, "Here am I, my son." Isaac observes that they have fire and wood, and asks where the lamb is. Abraham answers that God will see to the lamb, and adds, "my son." Then the line returns: "and they went both of them together." Read slowly, the second time is not the first. Before the question, they walk together in silence. After the question, they walk together knowing more, or perhaps the same. The text does not say. What it gives is the repetition, and the reader has to decide what changed between the two.

23.9

The Gaps and the Silence

Main ideaGenesis 22 leaves out what its characters feel, and this silence shapes how the story has been read for centuries.

Two students with a highlighter find what scholars have found. Genesis 22 never says what Isaac thinks. It does not give his age. It does not say whether he struggles when bound or what he does when released. Sarah, his mother, is not mentioned in the chapter at all. When it ends, the text says Abraham returned to his young men, and they went together to Beer-sheba. Isaac’s name is absent from that verse. The reader who wants to know what happened to him must look elsewhere or imagine.

The German scholar Erich , writing in exile in Istanbul during the Second World War, opened his book Mimesis (1946) by setting this chapter beside a scene from Homer’s Odyssey. Homer, he argued, lights everything: every object, every feeling, every past event is described in the foreground. Genesis does the opposite. It gives only what is needed, and everything else stays in the background, unspoken. Auerbach said this makes the biblical text "fraught with background" and demanding. It calls the reader into the story to supply what is missing.

Jewish readers had been answering that call for centuries. Genesis Rabbah gives Isaac words: he asks his father to bind him tightly so he will not flinch. Rashi on the next chapter connects Sarah’s death, reported in Genesis 23:2, to the news of what nearly happened on the mountain. The rabbis debated whether Isaac was a child or a grown man of thirty-seven, because Sarah’s age gives room for either answer. These fillings are not the text. They are midrash, and the tradition keeps them clearly marked as such, printed beside the verses rather than inside them.

The Ram, the Age and the Test

Main ideaThe end of the Akedah and the age of Isaac are read differently by midrash, Ibn Ezra and Ramban, showing how commentators weigh a text.

At the climax, an angel calls from heaven and Abraham answers, hineni, for the third time. He is told not to lay a hand on the boy, for now it is known that he fears God. Abraham lifts his eyes and sees a caught by its horns in a thicket, and offers it in place of his son. He names the place with a phrase built on the verb to see. The angel speaks again, promising blessing and many descendants. In the whole chapter, the ram is the one unexpected thing, and the rabbis gave it a history: some versions of a list in Pirkei Avot count the ram of Abraham among things made at the twilight of the sixth day of creation.

How old was Isaac? The text calls him a na’ar, a lad, a word used for both boys and young men. Genesis Rabbah reasons from Sarah’s death at 127 in the next chapter, which it connects to the Akedah, and concludes Isaac was 37. Ibn Ezra rejects this. If Isaac had been a grown man, he writes, the text would have recorded his obedience as a merit of his own, and it does not. Ibn Ezra estimates Isaac was near 13. This is a good example of two readers using the same verses and different rules of evidence. The midrash reads across chapters and fills gaps; Ibn Ezra reads the plain sense and asks what the text would have said.

What kind of test is it? Ramban asks the question that many students ask: God, who knows everything, does not need to learn anything from a test. Ramban answers that the test is for the one tested, to turn a good intention into a completed act, so that Abraham’s willingness becomes a deed and earns its reward. Other readers ask whether the test was whether Abraham would obey, or whether he would refuse. The text says only that the test happened and that Abraham passed, in the words of the angel. Every reading after that is a reading, and a careful student says whose it is.

The Akedah in the Year and in Modern Thought

Main ideaGenesis 22 is read aloud every Rosh Hashanah and has been argued over by modern thinkers of several traditions.

On the second day of , the Jewish new year, the Torah reading in the synagogue is Genesis 22. The Talmud, in tractate Megillah, sets this reading. The holiday’s central sound is the , usually a ram’s horn. In tractate Rosh Hashanah, Rabbi Abbahu asks why a ram’s horn is used, and answers in God’s voice: sound a ram’s horn before me so that I remember for you the binding of Isaac son of Abraham. The Zichronot, the remembrance prayers of the holiday’s additional service, ask God to recall the Akedah and treat the people with mercy for its sake. In Jewish practice, the chapter is not only a story about Abraham; it is an appeal.

Modern thinkers have pulled the chapter in other directions. The Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard, in Fear and Trembling (1843), made Abraham the model of faith precisely because his obedience cannot be explained by ethics. Kierkegaard called this a suspension of the ethical, and he wrote as a Christian for a Christian audience. The Israeli thinker Yeshayahu Leibowitz, in the twentieth century, read the Akedah as the purest picture of Jewish worship: serving God for God’s sake, not for human need. The British poet Wilfred Owen, writing from the trenches of the First World War, retold the story with the old man refusing the ram and killing his son, and half the sons of Europe with him.

After the Holocaust, the chapter grew heavier still. Israeli poets and painters returned to Isaac as the one who was bound and did not speak. Some Jewish thinkers asked whether the point of the story is the command or the angel’s voice that stops the knife. A public school class cannot settle these questions and should not try. It can read the nineteen verses carefully, name the readers and their traditions, and notice how a text this short has carried so much. That is the method of this whole unit: text first, then readers, then the student’s own reasoned response.

How the Narrator Works

24.1

Economy and Silence

Main ideaBiblical narrative tells its stories with very few words about looks, feelings and motives, and that restraint is a technique, not a lack.

Open a novel and you will usually learn what a character looks like, what she is thinking and why she does what she does. Open Genesis 25 and you learn that Esau came out red and hairy, that Jacob held his heel, that Esau hunted and Jacob stayed in tents, and that each parent loved a different son. That is nearly all. The text does not say why Rebekah loved Jacob. It does not say what Jacob felt as he held the heel or the stew. The , the voice that tells the story, says almost nothing that a camera could not see.

The literary scholar Robert , in The Art of Biblical Narrative (1981), argued that this is a deliberate art. Hebrew narrative gives the reader actions and speech and holds back judgment. When it does state a motive, as when it says Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his venison, the reason is so small that it becomes a comment. When it gives a physical detail, the detail matters later: Esau’s hairiness will be the tool of Jacob’s deception in Genesis 27. Nothing is decoration.

The silence puts work on the reader. Was Rebekah right to favor Jacob? Did Esau deserve to lose the blessing? The narrator will not say. Rabbinic readers filled the silence with midrash, sometimes making Esau a villain from the womb and sometimes finding merit in his care for his father. Modern literary readers keep the silence and ask what it does. Both are responses to the same feature: a text that shows and does not tell. Learning to read Genesis means learning to notice what is not on the page and to be careful about what you put there.

24.2

The Keyword

Main ideaA repeated word, the keyword, can tie scenes together and carry a judgment the narrator never states.

In the 1920s the German-Jewish thinkers Martin Buber and Franz Rosenzweig began a new German translation of the Bible. They noticed that Hebrew narrative repeats a single root word at key points, and they gave the device a name: Leitwort, leading word or . The repetition is easy to miss in translation, because translators often vary their words for style. In Hebrew the same sound comes back, and a listener hears the thread. Buber and Rosenzweig tried to keep the repetitions in German so readers could hear them too.

Genesis 27 turns on such a thread. Jacob, dressed as Esau, receives the , berakhah, meant for the firstborn. When Esau discovers the trick, he cries that his brother took his birthright, bekhorah, and now his blessing, berakhah. In Hebrew the two words share almost all their letters. The pun is Esau’s, and it sums up the whole chapter: the two things Jacob took are, in sound, nearly one thing. Later in the story the word blessing keeps returning, when Jacob wrestles for one at the Jabbok and when he blesses his sons before he dies.

The sharpest example runs between two chapters. In Genesis 37 the brothers dip Joseph’s coat in goat’s blood and send it to their father with the words, in Hebrew, haker-na, "recognize, please," whether it is his son’s coat. In Genesis 38, Judah, the brother who proposed selling Joseph, is handed his own seal, cord and staff by Tamar with the same two words: haker-na. Genesis Rabbah noticed the echo long ago and read it as measure for measure. Alter reads it the same way as a literary device. The narrator says nothing about justice; the keyword says it for him.

24.3

The Meeting at the Well

Main ideaBiblical narrative uses type-scenes, set patterns the audience knew, and tells character through how each telling changes the pattern.

Three times in the Torah a man arrives at a well in a foreign land, meets a young woman, water is drawn, she runs home with the news, and a marriage follows. In Genesis 24 Abraham’s servant meets Rebekah. In Genesis 29 Jacob meets Rachel. In Exodus 2 Moses meets the daughters of the priest of Midian. Alter called this a : a fixed pattern that an ancient audience would have recognized the moment a traveler reached a well, the way a modern audience knows what a first-date scene in a movie will do.

The art is in the variation. In Genesis 24 the servant is passive and prays; Rebekah does all the work, drawing water for ten camels, and the scene is long and formal. In Genesis 29 Jacob sees Rachel, rolls a heavy stone off the well’s mouth by himself, waters her sheep, kisses her and weeps. The scene is quick and physical, and it shows a Jacob who acts before he thinks. In Exodus 2 Moses drives off the shepherds who are bullying the daughters, which fits the man who has just struck down an Egyptian taskmaster. Each hero is shown by how he handles the same scene.

Knowing the pattern also lets a reader notice when it is missing. Isaac never goes to a well; a servant goes for him, and Isaac is the most passive of the three patriarchs throughout Genesis. Later readers have used the type-scene to think about the women, who in each scene are the ones with water to give. The rabbis had their own way of linking these scenes, noting for example that wells follow the family from Abraham to the well of Miriam in the wilderness. The literary reader and the midrashic reader are both hearing a pattern; they describe it in different words.

24.4

Two Sons and One Blessing

Main ideaGenesis 25–27 tells how Jacob gained Esau's birthright and blessing, and readers from Rashi to today have argued about what it means.

Before the twins are born, Rebekah feels them struggling inside her and asks God why. The answer, in Genesis 25:23, is an oracle: two nations are in her womb, and the elder shall serve the younger. The reader knows this before either parent acts. Esau, the elder, comes home famished and sells his birthright to Jacob for bread and a stew of lentils. The text says he ate, drank, rose, went his way, and despised his birthright. Five verbs in a row, and one judgment: the narrator almost never judges, so this one counts.

In Genesis 27 Isaac is old and blind and asks Esau to hunt game and prepare a meal so he can bless him. Rebekah overhears and sends Jacob in with goat meat and goat skins on his arms and neck. Isaac is suspicious. He feels the hairy arms and says a line that Jewish tradition has quoted for centuries: the voice is the voice of Jacob, but the hands are the hands of Esau. He blesses Jacob. When Esau returns, Isaac trembles, and Esau weeps and asks whether there is no blessing left for him. He receives a lesser one. He plans to kill his brother, and Jacob flees.

Readers have handled the deception in many ways. Rashi, following the midrash, softens Jacob’s lie by reading his words "I am Esau thy firstborn" as two statements: I am the one bringing you food; Esau is your firstborn. Rabbinic tradition also came to read Esau as Edom and later as Rome, so his loss became a story about nations. Ramban and others admit the plain sense: Jacob deceived his father. Many modern readers, Jewish and otherwise, point out that the story itself answers the deception. Jacob will be deceived in the dark by Laban, who gives him the elder sister when he expected the younger. The text does not say this is justice. It only tells both stories and lets the reader hear the echo.

Jacob asks the stranger’s name and is refused. He names the place , saying he has seen God face to face and lived. The sun rises as he crosses, limping. The text adds that for this reason the children of Israel do not eat the sinew of the hip, a rule Jewish law still keeps. Then, in Genesis 33, Esau runs to meet him, embraces him and weeps, and the two brothers part in peace. The night’s fight and the morning’s meeting sit side by side, and the text does not explain how one led to the other.

Who was the man? Hosea 12 says Jacob strove with an angel. Genesis Rabbah, and Rashi after it, identifies the angel as the guardian angel of Esau, so that the night fight is a preview of the morning’s meeting and of Israel’s long struggle with Edom. Ramban reads the episode as a sign of future generations who will be wounded but not destroyed. Modern readers, Jewish and others, have seen Jacob wrestling with his own past, his guilt, or God directly. Alter notes the wordplay: Jacob (Ya’akov) at the Jabbok (Yabbok) wrestles (ye’avek). Every reading has to account for the limp. Jacob leaves with a new name, and he leaves marked.

24.7

Dinah's Chapter

Main ideaGenesis 34 tells a hard story in which Dinah never speaks, and readers across the centuries have argued about her brothers' violence.

Genesis 34 is a chapter many teachers approach carefully. Dinah, Leah’s daughter, goes out to see the women of the land. Shechem, a local prince, takes her by force, then wants to marry her. Her brothers, angry, tell Shechem’s people that a marriage is possible only if all their men are circumcised. The men agree. While they are in pain, Simeon and Levi kill them all and take Dinah from Shechem’s house. Jacob says they have brought trouble on him. The brothers answer with the last words of the chapter: should our sister be treated as a harlot? The narrator does not reply.

What the chapter leaves out is as striking as what it tells. Dinah does not speak a single word. The text does not say what she wanted, felt or suffered afterward. The gap is total. Jewish readers have argued for centuries about the brothers. Maimonides, in his law code, held that the people of Shechem were liable because they failed to bring Shechem to justice. Ramban disagreed sharply, arguing that Jacob’s rebuke, repeated on his deathbed in Genesis 49, shows the killing was wrong. Both are reading the same silent chapter and the same words of Jacob.

On his deathbed, Jacob curses the anger of Simeon and Levi and scatters their tribes. Later readers have used this to argue that the text itself rejects the brothers’ act, while others note that the brothers get the last word in chapter 34. Modern readers, especially women, have asked why Dinah is silent and have written to give her a voice, as Anita Diamant did in her 1997 novel The Red Tent. In a public school, the chapter is read as a text about violence, honor and a family, with its readers named. It is not read as a verdict on any people, then or now.

Section 3

Joseph

24.8

Dreams, the Coat and the Pit

Main ideaGenesis 37 sets the Joseph story in motion with dreams told twice, a coat, a pit and a sale, and its seams have interested both rabbis and scholars.

Genesis 37 opens with Joseph at seventeen, tending sheep with his brothers and bringing their father a bad report of them. Jacob loves him more than the others and makes him a special coat, which JPS 1917 calls a coat of many colours; the Hebrew, ketonet passim, is uncertain, and some translations say a long-sleeved tunic. The brothers hate him. Then Joseph tells a : their sheaves bow to his. He tells another: the sun, moon and eleven stars bow to him. The brothers hate him more; his father rebukes him but, the text says, keeps the matter in mind.

Jacob sends Joseph to check on his brothers at Shechem. They see him coming and plan to kill him. Reuben, the eldest, persuades them to throw him into a pit instead, meaning to rescue him later. They strip the coat and put him in the pit. Then Judah proposes selling him to a passing caravan of Ishmaelites. The next verse says Midianite merchants pulled Joseph out and sold him to the Ishmaelites, who took him to Egypt. When Reuben returns, the pit is empty and he tears his clothes. The brothers dip the coat in goat’s blood and send it to their father.

Readers have noticed the seams. Who sold Joseph, the brothers, the Midianites or the Ishmaelites? Rashi works to make it one story, with several sales in a row. Rashbam, Rashi’s grandson, reads the plain sense: the Midianites found Joseph and sold him while the brothers were eating, so the brothers never sold him at all, which is why Reuben is surprised. Source critics take the doubled names as a sign that two versions were combined. The literary reader notes the repeated pairs: two dreams, two rescuers, two sets of merchants, and a goat’s blood that answers the goat skins Jacob once wore to deceive his own father.

24.9

Prison and Palace

Main ideaGenesis 39–41 moves Joseph from a prison to Pharaoh's court through dreams, and shows repetition with variation at work.

In Egypt Joseph is bought by Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, and rises to run his house. The text says the LORD was with him, a phrase it repeats. Potiphar’s wife tries to seduce him; he refuses; she accuses him falsely, and he is thrown into prison. There too he rises, put in charge of the other prisoners. Two of Pharaoh’s officers, the chief butler and the chief baker, have troubling dreams. Joseph asks whether interpretations do not belong to God, and then interprets: the butler will be restored, the baker hanged. Both come true. The butler forgets him for two years.

Then dreams: seven fat cows eaten by seven thin ones, seven full ears of grain swallowed by seven thin ones. His wise men cannot interpret. The butler remembers. Joseph is shaved and dressed and brought before the king. Pharaoh retells his dreams, and the retelling is not word for word: he adds that the thin cows were the worst he had ever seen, and that after eating the fat cows they looked no better. Alter and others point out that Hebrew narrative almost never repeats a speech exactly. The changes show Pharaoh’s fear, and the reader is meant to compare.

Joseph gives the to God, as he did in prison, and reads the two dreams as one: seven years of plenty, then seven of famine. Then he goes beyond interpreting and proposes a plan, a manager to store grain. Pharaoh gives him the job. Joseph receives an Egyptian name, Zaphenath-paneah, an Egyptian wife, Asenath, and Pharaoh’s ring. He is thirty. The boy in the pit is now second in Egypt, and the story is halfway done. Nothing in the chapter says why events fall as they do; Joseph will offer his own reading of that in Genesis 45 and 50.

24.10

Judah's Speech

Main ideaJudah's speech in Genesis 44:18–34 is the longest in Genesis and the turning point of the story, and Jewish tradition reads it as a model of teshuvah.

The famine brings Joseph’s brothers to Egypt to buy grain. They do not recognize the Egyptian official who tests them harshly, holds Simeon, and demands they bring their youngest brother, . Jacob refuses for a long time. Judah finally pledges himself as surety for the boy. On the second trip, Joseph hides his silver cup in Benjamin’s sack, has the brothers arrested and announces that Benjamin alone will stay as his slave. The others may go home. It is exactly the situation of chapter 37, with a Rachel’s son in danger and the brothers free to abandon him.

Judah steps forward and speaks. His speech, Genesis 44:18–34, runs seventeen verses, the longest single speech in Genesis. He retells the whole story from the brothers’ side: the official asked about their family; they told him of an old father and a young brother whose brother is dead; the father said his soul is bound up with the boy’s. Judah does not argue the law of the cup. He talks about a father. Then he offers himself as slave in place of Benjamin, because he cannot bear to see the grief that would come upon his father. At that, Joseph can no longer hold back.

The rabbis of the Talmud defined complete , return or repentance, as coming to the same situation and choosing differently; Maimonides wrote this definition into his law code. Many Jewish readers see Judah’s speech as its clearest picture in the Torah. The brother who once said, come, let us sell him, now says, take me instead. Literary readers point to the craft: the speech works by retelling, which lets the reader hear the brothers’ story with new feeling, and it never once mentions Joseph’s name. Jewish tradition also marks the moment. The weekly portion that begins at this verse is called Vayigash, and he drew near, from Judah’s first word.

Section 4

Recognition and Reading

24.11

The Recognition Scene

Main ideaJoseph's revealing of himself in Genesis 45, and his words in Genesis 50, give the story its own reading of why things happened.

Joseph sends every Egyptian from the room and weeps so loudly that the household hears. Then he says the words the brothers have not heard in twenty-two years of story time: I am Joseph. Is my father still alive? The brothers cannot answer. The text says they are dismayed at his presence. He tells them to come near, repeats his name, and adds that he is the brother they sold into Egypt. Then he tells them not to grieve, for God sent him ahead to preserve life. The scene the Greeks called , the moment a hidden identity comes to light, is the hinge of the second half of Genesis.

Joseph offers an interpretation of his own life, and the narrator neither confirms nor denies it. Twice in chapter 45 and once more in chapter 50, after Jacob’s death, Joseph says that what the brothers meant for evil, God meant for good, to keep many people alive. Jewish tradition has generally read the Joseph story through these words as a story of , God’s guidance working through human choices. Literary readers note that this is the only place in the story where anyone explains the plot, and that the explanation comes from a character, not from the narrator. The reader decides how much weight it carries.

Genesis ends in Egypt. Jacob blesses his sons and is carried home to Machpelah for burial. Joseph dies at 110 and is embalmed, and the last verse leaves him in a coffin in Egypt, having made his brothers swear to carry his bones home when God brings them up from the land. The Torah’s next book opens with those descendants enslaved. The German novelist Thomas Mann spent the years 1933 to 1943, the years of the Nazi rise and the war, writing Joseph and His Brothers, a four-volume retelling. A story of a family reconciled, ending in a coffin waiting to go home, has been read as many things. The text gives the coffin and the oath.

24.12

As Literature and as Torah

Main ideaThe same chapters can be read as literature and as Torah, and knowing both ways of reading is the goal of this unit.

Reading Genesis 25–50 as means asking how the text works: the narrator’s economy, the keyword, the type-scene, repetition with variation, the gap that the reader must fill. It means treating Jacob, Leah and Judah as characters who change, and treating the writer, whoever that was, as an artist. Scholars like Alter, Meir Sternberg and Adele Berlin built this approach in the 1980s. It can be practiced by anyone, of any faith or none, and it is the way a public school can read the text together.

Reading the same chapters as Torah means reading them as Jewish tradition does: as sacred teaching, divided into weekly parashot and read aloud on Shabbat through the year, surrounded by commentary, argued over in study pairs, with rules of practice drawn from them, such as the sinew of the hip. Rabbinic tradition says the Torah has seventy faces, meaning that many readings can be true at once. In this way of reading, the gaps are not only art; they are invitations, and midrash is the tradition’s answer. The plain sense, peshat, and the searching sense, , sit side by side on the same page.

The two ways are not enemies. Many Jewish scholars do both, and many literary readers learn from Rashi. Where they differ is in the question each asks. The literary reader asks what the text does. The reader of Torah asks what the text asks of the reader. A student in this course should be able to do the first, describe the second accurately, and say clearly which one is speaking at any moment. That skill, keeping the text, its readers and yourself distinct, is what close reading of Genesis has been teaching since Rashi’s classroom in Troyes and long before.
