Earth, Sky and Weather

Earth and Space Science

Look up. Look down. Look out the window. The sky, the sun, the moon, the rain, the ground under your feet. All of it is science. All of it has patterns. In this unit, you will find them.

You will chart the weather for a month. You will watch the moon grow and shrink. You will follow rainwater across a playground and see where it goes. You will learn what weather is dangerous and how to get ready.

By the end, you will be able to say what the weather is and why it matters. You will know why days are long in summer. You will know that most of Earth's water is salty. And you will know that water and wind can change the land.

The big question

What patterns can we find in the sky, the weather and the land, and how do they help us?

Weather
Big questionWhat is the weather doing today, and what pattern is it part of?
The story

A Month of Stickers

Every morning our class puts one sticker on the weather chart. What will the chart show after thirty days?

Every morning, Mr. Ortiz's class looks out the window. Is it sunny? Is it cloudy? Is it raining? One student picks a sticker. A yellow sun. A gray cloud. A blue raindrop. A white snowflake. The student sticks it on today's square of the chart.

At first the chart was empty. Then the stickers grew. One week had four sun stickers. Another week had three rain stickers in a row. Everyone wore boots that week. The floor by the door was full of puddles.

After thirty days, the class counted. Twelve suns. Nine clouds. Six raindrops. Three snowflakes. Ana said, 'It was mostly sunny.' Jamal said, 'But it rained a lot too.' They were both right. The chart showed it.

A chart turns thirty days of looking into one picture. Scientists who study weather do the same thing. They look every day. They write it down. Then they look for patterns. So can you.

The story

Seven Nights on the Porch

Nia's grandpa says the moon will change every night this week. She does not believe him. So she checks.

On Monday night, Nia and Grandpa sat on the back porch. The moon was a thin curve. Like a fingernail clipping. 'It will grow,' Grandpa said. Nia did not think so. She drew it in her notebook anyway.

On Wednesday, the moon was fatter. Half of a circle. On Friday, it was almost round. Nia drew it each night. Some nights clouds got in the way. She wrote 'cloudy' on those pages.

On Sunday, the moon was big and bright and round. It lit up the whole yard. Nia looked at her drawings. Thin. Half. Almost full. Full. 'It grew!' she said. Grandpa smiled. 'Keep watching. Now it will shrink.'

Nia did not just believe Grandpa. She looked for herself. She drew what she saw. Seven drawings showed a pattern. That is how scientists learn about the sky.

2. What happens to ice in a warm room?

Why: Warmth melts ice back into liquid water.

3. Ice and water are...

Why: Ice is frozen water. Melting turns it back. It is the same stuff.
Section 3

Changing the Land

9.5

Fast Change, Slow Change

Main ideaWind and water change the shape of the land, sometimes fast and sometimes slowly.

Water can move the land. In our story, a little river cut a ditch in the sand pile. Real rivers do the same thing. They carry away dirt. That is . Bit by bit, they carve their path.

Some changes are slow. A river bends a little more each year. It takes a lifetime to see. Some changes are fast. A can wash away a road in one day.

Wind moves land too. It blows sand into hills called dunes. The Indiana Dunes, near Chicago, were built by wind off Lake Michigan. That took thousands of years.

Think it through
The Sand Pile Test

Make a small hill of sand in a tray. Pour a cup of water slowly on top. Watch what happens. Now make a new hill. Pour a whole pitcher of water on it fast.

Which pour changed the hill more? Which one is like a flood?

Words to know
erosion
when wind or water carry away bits of land
flood
when water rises and covers land that is usually dry
Check yourself

1. What is erosion?

Why: Erosion is when wind or water carry land away, bit by bit.

2. Which is a fast change to the land?

Why: A flood can change the land in one day. The others take years.

3. What built the Indiana Dunes?

Why: Wind off Lake Michigan blew sand into hills over thousands of years.
9.6

Slowing It Down

Main ideaPeople can slow erosion by building walls and planting grass.

Wind and water carry land away. People do not always like that. A house on a hill could slide. A beach could wash away. So people find ways to slow it down.

One way is a wall. A stone wall along a shore blocks big waves. Another way is plants. Grass and tree hold the dirt like fingers. Bare dirt washes away. Grassy dirt stays.

You can test it. Fill two trays with dirt. Put grass in one. Tilt them and pour water. Which tray loses more dirt?

Think it through
Two Trays of Dirt

Two trays sit on a tilt. One holds bare dirt. The other holds dirt with grass growing in it. You pour the same cup of water on each. Muddy water runs out of one tray. Almost clear water runs out of the other.

Which tray gave muddy water? Why did the grass help?

Words to know
root
the part of a plant that grows down into the dirt and holds it in place
Check yourself

1. How do grass roots help the land?

Why: Roots grip the dirt like fingers, so water cannot carry it off.

2. What could people build to block big waves?

Why: A stone wall along a shore blocks waves and slows erosion.

3. Which dirt washes away more?

Why: Bare dirt has no roots to hold it, so it washes away.
