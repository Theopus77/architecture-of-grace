Capstone

Historians of fighting arts rarely watch the past directly. They work from what survives: a painted manuscript in Leeds, a Ming general's handbook, a swordsman's scrolls from Kumamoto, a boxing belt from London, a cup given by a Japanese crown prince, and early films shot in New Jersey and Nevada. Each object was made for its own reasons, and each leaves gaps.

Fighting arts also have a history of refusals. A champion refused Black challengers in the 1880s. Congress refused to let fight films cross state lines in 1912. Muhammad Ali refused the draft in 1967, and the Supreme Court sided with him in 1971. Iceland, Sweden and Norway each closed the ring by law, then later reopened it under licenses.

In this unit you will read objects and legal records as evidence. You will be able to date a source, say what it proves and what it cannot, and explain who held power over a sport at a given moment. You will also compare how the United States, Iceland, Sweden and Norway wrote laws about the ring.

Big questionWhat can a book, a prize or a strip of film prove about the history of a fighting art, and what can it not prove?
The story

A Manuscript Under Glass

A handwritten book about sword and buckler has survived for about seven hundred years.

In the Royal Armouries museum in Leeds, England, curators keep a small Latin manuscript catalogued as MS I.33. Scholars date it to around 1300. Its painted pages show a priest teaching a student to fight with a sword and a small round shield called a buckler.

Nobody knows for certain who made the book or where it was first used. Historians think it came from a German-speaking land. It passed through several owners over the centuries before it reached a British museum collection.

The manuscript is an object, and objects survive for reasons of their own. A rich owner paid for fine paint. A library kept it dry. Thousands of plainer notebooks, and most teaching that was never written down, did not survive at all.

Historians of martial arts work with objects like this one: books, belts, cups, masks, photographs and films. Each one can answer some questions well. Each one also leaves gaps, and a careful reader names those gaps before drawing a conclusion.

Books That Survived

33.1

The Leeds Sword Book

Main ideaRoyal Armouries MS I.33, from around 1300, is the oldest surviving European book about fighting with weapons.

A is a book that records a system of armed combat. In German such a book is called a , meaning fight book. The oldest known European example is Royal Armouries MS I.33, dated by scholars to around 1300 and held today in Leeds, England.

The manuscript mixes Latin text with painted figures. Most pages show a priest, called sacerdos in the text, and a student, called scholaris. Near the end, a woman named Walpurgis appears in the pictures, which makes the book an early record of a woman shown in armed practice.

The book survives as a single copy. Historians cannot name its author, and its exact place of origin is still debated, though most point to a German-speaking region. That is typical of an : it proves that someone recorded this teaching, but it does not show how widely it was taught.

Fiore's Flower of Battle

Main ideaFiore dei Liberi, an Italian master, wrote the Fior di Battaglia around 1400, and several copies survive in museums and libraries.

Fiore dei Liberi was a fencing master from Friuli, in northeastern Italy. Around 1400 he produced a manuscript called , the Flower of Battle. One copy is dedicated to Niccolò III d’Este, the lord of Ferrara, which ties the work to a court patron.

Unlike MS I.33, Fiore’s work survives in more than one copy. One is held by the J. Paul Getty Museum in Los Angeles and another by the Morgan Library and Museum in New York. The copies differ in their pictures and text, so scholars compare them line by line.

In his introduction Fiore described learning from many masters and said he had fought several duels. Historians treat such claims as the author’s own account. A , a wealthy person who paid for a book, often shaped how an author presented himself on its pages.

33.3

A Ming General's Handbook

Main ideaQi Jiguang's military handbook of the 1560s includes a chapter on boxing, one of the oldest dated Chinese texts on unarmed practice.

Qi Jiguang (1528–1588) was a general of China’s Ming dynasty. In the 1550s and 1560s he fought raiders along China’s southeast coast, whom Ming records called . Around 1560 he wrote the , often translated as New Book of Effective Discipline.

Most of the book is about training soldiers, organizing units and using weapons. One chapter is about boxing. Qi wrote that boxing did not seem useful for battle, but that it trained the body and built confidence for beginners. The book also covers spears, swords, halberds and the organization of troops.

The chapter names several boxing families known in Qi’s day and pictures a set of postures. Historians value it because it has a firm date and a named author. Many later schools told origin legends, but Qi’s book shows what one army officer actually wrote down in the 1560s.

33.4

Musashi's Book of Five Rings

Main ideaMiyamoto Musashi finished the Go Rin no Sho near Kumamoto in 1645, shortly before he died.

Miyamoto Musashi was a Japanese swordsman of the early Edo period. Late in life he lived in Kumamoto, on the island of Kyushu. In the 1640s he wrote , the Book of Five Rings, in a cave called Reigandō, according to long tradition. He was born around 1584.

The book has five scrolls, named for Earth, Water, Fire, Wind and the Void. Musashi gave it to his student Terao Magonojō shortly before his death in 1645. The original manuscript does not survive, so modern editions rely on later handwritten copies.

Musashi’s own life is known partly from records and partly from later stories, plays and novels. His famous 1612 duel with Sasaki Kojirō is told mainly in sources written long afterward. Historians separate the he wrote from the that grew around him.

33.5

The Lonsdale Belt

Main ideaBritain's National Sporting Club created the Lonsdale Belt in 1909, and a boxer could keep it by winning three title fights in one weight class.

The National Sporting Club was a private boxing club in Covent Garden, London. In 1909 it began awarding a belt for British champions. The belt was named for Hugh Lowther, the fifth Earl of Lonsdale, who supported the club. The first winner was the lightweight Freddie Welsh, in 1909.

The belt came with a rule. A who won three title fights in the same weight class could keep the belt for good. That rule rewarded boxers who defended a title, not just those who won it once. Welsh was a Welsh boxer whose real name was Frederick Hall Thomas.

The British Boxing Board of Control, formed in 1929, later took over the belt and still awards it. Many Lonsdale Belts now sit in family homes and museum cases. Each belt is a , and like a certificate it records a , a date and a weight.

33.7

The Fencing Mask

Main ideaWire-mesh fencing masks spread in France in the late 1700s, and they made faster practice with blunted weapons much safer.

For centuries, fencers practiced with blunted swords and little face protection. Teachers limited attacks to the body because an eye injury could be fatal. That limit shaped how people practiced and how contests were judged. Fencing had long been taught in European cities by licensed masters and guilds.

In the late 1700s, French fencing teachers began using masks of woven wire mesh. Many histories credit the Paris master Texier de La Boëssière with making the popular. The mesh let a fencer see and breathe while it stopped a blade from reaching the face.

Masks changed the practice room. With the face protected, fencers could exchange attacks more freely, and the rules of fencing developed around that faster play. The mask is a small object that stands for a large change: safety equipment making a new kind of contest possible.

Muybridge Photographs Motion

Main ideaEadweard Muybridge's Animal Locomotion (1887) included sequences of men boxing, wrestling and fencing, among the first photographs to freeze combat sports in motion.

Eadweard Muybridge was an English-born photographer working in the United States. In the 1880s the University of Pennsylvania in Philadelphia supported his study of movement. He lined up many cameras and triggered them one after another to record a moving body.

In 1887 he published , a set of 781 plates. The subjects included horses, birds and people doing everyday tasks. Some plates show men boxing, wrestling and fencing, each broken into a row of still frames. Other plates show runners, jumpers and people climbing stairs.

The plates were made for science, not for sport. Still, they are early photographic records of how athletes of the 1880s dressed and where they stood in a room. A in this sense is one printed page of images, and each one was staged for the camera.

Edison Films a Boxing Match

Main ideaIn 1894 Thomas Edison's company filmed a boxing exhibition in its Black Maria studio, with rounds shortened to fit the camera.

Thomas Edison’s company built a film studio in West Orange, New Jersey, in 1893. Workers called it the because it was dark and cramped like a police wagon. Its roof opened, and the building turned to follow the sun. It was among the first buildings made only for shooting films.

In September 1894 heavyweight champion James J. Corbett boxed Peter Courtney in the studio. The film was shown in a , a cabinet with a peephole that one viewer looked into at a time. Each round was shortened so it fit on the film the machine could hold.

The bout was arranged for the camera, and viewers paid to watch each round. That made boxing one of the first subjects sold as moving pictures. Historians see here an early tie between combat sport and film, a tie that later drew the attention of lawmakers.

Promoters filmed the fight with a camera called the , which used unusually wide film. The finished picture ran well over an hour, making it one of the longest films made up to that time. It was shown in theaters across the country. Enoch J. Rector led the filming.

Bans, Color Lines and Refusals

Capstone
Big questionWhen a champion, a court or a nation refused a fight, who gained power over the sport, and who lost it?
The story

A Film Stopped at the State Line

In 1910 a boxing film became a question for Congress.

On July 4, 1910, Jack Johnson, the first Black world heavyweight champion, fought James J. Jeffries in Reno, Nevada. Jeffries had retired undefeated and came back to fight Johnson. Newspapers had spent months calling him the hope of white America.

Johnson won in the fifteenth round. That night and in the days after, white crowds attacked Black Americans in many cities, and people were killed. Some city governments then banned showing the film of the fight.

Two years later, in 1912, Congress passed the Sims Act. It made it a federal crime to carry films of prizefights across state lines. The law stayed in place until 1940. Its chief sponsor was Representative Thetus W. Sims of Tennessee.

The story shows that a fight can be refused in many ways. A champion can refuse a challenger. A court or a Congress can refuse a film. A person can refuse a government's order. A nation can refuse the ring itself. Each refusal changed who controlled a fighting art.

A Title Crosses the Color Line

34.1

Sullivan Refuses a Challenger

Main ideaHeavyweight champion John L. Sullivan refused to fight Black challengers, including the respected boxer Peter Jackson.

In the 1880s John L. Sullivan of Boston was the most famous boxer in the United States. As heavyweight champion, he chose whom he would fight. He publicly refused to box any Black challenger, a practice later called the . He held the title from 1882 to 1892.

Peter Jackson was born in 1861 on St. Croix, then part of the Danish West Indies. He built his career in Australia and was one of the best heavyweights of his time. In 1891 in San Francisco, Jackson fought James J. Corbett for 61 rounds, and the bout was ruled a .

Sullivan never fought Jackson. Because a champion controlled his own title, one man’s refusal could keep a whole group of boxers from a chance at the top prize. The color line in heavyweight boxing held until 1908, when Jack Johnson finally got his title fight. Jackson died in Australia in 1901.

Johnson Wins in Sydney

Main ideaJack Johnson became the first Black world heavyweight champion by beating Tommy Burns in Sydney, Australia, in 1908.

Jack Johnson was born in Galveston, Texas, in 1878. By the early 1900s he was one of the best heavyweights in the world, but champions kept refusing him. He followed champion Tommy Burns, a Canadian, across the world to demand a fight. Burns held the title from 1906 to 1908.

On December 26, 1908, Johnson fought Burns in Sydney, Australia. Police stopped the fight in the fourteenth round, and Johnson was named the winner. He became the first Black world champion, breaking the color line in boxing’s top division.

Many white newspapers reacted with anger. Writers called for a former champion to return and take the title back. That campaign led to the 1910 fight in Reno, and the phrase came from it. Former champion James J. Jeffries, then retired on his farm in California, became the man they chose.

For decades, historians and members of Congress argued that the case was driven by racism. On May 24, 2018, President Donald Trump granted Johnson a posthumous , meaning a pardon given after death. The pardon did not erase the years Johnson lost.

Law Against the Fight Film

34.4

Reno, July 4, 1910

Main ideaJohnson's 1910 victory over Jeffries in Reno was followed by racist attacks in many U.S. cities.

James J. Jeffries had retired as heavyweight champion in 1905 without losing a fight. Under public pressure, he agreed to return and fight Johnson. The bout was first planned for San Francisco, but California’s governor blocked it, so promoter Tex Rickard moved it to Reno, Nevada.

On July 4, 1910, Johnson won in the fifteenth round. News of the result spread by telegraph within minutes. In many cities across the United States, white crowds attacked Black people who celebrated, and people were killed. The fight took place in an outdoor arena built for the day.

The violence showed how a single sporting result could carry the weight of a nation’s racial , a ranking of groups by power. Officials who could not reverse the result turned instead to the film of it. A line had carried the news; the film would carry the pictures.

The Sims Act of 1912

Main ideaThe Sims Act of 1912 banned carrying prizefight films across state lines, and it stayed law until 1940.

After Reno, many cities banned showing the Johnson–Jeffries film. In 1912 Congress went further. The made it a federal crime to transport films of prizefights between states or into the country. Its sponsors were Southern members of Congress.

The law did not ban boxing and did not ban filming a fight. It banned moving the film. Promoters found ways around it, such as filming in one state and showing the film only there. The law was enforced unevenly. Breaking the law could bring a fine or a prison term.

Congress repealed the Sims Act in 1940. By then Joe Louis, a Black heavyweight champion from Detroit, had held the title since 1937. Historians read the Sims Act as a clear case of power used to control what images of a Black champion Americans could see.

From the source
What the Law Forbade
It shall be unlawful to bring into the United States, or to carry from one state to another, any film or picture of any prizefight or encounter of pugilists, meant for public exhibition.
Sims Act (1912), U.S. Statutes at Large (paraphrased)

Section 3

A Champion Refuses the Draft

34.6

Houston, April 1967

Main ideaMuhammad Ali refused induction into the U.S. Army in 1967, citing his Muslim faith, and lost his title and his license to box.

Cassius Clay of Louisville, Kentucky, won the Olympic light heavyweight gold medal in Rome in 1960. In 1964 he won the world heavyweight title from Sonny Liston. Soon after, he announced he had joined the Nation of Islam and took the name Muhammad Ali.

During the Vietnam War, the government used the to call young men into the military. Ali asked to be classified as a , a person who refuses military service for reasons of religion or conscience. His request was denied. On April 28, 1967, in Houston, he refused to step forward for induction.

Boxing officials acted within days. The New York State Athletic Commission suspended his license, and the World Boxing Association took away his title. In June 1967 a federal jury convicted him, and he was sentenced to five years in prison, though he stayed free while he appealed.

34.7

Clay v. United States, 1971

Main ideaIn 1971 the U.S. Supreme Court overturned Ali's conviction in Clay v. United States, after he had lost more than three years of his career.

Ali did not box from March 1967 to October 1970, when he fought Jerry Quarry in Atlanta. Georgia had no state boxing commission, so a city license made the bout possible. A New York court later ordered the state to restore his license. Quarry was a leading heavyweight from California.

On June 28, 1971, the ruled in . The justices voted 8–0 to overturn his conviction, with Justice Thurgood Marshall not taking part. The Court found that the government had not properly explained why it denied his claim as a conscientious objector.

Ali lost his first fight after the ban, to Joe Frazier in 1971. He won the heavyweight title back in 1974 against George Foreman in Kinshasa, Zaire. His case is studied as a refusal that cost an athlete his livelihood and was later upheld by the nation’s highest court.

Section 4

Nations That Closed the Ring

34.8

Iceland Bans Boxing, 1956

Main ideaIceland's parliament banned boxing in 1956, and amateur boxing was allowed again only in 2002.

In 1956 the Althing, Iceland’s parliament, passed a law banning boxing. The law covered public matches and the teaching of the sport. Supporters of the ban pointed to serious injuries in the ring. The country had become a fully independent republic only twelve years earlier, in 1944, after centuries under Danish rule.

The ban lasted more than four decades. In 2002 the Althing voted to allow boxing again. Professional boxing stayed banned in Iceland, so Icelanders who wanted to box for pay had to compete in other countries. That was 46 years after the ban began.

Iceland’s ban shows a nation refusing a sport through ordinary , a law passed by a parliament. It differs from the Sims Act, which blocked only films, and from a champion’s color line, which a single man enforced. It also differs from Ali’s case, where a government punished one boxer rather than the sport.

Think it through
Three Kinds of No

No one: a champion refuses to meet a challenger. No film: a law stops fight pictures at the state line. No sport: a parliament bans matches and teaching across a whole country.

34.9

Sweden and Norway Say No to Pros

Main ideaSweden banned professional boxing in 1970 and Norway in 1981, while both kept amateur boxing legal.

Sweden had a famous professional champion, Ingemar Johansson, who won the world heavyweight title from Floyd Patterson in 1959. About a decade later, in 1970, Sweden banned boxing. Amateur boxing, with its shorter bouts, remained legal.

Norway passed its own ban on professional boxing in 1981. Norwegian boxers who wanted to turn professional had to compete abroad. Supporters of both bans cited medical concerns about repeated blows to the head. Norway’s law also covered other sports in which knockouts were the goal.

Both countries later changed course. Sweden allowed professional boxing again in 2006, under a licensing system. Norway’s parliament allowed professional matches under new rules in 2016. In both cases a state replaced an outright ban. Sweden’s new law took effect in 2007.

34.10

From Ban to License

Main ideaAcross these cases, refusals often ended not in freedom without rules but in a license, a commission or a court ruling that set new limits.

The color line in the heavyweight division fell in 1908 when a Black challenger finally forced a title fight. The Sims Act of 1912 ended by repeal in 1940. Ali’s conviction ended by a Supreme Court ruling in 1971. A different body ended each one: a challenger in the ring, Congress and the Supreme Court.

Nordic bans ended differently. Iceland in 2002, Sweden in 2006 and Norway in 2016 each replaced a ban with rules and , meaning ongoing supervision by an official body. Headgear rules, medical checks and licenses took the place of an outright no.

Historians compare such cases to ask who held power at each moment: a champion, a Congress, a commission, a court or a parliament. The answer changed over time. A , a rule enforced by an official body, was often the compromise. In the 1880s no official body ruled the heavyweight title, but by the 1960s commissions, associations and courts all claimed a say.
