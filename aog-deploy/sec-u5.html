The Knights Templar

Knights

About 900 years ago, a few knights in Jerusalem made a promise. They would guard travelers on dangerous roads. They would also live like monks. People called them the Knights Templar. In time, they grew rich and famous across Europe.

Then, one Friday morning in 1307, the king of France had them arrested. Their story ended in trials, a lost parchment and many legends. Some legends are still told today, about treasure, curses and secret survival.

In this unit, you will learn how the Templars lived and why they fell. You will read real records from the time. You will practice a skill historians use every day: sorting what the evidence shows from what a story only claims.

The big question

How can we tell what really happened to the Knights Templar from the legends people tell about them?

Big questionHow did a small group of knights guarding pilgrims grow into one of the richest groups in Europe?
The story

A Receipt for the Road

A traveler in France is scared to carry his money on a long trip. The knights next door have an idea.

It is a cold spring morning in France, about 850 years ago. A farmer we will call Pierre is packing for a long trip. He wants to walk to Jerusalem, a holy city far away. The trip may take many months. Pierre has saved coins for years to pay for food and boats on the way.

Pierre is worried. Robbers wait on the roads. A bag of coins is easy to steal. His neighbor tells him about the knights at the big stone house near the market. They wear white cloaks with red crosses. People say they keep money safe for travelers.

Pierre knocks on the heavy door. A brother in a plain cloak lets him in. Pierre sets his coins on a wooden table. The brother counts them slowly. Then he dips a feather pen in ink. He writes how much Pierre left on a small piece of parchment. He hands Pierre the written receipt.

Pierre folds the receipt and tucks it inside his shirt. Now he carries a piece of writing, not a heavy bag of gold. He walks out into the street feeling lighter. Pierre is a made-up person. But the knights and their money-keeping were real. Historians still study the records of how they did it.

Hugh of Payns Starts a Brotherhood

Main ideaAbout 1119 or 1120, a French knight named Hugh of Payns and a few friends promised to guard pilgrims.

Hugh of Payns was a knight from France. About 1119 or 1120, he and a few other knights made a plan in Jerusalem. They would guard pilgrims on the roads. They would also live like monks, with prayer and simple lives.

They called themselves the Poor Fellow-Soldiers of Christ. King Baldwin II of Jerusalem gave them rooms in part of his palace. The palace stood on the Temple Mount. People then believed King Solomon’s Temple had once stood there.

Because of that building, people called the knights the Templars. A is a member of this order. An is a group of people who live by the same religious rules. Their full name grew long: the Poor Fellow-Soldiers of Christ and of the Temple of Solomon.

9.3

Two Dates for One Beginning

Main ideaAn old writer said the Templars began in 1118, but most historians today think it was 1119 or 1120.

William of Tyre was an archbishop who lived in the Holy Land. In the 1170s and 1180s, he wrote a long history of the kingdom of Jerusalem. He told how the Templars began. He said it happened in 1118.

But William wrote about 50 years after the start. He was not there. Historians today compare his book with other old records. Most of them think the Templars began in 1119 or early 1120. The start is , but the exact year is .

William also said there were only nine knights for nine years. Other records show Hugh traveling in Europe to find new members by 1127. So many historians think the nine-knights story is too simple.

9.5

Three Promises and Quiet Meals

Main ideaTemplars promised to own nothing of their own, not to marry, and to obey their leaders.

When a man joined the Templars, he made three big promises. He would own nothing for himself. He would not marry. He would obey the leaders of the order. Promises like these are called vows. Each one is a .

The Rule filled each day with prayer. The brothers ate meals together in one hall. They were to eat quietly while someone read aloud. Their clothes were plain, with no fancy decorations.

Some rules were about knightly life. The Rule said how brothers should care for their horses. It also said the brothers must not go hunting for fun. The one animal they were allowed to hunt was the lion.

Gifts of Land

Main ideaPeople across Europe gave the Templars land and money, so the order grew rich and set up houses in many countries.

Many people in Europe admired the Templars. They wanted to help guard the pilgrims. So they gave gifts. Kings and nobles gave land, farms, mills and money. Some farmers gave a small field.

The Templars set up houses on this land. A Templar house that ran farms and land in one area is often called a . Soon there were Templar houses in France, England, Spain, Portugal, Italy and other lands.

Most brothers in Europe were not fighters. Many ran farms. They sold crops and wool. They sent money and horses east to the knights in the Holy Land. The farms in Europe fed the fight far away.

9.11

A Critic Speaks Up

Main ideaSome people, like William of Tyre, later said the Templars had grown too proud and too rich.

Not everyone praised the Templars. As the order grew rich, some people grew uneasy. One of them was William of Tyre, the archbishop who wrote the history we read before.

William said the Templars began humble and poor. But he wrote that later they became proud. He complained that they did not obey the bishops of the Holy Land. The pope had let them answer to him alone, not to local bishops. That made some church leaders angry.

A is a person who points out what he thinks is wrong. Bernard was a supporter. William was a critic. To understand the Templars, historians read both. Each one saw part of the story from his own place.

The story

The Parchment in the Wrong Place

In 2001, a historian in the Vatican finds a parchment that had been out of sight for hundreds of years.

It is September 2001, in Vatican City, inside Rome. Barbara Frale is a historian from Italy. She works in the Vatican's archive, a huge library of old papers kept by the popes. Shelves of boxes stretch down long, quiet halls.

Frale studies very old handwriting. She is looking for papers about the Knights Templar. She opens a box and unfolds a large, pale sheet of parchment. The ink is brown with age.

She reads the Latin slowly. The parchment is dated August 1308. It says three cardinals, sent by the pope, questioned the Templar leaders at a castle called Chinon. Frale's heart beats faster. This paper had been filed in the wrong place. An old list described it too vaguely for anyone to find it.

For about 700 years, almost no one had read it. Now it could help answer an old question. What did the pope really think about the Templars? In this chapter, you will see why this piece of parchment matters, and also what it cannot prove.

Three Cardinals at a Castle

Main ideaIn August 1308, three cardinals sent by Pope Clement V questioned Templar leaders at Chinon Castle and forgave them.

Pope Clement V was the head of the Catholic Church. He was French, and he lived in France, not Rome. King Philip pushed him hard to act against the Templars.

In August 1308, the pope sent three cardinals to Chinon Castle in France. A is a high leader in the Catholic Church. There they questioned Jacques de Molay and other Templar leaders.

The leaders asked to be forgiven. The cardinals gave them . That means they forgave them for their sins in the name of the Church. The cardinals let them take part in church services again. They wrote all of this on a large parchment.

From the source
What the Parchment Says
The three cardinals wrote that the Templar leaders had asked for mercy. After hearing them, the cardinals granted them absolution. They said the leaders could return to the sacraments of the Church.
The Chinon Parchment, 17-20 August 1308, Vatican Apostolic Archive (Archivum Arcis, Armarium D 217) (paraphrased)

The cardinals forgave the leaders. Does that mean the pope thought the whole order was innocent? What else would you want to know?

Found in 2001

Main ideaBarbara Frale found the Chinon Parchment in the Vatican's archive in 2001, after it had been misfiled for centuries.

An is a place that keeps old papers and records safe. The Vatican has one of the biggest archives in the world. It holds papers from many centuries.

The Chinon Parchment was in that archive all along. But it was in the wrong place. An old list described it too vaguely to find. So it was , and almost no one read it.

In September 2001, the historian Barbara Frale found it. She could read the old Latin handwriting. In 2007, the Vatican helped print an exact copy of the Templar trial papers. Only 799 numbered copies were made. One of them is in the Law Library of Congress, in Washington, D.C.

10.7

What the Parchment Proves

Main ideaThe parchment shows the leaders were forgiven in 1308, but it does not prove the pope declared the whole order innocent.

When news of the find came out, some headlines said the pope had found the Templars innocent. That went further than the parchment. The parchment says the cardinals forgave certain leaders. It did not end the trials.

Frale thinks Pope Clement wanted to fix the order, not destroy it. She thinks the king’s pressure was too strong for him. Many historians agree with parts of her view. Others are more careful. This is a question historians still .

A good reader asks two questions. What does the document really say? And what are people claiming it says? The distance between those answers is where mistakes can grow. Checking the yourself helps you see clearly.

10.8

The Pope Ends the Order

Main ideaIn 1312, Pope Clement V ended the Templar order without ever declaring it guilty, and most of its land went to the Hospitallers.

From 1311 to 1312, church leaders met at the Council of Vienne, in France. On 22 March 1312, Pope Clement V ended the Templar order. He chose to it. That means he shut it down for good.

But the pope did not say the order was guilty. He said he was ending it by his own power as pope, not by a court’s judgment. So the Templars were never found guilty as a whole group.

In May 1312, the pope gave most Templar lands to another order of knights, the . In Portugal, the king started a new group in 1319, with the pope’s approval. It was called the Order of Christ, and it took over the Templar lands there. Some brothers joined it.

From the source
The Pope's Decision
The pope wrote that he was ending the Order of the Temple. He said he did this by his power as pope, not by a final court sentence. He forbade anyone to join the order or to wear its clothes from then on.
Pope Clement V, Vox in excelso, Council of Vienne, 22 March 1312 (English in N. P. Tanner, ed., Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils, 1990) (paraphrased)

The pope ended the order but did not call it guilty. Why might he have chosen to do it this way?

10.9

The Last Grand Master

Main ideaJacques de Molay was put to death in Paris on 18 March 1314, and a legend of a curse grew up later.

Jacques de Molay was held in prison for about seven years. In March 1314, he spoke out in public. He said the order was holy and the charges were false. For this, he and another leader, Geoffroi de Charney, were put to death by fire. It happened on 18 March 1314, on a small island in Paris.

A writer from that time said Molay called on God to set things right. Later, the story grew bigger. People said Molay cursed the pope and the king. They said both would die within a year. This is a , a story told often that may not be true.

Here is a fact that helped the legend grow. Pope Clement died in April 1314. King Philip died in November 1314. Both died that year. But the full curse story was written down years later. The first writer does not record it.

10.10

Friday the 13th and Hidden Treasure

Main ideaStories about Templar treasure and bad-luck Friday the 13th are not backed by evidence from the time.

Many people say Friday the 13th is unlucky because of the Templar arrests. The arrest date is real. But written fears about Friday the 13th show up in the 1800s. A 1907 novel called Friday, the Thirteenth helped spread the idea. The Templar link was added even later.

Other stories say the Templars hid a great before the arrests. Some say ships sailed away with gold. Historians have looked hard through the trial records. They have found no solid proof of a treasure fleet.

No Templar treasure has ever been found. People have searched castles, churches and islands. A story can be exciting and still have no . Historians say what the evidence shows, and they name a legend as a legend.

10.11

Did They Secretly Survive?

Main ideaSome later groups borrowed the Templar name, but no record shows the medieval order secretly lived on.

Some people say the Templars did not really end. They say the knights hid. Later, they say, the knights became the Freemasons, a brotherhood that grew out of the lodges of stonemasons. But the first written link between Masons and crusading knights shows up in 1737. Claims about the Templars came in the 1750s. That is more than 400 years after 1312. No record from the Templars’ time links the two groups.

Later, some Masonic groups named themselves Knights Templar. In 1880, thousands of these American Knights Templar met in Chicago. They camped in tents by Lake Michigan and wore plumed hats. They even printed their own daily newspaper for the event.

These Chicago knights admired the old Templars. They were them. But they were not them. That means no line of members runs back to the medieval order. Knowing that difference is a big part of thinking like a historian.

Think it through
Same Name, Different Group

Your school has a team called the Knights. Their shirts have a shield and a sword. Five hundred years ago, real knights rode horses in Europe. Your school team took the name because they like knights.

Is your school team descended from real knights, or inspired by them? How is this like the Chicago Knights Templar of 1880?

Words to know
inspired by
getting ideas from someone or something you admire
descended from
coming in an unbroken line from an earlier person or group
