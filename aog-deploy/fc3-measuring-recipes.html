A cup is not one thing

A dry cup is filled to its own rim. A liquid cup is read at a line below the rim.

A cup is not one thing. How you fill it changes how much is in it. A scooped cup can hold up to a quarter more flour than a spooned one — which is why a recipe says spoon and level.

Four things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

A dry cup and a liquid cup hold the same amount, so either one will do.

They hold the same amount and they are read in different ways. A dry cup is filled past the top and struck flat, so it carries no lines. A liquid cup has lines that stop below the rim, so a full cup can be carried without spilling. You cannot strike a liquid flat, and a dry cup has no line to pour to.

One is struck flat. One is read at a line.

⚠️ People often think…

Pushing the cup down into the flour bag is the fast way to fill it.

The sides of the cup press the flour together and push the air out. A scooped cup can hold up to 25 percent more flour than a spooned one. In a four-cup recipe that is a whole extra cup, and the food comes out dry and heavy.

Scooping packs. Spoon it in, then level it.

⚠️ People often think…

tsp and Tbsp are two ways to write the same spoon.

tsp is a teaspoon and Tbsp is a tablespoon. It takes 3 teaspoons to fill 1 tablespoon. A tablespoon of salt where the recipe asked for a teaspoon is three times too much, and there is no way to take it back out.

The capital T is three times the small t.

⚠️ People often think…

A cup of flour weighs 8 ounces, because a cup is 8 fluid ounces.

A fluid ounce measures space. An ounce on a scale measures weight. One cup is 8 fluid ounces of space, but a cup of flour weighs only about 4 ounces, because flour is full of air between its grains.

fl oz is space. oz is weight.

How many teaspoons are in 1/4 cup?

  1. 3 teaspoons fill 1 tablespoon.
  2. 16 tablespoons fill 1 cup.
  3. So 1/4 cup is 16 ÷ 4 = 4 tablespoons.
  4. Trade each one for 3: 4 × 3 = 12.
  5. 1/4 cup is 12 teaspoons. ✓

Helpful Hints

🧭 The unit in one line

Read the whole recipe → set out mise en place → dry things go in a dry cup, spooned and leveled → liquid goes in the liquid cup, read at eye level → small amounts go in measuring spoons → know 3, 16 and 4 by heart → scale up or down with care → preheat, bake, serve the yield.

3 – 16 – 4: 3 tsp in a Tbsp, 16 Tbsp in a cup, 4 cups in a quart.

🔑 The one idea

A recipe is a set of instructions that only works if every cook measures the same way. The right tool, filled the right way, read the right way — that is what makes the same recipe taste the same twice.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • A dry cup and a liquid cup are not the same tool. A dry cup is filled to its own rim; a liquid cup has lines below the rim so you can carry it without spilling.
  • Do not scoop the cup into the flour bag. Scooping presses flour down and can pack in up to 25 percent more than the recipe wants.
  • Brown sugar is the exception: press it into the cup until it holds the shape of the cup when you turn it out.
  • A fluid ounce measures space; an ounce on a scale measures weight. One cup is 8 fluid ounces, but a cup of flour weighs only about 4 ounces.
  • Do not read a liquid from above. Set the cup on the counter and bend down so your eye is level with the line.
  • tsp is a teaspoon and Tbsp is a tablespoon. They are not the same: it takes 3 teaspoons to fill 1 tablespoon.
  • Never measure over the mixing bowl. One slip and the extra goes straight into the batter, where you cannot get it back.

🎯 How you will be asked

  • “Which cup would you use, and why?” — a dry ingredient or a liquid one.
  • A straight conversion: “How many teaspoons are in 1/4 cup?”
  • “Halve this recipe.” — and one of the amounts will not divide neatly.
  • “A cup of flour and a cup of water do not weigh the same. Explain.”
  • A printed recipe: “Point to the yield, the temperature and the first thing you would do.”

📚 Go to the sources

These open another website and need the internet. On MyPlate Kitchen, open any recipe and find its yield and serving size before you read a single step.

  • MyPlate Kitchen (USDA) — hundreds of free recipes, each with its yield, serving size and cost per serving
  • FoodData Central (USDA) — look up a food and see what one cup of it actually weighs in grams
  • NIST Office of Weights and Measures — the federal office that decides what a cup, a pound and a gallon legally mean
  • FoodSafety.gov — the safe internal temperature chart, for the day a recipe tells you to cook to a temperature instead of a time
