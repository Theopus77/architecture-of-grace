A cup measures space, not weight

Two cups exactly the same size. One is full of flour, one is full of sugar. They hold the same SPACE — and they do not weigh the same at all.

A cup is a measure of how much ROOM something takes up, and that is the single fact everything else in this room comes from. Because it measures room and not weight, how you fill it changes how much you get — so dry things are scraped flat with the back of a knife, and there is a whole separate tool for liquids with the line set below the rim so you can carry it. Read a liquid at eye level, because looking down at it makes the number lie to you. Nothing here is fussiness. Every one of these is somebody in the past discovering that a cake came out wrong and working out why.

Two things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

A cup is a cup. You can use the dry one for milk and the jug for flour.

They hold the same amount and they are built for different problems. A dry cup fills right to the rim, so you can scrape the top flat and know exactly where the measurement stops — but fill it with milk and you cannot carry it anywhere. A liquid jug has its line BELOW the rim, with room above, so a full measure can travel across a kitchen without going over the side — but you cannot scrape flour flat in it, so you end up guessing. Swap them and you have not broken a rule; you have just given yourself a job with no way of being accurate.

Dry fills to the rim so you can scrape it. Liquid has room above the line so you can carry it.

⚠️ People often think…

You read a recipe as you go — step one, do it, step two, do it.

You read the whole thing first, all the way to the end, before you touch anything. Recipes hide things in the middle. Step six says “add the melted butter” — melted when? Step eight says “pour into the greased tin” — nobody greased a tin. Step nine says “leave to chill for two hours” and it is already six o'clock. None of that is written at the top where you would see it, because a recipe is a list of actions and not a warning. Reading to the end takes ninety seconds and it is the cheapest thing in this entire subject.

Read to the end first. The surprises are always in the middle.

Measure something properly

1 Watch one

A recipe asks for one cup of flour.

  1. Flour is dry, so reach for the dry cup — the one that fills to the rim.
  2. Do not scoop with the cup. Pushing it into the bag packs the flour down and you get too much.
  3. Spoon the flour INTO the cup instead, loosely, until it is heaped over the top.
  4. Scrape the top flat with the back of a knife. The extra falls back in the bag.
  5. One cup of flour. ✓ Spoon it in, level it off — and it is the same amount every single time.

Space, not weight. So how you fill it is the measurement.

🔑 The one idea

There is one fact at the bottom of this room and everything else is a consequence of it: a cup measures how much ROOM something takes up. Weight would not care how you filled the cup — 200 grams is 200 grams whether you packed it or poured it. Space does care, and cares a lot: scooping flour instead of spooning it can add a quarter again. So levelling exists, and spooning exists, and two shapes of cup exist, and reading at eye level exists. None of them are fussiness. They are four answers to the same problem.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • A dry cup and a liquid jug hold the same amount and are NOT interchangeable. One is built to be levelled; the other is built to be carried.
  • Never scoop flour with the cup. Pushing it into the bag packs the flour and can add a quarter again. Spoon it in.
  • Level a dry measure with the BACK of a knife, not the edge — the edge digs in and pulls some out.
  • Level FIRST, then halve. Half of a heaped spoon is half of an amount nobody knows.
  • Read a liquid at eye level. Looking down into the jug makes it read high.
  • Brown sugar is the one dry thing you PACK. Everything else is spooned in loosely.
  • Read the recipe to the END first. Melting, greasing, chilling and preheating hide in the middle, never at the top.
