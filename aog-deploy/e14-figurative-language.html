Figurative Language

Last room a word carried a verdict. Here a word carries a whole other world. When someone calls a city a machine, they have asked you to import a set of properties — efficiency, parts, no feelings — and to leave the rest of the machine behind. Nobody votes on which properties come over; the figure decides silently, and that silent decision is where the argument lives. So this room teaches the eight figures you will be asked to name, and one method that works on all of them: name the two things, say what is carried across, then say what is quietly NOT carried across. Five tabs — cards for the figures and the method, hints with a naming table, a quiz that makes you say what a metaphor imported, and a workshop where you sort figures, take one apart in order, label an extended metaphor and say what a comparison smuggled in.

A comparison is a smuggling operation

“The city is a machine.” Two things that are not the same. Before asking what it means, ask what a machine actually is.

Every figure is a transfer: it takes two things that are not the same, carries some properties across and silently refuses to carry the rest. Naming the figure is the easy half. Say what crossed, say what did not, and propose the rival comparison — because what a figure refuses to carry is where the unexamined assumption is sitting.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

I named it — it is a metaphor. That is the answer.

Naming it scores almost nothing, because the question is nearly always about effect. What did the comparison make you accept that a plain sentence would have had to argue for? “The city is a machine” is a metaphor, yes — and it has just asserted that residents have no interior life worth consulting, with no sentence you can contradict.

Every figure question is an argument question wearing a costume.

⚠️ People often think…

Dead metaphors do not matter — nobody even hears them any more.

That is exactly what makes them the dangerous ones. A live metaphor announces itself and you can weigh it. A dead one has sunk into ordinary vocabulary, so it works with no resistance at all. A war on drugs stopped sounding like a comparison decades ago, and it still imports an enemy, a territory and a victory condition into a problem that has none of them.

The figures worth catching are the ones that stopped sounding like figures.

⚠️ People often think…

The object is described a lot, so it must be a symbol.

Described often is not the same as meaningful. A thing becomes a symbol when it starts carrying weight the plot does not need — it keeps appearing at the moments that matter and means more each time. The test is to delete it: if nothing else in the book stops working, it was scenery.

Delete the object. What else stops working? That is its real weight.

Two things, what crossed, what did not

1 Watch one

Take apart: “Her argument was a locked door.”

  1. Name the two things: her argument, and a locked door. A metaphor — no like or as, so the claim is made flat.
  2. What crossed: you cannot get through it, trying is pointless, and it was deliberately made that way.
  3. What did NOT cross: a locked door has a key, and somebody holds it. The figure declines to ask whether her argument has one.
  4. Rival comparison: “her argument was a wall” would drop the key entirely — no way through and never was one. Choosing door over wall left a door open, just barely.

Helpful Hints

⚡ The one idea

Every figure is a transfer. It takes two things that are not the same, carries some properties from one to the other, and silently refuses to carry the rest. “The city is a machine” brings over efficiency, moving parts and indifference — and leaves behind rust, an off switch and an owner. Naming the figure is the easy half of the question. Saying what crossed and what did not is the half that is actually being marked.

📐 Naming the eight

FigureWhat it doesExample
Metaphorsays one thing IS anotherThe city is a machine.
Similesays one thing is LIKE anotherThe city runs like a machine.
Personificationgives a thing human lifeThe city never sleeps.
Hyperboleoverstates on purposeI have said this a million times.
Understatementunderstates on purposeThe fire was a bit inconvenient.
Idioma fixed phrase you cannot take apartShe let the cat out of the bag.
Symbola real thing standing for an ideaThe locked gate, all through the novel.
Allusionborrows another story’s weightHe met his Waterloo.

🪜 Taking any figure apart

  • 1 · Name the two things. What is being compared to what?
  • 2 · Say what is carried across. Which properties came over?
  • 3 · Say what is NOT carried across. That is where the argument hides.
  • 4 · Ask what a different comparison would have claimed instead.

💀 Dead, extended, mixed

A dead metaphor has been used so often that nobody sees the picture any more — the leg of a table, the heart of the city. An extended metaphor keeps one comparison running for a paragraph or a whole book. A mixed metaphor puts two pictures together that cannot both be true at once — “we will bite that bridge when we come to it” — and the failure is a useful test: if you cannot draw it, the writer was not seeing it either.
