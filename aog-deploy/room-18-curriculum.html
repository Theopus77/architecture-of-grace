01

Crosswalk Matrix

Each key scene, matched to its lesson, its anchor chart and a clear neuro-affirming adjustment. Print on cardstock and keep it at the front of your teacher binder.

Ch. 1

Who Am I?

The cohort reconvenes for fourth grade. Mr. Patel introduces the Identity Web — five branches, drawn with the older kids’ more careful handwriting. Branch 5 is still optional.

Core SEL themeIdentity
Lesson alignmentU1·L1 — Who Am I? (Identity Web)
Anchor chart trigger

IDENTITY WEB (3-5) — name in center, five branches: Things I Love · Good At · Care About · My Special Thing · Something They Don’t Know (private).

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Pre-print the five branch labels so the student does not generate categories AND fill them simultaneously — two cognitive loads, not one. Branch 5 “private” is explicit permission to leave blank.

Ch. 4

My Inner Critic vs. My Inner Coach

Priya fills the Critic column in three minutes and stalls completely on the Coach column. She flips the paper over and writes “DEAR PRIYA, if Hannah said these things, I would say —”

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · The Critic Named
Lesson alignmentU1·L4 — Inner Critic vs. Inner Coach
Anchor chart trigger

INNER CRITIC vs. INNER COACH (3-5) — two columns of paired statements (“You are bad at this” ↔ “You are still learning”). Five paired rows.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Provide the Inner Coach scripts in advance — generating self-compassionate language on the fly is the wrong ask for many autistic brains. “Read these three Coach lines aloud when the Critic gets loud.” Internalization comes later, with repetition.

Ch. 5

The Toolkit

Mr. Patel introduces the Regulation & Repair Toolkit — concrete strategies the children build into desk-card kits. Box breathing, 5-4-3-2-1 grounding, the Kind Sentence to Self.

Core SEL themeSelf-Management · Regulation
Lesson alignmentRegulation & Repair — My Regulation Toolkit
Anchor chart trigger

MY REGULATION TOOLKIT (3-5) — four icons: breath (box 4-4-4-4) · senses (5-4-3-2-1) · body (push wall / squeeze hand) · words (one kind sentence).

Neuro-affirming adjustment

The toolkit must include a SENSORY tool, not just cognitive: weighted lap pad, fidget, noise-reducing earbuds, hood-up signal. Pre-rehearse the toolkit when calm — never the first time it is needed.

Ch. 6

Guilt vs. Shame

The cohort learns the distinction. Guilt = what I did. Shame = who I am. Guilt is functional; shame is corrosive. Marcus, beginning to remember the bike-and-Aaliyah incident more often, listens carefully.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · Guilt vs. Shame
Lesson alignmentU1·L5 — Guilt vs. Shame
Anchor chart trigger

GUILT vs. SHAME (3-5) — paired columns: “About what I did” / “About who I am” · “I made a bad choice” / “I am bad” · “Leads to repair” / “Leads to hiding.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Two desk cards. Yellow: “I made a mistake. That is GUILT. It tells me what to fix.” Red: “I AM BAD. That is SHAME. It is not true. I am the same kid. I made a mistake. That is all.” Color-coded literal sorting.

Ch. 7

I Am More Than My Worst Moment

The class makes a list together — “I am the kid who broke the vase AND the kid who babysits her cousin every Saturday. AND, not BUT.”

Core SEL themeIdentity · Wholeness After Failure
Lesson alignmentU1·L6 — I Am More Than My Worst Moment
Anchor chart trigger

I AM MORE THAN ___ (3-5) — sentence frame with three slots: “I am the kid who ___ AND the kid who ___ AND the kid who ___.” Practice in unison.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Provide pre-generated identity statements the child can match-and-claim, with a word bank of accurate options. Recognition is easier than generation for many autistic students.

Ch. 10

The Weight of Self-Criticism

PRIYA’S BREAKDOWN. The Bengal-tiger / jaguar group-project scene. Priya cries in Ms. Calloway’s office: “I am too much. I keep being too much.” Ms. Calloway: “That voice — is that you, or is that the Inner Critic?” Priya catches the Critic in the act for the first time.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · The Critic Caught Live
Lesson alignmentU2·L1–L2 — The Internal Critic in Real Time
Anchor chart trigger

THE CRITIC GETS LOUD WHEN… (3-5) — three triggers: when I made a mistake · when I am tired · when I am scared. “The Critic uses three words: ALWAYS, NEVER, TOO MUCH.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

The Critic-Catching Card: laminated, in the desk. Trigger words on one side (ALWAYS / NEVER / TOO MUCH / SHOULD HAVE). Hear one in your head → flip the card: “That is the Critic. Not me. I will name three things that are actually true.” Pattern-recognition over abstract introspection.

Ch. 11

Accountability Without Self-Punishment

Isabella’s garden-gnome scenario is referenced. The cohort learns the formula: OWN IT + UNDERSTAND IMPACT + REPAIR = DONE. Self-punishment after repair = STUCK.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · Accountability vs. Self-Punishment
Lesson alignmentU2·L3 — Three-Part Accountability Map
Anchor chart trigger

ACCOUNTABILITY vs. SELF-PUNISHMENT (3-5) — green box: Own it + Understand impact + Repair = DONE. Red box: Use mistake as evidence + Replay without repair = STUCK.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

The DONE marker is critical for students who loop on a mistake for weeks. Add a physical “DONE” stamp the child uses on the worksheet. Visual + tactile + verbal closure: “Once all three steps are done, say aloud: DONE. The script is the door.”

Ch. 13

Talking to Yourself Kindly

★ HIGH RISK

Priya, weeks after the breakdown, writes a Self-Compassion Letter: “DEAR PRIYA. If you were Hannah and you said these things to me, I would say…” She illustrates Hannah on the left, herself on the right, the same kind words in both bubbles.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · Friend Test
Lesson alignmentU2·L5 — The Friend Test
Anchor chart trigger

FRIEND TEST (3-5) — left: TO MY BEST FRIEND (kind, fair, honest, without punishment). Right: TO MYSELF (often harsh, sometimes cruel). “Would you say that to your best friend?”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Use a literal mirror, or place a photo of the student where the friend would be. “You are looking at a kid who deserves what you give Hannah. That kid is you.” Concrete proxy beats abstract self-talk.

Ch. 10 (rev.)

The Backpack Returns

Mr. Patel brings out a heavier version of the same blue backpack with the torn strap. The cohort, older now, can carry more stones — but the bending-over still happens. Marcus cannot put the Aaliyah stone down yet.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · The Heavy Backpack (3-5)
Lesson alignmentU2·L2 — The Weight We Carry (3-5)
Anchor chart trigger

THE BACKPACK (3-5) — stones labeled: things I did · things I said · things I am hiding · things others did to me. “All stones get heavier when carried in silence.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Provide a Stone Sorting Worksheet — three columns: MINE / NOT MINE / SHARED. Many students carry stones that belong to others without knowing they may hand them back. Sorting is justice work.

Ch. 15

When Self-Forgiveness Feels Impossible

★★ HIGHEST RISK

Marcus finally speaks about Aaliyah — a Saturday morning when he was eight and she was five and he said something cruel because he was angry. He has carried the stone for two years and does not know how to put it down.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · The Hardest Self-Forgiving
Lesson alignmentU2·L6 — I Am More Than My Worst Moment (Application)
Anchor chart trigger

SOME STONES TAKE LONGER (3-5) — a stone with a timeline: held · examined · understood · repaired · released. “Still carrying it is not failure. It is the work in progress.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Slow Release Protocol: (1) Name the stone on paper. (2) Identify repair already done. (3) Identify repair remaining. (4) If repair is complete, the stone may be put down even if the feeling has not caught up. The PROCEDURE supersedes the FEELING when feelings lag the right behavior.

Ch. 17

Your Hurt Is Real and It Matters

Maya, who lives with her grandmother and not her mother, brings up — in writing, not aloud — what it has been like to be the kid whose mom is somewhere else. “Adults say I should not feel bad about it. I feel bad about it. Both can be true.”

Core SEL themeForgiveness · Validating Hurt
Lesson alignmentU3·L1 — Your Hurt Is Real and It Matters
Anchor chart trigger

HURT IS REAL EVEN IF… (3-5) — “…they did not mean it · it was long ago · others have it worse · the adults minimized it.” None of these invalidate the hurt.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Many students have been told their distress is disproportionate when it is proportionate to their nervous system. Hurts from sensory invalidation, masking demands, and social exclusion ARE real hurts. Validate without qualification.

Ch. 19

Repair That Actually Works

The cohort learns the difference between performative apology and real repair. “I’m sorry IF I hurt you” vs. “I’m sorry FOR what I did. Here is what I will do differently.”

Core SEL themeForgiveness · Repair Protocol
Lesson alignmentU3·L2 — Repair Protocol
Anchor chart trigger

REPAIR PROTOCOL (3-5) — (1) I name what I did. (2) I name the impact on you. (3) I name what I will do differently. “IF” is not in this script. “BUT” is not in this script.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Distinguish REPAIR apology (after a specific action that caused specific impact) from APPEASEMENT apology (chronic self-blame for existing). Only the first is taught here. Disrupt the second — many autistic students have learned to apologize for being themselves.

Ch. 20

Forgiveness Is Not the Same as Trust

★★ HIGHEST RISK

The cohort sits with the distinction at the 3-5 level: forgiveness is INTERNAL; trust is EARNED. You can forgive and still set a limit. You can forgive and still not be best friends.

Core SEL themeForgiveness · vs. Reconciliation
Lesson alignmentU3·L3 — Forgiveness vs. Reconciliation
Anchor chart trigger

FORGIVENESS vs. RECONCILIATION (3-5) — two panels. Forgiveness: “What I do inside me.” Reconciliation: “What two people rebuild together.” Forgiveness requires nothing of the other; reconciliation requires BOTH.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

CRITICAL guardrail for students at elevated risk of repeated victimization: “Trust is a PATTERN over time, not a decision. Watch behavior. Consistent change over many weeks → trust may rebuild. No change → trust is not yours to give and not your fault to withhold.”

Ch. 23

Stories of Forgiveness in Practice

The cohort hears three real-world stories at age-appropriate distance: a man who forgave a drunk driver; a girl who forgave her best friend’s lie; a woman who never reconciled with the parent who hurt her — and was at peace anyway.

Core SEL themeForgiveness · The Three Shapes
Lesson alignmentU3·L4 — The Three Sides of Every Story
Anchor chart trigger

THREE SHAPES OF FORGIVENESS (3-5) — (1) Forgiveness + Reconciliation. (2) Forgiveness without Reconciliation. (3) Forgiveness from a safe distance. All three are valid.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Pre-script which shape fits which situation. Changed + apologized + safe → Shape 1 possible. Changed but rebuilding is not your job → Shape 2. NOT changed and unsafe → Shape 3, no obligation to engage. Decision tree, not vibe.

Ch. 25

What Does Grace Look Like?

Mr. Patel introduces grace not as a feeling but as a chosen action — generosity of spirit toward someone who did not earn it. The class begins the Grace Gap Field Assignment.

Core SEL themeGrace · Definition
Lesson alignmentU4·L1 — What Is Grace?
Anchor chart trigger

GRACE vs. DOORMAT (3-5) — GRACE: a choice · from strength · protects dignity · I decide what to give. DOORMAT: no choice · from fear · gives up dignity · others take.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Critical safety distinction. Many students have been trained to mistake doormat-behavior for kindness. Drill the difference: “If you cannot say no, it is not grace. It is fear with a smile.”

Ch. 26

What Does Mercy Look Like?

Mercy is named as the close cousin of grace — but distinct. Mercy is NOT giving someone what they deserve when what they deserve would harm them. “Mercy is the choice to spare. Grace is the choice to give.”

Core SEL themeGrace · Mercy distinct
Lesson alignmentU4·L2 — What Is Mercy?
Anchor chart trigger

GRACE vs. MERCY (3-5) — “GRACE GIVES what was not earned. MERCY WITHHOLDS what was deserved.” Two sides of the same coin of generosity.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Concrete examples paired. GRACE: “I gave Maya half my snack even though she did not ask.” MERCY: “Devon broke my pencil. I could have told the teacher. I chose not to.” Distinguish by direction: grace adds, mercy withholds.

Ch. 27

Boundaries from Strength

Ms. Calloway, in a guest visit, names the Limit. “A limit is what you will do — not what you demand of the other person. ‘If you yell at me, I will leave the room’ — that is a limit.”

Core SEL themeGrace · with Limits
Lesson alignmentU3·L7 — Limits (carried into U4)
Anchor chart trigger

A LIMIT IS (3-5) — “…a statement about what I will do, not a demand about what they must do. From self-respect, not retaliation. Spoken once, then enforced.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Pre-script three limits the student can rehearse before they are needed: “If someone yells, I will say ‘I am going to step away’ and leave.” “If someone touches my body without permission, I say ‘no’ and tell an adult.” “If a friend asks me to do something unsafe, I say ‘no thanks’ and walk away.” Limits in the body before limits in the mind.

Ch. 30

Small Acts (The Grace Gap Assignment)

The cohort takes the Grace Gap Field Assignment home. Each child commits to three small acts of grace over two weeks, keeps a journal, and debriefs without naming the recipients.

Core SEL themeGrace · Practice
Lesson alignmentU4·L4–L6 — Micro-Moment Menu / Grace Gap
Anchor chart trigger

MICRO-MOMENT MENU (3-5) — twelve small acts in a grid: “Smile at the kid eating alone. Thank the cafeteria worker. Hold the door. Compliment without expecting return. Forgive a small thing silently…”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Pre-print the twelve micro-moments. Let the student SELECT three to commit to — not generate their own. Three concrete behaviors with clear scripts beat one fuzzy “be more graceful.”

Ch. 34

Community Charter & Year-End

★ HIGH RISK

The cohort writes its Community Charter — what Room 18 became. “We had a Mistake Wall. We had stones. We learned what real apology sounds like. We learned the Critic does not get the last word.”

Core SEL themeAll Four · Integration
Lesson alignmentU4·L7 — Community Charter
Anchor chart trigger

ROOM 18 BUILT THIS (3-5) — one chart with all four pillars: IDENTITY · SELF-COMPASSION · FORGIVENESS · GRACE. Below each, the cohort writes what they learned in their own words.

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Provide a take-home Year-End Summary: same four pillars, same key vocabulary, same five anchor sentences. Predictable structure = retainable summary.

02

In-Margin Teacher Marker Guide

Print on labels or sticky notes and put each one where it says in your classroom copy of the novel. Each note has a Teacher Script and a Neuro-Affirming Prompt.

▸ Ch. 4 · When Priya fills the Critic column in three minutes and stalls on the Coach column.
Teacher script

“Priya can write the Critic’s words faster than she can write the Coach’s. That is true for most of us. The Critic has had more practice. The Coach is the muscle we are building.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

If the Coach column is hard to fill, that is information — that voice does not get enough practice. The fix is not effort. The fix is REPETITION. Write three Coach sentences. Read them aloud every morning for a week. The Coach gets louder by being said.

▸ Ch. 10 · The bathroom-floor moment when Priya cries with the stall door locked.
Teacher script

“Priya is not weak right now. Priya is the opposite of weak. She has carried something invisible for a long time, and her body is telling her she cannot carry it alone anymore. That is wisdom. That is her body asking for help.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Sometimes our bodies cry before our words can explain why. That is normal. That is not too much. The crying-on-the-floor is not failure. It is your nervous system saying: I am here. Please notice me. (Meltdowns and shutdowns are this same signal — a request, not a flaw.)

▸ Ch. 10 · When Ms. Calloway asks: “Is that voice you, or is that the Inner Critic?”
Teacher script

“This may be the most important sentence Ms. Calloway says all year. The question is not — am I right or wrong? The question is — WHO is talking? Once you know which voice it is, the conversation changes completely.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Three Critic-words to listen for: ALWAYS, NEVER, TOO MUCH. Hear any of those and stop: “Is that me, or is that the Critic?” The question itself slows the Critic down. The minute you notice her, she gets a little quieter. Pull out your Critic-Catching Card and flip it.

▸ Ch. 11 · The DONE stamp on the Three-Part Accountability Map.
Teacher script

“Look at the green box. Own it. Understand impact. Repair. Done. There is a word at the end of that formula and that word is DONE. Not ‘feel bad for another week.’ Not ‘replay it at bedtime.’ DONE.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

When you have named it, understood the impact, and made the repair, you have a right to say DONE. Some stamp it. Some say it three times. Some put the paper in a drawer and never look again. All okay. The replay-loop is not a higher form of accountability — it is the Critic stealing the credit you already earned.

▸ Ch. 13 · Priya writes “DEAR PRIYA. If you were Hannah…”
Teacher script

“Watch what Priya did. She used the Hannah she already loves as a translator. She knows how to be kind to Hannah. She does not yet know how to be kind to herself. So she goes through Hannah to get to herself. That is a tool you can use the rest of your life.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Pick someone you are good at being kind to — a little sister, a pet, a best friend. When you do not know how to talk to yourself, pretend you are talking to them. Then put your own name in it. That is not cheating. That is using the kindness you already have to reach the hardest place.

▸ Ch. 10 (rev.) · The blue backpack with the torn strap returns. Heavier.
Teacher script

“Same backpack. Same blue. Same torn strap. But fuller now. We are nine and ten. We carry more than we did at six. That is not a problem to solve — it is a fact to acknowledge. You are old enough to carry more. You are also old enough to put things down on purpose.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Take out the Stone Sorting Worksheet. Three columns: MINE. NOT MINE. SHARED. The MINE ones we work on with the Accountability Map. The NOT MINE ones we have the right to hand back. The SHARED ones we hold with somebody. You do not have to carry stones that belong to other people.

▸ Ch. 15 · Marcus finally speaks about Aaliyah.
Teacher script

“Marcus has been carrying this stone since he was eight. He is ten now. Two years of silent carrying. Today he set it down where someone could see it. That is the bravest sentence in this whole book.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Some stones are not for the whole class. They are for one trusted adult, one piece of paper, or one private journal. If you have a Marcus-stone, you have three options: (1) tell one safe adult, (2) write it and let an adult read it without you saying it aloud, (3) draw it. You do not have to use your voice to put down a stone.

▸ Ch. 20 · The Forgiveness vs. Reconciliation distinction.
Teacher script

“This is the single most important sentence in Unit 3. Read it twice. ‘Forgiveness is what I do inside me. Reconciliation is what two people rebuild together.’ Forgiveness is mine alone. Reconciliation requires both people.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Two paper plates, two markers. Plate one: FORGIVENESS — that is in MY heart. Plate two: RECONCILIATION — that lives in the air between two people. Set them apart. Some people you put on both plates. Some only plate one. That is not failing to forgive. That is wisdom about who is safe.

▸ Ch. 25 · Grace defined.
Teacher script

“Grace is the word in the title of this whole curriculum. Grace is the choice to give somebody something they did not earn. Generosity to the un-earning. That is the whole of it.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Grace is not being a doormat. Grace is a CHOICE. Doormat behavior is being unable to say no. If you cannot say no, you cannot give grace — you can only give appeasement. The first step to grace is the right to say no.

▸ Ch. 34 · The Community Charter.
Teacher script

“Look at what Room 18 made this year. Look at the four pillars. We did not finish them. We started them. We will keep building them in fifth grade, in middle school, in high school, and the rest of our lives. The room we built does not get torn down. It gets added onto.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Take home your Year-End Summary card. The four pillars. The five anchor sentences. The names of the people in this room. Next year you will still know all of these. The next room you walk into will be different. The room inside you is the same.

03

Grace in Practice

What grace LOOKS like in a 3-5 classroom

Grace is giving generosity to someone who did not earn it — quietly, without expecting anything back, including credit.

1

DEFEND AN ABSENT PEER

Observable

A child is present when others are talking about a kid who is not in the room. The child changes the subject, says something positive, or says “I don’t want to talk about her like that.”

Teacher cue

Name it later, privately: “I heard you change the subject when they were talking about Devon. He wasn’t there to defend himself. You did it for him. That was grace.”

Not this

Joining in and then feeling bad afterward is normal. Grace is the choice not to join, in the moment, when the social cost is real.

2

FORGIVE A SMALL WRONG WITHOUT BEING ASKED

Observable

A peer does something small that bothered the child — cut in line, didn’t return a pencil, said something mildly thoughtless. The child notices, decides not to confront it, and does not bring it up later.

Teacher cue

Name it privately later: “I noticed Devon cut in front of you in the lunch line. You did not say anything. That was a choice. That was grace.”

Not this

Suppressing a real grievance is NOT grace — that is being a doormat. Grace is letting go of the SMALL thing on purpose. The Three-Strike Rule still applies for repeated harm.

3

COVER A CLASSMATE’S SMALL MISTAKE

Observable

A child sees a peer make a small mistake (forgot homework, wrong group, mismatched supplies) and quietly helps — gives a pencil, slides the page number — without making a thing of it.

Teacher cue

Name it privately: “Sofia, I saw you slide your paper over to Hannah when she could not find hers. You did not say anything. You just did it. That was grace.”

Not this

Showing off how kind you are = performance, not grace. Grace is the help that LOOKS like nothing from the outside.

4

ASSUME A KIND REASON FIRST

Observable

When a friend does not say hi back or seems off, the child runs the three-maybes drill: “Maybe they did not hear. Maybe they had a bad morning. Maybe it is not about me.” They wait before deciding the friend was mean.

Teacher cue

Name it when you hear them say it: “You just said ‘maybe she did not see me.’ That is the Pause we have been talking about. That is grace.”

Not this

Overriding accurate threat detection is NOT grace. Three-Strike Rule: first time → charitable interpretation. Second → notice the pattern. Third → trust the pattern. Distance is wisdom, not bitterness.

5

SAY THE QUIET COMPLIMENT

Observable

A child notices something a peer did well and tells them — privately, without an audience. Not the obvious thing; the smaller, harder-to-notice thing. “You stuck with that math problem for a long time. That was hard.”

Teacher cue

If you overhear it, name it privately later: “That compliment you gave Theo at lunch — you said it just to him. No one else heard. That was grace.”

Not this

Loud public praise = social currency. Quiet private compliment = grace. The audience matters: audience-of-one is the grace version.

Five behaviors. Each one harder than it sounds at nine or ten. Each one is the curriculum becoming character. A 3-5 student who has practiced these five behaviors a hundred times across a year does not just know grace. They ARE grace. The construct has become the kid.
