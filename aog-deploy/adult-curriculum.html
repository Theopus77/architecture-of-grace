Session 1 · Phase I · Foundation

Arrival & The Group Compact

From the K–12 rooms. Mirrors the Classroom Compact that opens every K-12 year — the room is made safe before any content, on the same four agreements.

ObjectiveParticipants arrive into a held space, learn the structure of the program, co-author the Group Compact, and meet the four foundational distinctions in their introductory form. By the close of Session 1, every participant has spoken at least once and has a private sense of whether they are in the right room.
Time90 minutes
Key wordscompact · the four distinctions · the right to pass · containment
Before this sessionAll participants screened per Section 2.2–2.3. Workbook prepared. Anchor Concept Card 1 ("The Compact") posted.
MaterialsWorkbooks · pens · tissues · water · Anchor Card 1 posted · printed Group Compact template (Appendix B) · clipboard for facilitator notes · the Closing Exit script printed where the primary facilitator can see it
CareStandard care
Anchor idea · The Compact

The Compact is the agreement we make about how this room will work. It is not a list of rules. It is the relational contract that makes the rest of the work possible. Five clauses. Co-authored. Posted in the room for all twelve sessions. 1. What is said here, stays here. The exceptions are the legal exceptions — imminent harm, abuse, court order — named at the start. 2. You may pass at any time, at any prompt, without explanation. 3. We do not give advice unless asked. We listen. 4. We do not narrate other people's stories. We speak only to our own. 5. The work is between you and the page. The room witnesses; it does not extract.

  1. 01Arrival & Grounding8 min

    As participants enter, greet each by name if you know them; introduce yourself simply if you do not. Indicate where they may sit — any open chair. Do not direct seating beyond that. Wait until all expected participants are seated, then begin.

    Pause for ninety seconds. Time it. The temptation will be to cut it short. Do not.

    Primary facilitator gives a real one-word answer (e.g., "Tired," "Curious," "Cautious," "Steady"). Co-facilitator follows. Then around the circle. Some participants will pass. Honor it without comment. The check-in is data, not performance.

  2. 02Program Orientation5 min

  3. 03The Compact — Naming What This Room Is8 min

    Move physically to the wall where Anchor Card 1 is posted. The physical movement signals that the work is starting. Read the five clauses aloud, slowly, with eye contact around the room.

    Allow 60 seconds of silence if needed. Most groups will have one or two additions — common ones include "we don't fix each other" or "what's said in tears stays in the room."   4. 04The Four Foundational Distinctions12 min

    Write each on a flipchart or board as you say it. Leave them visible through the rest of Session 1.

    First. Identity is not behavior. A mistake is something I did. It is not who I am. The harm I caused is real, and what I did matters — and it does not exhaust who I am or determine what I am capable of. This is the bedrock of the program. We come back to it constantly.

    Second. Feelings are messengers. Every feeling carries information. Naming a feeling makes it workable. No feeling is bad — although what we do with a feeling can be helpful or unhelpful. Anger is a messenger. Shame is a messenger. Grief is a messenger. We learn to read what they are telling us before we decide what to do with what they are saying.

    Third. There is more than one voice inside. The voice that says "You'll never get this right" is not the only voice. The voice that says "That was the wrong choice and I can do better starting now" is also a voice. We get to choose which one we listen to. We don't get to silence either of them. We do get to choose which one we let lead.

    Fourth. You can put it down. Not forget it. Not pretend it didn't happen. Not minimize what was real. Choose, for today, not to carry it the same way you've been carrying it. We will spend most of Phase II naming what you are carrying. We will spend most of Phase III deciding what to do with each thing. Session 12 is the audit — what stays, what goes, what stays but in a different form.

  6. 06Practice — the Arrival Letter25 min
    The practice · The Arrival Letter

    Each participant has the workbook open to the Session 1 page. The prompt is at the top of the page. Twenty minutes of writing. Private. The letter is not collected, not read by anyone but the participant, and is sealed in the workbook envelope at the close of Session 1 to be reopened at Session 12 and not before. The prompt: "Write a letter to yourself — to the version of you who is sitting in this room right now, on Day One of this program. Tell that person what brought you here. Tell them what you are hoping for, even if you are afraid to hope. Tell them what you are afraid of. Tell them anything you want them to know on Day One that they may forget by Session 12. This letter is sealed today and reopened in twelve weeks. No one but you will read it. Tell the truth."

    At minute 20: "Two minutes left." At minute 22: "Time to wrap up."

    This sealing-and-postponing ritual is doing several things at once. It is making the writing safe (no one will read it). It is creating a temporal anchor (the participant has now made a small commitment to be here at S12). It is establishing the program's relationship to time — that we are doing slow work, that what is done today is not finished today, that the audit at the end matters because the writing at the start was real.

Session 2 · Phase I · Foundation

Identity Is Not Behavior

From the K–12 rooms. The K-12 Identity pillar — Mask vs. Mirror (6–8), Beyond the Brand (9–10), ‘Oops vs. I Am Bad’ (K–2) — taught here as the adult distinction identity ≠ behavior.

ObjectiveParticipants establish — operationally, not just theoretically — the distinction between identity and behavior. By the end of Session 2, every participant has named one piece of behavior they have been letting define them, and has practiced the language that separates the two without erasing accountability.
Time90 minutes
Key wordsidentity · behavior · the bedrock distinction · accountability without identity-collapse
Before this sessionSession 1 completed. Compact posted. Anchor Card 2 prepared.
MaterialsWorkbooks · pens · tissues · water · Anchor Cards 1–2 posted · scenario cards B-2A and B-2B printed · index cards for the I-Statement / B-Statement practice
CareStandard care
Anchor idea · Identity Is Not Behavior

The mistake is something I did. It is not who I am. What I did matters. The harm was real. The accountability is real. And — none of it exhausts who I am or determines what I am capable of. When identity collapses into behavior, two things go wrong at once: I cannot be honest about what I did (because honesty would mean I am a permanent monster), and I cannot change (because what is essential cannot be revised). The distinction is not a trick. It is the bedrock. Every other piece of work in this program rests on it.

  6. 06Practice — the I → B Translation25 min
    The practice · The I → B Translation

    Each participant writes privately. Two columns, drawn down a fresh page in the workbook. LEFT COLUMN: Three I-statements — three sentences that begin with 'I am — ' that name something you have been telling yourself about who you are. The harder ones. Not 'I am tired.' Closer to 'I am a failure as a parent.' 'I am someone who can't be trusted.' 'I am damaged goods.' Three of them. Five minutes. RIGHT COLUMN: For each I-statement, a B-statement. The behavior or experience underneath, named specifically, in its actual shape, without the identity collapse. 'I am a failure as a parent' becomes — for example — 'I missed my daughter's 14th and 15th birthdays. I have apologized twice. I have not changed the pattern.' Five minutes per translation; total practice is 20 minutes of writing. Underneath the right column, one more sentence: 'What the B-statement makes possible that the I-statement does not — is — '

    Scenario card · C-2A · MARCUS AND THE HOSPITAL VISIT

    THE SITUATION Marcus is 41. Eight years sober. Eight years ago, when he was using, he failed to visit his mother in the hospital during her last week. He has never been able to talk about this without saying, immediately, 'I'm a piece of shit.' His sponsor, his wife, his therapist have all gently pushed back. He cannot accept the pushback. What is it doing for him — and what is it preventing? 2. If you wrote the B-statement for Marcus's situation, what would it say? Be specific. 3. What does the B-statement make possible that the I-statement does not? 4. Marcus's resistance to the distinction is itself information. What might it be telling him?

    Scenario card · C-2B · LINDA AND THE DIAGNOSIS

    THE SITUATION Linda is 52. Six months ago she was diagnosed with bipolar II — a diagnosis that, in retrospect, accounts for fifteen years of intermittent destructive behavior in her marriage. Her husband has stayed. Linda has begun saying, repeatedly, 'I'm not a person, I'm a diagnosis.' She is starting to refuse her own preferences ('Whatever, that's just the bipolar talking') and to disclaim her own decisions ('I don't know if I want this or if it's my disorder'). Her husband says he misses her. DISCUSSION QUESTIONS 1. What is the I-statement Linda is making? Notice that this one is collapsing identity into a diagnosis rather than a behavior — but the structure is the same. 2. What is Linda's I-statement preventing her from doing? 3. What would a B-statement look like for Linda's experience? How does it differ from her current way of speaking about herself? 4. How is this similar to, and different from, Marcus's situation?

The Two Voices — Self-Talk Made Visible

From the K–12 rooms. The Inner Critic vs. Inner Coach work (K–2 ‘My Inside Voice’ → 6–8 The Internal Critic) becomes the adult Two Voices.

ObjectiveParticipants externalize their internal self-talk into two named voices, recognize that both voices speak in nearly every situation, and practice choosing which one they let lead. Anchor idea · The Two Voices

The voice that says "You'll never get this right." is not the only voice. The voice that says "That was the wrong choice and I can do better starting now." is also a voice. Both voices speak. Always. We do not get to silence either one. We do get to choose which one we listen to. Naming the voices makes them visible. Visible self-talk is workable self-talk.

Session 4 · Phase II · Interior

Feelings as Messengers

From the K–12 rooms. Emotional literacy from ‘My Feelings Are Friends’ (K–2) and ‘Emotions Are Information’ (3–5) — feelings as messengers, now body-anchored for trauma.

ObjectiveParticipants reframe emotions as carriers of information rather than as problems to be eliminated. By the end of Session 4, every participant has expanded their working emotional vocabulary, has practiced the move from sensation → naming → message, and has identified at least one feeling they have been treating as an enemy rather than as a messenger.

Before this sessionSessions 1–3 completed. End-of-Phase-I review with consulting clinician complete. Anchor Cards 1–3 posted.

CareStandard care
Anchor idea · Feelings as Messengers

Every feeling carries information. Naming a feeling makes it workable. No feeling is bad — although what we do with a feeling can be helpful or unhelpful. Anger is a messenger. Shame is a messenger. Grief is a messenger. Joy is a messenger. Even numbness is a messenger — it tells you something is being kept at a distance. The work is not to eliminate the feeling. It is to read what it is telling you, and then to choose what to do with the information.

  4. 04Direct Instruction — the Three-Step Move10 min

    Write on the board:

    STEP 1. SENSATION. Notice the body. Where in your body do you feel something? Is your chest tight? Are your shoulders up? Is your stomach hollow? Is your jaw set? The body knows before the mind names. Start with the sensation.

    STEP 2. NAMING. What is the feeling, named as specifically as you can? Not 'I'm upset.' 'Upset' is a placeholder for fifteen different feelings. Closer: 'I am irritated' or 'I am hurt' or 'I am embarrassed' or 'I am scared' or 'I am ashamed' or 'I am grieving.' If you cannot find the right word, look at the feeling list. The list is not exhaustive; it is a starting place.

    STEP 3. MESSAGE. What is the feeling telling you? Not what you should do about it — that comes later. What is it informing you about? 'I am irritated. The message is: a boundary I care about just got crossed.' 'I am scared. The message is: I am being asked to do something I don't yet know how to do.' 'I am ashamed. The message is: I am imagining how I look to someone whose opinion of me feels load-bearing.'

    The practice · Sensation → Naming → Message

    Pick one moment from the last 7 days when you noticed a strong feeling. It does not have to be a hard moment. A small one is fine. Write briefly: what was the situation? Two or three sentences. Anchor idea · Grief vs. Resentment

Grief says: "I wish things had been different." It is a response to loss. Resentment says: "I blame." It is a response to perceived culpability — someone (sometimes me) is responsible, and the responsibility has not been resolved. Both can be appropriate. Both can be painful. They require different tools. Grief is moved through with mourning, time, witness, and ritual. Resentment is moved through with the work of forgiveness — which is different from reconciliation, and different from forgetting. When we apply the wrong tool — when we try to mourn what is actually resentment, or try to forgive what is actually grief — the work does not move. The weight does not lift. The distinction matters operationally, every day.

Anchor idea · Inherited Shame

Some of what is in your backpack is not yours. It was placed in you — by a family, by a community, by a culture — before you were old enough to refuse it. Inherited shame is shame about who you are, what your people are, what your body is, what your history is — that you did not author and could not have authored.     1. Specific. You can answer the question 'did I do this today?' with a yes or a no. 'I will be a better mother' is not specific. 'I will spend twenty undistracted minutes with my daughter every weekday after school, with my phone in another room' is specific.

    2. Sustained. It is not a one-time act. It is a practice across time. The living amend has a duration — usually months or years — built into it. Without sustainment, it is just an unusually specific apology.

    3. Witnessed by yourself, not by the person you wronged. This is the hardest property. The living amend is not announced to the person you wronged. You do not call the person you hurt and say, 'I am making a living amend to you.' That is another apology, and it usually serves you more than them. The amend is silent and sustained. Over time, the people who know you will see it. The Compassionate Witness — your inward Witness — sees it daily.

    The practice · The Living Amend

    Open the workbook to the Living Amend worksheet. Look at your inventory. Identify one pattern in your life that has produced repeated apologies — to others, to yourself, or both. Not a single incident. A pattern. Write it briefly. Five minutes. Write the apology you have made (or that you have been making to yourself) about this pattern. Two or three sentences in the actual language. Three minutes. (This is to make the apology visible — to see what you have been doing instead of changing the pattern.) Now design the living amend. Three required pieces: (a) the specific behavioral change — what you will start or stop doing, in language specific enough that you can answer 'did I do it today?' (b) the duration — how long you commit to sustain it (suggest twelve months for a first living amend; never less than three). (c) the sustainability question — what will you do when this gets hard, when you are tired, when something in your life makes the commitment seem reasonable to abandon? Twelve minutes. Optional fourth piece: if the amend is to a specific person, write a brief paragraph about why this amend will be silent — why you are not announcing it to them. The discipline of silence is itself part of the practice. Five minutes if you have time.

Anchor idea · Forgiveness Is Not Reconciliation

Forgiveness is internal. It is the release of the verdict you have been holding against another person — or against yourself. Reconciliation is interpersonal. It is the restoration of a relationship. It requires conditions forgiveness does not — including the consent and capacity of the other person, and the safety of both parties. Forgiveness can happen without reconciliation. Reconciliation, to be real, requires more than forgiveness. Conflating the two has cost many people their safety, their integrity, or their forgiveness itself. The distinction is the difference between an inward gift you can give yourself and an outward arrangement that is not yours alone to make.

    The practice · The Two-Column Sort

Anchor idea · The Audit

The Audit is the explicit, structured decision about what stays in the backpack going forward. Three categories: WHAT YOU PUT DOWN — items you are choosing not to carry the way you have been carrying them. WHAT YOU CARRY DIFFERENTLY — items you will continue to carry but in a changed form, with the Witness's voice, in the company of the work you have done. WHAT YOU CHOOSE TO CARRY — items that are appropriately yours and that you carry consciously, going forward, with the tools you have built. The Audit does not solve anything. It names what you have decided. The decision is the work. The Audit is followed by the reopening of the Arrival Letter — the version of you on Day One, met by the version of you twelve weeks later.
