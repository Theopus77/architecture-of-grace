Reading to Learn

Informational Text

Devon stares at a two-page article about honeybees and wants to give up. Twenty minutes later he can say the whole thing in two sentences, and he did not copy one word. Priya reads two articles about the same tornado and sees that one is built like a clock and the other like a chain of arrows. A class opens a dusty yearbook and learns something true about their own school that nobody in the building knew.

This unit is about reading to learn: articles, science pages, history pages, charts, maps and websites. You will find the main idea and the details that hold it up. You will spot the five shapes a text can take and take notes that match. You will read a chart like a paragraph, tell a fact from an opinion, and tell a primary source from a secondary one.

By the end, you will be able to take a real question nobody can answer, find good sources, take honest notes, and write an answer with evidence. That is what scientists, reporters and historians do every day. It starts with one sentence in an article that all the others hold up.

The big question

How do you read a text you did not choose and come away knowing something true?

The steps
Step 1

Skim the title, headings and pictures and ask what you want to find out.

Step 2

Read a paragraph and find the sentence the others hold up: the main idea.

Step 3

Sort the details that support the idea from the sentences that are just extra.

Step 4

Read the captions, diagrams, maps and sidebars as part of the text.

Step 5

Spot the shape: sequence, description, compare, cause and effect, or problem and solution.

Step 6

Take notes in the shape of the text: a list, two columns, or boxes with arrows.

Step 7

Read a second text on the same topic and compare what each one keeps and leaves out.

Step 8

Ask a research question that is specific and that nobody in the room can answer yet.

Step 9

Find primary and secondary sources, judge which are reliable, and note where each fact came from.

Step 10

Write the answer in your own words, back it with evidence, and say what is still unknown.

The story

The Sentence That Holds the Hive Together

A class reads an article about honeybees, and one student finds the sentence that every other sentence leans on.

Mr. Bell hands out an article called "A City in a Box." It is about honeybees. Devon groans. It is two whole pages. There are words like colony and nectar. "Read the first paragraph," Mr. Bell says. "Then stop. Find the sentence that the other sentences hold up."

Devon reads. One sentence says a hive can hold many thousands of bees. Another says every bee has a job. Another says worker bees fly out to flowers and bring back nectar. Another says guard bees stand at the door. Devon frowns. Which one is the big one?

Then he sees it. The second sentence says, "A honeybee hive works like a busy city, and every bee has a job." The nectar sentence is a job. The guard sentence is a job. The thousands of bees are the people of the city. Every other sentence is an example of that one.

"That's the main idea," Devon says out loud. Mr. Bell nods. "The rest are details. They hold it up like legs hold up a table." Devon reads the second page. It has a heading, a picture of a waggle dance, and a caption. Now the two pages do not feel so long.

Before the bell, Mr. Bell asks the class to say the whole article in two sentences. Devon writes: "A hive is like a city where each bee has a job. Workers gather nectar, guards protect the door, and the queen lays the eggs." He did not copy one line. He got it.

Talk about itDevon found the main idea by asking which sentence the others held up. Where else could you use that question?
Section 1

Topic and Main Idea

14.1

The Topic Is Not the Point

Main ideaThe topic is what a text is about in a word or two; the main idea is what the text says about that topic.

Read this short paragraph. "Dogs need a walk every day. A walk keeps a dog’s legs strong. It lets the dog sniff and see new things. A dog that walks is calmer at home." What is it about? Dogs. That is the . The topic is just the subject, one or two words.

Now ask a harder question. What does the paragraph say about dogs? It says dogs need a walk every day. That is the . The main idea is a whole sentence. It is the point the writer wants you to walk away with.

Here is a quick test. If your answer is one word, you found the topic. If your answer is a sentence with a verb, you found the main idea. "Honeybees" is a topic. "A honeybee hive works like a busy city" is a main idea.

Think it through
Two Answers for One Paragraph

Picture a paragraph about the school lunchroom. It says the lunchroom is loud. It says four hundred kids eat there at once. It says the tables are close together. It says the tile floor bounces every sound. A student says the topic is "the lunchroom." Another says the main idea is "The lunchroom is loud because so many kids eat in a hard, crowded room."

Why is the second answer a main idea and the first answer only a topic?

Words to know
topic
the subject of a text, said in one or two words
main idea

Check yourself

1. What is a topic?

Why: The topic is just the subject, like dogs or honeybees. It is not a whole point.

2. Which one is a main idea, not a topic?

Why: A main idea is a whole sentence that makes a point. The other three are one- or two-word topics.

3. You answer with a single word. What did you most likely find?

Why: One word is a topic. A main idea needs a sentence with a verb.
14.2

Details Hold Up the Idea

Main ideaSupporting details are the facts, examples and reasons that prove or explain the main idea.

Think of a table. The top is the main idea. The legs are the . "A honeybee hive works like a busy city." That is the top. "Workers gather nectar. Guards stand at the door. The queen lays eggs." Those are the legs. Take the legs away and the top falls.

A detail can be a fact, an example, a number or a reason. In the dog paragraph, "a walk keeps a dog’s legs strong" is a reason. "It lets the dog sniff new things" is an example. Each one answers the question, "How do you know?"

Not every sentence is a detail. Some sentences are just extra. If a paragraph about bee jobs says, "Bees are yellow and black," that sentence does not hold up the idea. Good readers sort the legs from the extra.

From the source
One Idea, Three Legs
Chicago winters are hard on sidewalks. Water gets into tiny cracks and freezes. Ice takes up more room than water, so the cracks get wider. Salt trucks help drivers, but salt eats into the concrete too. By spring, many blocks have new holes.

detail

Check yourself

1. What do supporting details do?

Why: Details are the legs that hold up the main idea. They answer, "How do you know?"

2. In the sidewalk paragraph, which sentence is a detail?

Why: The freezing water is a fact that shows how winter hurts sidewalks. The first choice is the main idea itself.

3. A paragraph is about bee jobs. Which sentence is extra, not a detail?

Why: Color does not explain a bee's job. The other three sentences all hold up the idea about jobs.
14.3

The Main Idea of a Whole Article

Main ideaA whole article has one main idea, and each paragraph's main idea is a detail that supports it.

One paragraph has a main idea. A whole article has one too. Think of a bigger table. The article’s main idea is the top. Each paragraph’s main idea is a leg. The details in each paragraph are the small screws that hold each leg on.

Take the bee article. Paragraph one says every bee has a job. Paragraph two says the hive stays warm because bees shiver together. Paragraph three says bees dance to share where flowers are. Put them together: "Honeybees survive because they work as a team." That is the article’s main idea.

To find it, read the title. Read the first and last paragraphs closely. Then ask what all the paragraph ideas have in common. The answer is often stated near the start or the end. Sometimes it is not stated at all, and you must build it yourself.

Section 2

Saying It Short

14.4

A Summary in Your Own Words

Main ideaA summary tells the main idea and the biggest details in a few sentences, in your words, not the author's.

Devon said a two-page article in two sentences. That is a . A summary is short. It gives the main idea and the most important details. It leaves out the small stuff, the jokes and the repeats.

Here is the trap. Copying a sentence is not a summary. If you write the author’s words, you did not show that you understood them. Close the book. Say the idea out loud to a friend. Then write what you said. Those are your own words.

Try it with this. "Sea otters float on their backs. They hold hands while they sleep so they do not drift apart. They also wrap themselves in kelp, a kind of seaweed." Here is a summary. "Sea otters use holding hands and kelp to stay in place while they sleep." It is short. It is true. It is in your words.

Think it through
Copy or Summary?

Two students summarize the same paragraph about the moon. Ana writes: "The moon has no air, so there is no wind. The moon has no air, so footprints stay for a very long time." Luis writes: "Because the moon has no air or wind, marks on it last a very long time." The original paragraph had the exact sentence, "The moon has no air, so there is no wind."

Which student wrote a real summary? What did the other student do instead?

Words to know
summary
a short retelling of the main idea and biggest details in your own words
kelp
a large brown seaweed that grows in the ocean
Check yourself

1. What belongs in a summary?

Why: A summary is short and keeps only what matters most.

2. Why is copying a sentence not summarizing?

Why: Putting the idea in your own words proves you understood it. Copying skips that step.

3. Which is the best trick for using your own words?

Why: When you say it aloud without looking, the words that come out are yours.
14.5

What It Says and What It Hints

Main ideaA text says some things explicitly, right on the page, and implies other things that you must figure out from clues.

Read this. "Maya grabbed her umbrella and her boots before she left for school." Does the sentence say it is raining? No. It says umbrella and boots. But you know. The text implies rain. To imply is to hint without saying.

Now read this. "It was raining hard, so Maya grabbed her umbrella." This time the text says it. It is , right there in plain words. Good readers know the difference. When a teacher asks, "What does the text say?" she wants the explicit part. When she asks, "What can you tell?" she wants what it implies.

Check your hints against the words. If a text says a bee "shivers to make heat," you may infer bees can be cold. You cannot infer bees have fur. Nothing on the page points to that. A good inference always has a clue you can point to.

From the source
Find the Hint
The bus was late again. Jamal checked his watch three times. He shifted his backpack from one shoulder to the other. When the bus finally came, he ran the last few steps and dropped into a seat with a long breath.
written for this lesson

to figure out something the text does not say, using clues
Check yourself

1. A text says, "Maya grabbed her umbrella and boots." What is implied?

Why: Umbrella plus boots are clues for rain. The text hints it without saying it.

2. What does explicit mean?

Why: Explicit information is right there in the words, like "It was raining hard."

3. A text says bees shiver to make heat. Which inference is fair?

Why: Shivering for heat points to cold. Nothing on the page points to fur, fear or snow.
Section 3

Reading the Parts of a Page

14.6

Headings and Bold Words

Main ideaHeadings tell you what a section is about, and bold words point to terms the author wants you to learn.

Open a science book. The page is not one gray block. There is a big title. Under it are smaller titles, like "How Bees Find Flowers" or "Inside the Hive." Those are . A heading is a label for a chunk of text. It tells you the topic of that chunk before you read it.

Some words on the page are darker and thicker than the rest. They are in . Writers put a word in bold when it is a key term. Often the meaning is right there in the same sentence. "Bees carry , a yellow dust from flowers, on their legs." The bold word plus the words after it give you a free definition.

Use these parts before you read. Run your eyes down the headings. Now you know the map of the article. Then read the bold words. Now you know the vocabulary. You have not read a full paragraph yet, and you already know a lot.

Think it through
A Map Made of Headings

An article about the Chicago River has these headings: "A River That Ran Backward," "Why the City Changed It," "The River Today." A student has not read one paragraph. But she says, "I bet this is about how people changed the river's direction, and what it is like now."

How could she guess the article's shape before reading it? Which heading would you read first if you wanted to know why the change happened?

Words to know
heading
a short title above a section that tells what the section is about
bold
dark, thick letters that make a key word stand out
pollen
a fine yellow dust that flowers make; bees carry it from flower to flower
Check yourself

1. What does a heading do?

Why: A heading is a label for the chunk of text below it.

2. Why do writers put a word in bold?

Why: Bold marks a key term, and its meaning is often given in the same sentence.

3. You read the headings and the bold words before reading the article. What do you gain?

Why: Headings give the map; bold words give the vocabulary. Then the article is easier.
14.7

Captions, Diagrams, Maps and Sidebars

Main ideaPictures, diagrams, maps and sidebars carry facts that the paragraphs may not say, so read them as part of the text.

Under the picture of the waggle dance is one line of small print: "A worker bee dances in a figure eight to show where flowers are." That is a . Many readers skip captions. That is a mistake. The caption often has a fact that is nowhere else on the page.

A is a drawing with labels. A diagram of a bee might label the wings, the stinger and the pollen baskets on its legs. A map shows where. A map of Illinois might mark the towns with the most beekeepers. Read a map the way you read a sentence: look at the title, the labels and the key.

A is a box of text set off from the rest, often with its own color. It tells a small side story, like "How Honey Is Made." Sidebars are extra, but they are often the most interesting part. Read the picture, the caption, the diagram and the box. All of it is the text.

Section 4

Reading With a Plan

14.8

Skim First, Scan for Answers

Main ideaSkimming means reading fast to get the general idea; scanning means moving your eyes to find one specific thing.

You have two minutes and a four-page article. Do not start at word one. it. Read the title, the headings, the first sentence of each paragraph, and the last paragraph. Your eyes move fast. You are looking for the big shape, not the small facts. In two minutes you know what the article is about.

Now the teacher asks, "How many eggs can a queen bee lay in a day?" You do not skim for that. You . Scanning is hunting for one thing. Your eyes jump down the page looking for a number, or the word "eggs," or the word "queen." You ignore everything else until you land on it.

Skim to learn the shape. Scan to find a fact. Read closely to understand. Good readers use all three, and they choose on purpose. If someone says, "Just find the date," scan. If someone says, "Tell me what this is about," skim, then read.

For the first job she reads the title, the headings and the first sentence of each paragraph. For the second she runs her finger down the page looking for a month name.

2. The teacher asks how many eggs a queen lays in a day. What should you do?

14.9

Ask Before, During and After

Main ideaGood readers ask questions before they read, while they read and after they finish, and then check whether the text answered them.

Before Devon read the bee article, he looked at the title and asked, "Why is a hive like a city?" That question gave him a reason to read. It is a before question. Look at the title and the pictures. Ask what you already know. Ask what you want to find out.

While reading, keep asking. "Wait, what does colony mean?" "Why do the bees shiver?" "Does this fit with what the first paragraph said?" These during questions catch you when you drift. If you cannot answer a during question, stop and reread. That is not slow reading. That is smart reading.

After the last line, ask again. "Did I find out why a hive is like a city?" "What was the main idea?" "What surprised me?" If your before question never got answered, that is worth knowing too. It might be your next research question.

How a Text Is Built

Text Structure
Big questionHow does knowing the shape of a text help you understand and remember it?
The story

Two Articles, One Tornado

One article tells what happened minute by minute. The other tells why the sky turned. Same storm, two shapes.

A tornado touched down west of a small Illinois town on a June afternoon. The next week, Ms. Okafor's class got two articles about it. "Read both," she said. "Then tell me how they are different." Priya read the first one. It felt like a movie.

The first article began at 3:10 p.m., when the sky went green. At 3:25 the sirens went off. At 3:40 the funnel touched a cornfield. At 3:52 it lifted. The article walked through the storm one minute at a time. Priya could almost hear the clock ticking.

The second article did not have a single clock time in it. It started with warm, wet air from the south. It said the warm air met cool, dry air from the north. The warm air rose, the storm began to spin, and the spin tightened into a funnel. Every sentence answered the question why.

"The first one is a sequence," Priya said. "First, next, then. The second one is cause and effect. This happens, so that happens." Ms. Okafor wrote both words on the board. "Two writers. Same tornado. Two shapes," she said.

Then she asked the class to take notes on each. For the first article, Priya drew a line with times on it. For the second, she drew arrows: warm air meets cold air, arrow, air rises, arrow, spin, arrow, funnel. Her notes looked like the articles. That was the whole point.

Talk about itPriya's notes for each article had a different shape. Why would a line work for one and arrows for the other?
Section 1

Five Shapes of a Text

15.1

Sequence and Description

Main ideaA sequence text tells events or steps in order, and a description text tells what something is like, feature by feature.

Read this. "First, the sky turned green. Next, the sirens sounded. Then the funnel touched down. Finally, it lifted." Each event comes after the one before. This is a text. Recipes, directions, and the story of a day are all sequences. Order is the whole point.

Now read this. "A tornado is a spinning column of air. It reaches from a storm cloud to the ground. It can be thin as a rope or wider than a town. Most last only a few minutes." Nothing happens in order here. Each sentence adds a feature. This is a text. It tells what something is like.

How do you tell them apart? Ask, "Could I shuffle these sentences?" In a sequence, no. Mixing the order breaks it. In a description, mostly yes. The tornado is still a spinning column even if you read the width sentence first. Every text you read has a shape. Sequence and description are the first two.

From the source
Shuffle Test
Lake Michigan is the only Great Lake that lies entirely inside the United States. Its water is cold even in July. Its beaches in Chicago have soft sand. On a clear day the water looks blue-green near the shore.
written for this lesson

Words to know
sequence
a text shape that tells events or steps in time order
description
a text shape that tells what something is like, one feature at a time

Check yourself

1. Which shape puts events in time order?

Why: Sequence means first, next, then. Order is the point.

2. "A tornado can be thin as a rope or wider than a town." What kind of sentence is this?

Why: It tells what a tornado is like, one feature. That is description.

3. What does the shuffle test show?

Why: Mixing steps breaks a sequence. Mixing features usually leaves a description fine.
15.2

Compare and Contrast

Main ideaA compare-and-contrast text shows how two things are alike and how they are different.

"A tornado and a hurricane both spin. Both can flatten houses. But a hurricane is hundreds of miles wide and lasts for days. A tornado is usually less than a mile wide and lasts minutes." This paragraph holds two things side by side. That is .

To is to show what is the same. To is to show what is different. Watch the words. "Both" and "also" and "alike" signal same. "But," "however," "while" and "unlike" signal different. Writers use these words like turn signals.

Try it on two things you know: a bus and a train. Both carry many people. Both have a driver. But a train runs on rails and a bus runs on streets. A train cannot turn to avoid traffic. When you can say both and but, you are thinking in this shape.

15.3

Cause and Effect

Main ideaA cause-and-effect text explains why something happens: this cause leads to that effect.

The second tornado article answered one question over and over: why? "Warm, wet air pushed north. Cool, dry air pushed south. Where they met, the warm air rose fast. Because the winds blew at different speeds, the rising air began to spin." Each sentence is a pushing an .

A cause is the reason. An effect is the result. Watch for "because," "so," "since," "as a result" and "led to." Also watch for chains. Warm air rising causes spin. Spin causes a funnel. The funnel causes damage. One effect becomes the next cause.

Here is a trap. Two things happening close together does not mean one caused the other. The sirens sounded, and then the funnel touched down. The sirens did not cause the tornado. Ask, "Did the first thing make the second thing happen?" If not, it is a sequence, not a cause.

Think it through
Is It a Cause?

Read two pairs. Pair one: "The pond froze, so the ducks left." Pair two: "The bell rang, and then it started to snow." In the first pair, the freezing pond took away the ducks' food and swimming water. In the second, the bell and the snow just happened close in time.

Which pair is cause and effect? Which is only a sequence? How do you know?

Words to know
cause
the reason something happens
effect
the result; what happens because of a cause
chain
a line of causes and effects where each effect starts the next cause
Check yourself

1. In "the warm air rose, so the storm began to spin," what is the effect?

Why: The spinning is the result. The rising warm air is the cause.

2. What is a chain of causes?

Why: Rising air causes spin, spin causes a funnel, the funnel causes damage. Each effect leads to the next.

3. The sirens sounded, then the funnel touched down. Did the sirens cause the funnel?

Why: Close in time is not the same as cause. The sirens did not make the tornado happen.
15.4

Problem and Solution

Main ideaA problem-and-solution text names something wrong and then tells how people fixed it or could fix it.

"Tornado sirens are loud, but people inside cannot always hear them. So the town added phone alerts. Now a warning buzzes in every pocket." First a . Then a . This shape shows up in science, history and the news all the time.

Watch for "problem," "trouble," "issue" and "the challenge was." Then watch for "solved," "fixed," "one answer" and "so they." Sometimes a text gives more than one solution. Sometimes it gives a solution that failed and then a better one. Keep track of which is which.

This shape is close to cause and effect, and students mix them up. Ask, "Is the writer telling me what went wrong and how to fix it?" That is problem and solution. "Is the writer telling me why something happens?" That is cause and effect. A problem is a kind of cause, but only one that someone tries to fix.

From the source

After the storm, the town had one shelter, and it was two miles from the school. Kids could never reach it in time. The school board voted to build a storm room inside the school itself. It has thick concrete walls and no windows. Now every student is thirty seconds from safety.

1. "People could not hear the sirens, so the town added phone alerts." What shape is this?

Why: Something was wrong (no one heard), and the town fixed it (phone alerts).

2. In the shelter story, what is the solution?

3. How is problem and solution different from cause and effect?

Section 2

Signal Words and Notes

15.5

Words That Give Away the Shape

Main ideaSignal words like first, both, because and solved tell you which structure a text is using before you finish it.

Read just the first few words of a paragraph: "First, crack two eggs." You already know it is a sequence. "Unlike a frog, a toad..." Compare and contrast. "Because the river flooded..." Cause and effect. "The trouble with old bridges is..." Problem and solution. These are . They give away the shape early.

Learn a few for each shape. Sequence: first, next, then, after, finally, dates and clock times. Description: for example, such as, looks like, is made of. Compare and contrast: both, also, alike, but, however, unlike. Cause and effect: because, so, since, as a result. Problem and solution: problem, trouble, so they, solved, one answer.

Signal words are clues, not proof. A description might use "first" just to start a list of features. So read a little more and check. If the sentences must stay in order, it is a sequence. If they explain why, it is cause and effect. The signal word points; the sentences tell.

15.6

Notes That Match the Shape

Main ideaTake notes in the shape of the text: a numbered list for sequence, a two-column chart for compare and contrast, arrows for cause and effect.

Priya drew a line with times for the first article. She drew arrows for the second. Her notes matched the shape of each text. That is not a trick. It is the best way to remember. When your notes look like the text, your brain stores the ideas the way the writer built them.

Here is the plan. Sequence: a numbered list or a timeline. Description: a web, with the topic in the middle and features around it. Compare and contrast: two columns, or two circles that overlap. Cause and effect: boxes with arrows. Problem and solution: one box for the problem, one for each fix.

Try it with the shelter paragraph. Problem box: "Shelter two miles away; kids cannot reach it." Solution box: "Storm room inside school, concrete walls." Two boxes, ten words. You could tell the whole story from that. Notes that match the shape are short, and they stick.

Two Texts, One Topic

15.7

Comparing Two Texts

Main ideaWhen two texts cover the same topic, compare what each one includes, what each leaves out, and how each is built.

The two tornado articles covered the same storm. But they did not say the same things. The first had clock times and no reasons. The second had reasons and no clock times. Reading both, Priya knew more than she could learn from either one alone. That is why teachers give you two texts.

To compare two texts, ask three questions. What facts are in both? What facts are only in one? How is each text built? Make two columns. Put shared facts in the middle. Put the facts only in the first text on the left, and only in the second on the right.

Then ask why. Why did the first writer leave out the causes? Maybe she wanted readers to feel the minutes tick. Why did the second writer skip the clock? Maybe he wanted to teach the science. Each writer chose. Seeing the choice is the deepest kind of reading.

15.8

The Author's Point and Reasons

Main ideaAn author often makes a point and gives reasons for it; your job is to find the point and check whether the reasons really hold it up.

Some texts do more than tell. They argue. "Every school in Illinois should have a storm room. Tornadoes hit our state every year. Kids cannot reach a shelter two miles away. A storm room costs less than one school bus." The first sentence is the author’s . The next three are the .

Find the point first. It is usually a sentence with "should," "must," "best" or "most important." Then find each reason. Ask, "Does this reason really hold up the point?" "Tornadoes hit every year" holds it up. "Storm rooms are painted gray" would not. A point with weak reasons is a weak point.

Then count. One reason is thin. Three good reasons with a fact for each is strong. Also notice what the author does not say. The storm-room author never mentions who pays. A careful reader sees the point, checks the reasons, and notices the hole.

Section 4

Science, History and Time

15.9

Reading Science and History

Main ideaA science text is usually built on causes, steps and diagrams, while a history text is built on dates, people and reasons, so read each with a different set of questions.

A science text about tornadoes asks, "How does this work?" It is full of causes, steps and diagrams. Read it slowly. Check every diagram against the words. Ask, "Could I explain this to a friend with my hands?" If the text says warm air rises, picture it rising.

A history text about a tornado asks, "What happened to people, and why did it matter?" It has dates, names and places. It tells what people did before and after. Read it asking, "Who is telling this? What did they see? What changed because of it?"

One real storm, the Tri-State Tornado of March 18, 1925, crossed Missouri, Illinois and Indiana and was one of the deadliest in United States history. A science text about it would explain the air and the spin. A history text would tell about the towns and the people who rebuilt. Same storm, different questions, different reading.

15.10

Timelines and Steps

Main ideaTimelines and numbered steps are sequences drawn out, so read them left to right or top to bottom and check the spacing between events.

A timeline is a sequence you can see. Events sit on a line, earliest on the left, latest on the right. Priya’s tornado timeline had 3:10, 3:25, 3:40 and 3:52. The spaces between the marks matter. The gap from 3:10 to 3:25 is fifteen minutes. From 3:40 to 3:52 is twelve. You can read time with your eyes.

Numbered are a sequence too. "1. Go to the lowest floor. 2. Get away from windows. 3. Cover your head." Read them in order. Do not skip. Step three makes no sense if you have not done step one. When steps have pictures, match each picture to its number before you move on.

Both tools can hide gaps. A timeline of a town might jump from 1925 to 1950. Ask what happened in between. A recipe might say "bake" without saying for how long. Good readers notice what a timeline or a list of steps leaves out, and they ask.

The Yearbook in the Closet

A class wants to know when their school got its name. The answer is not online. It is in a dusty box.

Ms. Reyes's class had a question nobody could answer. "Why is our school called Harper Elementary?" Nate said it was named after a basketball player. Zoe said it was a mayor. The principal shrugged. "It was before my time." So the class decided to find out.

Then the librarian remembered a box in the closet. Inside were old yearbooks. The oldest was from 1959. On the first page was a photo of a woman in glasses and a note: "Our school is named for Dr. Alma Harper, who taught in this town for forty years." It was signed by the first principal.

The class made a chart of every source they had used and how much they trusted each one. The yearbook went at the top. The website with the wrong school went at the bottom. Nate was a little sad it was not a basketball player. But now he knew something true that no one in the building had known.

Numbers You Can Read

16.1

Reading a Chart or Graph

Main ideaA chart or graph is a text made of shapes and numbers, so read its title, its labels and its key before you read its bars or lines.

A bar about Harper Elementary shows five bars. The title says "Students at Harper by Year." The bottom says the years: 1960, 1980, 2000, 2020. The side says "number of students." The tallest bar is 1980. Without reading a sentence, you know the school was biggest in 1980.

Read a chart in this order. Title first: what is this about? Then the on the bottom and the side: what does each direction mean? Then the , if there is one: what does each color stand for? Only then look at the bars or the line. Readers who skip to the bars often read the chart backward.

A line graph works the same way, but it shows change over time. A line going up means more. A line going down means less. A flat line means no change. Ask the same question you ask of a paragraph: "What is the main idea of this graph?" For the Harper chart, it is "The school grew, then shrank."

Where Facts Come From

16.3

Primary and Secondary Sources

Main ideaA primary source was made at the time by someone who was there; a secondary source was made later by someone who studied it.

The 1959 yearbook was made in 1959, by people who were in the building. It is a . A letter, a photo, a diary, a map from that year, a speech, a report card: all primary. They are the closest you can get to being there.

A website written last year about the school’s history is a . So is an encyclopedia article or a textbook. The writer was not there. She read primary sources, or other secondary sources, and then wrote her own words. Secondary sources are useful. They gather a lot in one place. But they can pass along mistakes.

Here is the test. Ask, "Was this made at the time, by someone who saw it?" If yes, primary. If it was made later, by someone looking back, secondary. Both kinds have a place. Start with secondary sources to learn the big picture. Then check the facts against a primary source when you can.

16.4

Fact or Opinion

Main ideaA fact can be checked and shown true or false; an opinion is what someone thinks or feels, and it cannot be proved.

"Harper Elementary opened in 1959." You can check that. You can find the yearbook and see. It is a . A fact can be true or false, but either way it can be checked. "Harper is the best school in town." Who says? How would you check it? That is an .

Opinions often carry words like best, worst, should, beautiful, boring, and I think. Facts often carry numbers, dates and names. But be careful. "The school has the best test scores in the district" sounds like an opinion because of "best." It is really a fact, because scores can be checked.

Opinions are not bad. Writers are allowed to have them. The trouble comes when an opinion dresses up as a fact. "Everyone knows the old gym was better." Everyone? Ask, "Could I check this?" If the answer is no, you are holding an opinion, no matter how sure the writer sounds.

Doing Your Own Research

16.5

Asking a Research Question

Main ideaA good research question is specific, can be answered with sources, and is something you do not already know.

Ms. Reyes’s class started with a question: "Why is our school called Harper?" That is a good . It is specific. It can be answered by looking at sources. And nobody in the room knew the answer. If they had already known, there would be nothing to research.

Bad research questions come in two sizes. Too big: "What is the history of schools?" You could spend a lifetime on that. Too small: "What color is our school door?" Just go look. A good question sits in the middle. "When did our school get its gym, and why?" You could answer that in a week with a few sources.

Shape your question before you search. Start with a topic: our school. Ask what you wonder: who is it named for? Add a word that makes it specific: why. Then check: can a source answer this? Ms. Reyes says the best questions make you a little nervous, because you truly do not know.

Trusting a Source

16.8

A Solid Website or a Shaky One

Main ideaTrust a website more when you can tell who made it, when it was made, and where its facts came from; trust it less when it hides those things or makes wild claims.

The class found three websites. One was the town’s own site, with the town seal, a date at the bottom, and a phone number. One was a page with no author, no date, and ads flashing everywhere. One had a photo of a different Harper school in another state. Which one would you trust?

Ask three questions. Who made this? A school, a library, a museum, a newspaper or a government office usually has people checking facts. A page with no name has no one to ask. When was it made? A page from ten years ago may be out of date. Where did the facts come from? A solid site tells you. A shaky one just says "trust me."

Watch for warning signs. Spelling mistakes in the title. Claims that sound too big: "the most famous school in America!" Pictures that do not match the words. Also check the site against another source. If the town site and the yearbook agree, you are on solid ground. If a lone site says something no one else says, be careful.

0 / 15

1. "Honeybees" is a topic. Which of these is a main idea?

Why: A main idea is a whole sentence that makes a point about the topic.

2. What do supporting details do for a main idea?

Why: Details are facts, examples and reasons that prove the main idea.

3. Which is a real summary of a paragraph?

Why: A summary is short and in your own words.

4. "Maya grabbed her umbrella and boots." The rain is...

Why: The text never says rain, but the clues point to it.

5. Where is a key term's meaning often found on a page?

Why: Writers bold a key term and often define it in the same sentence.

6. You need one date from a long article. Which way of reading is fastest?

Why: Scanning hunts for one specific thing like a number.

7. "First the sky turned green. Next the sirens sounded. Then the funnel touched down." What shape is this?

Why: First, next, then: events in time order.

8. "Warm air rose, so the storm began to spin." Which is the effect?

Why: The spinning is the result of the rising air.

9. Which note shape fits a compare-and-contrast text?

Why: Two columns put two things side by side.

10. The sirens sounded and then the funnel touched down. Did the sirens cause the funnel?

Why: Two events close together are not always cause and effect.

11. Which is a primary source about a school's opening day in 1959?

Why: The letter was made at the time by someone who was there.

12. "The playground has three slides." Fact or opinion?

Why: It can be checked by counting, so it is a fact.

13. Which research question is the right size?

Why: It is specific, sources can answer it, and you cannot just look.

14. You want to keep a source's exact words in your notes. What do you do?

Why: Quotation marks are a promise that the words are not yours.

15. Which website is most reliable for a school-history question?

Why: You can tell who made it and when, and you can check it.
