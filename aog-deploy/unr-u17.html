Heiser's Legacy, and Capstone

Capstone

This last unit looks back and then looks forward. First it looks at Michael Heiser's legacy: where he worked, how he taught, and how his ideas about the divine council, the Watchers and the gods of the nations spread through books, a long-running podcast and popular teaching. It also looks at what happened when other people used his name, sometimes carefully and sometimes for claims he rejected, such as ancient astronauts or giants building the pyramids.

Then the unit turns to you. In the capstone you choose one hard question from this course, gather the primary texts from the Hebrew Bible, 1 Enoch and the New Testament, and state Heiser's reading beside at least one other. You weigh the evidence and write a fair argument. You cite every text by book, chapter, verse and translation, and you present it calmly to classmates who may believe differently.

By the end you will be able to check a claim against the text and against a scholar's own words, and to explain a disputed passage fairly. You will also be able to disagree with respect. These skills reach well beyond this course. They help with any text, any scholar and any viral video.

The big question

How do we test claims about ancient texts, and argue fairly about them with readers who see them differently?

The story

The Giants Video

A viral clip says the Bible proves giants built the pyramids, so Lena decides to check it.

Lena was scrolling on the bus home from school in Joliet when a video stopped her. A deep voice said the Bible proves that giants built the pyramids of Egypt. Loud music played over pictures of huge stone blocks. The caption said a Bible scholar named Michael Heiser had uncovered the secret. The video had more than a million views. Lena had read part of Heiser's book The Unseen Realm for a class, and the claim did not sound like what she remembered.

At home she opened her notebook. She wrote the claim at the top of a page in two parts. First, the Bible says giants built the pyramids. Second, Heiser said so. Then she drew two columns: what the text says, and what Heiser wrote. Her teacher had told the class that a claim about a book has to be checked against the book itself, not against a clip about the book.

She started with Genesis 6:4, the verse the video flashed on screen. She read it in the King James Version and in a modern translation. The verse speaks of the Nephilim and of mighty men of old. It says nothing about Egypt, stone or building. She searched all of Genesis for a pyramid and found none. The Bible's giant stories, she noticed, are set mostly in Canaan, not along the Nile.

Next she checked Heiser. In The Unseen Realm he treated the Nephilim as part of a story about rebellion in the unseen world, not as builders of monuments. She also learned that he spent years answering ancient-astronaut writers such as Zecharia Sitchin. He argued that they misread the ancient texts. The video had borrowed his name for the kind of idea he rejected.

Lena wrote one line at the bottom of her page: check the text, then check the scholar. She sent the page to her class group chat with the verse, the book title and the year. Nobody had to agree with Heiser to see the problem. The video had simply said things that the sources did not say.

A Scholar's Working Life

33.1

Scholar-in-Residence at Logos

Main ideaHeiser did much of his public work at Logos Bible Software (later Faithlife), where he wrote, edited and taught for a wide audience.

Michael S. Heiser (1963–2023) earned a PhD in Hebrew Bible and Semitic languages at the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 2004. are the family that includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Akkadian and Ugaritic. Reading them let him study the Bible alongside the older texts of its world. Soon after, he went to work for Logos Bible Software, a company in Bellingham, Washington, that makes digital tools for studying the Bible. The company later took the name Faithlife.

At Logos, Heiser served as a : a scholar employed by an organization to research, write and teach from inside it. He helped build study resources for the Hebrew and Greek texts and wrote for a wide audience. His best-known books came from Lexham Press, the publishing arm of the same company. published The Unseen Realm (2015), Supernatural (2015), Angels (2018) and Demons (2020).

This setting shaped his influence. A university professor often writes mainly for other specialists. Heiser wrote both for specialists and for pastors, students and curious readers. His dissertation had studied the divine council in ancient Jewish writings. His later books carried that research to people who would never read a dissertation. Some scholars welcomed this; others thought popular books moved too quickly past hard questions. Both reactions are part of his legacy.

33.2

The Naked Bible Podcast

Main ideaFrom 2014 to 2023 the Naked Bible Podcast let Heiser teach passage by passage to a large listening audience.

In 2014 Heiser began the . It ran until 2023, the year he died, and produced hundreds of episodes. He explained the name in his own way: he wanted to read the Bible stripped of later traditions, in the setting of the ancient world that produced it. The word naked here means uncovered, not shocking.

Many episodes worked through a single book of the Bible, a few verses at a time. Others took up one hard passage, such as Psalm 82 or Genesis 6, or answered listeners’ questions. Heiser often brought the Hebrew or Greek word onto the table, explained it plainly and then asked what an ancient reader would have heard. That method is called reading in : the words, history and culture around a passage that shape its meaning.

The podcast mattered for his legacy in a simple way. It made a long, technical argument into weekly conversation. Listeners could follow a whole book of the Bible with him over months. It also created a public record of what he actually said. Today a careful student can check a claim about Heiser against his own recorded words, not just against someone’s summary.

33.3

Teaching to the End

Main ideaIn his last years Heiser kept teaching, and after his death in February 2023 his books and recordings carried his work forward.

Heiser left his role at Faithlife and spent his last years teaching in other settings. One was the Awakening School of Theology and Ministry, connected with a church in Jacksonville, Florida, where he helped lead the school’s teaching. He also kept recording the podcast and writing. His aim stayed the same: to help ordinary readers hear the Bible in its ancient setting.

Heiser died in February 2023 after an illness. The podcast ended that year. Students, listeners and colleagues wrote tributes that described him as a patient teacher who liked hard questions. A person’s is what continues after them: ideas, books, students and habits of thinking. For Heiser that includes a shelf of books, hundreds of recorded episodes and many readers who first met the idea of the through him.

A legacy is not only praise. It also includes the debates a scholar started and the ways people use, or misuse, his work. The rest of this chapter looks at both. It asks where his ideas traveled, how other scholars answered him, and how some popular writers stretched his name to cover claims he did not make.

Section 2

How His Ideas Spread

33.4

From Dissertation to Bookshelf

Main ideaHeiser's 2004 dissertation on the divine council grew into The Unseen Realm for serious readers and Supernatural for everyone.

Heiser’s , the long research study that earns a PhD, was titled The Divine Council in Late Canonical and Non-Canonical Second Temple Jewish Literature (2004). The runs from the rebuilding of the temple in Jerusalem, about 516 BCE, to its destruction by Rome in 70 CE. Heiser asked how Jewish writers of that era, inside and outside the Bible, talked about God’s heavenly assembly.

A key text for him was Psalm 82. In the Hebrew the psalm opens with God standing in a council and judging among the elohim. Heiser argued that these elohim are heavenly beings, members of the divine council, not human judges. He connected the psalm with Deuteronomy 32:8–9 and with texts from Ugarit that describe a council of gods. Earlier scholars had studied the divine council too; Heiser’s work built on theirs.

Eleven years later the research reached a much wider audience. The Unseen Realm (2015) laid out his full reading of the Bible’s supernatural worldview, with notes for serious students. Supernatural (2015) told the same story in a short, plain book for general readers. The pattern matters: a simplifies. A reader who wants the full argument, or its evidence, goes back to the longer work.

33.5

Reaching Popular Teaching

Main ideaHeiser's ideas spread through churches, study groups and media, including BibleProject's series on spiritual beings.

Heiser’s books sold well beyond the classroom. Pastors taught from them, small groups read them, and podcasters discussed them. Many readers first learned the term divine council, or first heard that Genesis 6 might describe heavenly beings, through The Unseen Realm or Supernatural. That spread is what scholars call : the ways one person’s work shapes what others think and teach.

One well-known example is BibleProject, a group that makes animated videos and podcasts about the Bible. Its series on explores elohim, the divine council, angels and the rebel powers. The series covers themes close to Heiser’s, and its teachers have named his work among the scholarship they learned from. It does not simply repeat him; it presents its own teaching in its own words.

Influence runs in more than one direction. Heiser learned from earlier scholars of Ugarit and the Hebrew Bible, and later teachers learned from him. When a student meets an idea in a video, a good habit is to trace it back: who first argued it, in which book, and on what texts. That habit keeps the credit and the evidence in the right place.

33.6

Readers Who Push Back

Main ideaOther scholars and traditions read Heiser's key passages differently, and a fair study of his legacy includes them.

A scholar’s influence also shows in the , a careful, reasoned response, that his work draws. Some scholars think Heiser leaned too heavily on a few passages, such as Deuteronomy 32:8, whose wording differs among manuscripts. Others think he gave 1 Enoch too much weight in reading books that were written earlier. Heiser answered such points in his books and on his podcast.

Psalm 82 shows how readings differ. Heiser read its elohim as heavenly beings. A long line of Jewish and Christian readers has understood them as human judges or rulers, called gods because they judged on God’s behalf. In John 10:34, Jesus quotes the psalm’s words Ye are gods in a debate, and interpreters read that verse in both ways. Each reading works from the same Hebrew text.

Genesis 6 is another case. Heiser, like 1 Enoch and many early Jewish readers, took the sons of God to be heavenly beings. The , taught by Augustine and many later Christians, reads them as descendants of Adam’s son Seth marrying other people. A third view reads them as powerful human kings. Knowing these readings is part of understanding why Heiser argued as he did.

Ancient Astronauts and Other Claims

33.7

Sitchin and the Anunnaki

Main ideaHeiser publicly argued that Zecharia Sitchin's ancient-astronaut claims misread the Mesopotamian texts they cited.

In 1976 the writer Zecharia Sitchin published The 12th Planet. He claimed that ancient Sumerian texts describe the as beings from a hidden planet called Nibiru who came to earth and shaped humanity. His books sold widely and helped popularize : the claim that visitors from space, remembered as gods, shaped early human history.

Heiser, trained in ancient Semitic languages, argued that Sitchin’s claims did not match the texts. The Mesopotamian texts were written in , wedge-shaped signs pressed into clay. In those texts the Anunnaki are a group of gods, not space travelers. Heiser pointed out that Babylonian texts use the name Nibiru for a heavenly body seen in the sky, not for an unseen twelfth planet. He posted his answers online, on a website he titled Sitchin Is Wrong, so readers could compare them with the sources.

Heiser’s method mattered as much as his conclusion. He did not ask readers to trust him. He showed where the tablets are published, what the signs say and how specialists translate them. Anyone can repeat that check. A claim about an ancient text is only as strong as its match with the text itself.

33.8

Are the Nephilim Aliens?

Main ideaHeiser read the Nephilim through Hebrew, Aramaic and Jewish texts, and rejected the claim that they were aliens.

The word appears twice in the Hebrew Bible: in Genesis 6:4 and in Numbers 13:33, where Israel’s spies report seeing Nephilim in Canaan. Some popular writers claim the word means those who came down from the sky, and they treat the Nephilim as aliens. The Hebrew verb naphal means to fall, and many readers have linked the name with it.

Heiser argued for a different origin. He held that the word most likely comes from , a language close to Hebrew, where a similar word means giant. That fits the ancient translators. The , the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible made by Jewish scholars in the last centuries BCE, renders Nephilim as gigantes, giants. 1 Enoch, too, calls the children of the Watchers giants.

For Heiser the Nephilim belonged to a theological story about rebellion against God, told in Genesis and retold in 1 Enoch. They were not visitors from another planet. Other scholars debate the word’s origin, and some prefer the link with naphal. None of these readings needs spaceships. That is the point of checking: the texts give real options, and aliens are not among them.

33.9

Giants and the Pyramids

Main ideaThe Bible never links giants with the pyramids; its giant stories are set in Canaan, and the texts disagree even on Goliath's height.

A popular claim says giants built the pyramids of Egypt. Start with the facts. The Great Pyramid at Giza was built for the king Khufu, around 2560 BCE. Archaeologists have found the villages, bakeries and tombs of the Egyptian workers who built the Giza pyramids. Genesis never mentions a pyramid, and the Bible’s giant stories never place giants at Giza.

Where the Bible does speak of tall peoples, it places them in Canaan and nearby lands. Numbers 13 names the Anakim at Hebron. Deuteronomy 2–3 mentions the , and says the bed of Og, king of Bashan, measured nine cubits long. Heiser read these peoples as part of the Bible’s theme of rebellious powers resisting God’s people. He did not claim they built monuments in Egypt.

Even the Bible’s most famous giant shows why checking manuscripts matters. The , the standard Hebrew text, gives Goliath’s height as six cubits and a span, about nine and a half feet. A Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel and the Septuagint give four cubits and a span, about six and a half feet. A was about eighteen inches. Careful readers compare the witnesses before repeating a number.

Checking a Claim Yourself

33.10

Check the Text

Main ideaTo test a claim about the Bible, find the passage, read its context, compare translations and note what it does not say.

A is the text itself: Genesis, 1 Enoch, a clay tablet. A is a writing about it: a commentary, a book by Heiser, a video. Both are useful. But a claim about what a text says must be checked against the primary source, because only the text can show what it says.

Here is a simple method. First, find the exact passage by book, chapter and verse. Second, read the verses before and after it, so you know the context. Third, compare at least two translations, one older and one modern. Fourth, if a single word carries the claim, look up the Hebrew or Greek word in a reliable dictionary or study tool. Fifth, write down what the passage does not say.

The fifth step catches most sensational claims. Genesis 6:1–4 describes the sons of God, human women, the Nephilim and mighty men of old. It says nothing about spaceships, pyramids or modern bloodlines. A claim that adds such things is an , not a report of the text. It may still be offered, but it must be labeled and supported.

33.11

Check the Scholar's Own Words

Main ideaBefore repeating what Heiser said, find it in his own books or recordings and note how firmly he said it.

Popular writers sometimes stretch a scholar’s ideas. A video may say Heiser proved something, when he argued for it as the best reading. Or it may attach his name to a claim he rejected, such as giants building the pyramids or aliens posing as gods. Using someone’s name for words he never said is called .

The fix is to go to the source. Name the book and year: The Unseen Realm (2015), Reversing Hermon (2017), Demons (2020). Find the passage where he makes the point. Then notice how he states it. Did he say the evidence points one way, or did he say the matter is settled? Scholars mark their confidence carefully, and a fair summary keeps those marks.

When you write about Heiser, put his ideas in your own words and give the source. This is : saying clearly whose idea it is. Write Heiser argued, not the Bible proves. If you cannot find where he said something, do not say he said it. That rule protects his legacy and your own credibility.

A Question the Texts Can Answer

Main ideaA strong capstone question is narrow, open to more than one reading, and answerable from texts you can cite.

Main idea1 Enoch 6 retells Genesis 6 at length, and it is one of the earliest witnesses to how Jewish readers understood the sons of God.

It does not name the sons of God, explain where they came from or tell what happened next. Every reader has to fill in something.

Deuteronomy 32:8 in Three Witnesses

Main ideaDeuteronomy 32:8 reads sons of Israel in the Masoretic Text but sons of God in a Dead Sea Scroll, and Heiser built on the latter.

Deuteronomy 32:8 describes God dividing the nations and setting their borders. The says he set them according to the number of the sons of Israel. The King James Version follows it. But a Dead Sea Scroll copy of Deuteronomy, known as 4QDeut-j, reads sons of God. Many Septuagint manuscripts read angels of God, and some read sons of God.

Comparing such witnesses to find the most likely original wording is called . Heiser, with many textual critics, argued that sons of God is the older reading. He held that later scribes likely changed it to avoid any hint of other gods. Several modern translations now print sons of God in this verse, with a note.

From this verse Heiser drew a wide picture. He read it with verse 9, where the LORD keeps Jacob as his own portion. At Babel, in his reading, God placed the nations under lesser heavenly beings and kept Israel for himself. Those beings later became corrupt, as Psalm 82 describes. Readers who prefer the Masoretic reading link the verse instead to the seventy nations of Genesis 10.

34.7

Stating Heiser's Reading Fairly

Main ideaPresent Heiser's reading in its strongest form, in your own words, with the book and year.

A fair paper states an argument so well that the person who made it would agree with the summary. Some call this the : presenting a view at its strongest before you weigh it. For Heiser, that means giving his main texts, his reasons and his conclusion, not a weaker version that is easy to knock down.

Here is an example for Genesis 6. Heiser argues, in The Unseen Realm (2015), that the sons of God are heavenly beings. He points out that the same phrase names heavenly beings in Job 1:6 and Job 38:7. He notes that 1 Enoch, other early Jewish writings, 2 Peter and Jude read the story this way. He concludes that the passage belongs to a larger biblical theme of heavenly rebellion.

Notice what the example does. It names the source and year. It uses verbs like argues, points out and concludes, which keep the idea attached to Heiser. It gives his evidence, so readers can check it. It does not quote his book, because his books are under ; it puts his ideas in your own words, and that is the right way to handle them.

In this reading the sin was a mixing of faithful and unfaithful families.

Jewish tradition offers another reading. Genesis Rabbah, a rabbinic commentary on Genesis compiled around the 400s CE, records the view that the sons of God were sons of judges or nobles. Some ancient Aramaic translations of Genesis read the phrase in a similar way. This is often called the : the sin was powerful men taking whomever they wished.

Each reading has reasons. The Sethite view fits Genesis 4–5, where Cain’s and Seth’s lines are listed. The royal view fits the ancient practice of calling rulers sons of a god. Heiser’s view fits Job and 1 Enoch. A fair paper states each reading’s best evidence, then weighs them, using the same for all.

34.9

Weighing the Evidence

Main ideaWeigh readings by the age of the witnesses, the fit with context, and how much of the evidence each reading explains.

Once the readings are stated, weigh them. Four questions help. How old are the witnesses for each reading? How well does each reading fit the passage’s own ? How early do other readers support it? And how much of the evidence does it explain without strain? A reading that explains more evidence, more simply, has a stronger case.

Apply these to Genesis 6. The heavenly-beings reading has early witnesses: 1 Enoch, parts of which date to the 200s BCE or earlier, and the Septuagint tradition that renders Nephilim as giants. The Sethite view appears later, in Christian writers such as Julius Africanus and Augustine. Supporters of the Sethite view answer that it fits the family lists in Genesis 4–5 and avoids reading angels as able to marry.

Weighing is not the same as deciding what is true about God or the unseen world. It is a judgment about which reading best explains a text. Your conclusion can be firm, measured or open: the evidence favors, the evidence is divided, or more study is needed. Each can be a strong if it follows honestly from what you have shown.

34.10

Building the Argument

Main ideaA capstone paper moves from thesis to evidence to counterargument to conclusion, and cites every text by book, chapter, verse and translation.

A clear paper follows a simple order. The introduction states the question and your thesis. The body presents the key passages and what they say. Next come the readings: Heiser’s first, then at least one other, each with its evidence. Then the weighing. Last, the conclusion restates the thesis and says how firmly the evidence supports it.

A strong paper includes a : the best case against your thesis, answered fairly. If you argue that 1 Peter 3:19 refers to the Watchers, give the strongest case for the Noah reading, then explain why you still hold your view, or why the question stays open. Readers trust a writer who faces the hard evidence.

Every names the text and the translation. Write Genesis 6:4, King James Version (1611), or 1 Enoch 6:6, R. H. Charles (1917), or Heiser, Reversing Hermon (2017), paraphrased. Quote only public-domain translations exactly, and mark everything else as a paraphrase. Never invent a page number, quotation or statistic.

34.11

A good presentation lets all of them follow the evidence. It uses : words that report a view without mocking it or pushing the listener to adopt it.

10. Which is a primary source for a paper on 1 Peter 3:19?

Why: The passage itself is the primary source; everything written about it is secondary.

11. Which sentence avoids misattribution?

Why: It reports a view Heiser actually held, with the book and year.

12. What is a counterargument?

Why: A strong paper states the best case against its thesis and answers it fairly.

13. Which translation may be quoted word for word in this course?

Why: The KJV is in the public domain. Modern translations and Heiser's books are under copyright.

14. What does weighing the evidence decide in a capstone?

Why: Weighing judges which reading best explains the text, not what anyone should believe.

15. A classmate disagrees during your presentation. What is the best response?

Why: Turning to the evidence keeps the disagreement calm, respectful and useful.
