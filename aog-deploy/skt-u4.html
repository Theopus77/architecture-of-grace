From Clay Wheels to Urethane

History

The first skateboards were planks of wood with metal roller-skate wheels nailed on. Surfers in California rode them on sidewalks when the ocean was flat. By the early 1960s, companies sold boards in stores, and a national contest was on TV. Then the craze faded, because the wheels slipped, jammed and threw riders off.

In the 1970s, a new wheel made of urethane changed everything. It gripped the ground and rolled over cracks. A kicktail let riders lift the nose. Smooth bearings spun longer. A team from Santa Monica brought a low surf style, and empty pools in a drought gave skaters curved walls to ride.

In this unit you will meet the people, places and inventions of skateboarding's first two booms. You will learn why a hard wheel stops at a pebble and why a soft one rolls on. You will practice a tail press safely. You will see how energy changes on a curved wall.

The big question

How did better wheels and new places to ride turn a fading fad into a lasting sport?

The First Boom

1950s–1960s
Big questionHow did a homemade toy become a craze across America, and why did that craze fade so fast?
The story

Stopped by a Pebble

A girl rolls down a California sidewalk in 1964, and one tiny stone ends her ride.

It is a warm afternoon in a California beach town in 1964. Ruth stands on her new board. It is a short wooden plank. Under it are four metal wheels from an old pair of roller skates. The ocean is flat today, so there are no waves to surf.

Ruth pushes off. The steel wheels clatter and buzz on the sidewalk. She can feel every crack through her feet. She bends her knees and leans a little, the way she leans on a surfboard. The board curves gently to one side.

Then the front wheel hits a tiny pebble. The hard metal wheel cannot roll over it. The board stops dead. Ruth does not stop. Her body keeps moving forward, and she tumbles onto the grass beside the sidewalk.

Ruth sits up and checks her hands. They are scraped, but she is fine. She looks back at the pebble. It is smaller than a pea. "How can something so small stop a whole board?" she wonders.

Thousands of riders in the 1960s asked the same question. The boards of that time were fun, but their wheels were rough and slippery. It would take years, and a brand-new kind of wheel, to fix the problem.

Making a Board at Home

Main ideaThe first skateboards were homemade: a wooden plank with roller-skate wheels fastened underneath.

The first skateboards were not bought in stores. Kids made them at home. They took an old pair of metal and pulled them apart. Each skate had a front set of wheels and a back set.

Next, they fastened the wheel sets under a plank of wood. Some used nails. Some used screws. A short piece of a two-by-four board worked. So did a wooden crate or a piece of old furniture.

These homemade boards were stiff and flat. They had no curved tail. The wheels were small and made of metal. But they rolled, and kids could ride them. That was enough to start something new.

Who Made the First One?

Main ideaNo one person is known to have invented the skateboard; many people built them in many places.

People love to ask who invented the skateboard. You may hear a story about one boy or one town. These are stories. No record shows a single first inventor.

Historians look for proof, like dated photos, ads and newspaper stories. For the first skateboards, there is very little. Kids built boards in garages and did not write it down. Many people likely had the same idea in different places.

So the honest answer is that we do not know. What we do know is where the idea grew. It grew among surfers and kids in California in the late 1950s. A says what the proof shows, and says when the proof runs out.

7.5

Clay Wheels

Main ideaMany 1960s boards rolled on clay wheels, which were quieter than metal but slid out easily.

By the mid-1960s, many store boards rolled on . They were not made of plain clay from the ground. They were a hard, pressed mix of materials. People called them clay because they looked and felt like it.

Clay wheels were quieter than metal wheels. They felt a little smoother on a sidewalk. But they had a big problem. They did not grip the ground well. On a turn, a clay wheel could slide sideways and throw the rider off.

Clay wheels also wore down fast. They chipped on rough concrete. A rider might need new wheels often. These wheels were better than steel, but not by much.

7.6

Steel Wheels and Noise

Main ideaSteel wheels were loud, shaky and slippery, and they jammed at even a tiny stone.

The earliest boards rolled on from roller skates. Steel is a hard metal. It does not squish at all. On a sidewalk, steel wheels made a loud clattering, buzzing sound.

Because steel is so hard, every bump went right into the rider’s feet. Riders felt each crack in the concrete. The board shook and rattled. On smooth, slick ground, steel wheels also slid easily.

The worst problem was small stones. A hard wheel cannot squeeze around a pebble. It hits the pebble and . The board stops in an instant, and the rider is thrown forward. Riders had to watch for tiny stones all the time.

7.7

Why the Rider Keeps Going

Main ideaWhen a board stops suddenly, the rider's body keeps moving forward until something stops it.

Think about Ruth and the pebble. The board stopped, but Ruth flew forward. Why? A moving body keeps moving. It keeps going the same way until something pushes or pulls on it.

The pebble pushed back on the wheel. That stopped the board. But nothing pushed back on Ruth’s body. Her feet were only resting on the deck. So she kept going forward and fell off. This is called .

You feel inertia in a car, too. When the car stops fast, your body leans forward. A seat belt is what stops you. Skaters learn to bend their knees and watch the ground ahead. That helps them catch their balance or step off safely.

The Boom Fades

Main ideaAround 1965 to 1966 the first boom faded, as many riders got hurt on boards with poor wheels.

In the early 1960s, huge numbers of skateboards were sold. People called this the first . A boom is a time when something grows very fast. Then, around 1965 and 1966, the boom faded almost as fast as it came.

One big reason was injuries. Clay and steel wheels slipped and stopped short. Many riders fell and got hurt. Few people wore helmets or pads back then. Some towns made rules against skating on streets and sidewalks.

Soon many kids put their boards away. Stores were left with boards no one bought. Skating did not disappear, but it got very small. A better wheel would bring it back in the 1970s.

Over the Crack

In 1975 a skater rolls over a crack that used to throw riders off, and keeps on going.

Marcus laughs and rides back to the corner to try again. All over California, other riders are finding the same thing. Skateboarding, which had nearly faded away, is about to boom again.

Why Urethane Grips

Main ideaUrethane wheels squish a little and grip the ground, so they roll over bumps and hold on in turns.

is a tough plastic that can bend a little and spring back. It is used for many things, like shoe soles and roller-skate wheels. On a skateboard, that small bit of give makes a big difference.

When a urethane wheel rolls onto a pebble or crack, it squishes around it for a moment. Then it springs back into shape. So the wheel rolls over small bumps instead of jamming. The ride feels smooth and quiet.

Urethane also has good with concrete. Friction is a force that pushes against sliding. When you lean into a turn, friction holds the wheels in place. The board curves instead of skidding out.

8.3

Soft and Hard Wheels

Main ideaWheel hardness is measured on the durometer A scale, from about 78A for soft wheels to about 101A for hard ones.

Not all urethane wheels feel the same. Some are softer and some are harder. Skaters measure this with the scale. A durometer is a tool that presses into a material to test how hard it is.

Skateboard wheels use the A scale. The number is followed by the letter A. Soft wheels are about 78A. Very hard wheels are about 101A. A bigger number means a harder wheel.

Soft wheels squish more. They roll over cracks and rough ground well and grip strongly. Hard wheels squish less. They feel fast on smooth ground and slide more easily. Skaters pick the hardness that fits where they ride.

The Kicktail

Main ideaLarry Stevenson's kicktail, patented in 1971, let riders press the tail to lift the front of the board.

Early boards were flat from end to end. Then came the . A kicktail is a back end that bends upward. Larry Stevenson, who started Makaha, got a patent for it. The patent was granted in 1971.

With a kicktail, a rider can step on the and press down. The front of the board, called the , lifts up. The back wheels act like the middle of a seesaw. Pressing one end lifts the other end.

This small change opened up new moves. Riders could lift the nose to turn sharply or to get up a curb. Kicktails became normal on boards. Later, boards got a curved-up nose too.

Main ideaPractice pressing the tail on grass or carpet first, with your back foot on the tail and your front foot near the front bolts.

Before you start, buckle a helmet that fits snugly. Wear wrist guards, knee pads and flat shoes. Set your board on grass or carpet so it cannot roll. Have a grown-up nearby. Riding with your left foot forward is called regular. Riding with your right foot forward is called goofy. Both are normal.

First, put your front foot across the board, just behind the front . Bolts are the four screws that hold each truck on. Next, put the ball of your back foot on the tail. Then bend your knees and keep your shoulders right over the board.

Now press the tail down slowly with your back foot. The nose rises. Let your front foot ride up gently. Then press with your front foot to set the nose back down. If the board tips you backward, you leaned back too far. Keep your weight over the middle and press softly.

8.6

Smooth Spinning Bearings

Main ideaIn the mid-1970s, sealed precision bearings let wheels spin smoothly and kept dirt out.

Inside each wheel is a . A bearing is a ring of small steel balls between two metal rings. The wheel spins around the balls instead of rubbing straight on the axle. That means less friction and a smoother roll.

Early wheels had loose balls packed in by hand. Dirt and sand got in easily. The balls could fall out. In the mid-1970s, came to skateboarding. Each one was made with care and closed in by a cover.

The cover kept out dirt. The careful making kept the balls lined up. Wheels spun longer and more smoothly. Bearings are often marked with an ABEC number. That number tells how exactly a bearing was made. It is not a speed rating.

Section 3

The Zephyr Team

8.7

Surf Style from Dogtown

Main ideaThe Zephyr team from Santa Monica and Venice, California, skated low and fast, copying surfers.

In the early 1970s, a surf shop called Zephyr stood near the beach in Santa Monica, California. The area of Santa Monica and Venice around it was nicknamed . The shop had a surf team, and then a skate team.

The included Peggy Oki, Tony Alva, Jay Adams and Stacy Peralta. They surfed in the morning and skated after. They rode their boards the way they rode waves. They crouched low, swung their arms and dragged a hand on the ground in turns.

Peggy Oki was the only girl on the team. She skated in the same low, strong style as the others. Several team members became famous. Stacy Peralta later helped start the Powell Peralta company.

8.8

Del Mar, 1975

Main ideaAt the 1975 Del Mar Nationals, the Zephyr team's low surf style surprised the crowd and changed contest skating.

In 1975, a big contest called the was held in Del Mar, California. Skaters came from many places. Most freestyle riders did neat tricks standing up tall, like spins and handstands.

Then the Zephyr team rode. They crouched low and carved fast, sharp turns. They touched the ground with their hands. Their style looked like surfing on concrete. Many people at the contest had never seen anything like it.

After Del Mar, magazines wrote about the Zephyr skaters. Other riders began to copy their low, aggressive . Skating started to look less like dance and more like surfing. That style would soon move into empty pools.

Section 4

Empty Pools and First Parks

8.9

A Dry California

Main ideaDuring the California drought of 1976 to 1977, many backyard swimming pools sat empty, and skaters rode them.

In 1976 and 1977, California had a . A drought is a long time with very little rain. Water was scarce, and people were asked to save it. Many backyard swimming pools were drained or left empty.

Skaters saw something special in those empty pools. The walls of a pool curve smoothly up from the floor. On urethane wheels, skaters could ride up those curves like riding up a wave.

Some skaters went into empty pools without asking the owners. That was trespassing, which means going on private property without permission. Today, skaters ride curved walls built for them at skateparks.

8.10

Riding the Curved Wall

Main ideaRolling up a curved wall turns motion energy into stored energy, and rolling back down turns it back into motion.

The curve where a floor bends up into a wall is called . Skaters call it tranny for short. A skater rolls fast across the flat bottom and up the transition. As the skater goes higher, the board slows down.

At the highest point, the skater stops for an instant. Then gravity pulls the skater back down, and the board speeds up again. Going up, changes into . Coming down, stored energy changes back into motion energy.

Each trip loses a little energy to friction and air. So skaters bend and straighten their legs at the right moments to keep going. This is called pumping. It is like pumping on a swing.

Where in the bowl is the marble moving fastest? Where does it have the most stored energy?

8.11

The First Skateparks

Main ideaIn 1976, Carlsbad Skatepark in California and Skateboard City in Florida opened as two of the first skateparks.

Skaters needed places where they were welcome. In 1976, two of the first opened. One was Carlsbad Skatepark in Carlsbad, California. The other was Skateboard City in Port Orange, Florida.

These parks had smooth concrete runs, bowls and curved walls. Many parks charged a fee to ride. They did not have to sneak into backyard pools. Builders shaped the concrete to copy the curves skaters loved.

Many more parks opened in the late 1970s. The second boom was in full swing. Better wheels, kicktails, smooth bearings and new parks all worked together. Skateboarding had come back bigger than before.
