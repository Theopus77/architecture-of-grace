Out of the world, then running half of it

1914. Europe goes to war and the United States deliberately stays out of it for three years.

In 1914 the United States wanted to stay out of the world. By 1945 it was running half of it. Every event in this unit is a step in that turn — and the second half of the unit is what that new role cost at home.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

The United States fought in World War I from 1914.

It entered in 1917 — three years late, after unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann telegram. The same pattern repeats in the second war: Europe from 1939, the United States from 1941.

US in 1917 and 1941. Always late.

⚠️ People often think…

Roosevelt was president when the stock market crashed.

HOOVER was president in October 1929 and for three more years. Roosevelt took office in March 1933, at the bottom, and brought the New Deal. Getting this backwards scrambles every cause-and-effect question in the unit.

Hoover at the crash, Roosevelt at the bottom.

⚠️ People often think…

The New Deal ended the Great Depression.

It eased the suffering and rebuilt the system — banking rules, Social Security, labor rights, much of which is still here. But unemployment stayed high until wartime production absorbed it. The WAR ended the Depression; the New Deal reformed what came after.

The war ended it. The New Deal reformed it.

⚠️ People often think…

The Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan were the same program.

One is a promise, the other is a budget. Truman Doctrine = the commitment to support countries resisting takeover. Marshall Plan = the actual billions rebuilding Western Europe. NATO is a third thing again: the military alliance.

Promise, money, alliance — three things.

⚠️ People often think…

The Berlin Wall falling and the Soviet Union ending were the same event.

The wall fell in 1989; the Soviet Union dissolved at the end of 1991. Two years of enormous change sit in between, and questions about the end of the Cold War often turn on which of the two a document is describing.

Wall 1989, Soviet Union 1991.

⚠️ People often think…

The home front during the wars was mostly a story of unity.

It was both. Enormous mobilization and shared sacrifice — and at the same time Japanese American incarceration, segregated units, and the Double V campaign demanding victory abroad and at home. A full answer holds both halves.

Mobilization and injustice, at the same time.

Worth knowing cold

Containment, in four pieces

These four get swapped constantly. Each one is a different KIND of thing — a promise, money, an alliance, a wall.

  1. Truman Doctrine (1947) The PROMISE — the United States would support free peoples resisting takeover. Words, and a policy.
  2. Marshall Plan (1948) The MONEY — billions to rebuild Western Europe, on the theory that prosperous countries do not turn communist.
  3. NATO (1949) The ALLIANCE — an attack on one is an attack on all. This is the military commitment, not the aid.
  4. Berlin Wall Built 1961, fell 1989. The Soviet Union itself dissolved in 1991 — two years after the wall, not the same event.

Helpful Hints

🧭 The story in one line

A reluctant war (1917) → boom (1920s) → bust (1929) → New Deal (1933) → a total war (1941–45) → superpower → forty-five years of Cold War → 1991.

War, boom, bust, Deal, war, Cold War, wall falls.

🤝 The one idea

In 1914 the United States wanted to stay out of the world. By 1945 it was running half of it. This unit is how that happened — and what it cost at home.

⚠️ Traps the test loves

  • U.S. enters WWI in 1917, not 1914. U.S. enters WWII in 1941, not 1939.
  • Hoover was president at the crash. Roosevelt brought the New Deal.
  • The WAR ended the Depression. The New Deal eased it and reformed the system.
  • Truman Doctrine = promise of aid. Marshall Plan = the money. NATO = the alliance.
  • Berlin Wall: built 1961, fell 1989. Soviet Union dissolved 1991.

🎯 How the test will ask

  • A graph of unemployment 1929–1941 — “when did it peak, and what ended it?”
  • A photograph or poster from the home front — “what is it asking people to do?”
  • A short excerpt from a speech — “which policy is this?”
  • A civil liberties case — “what was restricted, and was it justified?”
  • A Cold War event — “how is this an example of containment?”
