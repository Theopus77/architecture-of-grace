Reading Real Passages: Berakhot and Shabbat

Texts Close Up

This unit opens two real pages of the Babylonian Talmud and reads them closely. The first is Berakhot 2a–3a, the Talmud's opening page, about when to say the evening Shema. The second is from tractate Shabbat: the stories of Hillel and three visitors on 31a, and the rules for Hanukkah lights on 21b.

You will read each passage in translation, with Rodkinson's public-domain English (1896–1903) as one tool and modern translations in paraphrase as another. You will follow the moves of the argument: question, proof, objection and answer. You will also ask what each ruling protects, such as a tired family, a curious stranger, or a household in danger.

By the end you will be able to cite a passage by tractate and page, name who is speaking, and explain how different readers have understood it. You will describe what the text says and how Jews have read it, without deciding what anyone should believe.

The big question

How do close readings of real Talmud pages show argument and mercy working together?

Berakhot 2a–3a: The Evening Shema

Berakhot
Big questionHow does the Talmud turn one short question about the evening prayer into a whole way of reading?
The story

Where Does the Teacher Stand?

The Talmud's very first question is not about the answer. It is about where the question came from.

A student opens a printed volume of the Babylonian Talmud to its first page. The page number is not 1. It is 2a, because printers counted the title page as page one. In the center sits a short block of Hebrew. That block is the Mishnah, a law code edited around the year 200 CE. Its first line asks a practical question: from what time may a person say the Shema in the evening?

The Shema is a prayer built from verses in Deuteronomy. Rabbinic tradition holds that it is said twice a day, at night and in the morning. The Mishnah gives three answers about the evening time. Rabbi Eliezer says one thing. The Sages say another. Rabban Gamliel says a third. No one is silenced, and all three views stay on the page.

Then the Gemara begins. The Gemara is the long discussion that later teachers built around the Mishnah. Its first words do not ask which answer is right. They ask something stranger: where does the tanna stand? A tanna is a teacher of the Mishnah. The Gemara wants to know what the teacher already assumed when he asked "from what time."

That small question opens the whole book. It says that every rule rests on something earlier, and that a reader should dig for it. Students have started their Talmud study on this page for centuries. In this chapter you will read it closely, move by move, in translation.

Talk about itWhy might a book of law begin by asking where a question comes from instead of giving a rule?
Section 1

The First Line

23.1

A Tractate Called Blessings

Main ideaBerakhot, meaning "blessings," is the first tractate of the Talmud, and it opens with the evening Shema.

The Babylonian Talmud is arranged in six orders, and each order holds several tractates. A tractate is one book of the Mishnah and its Gemara. The very first tractate is called , which means "blessings." It deals with prayers and blessings: the Shema, the standing prayer, and blessings said over food.

Berakhot opens with the . The prayer takes its name from its first Hebrew word, "hear," in Deuteronomy 6:4. Rabbinic tradition holds that Jews recite it every evening and every morning. That practice comes from how the rabbis read Deuteronomy 6:7, which speaks of words said "when thou liest down, and when thou risest up."

Why start the whole Talmud with the evening prayer and not the morning? The Gemara itself raises that question a little later on the page, as you will see. For now, notice the choice. The Talmud’s first topic is a daily practice that ordinary people did at home. Law in this book begins with something close to everyday life.

23.2

Three Answers, All Kept

Main ideaMishnah Berakhot 1:1 gives three different end times for the evening Shema and keeps every opinion.

The Mishnah’s first line reads, in paraphrase: from what time may one recite the Shema in the evening? From the hour when the priests go in to eat their . Terumah was the gift of food that Israelites gave to priests. The Mishnah uses the priests’ mealtime as a clock for the start of night.

Then come three end times. Rabbi Eliezer says until the end of the first watch of the night. The Sages say until midnight. Rabban Gamliel says until the dawn rises. Rabbi Eliezer and Rabban Gamliel were leading teachers of the late first and early second centuries CE. "The Sages" means the majority of the teachers.

Notice what the Mishnah does. It does not name a winner in this line. It lists the voices side by side. Later Jewish law generally follows the Sages’ midnight as the ideal, while treating dawn as the outer limit after the fact. Keeping the losing views is a habit you will see on almost every page. The Talmud treats a minority opinion as worth remembering.

From the source
Mishnah Berakhot 1:1
From when may one read the Shema in the evening? From the hour the priests go in to eat their terumah, until the end of the first watch, says Rabbi Eliezer. The Sages say: until midnight. Rabban Gamliel says: until dawn comes up.
Mishnah Berakhot 1:1 (Berakhot 2a), paraphrased from the Hebrew; compare Rodkinson's translation (1896–1903) (paraphrased)

23.3

The Sons Come Home Late

Main ideaThe Mishnah tells how Rabban Gamliel's sons came home after midnight and he let them say the Shema, showing mercy inside the law.

Right after the three opinions, the Mishnah tells a short story. Rabban Gamliel’s sons came home from a wedding feast after midnight. They told their father they had not yet said the Shema. The Sages had set midnight as the limit. Had the sons missed their chance?

Rabban Gamliel answered, in paraphrase: if dawn has not yet risen, you are still obligated to say it. Then the Mishnah explains the Sages’ own view. When they said "until midnight," they meant it as a fence. A fence is a safety rule. It keeps a person far from breaking the real limit, so no one lazily waits until morning.

The story shows the course’s second thread, mercy. The young men were not scolded for going to a wedding. They were given a way to keep the practice. It also shows the first thread, argument. The Mishnah does not just state a limit. It explains why a stricter limit was set. A reader learns both the rule and the reasoning that protects it.

Think it through

Section 2

Where Does the Tanna Stand?

23.4

The Gemara's First Question

Main ideaThe Gemara opens by asking what earlier source the Mishnah's teacher assumed when he asked "from what time."

The Gemara on Berakhot 2a begins with a short Aramaic question, often quoted as "tanna heikha kai?" It means "where does the stand?" A tanna is a teacher from the time of the Mishnah. The question points at a hidden assumption. The Mishnah asks "from what time" as if everyone already knows there is a duty to say the Shema at night.

So where did that duty come from? The Gemara wants the root. This is a key move in almost every . A sugya is one unit of Talmudic discussion on a topic. It often begins by asking for the source of a rule, the reason behind it, or the assumption it hides.

Readers across Jewish life notice this opening. Traditional yeshiva students learn to ask "where does the tanna stand?" as a habit. Academic scholars read it as a sign of how the editors of the Talmud worked: they tie each rule back to Scripture or to an earlier teaching. Both kinds of readers agree that the Gemara prizes questions.

Think it through

How is this student's question like the Gemara's first question on Berakhot 2a?

23.5

Two Roots for One Rule

Main ideaThe Gemara offers two sources for the evening Shema: the verse about lying down, and the creation story's "evening and morning."

The Gemara gives a first answer: the tanna stands on the verse. Deuteronomy 6:7 says to speak these words "when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." The rabbis read lying down as nighttime and rising up as morning. The duty exists because the verse says so, and the Mishnah just asks when it starts.

Then the Gemara adds a second possibility. Maybe the tanna learned it from the story of creation. Genesis 1:5 says, "And there was evening and there was morning, one day." Evening comes first in the verse. So, the Gemara suggests, the Mishnah teaches the evening Shema first too.

This answers a question you met earlier. Why does the Talmud start with the evening and not the morning? The creation verse gives a reason: in the Hebrew Bible the day begins at evening. This is why Jewish holidays and Shabbat begin at sundown. Two proofs, one from law and one from creation, support the same rule.

23.6

Priests and Stars

Main ideaThe Gemara reads the priests' mealtime as a sign of nightfall, the time when the stars come out.

Why does the Mishnah use priests eating terumah as its clock? The Gemara explains through a verse in Leviticus 22:7. A priest who became ritually impure had to immerse in water. Then, in the verse, when the sun is down he is clean, and afterward he may eat of the holy food.

So the priests went in to eat their terumah once night had truly come. The Gemara connects that moment with the appearance of the stars. Later Jewish practice still uses the phrase , "the coming out of the stars," to mark nightfall. Many Jewish calendars print this time each day.

Here the Gemara reads the Mishnah’s words as a code. The Mishnah could have said "when the stars come out." Instead it named a practice from the Temple, which had been destroyed in 70 CE by the time the Mishnah was edited. Some readers say this kept the memory of the Temple alive in daily prayer. Others see it simply as a well-known sign of night.

Think it through

Section 3

Night, Watches and a Harp

23.7

The Poor Man's Dinner

Main ideaA teaching quoted in the Gemara marks nightfall by when a poor person sits down to bread and salt.

The Gemara on Berakhot 2b brings a . A baraita is a teaching from the time of the Mishnah that was not included in it. This baraita offers a different sign for the start of the evening Shema. It says: from the time a poor person comes in to eat bread with salt.

The Gemara then compares this sign with the priests’ sign. Are they the same moment or not? The Gemara tests one against the other, asking which comes earlier. It even brings a third opinion about when people sit down to their Friday night meal. The discussion goes back and forth, and not every question is neatly closed.

Readers notice who is used as the clock here. First it was priests, the most honored class. Now it is the poor, who worked until dark and ate the simplest meal. Some teachers point out that the Talmud puts the poor worker’s evening on the same page as the priest’s. The rule about prayer quietly records how ordinary people lived.

23.8

Three Watches or Four?

Main ideaThe Gemara records a disagreement over whether the night has three watches or four, and gives signs for each.

The Mishnah’s Rabbi Eliezer said "until the end of the first watch." So the Gemara on Berakhot 3a asks: how many watches does the night have? A watch is a shift of the night, like a guard’s turn. Rabbi Eliezer holds there are three. Rabbi Natan, another tanna, holds there are four.

The Gemara adds signs for each watch in Rabbi Eliezer’s view. In the first watch, a donkey brays. In the second, dogs bark. In the third, a baby nurses at its mother’s breast and a wife talks with her husband. These are sounds anyone in an ancient village would know. The Gemara also records an there: at each watch God roars like a lion, grieving over the Temple.

Here law and story sit side by side. A question about timing leads to a picture of a whole village at night, and to a teaching about sorrow for the destroyed Temple. Readers have long found this mix typical of the Talmud. The book moves easily from a legal detail to a scene of grief and back.

23.9

David's Harp at Midnight

Main ideaOn Berakhot 3b the Gemara tells that a harp over King David's bed played at midnight and woke him to study, linked to Psalm 119:62.

A little further on, the Gemara turns to midnight. It quotes a verse from Psalms, which tradition credits to King David: "At midnight I will rise to give thanks unto Thee." How did David know the exact moment of midnight? The Gemara on Berakhot 3b answers with a story.

A harp hung above David’s bed. When midnight came, a north wind blew through its strings and it played by itself. David would rise and study Torah until dawn. The story is aggadah. It is a picture of a king who set aside part of every night for learning.

Readers take this story in different ways. Some traditional readers hold it as a true account of David. Many modern and academic readers treat it as a teaching image about devotion and study. Rashi, the great commentator from France (1040–1105), explains details of the scene. All these readers agree the story ties the question of time to a love of learning.

Section 4

Reading the Translation

23.10

Rodkinson Against the Page

Main ideaMichael Rodkinson's English Talmud (1896–1903) is public domain and readable, but it is partial and abridged, so readers check it against the page.

Most students first meet the Talmud in translation. One of the earliest English versions was made by Michael L. Rodkinson and published in New York from 1896 to 1903. Because it is old, it is now in the public domain. That means anyone may copy and share it freely. It is easy to find online.

Rodkinson’s version has real strengths. It was one of the first attempts to put Talmud tractates into plain English for general readers. But scholars point out its gaps. It is partial, since it covers only some tractates. It is abridged, since Rodkinson shortened and rearranged passages. Its quality is uneven from volume to volume.

So a careful reader compares. Put Rodkinson’s lines next to the Hebrew and Aramaic page, or next to a modern translation such as the Steinsaltz or Schottenstein editions or the William Davidson Talmud on Sefaria. Those modern versions are not public domain, so they are cited and paraphrased. Ask: did the translator skip a step of the argument? Checking translations is part of reading closely.

23.11

Mapping the Moves

Main ideaReading Berakhot 2a as a map of moves — question, proof, second proof, test — shows how a sugya works.

Now put the pieces together as a map. First comes the Mishnah: a question and three opinions. Next the Gemara asks for the root: where does the tanna stand? Then come two proofs, one from Deuteronomy 6:7 and one from Genesis 1:5. After that the Gemara tests signs of nightfall, the priests and the poor man, against each other.

Each step has a name students can learn. A is a hard question or objection. A is an answer that resolves it. "Mai ta’ama?" means "what is the reason?" When the Gemara cannot settle a point, it may say , "let it stand." Learning these names helps you see the shape under the words.

Many Jewish students learn these moves with a , a study partner. Partners read aloud, argue, and push each other to explain. Orthodox yeshivas, Conservative and Reform schools, and university classes all use partner study in different ways. The goal is the same: to follow the argument and to see what each step protects.

Shabbat 31a and 21b: Hillel's Convert and the Hanukkah Lights

Shabbat
Big questionWhat do the stories of Hillel's patience and the rules for Hanukkah lights show about how the Talmud joins law and kindness?
The story

The Bet of Four Hundred Zuz

Two men bet money on whether anyone could make Hillel lose his temper.

The Talmud tells this story on Shabbat 30b–31a. Two men made a bet. Whoever could make Hillel angry would win four hundred zuz. A zuz was a silver coin, so this was a large sum. Hillel was a famous teacher who lived around the end of the first century BCE. The Talmud remembers him for his patience.

It was Friday afternoon, just before Shabbat. Hillel was washing his hair. One man came to his door and shouted, "Is Hillel here?" Hillel wrapped himself and came out. "My son, what do you need?" The man asked a silly question about why people from one land have round heads. Hillel treated it as a real question and gave a calm answer.

The man came back again, and then a third time, each time with another odd question about people from far places. Each time Hillel came out, called him "my son," and answered with care. At last the man burst out that Hillel had cost him four hundred zuz.

Hillel replied, in paraphrase: better that you lose four hundred zuz, and four hundred more, than that Hillel should lose his temper. The Gemara then tells three more stories of people who came to Hillel and to his colleague Shammai. In this chapter you will read those stories, and then a page about Hanukkah, from the same tractate.

Talk about itThe man's questions were meant to annoy. Why do you think Hillel answered them as if they were real questions?
Section 1

Three Visitors

24.1

Hillel and Shammai

Main ideaHillel and Shammai were leading teachers of the late first century BCE, and the Talmud often sets Hillel's patience beside Shammai's strictness.

Hillel and Shammai are two of the most famous names in the Talmud. They lived in the land of Israel around the end of the first century BCE and the start of the first century CE. Rabbinic tradition lists them as the last of the "pairs" of leaders in Mishnah Avot. Hillel had come from Babylonia, and tradition says he was poor in his youth.

Each founded a school of students. The schools are called Beit Hillel and Beit Shammai, the House of Hillel and the House of Shammai. The Talmud records hundreds of their disputes. In most cases Jewish law follows the House of Hillel. The Talmud on Eruvin 13b explains why: the students of Hillel were gentle and humble, and they taught Shammai’s view before their own.

The stories on Shabbat 31a use these two men as contrasts. Shammai is shown as strict and quick to send people away. Hillel is shown as patient and welcoming. Readers note that the Talmud still keeps Shammai’s opinions and calls him a sage. The contrast is about how to teach, not about who was a good person.

Which teacher is more like Shammai in these stories, and which is more like Hillel? Could both have good reasons?

24.2

On One Foot

Main ideaA man asked to learn the whole Torah on one foot; Shammai sent him away, and Hillel answered with a single rule of kindness.

On Shabbat 31a a man who was not Jewish came to Shammai. He said he would convert if Shammai taught him the whole Torah while he stood on one foot. Shammai pushed him away with the builder’s measuring rod in his hand. The man then went to Hillel with the same request.

Hillel accepted him and said, in paraphrase: what is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow. That is the whole Torah. The rest is its explanation. Now go and learn it. Hillel’s saying is close to the , but it is phrased in the negative: do not do what you would hate.

Readers have taken this answer in several ways. Many stress the first half, a short summary of ethics. Many also stress the last words, "go and learn." Hillel did not say the rest was unimportant. He gave a starting point and sent the man to study. Reform, Conservative and Orthodox teachers all quote this passage, often with different emphasis.

24.3

Only the Written Torah

Main ideaA second visitor accepted only the Written Torah, and Hillel showed him through the alphabet that reading itself depends on teachers.

The second visitor asked Shammai how many Torahs Jews have. Shammai said two: the Written Torah and the Oral Torah. The man said he trusted the written one but not the oral one. He wanted to convert on that condition. Shammai sent him away. Rabbinic tradition holds that the Oral Torah is the teaching passed down beside the written text.

Hillel accepted him and began to teach him the Hebrew alphabet: aleph, bet, gimel, dalet. The next day Hillel taught him the letters in a different order. The man objected that yesterday it was different. Hillel answered, in paraphrase: you had to rely on me for the letters. Rely on me for the oral teaching too.

The story makes a point about reading. Even letters need a teacher to explain them. Rabbinic tradition used this to defend the Oral Torah. Groups that accept only the written Bible, such as the Karaites who arose in the eighth and ninth centuries CE, have read it differently. This course describes these views without judging which is true.

24.4

The Man Who Wanted to Be High Priest

Main ideaA third visitor wanted to become High Priest; Hillel let him study until he discovered the limit himself, and all three later praised Hillel's patience.

The third visitor passed a synagogue and heard the High Priest’s fine clothes being described. He went to Shammai and asked to convert so he could become High Priest. Shammai pushed him away too. Hillel accepted him, and said, in paraphrase: does anyone become a king without learning the ways of the kingdom? Go and study.

The man studied. He reached a verse in Numbers 1:51, which warns that an outsider who comes near the holy service shall be put to death. He asked whom it meant. Hillel said it included even King David. The man reasoned that if even David could not serve, then surely a newcomer could not. He accepted this, and he stayed.

Later the three converts met. The Talmud gives their words, in paraphrase: Shammai’s impatience nearly drove us from the world. Hillel’s patience brought us under the wings of the Divine Presence. The passage ends by praising patience in teaching. It shows as a way of teaching: let a learner reach the answer at their own pace.

Section 2

What Is Hanukkah?

24.5

The Question on 21b

Main ideaOn Shabbat 21b the Gemara stops a discussion of lamp oils to ask, "What is Hanukkah?"

Tractate is about the laws of the day of rest. Its second chapter begins with which oils and wicks may be used for the Shabbat lamp. That talk of lamps leads to Hanukkah lights. On Shabbat 21b the Gemara asks a plain question: "What is Hanukkah?"

The Mishnah itself says almost nothing about Hanukkah. There is no Mishnah tractate for it. So the Gemara answers with a baraita. It says that from the 25th of the month of Kislev there are eight days of Hanukkah. On them people do not give mourning speeches or fast.

Historians connect Hanukkah with the Maccabees, a Jewish family that led a revolt against the Seleucid Greek king Antiochus IV. They rededicated the Temple in Jerusalem around 164 BCE. The books of Maccabees tell that history. The Talmud’s own answer, as you will see, focuses on a different detail: the oil.

24.6

The Cruse of Oil

Main ideaThe Gemara's answer tells of one sealed jar of oil that lasted eight days, and of the holiday set the next year.

The baraita on Shabbat 21b continues. When the Greeks entered the Temple, they made all its oils impure. When the Hasmonean family won, they searched and found one of oil. A cruse is a small jar. It still had the High Priest’s seal, so it was pure.

The jar held enough oil to light the Temple lamp for only one day. The Talmud says a miracle happened, and they lit from it for eight days. The next year, it says, the sages set these days as a festival for praise and thanksgiving. Praise here means Hallel, a set of psalms sung on holidays.

Readers compare this account with the books of Maccabees, which tell of the battles and the rededication but not the oil. Some traditional readers accept both accounts. Some historians suggest the rabbis stressed the oil to focus on God’s help rather than on war. This course reports those readings without deciding among them.

Shabbat 21b, paraphrased from the baraita; compare Rodkinson, Tract Sabbath (1896–1903) (paraphrased)

24.7

The Lamp at the Door

Main ideaThe Hanukkah lamp is placed at the door facing the street, to make the miracle public, but inside on a table in time of danger.

The Gemara on Shabbat 21b also gives rules for where to put the lamp. It says, in paraphrase, that it is a to place the Hanukkah lamp at the entrance of one’s house, on the outside. The purpose is to publicize the miracle, so people passing by can see it.

There are exceptions. A person who lives upstairs may place it at a window facing the street. And in a time of danger, the Gemara says, it is enough to set it on one’s table inside. The rabbis knew that in some times and places a public religious sign could put a family at risk.

The rule shows both threads of the course. The law is careful: where, when and why. And it bends to protect life. Across Jewish communities today, some light at the doorway, some at a window, and many at a table. Chicago families, like families in many cities, often set their lamps in a front window facing the street.

Section 3

Two Houses Argue

24.8

Up or Down?

Main ideaThe House of Shammai lights eight lights on the first night and one fewer each night; the House of Hillel starts with one and adds one.

The Gemara on Shabbat 21b lists levels of the practice. The basic mitzvah is one light for each household each night. Those who beautify the practice light one for each person in the house. For those who beautify it most, the Houses of Shammai and Hillel disagree.

The House of Shammai says: on the first night light eight lights, and then one fewer each night. The House of Hillel says: on the first night light one, and then one more each night. Both agree there are eight nights. They differ in direction, down or up.

Jewish practice today follows the House of Hillel. That is why most Hanukkah lamps grow brighter as the week goes on. But the Talmud keeps Shammai’s view. A student reading the page sees a real argument with two sides, not only the answer that later won.

24.9

The Reasons Behind the Numbers

Main ideaThe Gemara gives reasons for each house: days still to come or already gone, the Sukkot bulls, and "raise holiness, do not lower it."

The Gemara asks: what is the reason? Two amoraim in the land of Israel, it says, gave explanations. An is a teacher of the Gemara period. One explanation: Shammai counts the days still to come, so the number goes down. Hillel counts the days already gone, so the number goes up.

The other explanation links Shammai to the bulls offered on Sukkot. Numbers 29 lists thirteen bulls on the first day and one fewer each day after. Hillel’s reason is a principle: in matters of holiness, we go up and do not go down. A holy practice should grow, not shrink.

The page shows how the Gemara works. It does not just record who said what. It asks for reasons and offers more than one. Readers often note that Hillel’s rule, "go up in holiness," says something beyond Hanukkah. It asks a person to keep growing. Shammai’s reasons, based on counting and Temple offerings, are kept alongside it.

Think it through

Section 4

Law and Story Together

24.10

Halakhah Beside Aggadah

Main ideaShabbat 21b and 31a mix law and story on the same pages, and each helps explain the other.

The Talmud has two kinds of material. is law: how, when and where to act. Aggadah is story and teaching: the lives of the sages, legends and sayings. In Shabbat 21b, the history of the oil (aggadah) sits right beside the rules for the lamp (halakhah).

The Hillel stories on 31a are aggadah too. Yet they teach something about law. Hillel’s answer to the first convert sums up the Torah as a rule for how to treat others. The patience stories show how a teacher should apply the law to a person who stands before him.

Rabbinic readers have long debated how the two fit. Some say aggadah gives the reason behind halakhah. Some treat aggadah more freely, as teaching rather than law. Most agree the Talmud meant them to be read together. You cannot fully understand the lamp rule without the oil story, or Hillel’s law without his patience.

legend
a traditional story passed down about the past

24.11

Two Translations Side by Side

Main ideaSetting Rodkinson's older English beside a modern paraphrase shows what translators add, cut and change.

Rodkinson’s translation of tractate Shabbat is one of the older public-domain English versions. His style is formal, with a nineteenth-century sound. He also shortened and rearranged some passages. So when you read the Hillel stories or the Hanukkah page in his version, check each step against a modern translation or the Hebrew and Aramaic page.

Modern translations such as the Steinsaltz edition, the Schottenstein edition and the William Davidson Talmud on Sefaria add explanations inside the text, often in a different type. That helps new readers follow the argument. But it also means the reader sees the translator’s choices mixed in with the Talmud’s words.

No translation is the page itself. A good habit is to ask three things. What words did the translator choose? What did the translator add to explain? What did the translator leave out? Readers from yeshivas, from university classrooms and from synagogue study groups all use this habit in their own ways.

Think it through

What does Version 2 add? What might a reader gain or lose from that addition?
