Religion and the World

Religion in Society

Religion does not stay inside houses of worship. Walk through Chicago and you pass hospitals founded by nuns, food pantries run by mosques, gospel choirs that began on the South Side, and a nine-sided temple on the lakefront in Wilmette. Read the news and you find courts deciding what a public school may do about prayer, and reports of communities driven from their homes because of their faith. This unit follows the world's traditions out into law, art, science, charity, war, peace and the question of freedom.

The first chapter looks at how religions have shaped culture: law codes from Hammurabi's stone to sharia, canon law and the First Amendment; temples, cathedrals, mosques and chant; the long story of religion and science, from the House of Wisdom to Galileo and Darwin; and the giving that traditions call zakat, tzedakah, charity, seva and dana. The second chapter looks at conflict and peace: the Crusades, Europe's wars of religion, the Partition of India and Northern Ireland, and then Gandhi, King, Tutu and interfaith peacemakers, the rights the First Amendment protects, and Chicago's own interfaith history.

By the end of the unit you should be able to explain how a tradition's texts shaped a law, a building or an act of service; describe a conflict in which religion played a part without blaming a whole tradition; explain the Establishment and Free Exercise Clauses; and tell the difference between teaching about religion and practicing it. As always in this course, you will describe and attribute, not judge.

The big question

How have religious traditions shaped the wider world, for building and for breaking, and how can a society keep every faith, and people of no faith, free?

The story

Three Doors, Three Communities

A hospital, a food pantry and a school, each opened by a community of faith, and each open to anyone who walks through the door.

In 1852 a small group of Catholic nuns, the Sisters of Mercy, opened the first chartered hospital in Chicago. The city was young, muddy and crowded, and cholera swept through it more than once. The sisters nursed patients who could not pay and patients who did not share their faith. They kept records, trained nurses and begged for donations. Mercy Hospital served Chicago's South Side for more than 160 years. Its founders said they were doing what their tradition asked: to visit the sick and care for the stranger.

On a Saturday morning on Chicago's Southwest Side, volunteers at a mosque line up paper bags on folding tables. Rice, lentils, canned tomatoes, a bag of onions, a box of cereal. The money came partly from zakat, the yearly share of wealth that Muslims give to those in need, and partly from voluntary gifts. The line outside includes Muslim families, a Mexican grandmother with a wire cart, and a retired man from the block who has never set foot inside the prayer hall. No one asks anyone what they believe.

Outside a town in Punjab, India, a Sikh congregation pooled donations for years and built a school. The classrooms have ceiling fans and a computer lab. At noon the students file into the langar, the free kitchen that every gurdwara keeps, and sit in long rows on the floor to eat lentils and flatbread. Hindu, Muslim and Sikh children sit side by side. The volunteers who cook and serve call their work seva, service done for its own sake and not for pay or praise.

Three communities, three traditions, three doors that stay open to outsiders. Each community would tell you it is following its own text: the words of Jesus about feeding the hungry, the Qur'an's command to give, the Sikh Gurus' teaching about equality and service. This chapter follows religion out of the house of worship and into the wider world. It looks at four places where religion and culture meet: law, art, science and the care of people in need. In each, the traditions differ, and in each, they have left marks you can still see.

43.1

Hammurabi's Stone and Sinai's Law

Main ideaAncient Near Eastern law codes and the Torah's laws share forms and topics, but the Torah presents its law as the terms of a covenant with God.

A black stone pillar more than seven feet tall stands in the Louvre in Paris. It was carved for Hammurabi, king of Babylon, around 1754 BCE. At the top, the king stands before Shamash, the Babylonian sun god who oversaw justice, and receives from him the symbols of authority. Below are about 282 laws about wages, contracts, marriage, theft and injury. This is the , one of the oldest law collections in the world. From the very beginning, law and religion were carved on the same stone.

The Torah, the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, also contains law, mainly in Exodus, Leviticus and Deuteronomy. In the biblical story, God gives these laws to Moses at Mount Sinai as the terms of a , a binding agreement between God and the people of Israel. Scholars have noticed that some of the Torah’s laws resemble Hammurabi’s. Both deal with an ox that gores a person, and both use the formula of , a Latin phrase meaning the law of retaliation: an eye for an eye. The Torah’s framing is different, though. Its law is presented as a relationship, not only a royal decree, and it returns again and again to the stranger, the widow and the orphan.

How a law is read matters as much as what it says. In Jewish tradition, the rabbis of the Talmud read an eye for an eye as a rule of fair compensation, meaning the value of an eye, not the taking of one. Jewish tradition counts 613 commandments, or , in the Torah, and the body of law worked out from them is called , a Hebrew word meaning the way one walks. Christians read the same laws through the New Testament. Many Christians hold that the Torah’s moral commands still bind them while its ritual laws were fulfilled in Jesus. Jews and Christians share the text but not the way of reading it.

43.2

Sharia and the Schools of Law

Main ideaSharia is what Muslims understand as God's guidance for life; fiqh is the human scholarship that works it out, and that scholarship has several schools.

In a courtyard at al-Azhar in Cairo, one of the oldest centers of Islamic learning, students sit in circles around a teacher and argue about a case. What if a traveler cannot find water for washing before prayer? What makes a sales contract fair? These are questions of . The Arabic word originally meant a path to water. For Muslims, sharia is the whole of God’s guidance for how to live, covering worship, family, business and conduct. The human effort to understand that guidance is called , which means understanding. Muslim scholars, the , have always distinguished the two. Sharia is understood as God’s; fiqh is human work that can be debated and revised.

Sunni scholars traditionally draw on four sources. The first is the Qur’an. The second is the , the example of the Prophet Muhammad, known through the hadith, the reports of his words and actions. The third is consensus among qualified scholars. The fourth is reasoning by analogy. For example, the Qur’an forbids wine. Later scholars reasoned that other intoxicating substances share the same harm and so fall under the same rule. Over the centuries, four major Sunni schools of law took shape: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi’i and Hanbali, each strongest in different regions. Twelver Shia Muslims follow the Ja’fari school. The schools differ on many details, such as the exact posture in prayer, and have generally recognized one another as valid.

A qualified scholar may issue a , a legal opinion in answer to a question. A fatwa is advice, not a court order, and a Muslim may seek a second opinion. Today most Muslim-majority countries use national law codes for crime and commerce, and sharia-based law applies mainly to family matters such as marriage and inheritance. How far religious law should shape state law is debated among Muslims themselves, with a wide range of views. In the United States, many Muslims follow sharia in personal life, such as prayer, fasting and halal food, in the same way that observant Jews follow halakha, under the civil law that governs everyone.

43.3

Canon Law, Halakha and the First Amendment

Main ideaReligious communities keep their own internal law, such as canon law and halakha, while the U.S. Constitution keeps government from establishing or restricting religion.

Around 1140 a teacher in Bologna named Gratian, traditionally said to be a monk, gathered thousands of church rulings that seemed to contradict one another and tried to reconcile them. His book, the , became the foundation of , the law of the Christian church. The word canon comes from a Greek word for a measuring rule. Church councils had issued canons since the Council of Nicaea in 325. Today the Catholic Church’s Code of Canon Law, issued in 1917 and revised in 1983, governs the sacraments, the clergy, marriage cases and church courts. Orthodox churches follow ancient canons as interpreted by their synods. Protestant churches have their own governing documents, such as a Book of Discipline or a Book of Order.

Jewish communities have long kept a parallel system. A , a rabbinic court of three judges, rules on conversion, on religious divorce and on disputes that the parties agree to bring before it. Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Jews differ on how binding halakha is and on who may interpret it. In medieval Europe, church courts, Jewish courts and royal courts operated side by side, and a person might live under two or three legal systems at once. That world of overlapping laws is the background to the American arrangement.

In 1791 the United States adopted the First Amendment. It says that Congress shall make no law respecting an of religion or prohibiting the of it. An established church is an official one, supported by the state; England still has one, and several American states kept establishments into the 1800s, Massachusetts until 1833. Under the First Amendment, religious communities are free to run their own law inside their own communities. A bet din or a church court can decide who is a member in good standing. But civil courts do not enforce religious law as such, though they may uphold an arbitration both parties freely agreed to, and religious law cannot override civil law. A religious wedding still needs a civil license, and no religious court can fine or jail anyone.

43.4

Buildings That Teach

Main ideaSacred architecture puts a tradition's beliefs into stone: which way you face, where the light falls and who may enter all carry meaning.

Step inside a traditional Hindu temple and the building leads you inward. Past the porch and the pillared hall is a small dark room called the , a Sanskrit word meaning womb chamber, where the image of the deity is kept. A tower rises above it, often shaped like a mountain peak, because many Hindus picture the gods dwelling on Mount Meru at the center of the world. Worshipers walk clockwise around the shrine, a practice called pradakshina. Buddhist builders used a different form, the , a solid mound built over relics, which pilgrims also circle. In East Asia the stupa became the tiered pagoda.

In medieval Europe, Christians built to bring in light. At Chartres Cathedral in France, mostly complete by about 1220, pointed arches and outside supports called carry the weight of the roof. That freed the walls for enormous windows of colored glass. Church leaders such as Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis taught that light was a symbol of God. The windows told Bible stories to people who could not read, and the floor plan formed a cross. Orthodox Christians favored a central dome, with an image of Christ as ruler of all painted at its top. Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, finished in 537, is the great example. It became a mosque in 1453, a museum in 1934 and a mosque again in 2020.

A mosque is built around a direction. One wall, the wall, faces Mecca, and a niche in it, the , marks the direction of prayer. Beside it stands the minbar, a stepped pulpit for the Friday sermon. A tower, the , carries the call to prayer, and a fountain in the courtyard serves for washing before prayer. There are no statues or pictures of people; the decoration is geometric pattern and calligraphy from the Qur’an. The prayer hall is open floor, so that rows of worshipers can stand shoulder to shoulder. A Sikh gurdwara, by contrast, is defined by openness. The Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, the Golden Temple, has four doors, one on each side, which Sikhs understand as a welcome to people of every caste and faith.

In Wilmette, Illinois, on the lakefront north of Chicago, stands the Bahá’í House of Worship, dedicated in 1953. It is the oldest surviving Bahá’í temple in the world. It has nine sides and nine doors, because the number nine symbolizes unity and completeness in the Bahá’í faith. Its lacework dome is made of concrete and crushed quartz. Like the mosque’s qibla wall or the temple’s inner chamber, its shape is a teaching. When you read a sacred building, ask three questions: Which way does it face? Where does the light go? Who is invited in?

43.5

Images, Icons and Empty Space

Main ideaTraditions disagree about whether the sacred may be pictured, and their art, from icons to calligraphy to empty niches, shows the answer each one gives.

The Ten Commandments in Exodus forbid making a graven image. Jewish tradition has read this as a ban on images of God, and synagogue art has usually used symbols instead: a menorah, lions, a crown for the Torah. Yet an ancient synagogue at Dura-Europos in Syria, from the third century CE, had wall paintings of biblical scenes, so the rule was not always applied the same way. Islam takes a similar path. The Qur’an condemns idols, and many hadith discourage pictures of living beings, so mosques are decorated with , geometric pattern and vines. This avoidance of images is called . It was never total. Persian, Ottoman and Mughal artists painted people in books, and some Persian and Ottoman painters even showed the Prophet, often with his face veiled.

Christians argued about images for more than a century. In Orthodox Christianity an is a painted image of Christ, Mary or a saint, and believers say it is a window toward heaven. They venerate it with a kiss or a bow but say they worship God alone. Between 726 and 843 the Byzantine emperors twice tried to destroy icons, a movement called , meaning image-breaking. The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 ruled that veneration of images was right. In the sixteenth century the argument returned. Reformers such as Zwingli and Calvin removed images from churches, while Luther kept many, and Catholic churches remained full of statues and glass.

Hindu practice goes further in the other direction. A is an image of a deity into which, through a ceremony, the deity is invited to be present. Worshipers offer it flowers, food and light. Hindus explain that they are not worshiping a statue; the image is a form through which the formless can be approached. Buddhist art tells a story of change. The earliest Buddhist carvings, at Sanchi in India in the first century BCE, never showed the Buddha’s body. They showed his footprints, an empty seat or the tree he sat under. Around the first and second centuries CE, sculptors in Gandhara and Mathura began carving the Buddha in human form, and those images spread across Asia.

43.6

Chant, Recitation and Song

Main ideaIn most traditions the sacred text is heard as much as read, and sound carries it: chant, recitation, kirtan and gospel song.

Psalm 150 lists trumpet, harp, drum, strings and cymbals and tells everything that breathes to praise God. The Hebrew Bible describes choirs of Levites singing in the Temple in Jerusalem. In the synagogue today a cantor chants the Torah using , a system of marks written above and below the words that tell the reader how each phrase rises and falls. Christian monks sang the Psalms daily. The plain, unaccompanied melody of the Western church became known as . It is named for Pope Gregory I, around 600, though it was written down centuries later. Luther wrote hymns for congregations to sing. Bach composed cantatas for Lutheran services, and Orthodox churches still sing without instruments.

The word Qur’an means recitation, and Muslims hear the text far more often than they see it. A trained reciter follows , the rules for pronouncing every letter and pause, and recitation contests draw large audiences. Many Muslims do not call this music, and the distinction matters to them. Sikhs sing their scripture. The hymns of the Guru Granth Sahib are arranged by musical mode, and , devotional singing with harmonium and drum, fills a gurdwara for hours. Hindus sing bhajans and kirtan to Krishna, Rama and the Goddess, a practice that the saint Chaitanya spread through Bengal in the sixteenth century. Buddhist monks chant sutras, and Tibetan monks are known for a deep, layered chant.

Chicago gave the world a new sacred music. In 1932 Thomas A. Dorsey, a blues pianist who had turned to church music, began directing the choir at Pilgrim Baptist Church on the South Side. That year, after the deaths of his wife and infant son, he wrote Precious Lord, Take My Hand. He joined the harmonies of the spirituals to the rhythm of the blues, and the result was . Mahalia Jackson, who had moved to Chicago from New Orleans in 1927, became its most famous voice. In 1963 she sang at the March on Washington just before Martin Luther King Jr. spoke. Gospel spread from Chicago’s Black churches to the whole world.

Religion and Science

43.7

Observatories and Scriptoria

Main ideaFor centuries religious needs and religious institutions drove much of the world's science, from Islamic astronomy and algebra to monastic and university scholarship in Europe.

Islam gave its scholars mathematical problems to solve. Muslims pray five times a day at times set by the sun, so they needed accurate tables of sunrise, noon and dusk. They pray facing Mecca, so they needed to calculate that direction from any city on a round earth. Their calendar follows the moon, and the shares of an inheritance are set in the Qur’an as fractions. In Baghdad in the early 800s the caliph al-Ma’mun supported the , where scholars translated Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic. There al-Khwarizmi wrote a book on solving equations whose title gave us the word ; his own name became the word algorithm. Muslim astronomers refined the , a brass instrument for finding the time and the stars. Ibn al-Haytham tested how light and vision work with experiments, and Ibn Sina’s medical textbook was taught in Europe for centuries.

In Christian Europe the monastery was the library. Benedict’s Rule, written around 530, set monks to prayer and work, and the work often meant copying books by hand in a room called the . Monks preserved Roman and Greek texts that would otherwise have vanished. The English monk Bede, who died in 735, worked out the calculation of the date of Easter, a science called , and popularized counting years from the birth of Jesus. Cathedral schools and church scholars helped give rise to the first universities, at Bologna, Paris and Oxford, which trained the clergy and lawyers that church and rulers needed. Much later, Gregor Mendel, a friar in Brno, bred pea plants in the monastery garden and founded genetics with a paper published in 1866. Georges Lemaitre, a Belgian priest and physicist, proposed in 1927 that the universe is expanding, the idea that became the Big Bang.

Other traditions have their own stories. Indian mathematicians such as Brahmagupta, writing in 628, developed rules for zero and negative numbers, and the oldest Indian geometry appears in texts about building Vedic altars. The Buddhist monk Yi Xing measured the length of a degree of the earth’s meridian for the Tang court in the 720s. Jesuit missionaries ran the imperial astronomy bureau in Beijing in the 1600s. The Jewish philosopher Maimonides was a physician to the sultan’s court in Cairo. For most of these people, studying nature was a religious act. Psalm 19 says the heavens declare the glory of God, and many scholars in the Abrahamic traditions took that as an invitation to look closely.

43.8

Galileo and the Church

Main ideaThe Galileo affair of 1616 to 1633 was a conflict over who may interpret Scripture and nature, not a simple war between religion and science.

In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus, a Catholic church official in Poland, published a book arguing that the earth moves around the sun. He dedicated it to the pope, and for decades it caused little stir. Then, in 1609 and 1610, Galileo Galilei pointed a telescope at the sky. He saw four moons circling Jupiter and saw Venus go through phases like the moon. Both fit a sun-centered system far better than the old earth-centered one. Galileo was a devout Catholic whose two daughters became nuns, and at first church astronomers in Rome welcomed his discoveries.

The trouble was the Bible. Verses such as Joshua’s command to the sun to stand still, and a psalm that says the world cannot be moved, seemed to place the earth at rest. After the Protestant Reformation, the Catholic Church had ruled at the Council of Trent that individuals must not interpret Scripture against the agreed reading of the early church fathers. In a 1615 letter, Galileo argued that the Bible teaches how to reach heaven, not how the heavens move. In 1616 church officials declared contrary to Scripture and told Galileo not to hold or defend it. In 1632 he published a Dialogue that clearly defended it anyway. In 1633 the Roman tried him, found him vehemently suspect of heresy, and made him . He lived under house arrest until his death in 1642, and his book stayed on the until 1835.

Main ideaGandhi built a method of nonviolent resistance, satyagraha, from Hindu and Jain ideas of ahimsa, the Bhagavad Gita and the Sermon on the Mount.

Religious Freedom in the United States

44.8

Two Clauses, One Freedom

Main ideaThe First Amendment protects religious freedom in two ways: the Establishment Clause keeps government from sponsoring religion, and the Free Exercise Clause keeps it from restricting religion.

Many colonies had official churches. Massachusetts taxed residents to support Congregational churches, and Virginia jailed Baptist preachers who preached without a license. Roger Williams, banished from Massachusetts, founded Rhode Island in 1636 on the principle of liberty of conscience. In 1786 Virginia passed the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, drafted by Thomas Jefferson and pushed through by James Madison. It declared that no one could be forced to attend or support any church. Baptists, Presbyterians and other minority churches were among its strongest supporters, because they had felt the weight of an official church themselves.

The First Amendment, ratified in 1791, begins with two clauses about religion. The says Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion. The says it shall not prohibit the free exercise of religion. The first keeps government from sponsoring, funding or favoring a faith. The second keeps it from punishing or burdening people for practicing one. In an 1802 letter to the Baptists of Danbury, Connecticut, Jefferson described the First Amendment as building a wall of separation between church and state. Courts and citizens still argue about how high and how solid that wall should be.

At first the First Amendment limited only the federal government. After the Fourteenth Amendment of 1868, the Supreme Court applied it to the states as well, the Free Exercise Clause in 1940 and the Establishment Clause in 1947. The two clauses can pull against each other. If a state pays for school buses to religious schools, is that establishment, or fair treatment of religious families? Judges on the Court have answered differently over the decades. Americans of every faith, and of none, have taken such questions to court.

Free Exercise in the Courts

Main ideaSupreme Court cases brought by Jehovah's Witnesses, the Amish, Native Americans and others have shaped how far government must make room for religious practice.

In 1940 many public schools required students to salute the flag and recite the Pledge of Allegiance. Jehovah’s Witnesses refused, because they believe saluting a flag breaks the commandment against bowing to images. Their children were expelled, and some Witness families were attacked. In 1943, in West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, the Supreme Court ruled that no official can force citizens to profess a belief. Justice Robert Jackson wrote that no official can prescribe what shall be orthodox in politics, religion or other matters of opinion. Jehovah’s Witnesses brought dozens of cases in those years, and many of them expanded freedoms for everyone.

In 1972, in Wisconsin v. Yoder, the Court sided with Amish parents who would not send their children to school past the eighth grade. The Amish teach separation from worldly life, and the Court held that the state’s interest did not outweigh their faith. But in 1990, in Employment Division v. Smith, the Court ruled against two members of the Native American Church. They had been fired and denied unemployment pay for using , a cactus eaten as a sacrament in their ceremonies. The Court held that a law that applies to everyone and does not target religion can be enforced even if it burdens a religious practice.

The Smith ruling alarmed religious groups across the spectrum. In 1993 Congress passed the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, or , almost unanimously. It requires the federal government to show a compelling reason before it substantially burdens someone’s religious practice. In 1994 Congress also protected the sacramental use of peyote by members of Native American tribes. Later cases have used these laws and a related 2000 law covering prisons. In 2015 the Court ruled that an Arkansas prison had to let a Muslim prisoner grow a short beard. The pattern is steady: minority faiths, whose practices a majority may overlook, bring most free-exercise cases.

Teaching About Religion in Public Schools

Main ideaPublic schools may not lead or sponsor worship, but they may teach about religion, and students keep the right to practice their own faith.

Until the 1960s many public schools opened the day with a prayer or a Bible reading. In Engel v. Vitale in 1962 the Supreme Court ruled that a prayer written by New York state officials for students to recite broke the Establishment Clause. The next year, in Abington School District v. Schempp, it ruled the same about required daily Bible reading. The families who sued in the two cases included Jews, Unitarians and nonbelievers. The Court did not forbid the Bible in school. Justice Tom Clark wrote that a person’s education is not complete without studying comparative religion and the history of religion, and that the Bible may be studied for its literary and historic qualities.

That distinction is the rule this course follows. Scholars sum it up this way: a public school may teach about religion but may not practice or promote it. Teaching about religion, the , describes what adherents believe and do, attributes every claim, and treats all traditions fairly. teaching aims to bring students to faith, and that belongs to families and religious communities. A lesson that says Muslims fast in Ramadan is academic. A teacher leading students in prayer is devotional, and in a public school that crosses the line.

Students have religious rights too. A student may pray alone or with friends, say grace before lunch, read a sacred text during free time, and write about faith in an assignment if it fits the topic. Under the of 1984, if a public high school allows student clubs that are not about classes, it must allow religious clubs on the same terms. Students may wear religious clothing, such as a hijab, a kippah, a turban or a cross, with room made for their practice. In 2022, in Kennedy v. Bremerton, the Supreme Court ruled that a football coach’s brief, quiet prayer at midfield after games was protected private speech, a decision that has been debated since.

Main ideaThe 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago began the modern interfaith movement, and the city remains a center of interfaith life.
