Close Reading the Dhammapada

Dhammapada

The Dhammapada is small enough to carry in a pocket and old enough to have been memorized for more than two thousand years. Its 423 verses are short. Many fit on one line. Yet Buddhist teachers have spent whole lifetimes explaining them.

In this unit you will read the Dhammapada closely in F. Max Muller's 1881 translation, which is in the public domain. You will read its famous opening pairs, its chapter on the mind, and its chapters on the fool, the wise, the thousands, the self, anger and the Brahmana. You will compare Muller's words with modern translations and meet the commentary stories behind the verses.

By the end, you will be able to close read a verse: its words, its neighbors, its Pali key term and its readers. You will also be able to explain how Buddhist teachers fit a line like 'self is the lord of self' with the teaching of not-self, without deciding the question for anyone.

The big question

How do short verses carry a whole way of life, and how do readers decide what they mean?

The Twin Verses and the Mind

Dhammapada 1–3
Big questionWhat does the Dhammapada claim about the mind, and how do its readers turn those claims into a way of living?
The story

Two Men, One Insult

The same harsh words land on two men; one carries them home, and one sets them down.

Picture a market in a town in ancient India. A trader loses his temper and shouts at two men who bumped his stall. He calls them thieves and fools. Both men hear every word. Both walk away. It is an ordinary morning.

The first man replays the insult all the way home. He tells his wife. He lies awake adding new replies. By morning he is planning how to get even. The second man notices the sting, then lets it go. He thinks the trader is having a bad day. He sleeps.

This small scene is not in the Dhammapada. But the Dhammapada's opening chapter is built on scenes like it. Its verses come in pairs. One verse shows a mind that holds on to harm, and the next shows a mind that lets it go. The pairs are why the chapter is called the Twin Verses.

The Dhammapada is a collection of 423 short verses in 26 chapters. It is among the most translated Buddhist texts. In this chapter you will read its first chapter and its chapter on the mind in F. Max Muller's 1881 translation, and compare his wording with modern versions.

Talk about itThe two men heard the same words. What made their nights different?
Section 1

Meeting the Dhammapada

25.1

A Book of Verses

Main ideaThe Dhammapada is a Pali collection of 423 verses in 26 chapters, loved across Buddhist traditions.

The sits in the Khuddaka Nikaya, the Minor Collection of the Pali Canon. Its name joins dhamma, teaching or truth, with pada, which can mean path, step, word or verse. So the title can be read as Verses of the Dhamma or Path of the Dhamma. Translators choose, and both readings fit.

The collection has 423 verses in 26 chapters, each gathered around a theme: the mind, flowers, the fool, the wise, anger, the Buddha. Scholars think it was compiled from older sayings, many of which also appear elsewhere in the canon. Related collections survive in other languages, such as the Gandhari Dharmapada and the Sanskrit Udanavarga.

Theravada monks and laypeople memorize its verses, and children in Sri Lanka and Myanmar often learn them early. F. Max Muller’s translation appeared in Sacred Books of the East volume 10 in 1881 and is in the public domain. Many modern translations exist, by Narada Thera, Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Gil Fronsdal and others.

25.2

Verses and Their Stories

Main ideaA Pali commentary tells a story behind each verse, and Buddhist teachers often teach the verse through its story.

The verses stand alone, but Theravada tradition does not read them alone. A commentary called the Dhammapada Atthakatha, traditionally credited to Buddhaghosa in the fifth century CE, gives a story for each verse. The story tells when and why the Buddha is said to have spoken it.

For verse 1, the commentary tells of Cakkhupala, a blind monk who walked at night and stepped on insects without seeing them. Other monks complained. The Buddha, in the story, says he had no intention to kill, so he did no wrong. The story links the verse’s claim about mind to the idea of .

E.W. Burlingame translated these stories as Buddhist Legends in 1921. Scholars see the stories as later than the verses. Buddhist teachers still use them because a story makes a short verse easier to remember and apply. A close reader can ask: does the story narrow the verse, or open it up?

Section 2

The Twin Verses

25.3

Mind Comes First

Main ideaVerse 1 claims that what we are comes from our thoughts, and that suffering follows an evil thought.

The Dhammapada opens with a claim about the mind. Muller renders it: all that we are is the result of what we have thought. The verse goes on: if a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage.

The simile is concrete. An ox pulls a cart. The wheel rolls in its tracks and cannot turn aside. In the same way, the verse says, pain follows a person who acts from a harmful mind. Buddhist teachers read this as a teaching on : actions shaped by intention bring results that follow the actor.

The Pali behind the English is short: manopubbangama dhamma, manosettha manomaya. Muller made it a sentence about what we are. Modern translators often write that mind precedes all mental states, mind is their chief, they are mind-made. The word , mind, is the key. What it governs, dhamma, is harder to pin down.

Verse 1 in Muller
All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts.
Dhammapada 1, tr. F. Max Muller, Sacred Books of the East 10 (1881)

25.4

The Twin of Verse 1

Main ideaVerse 2 mirrors verse 1: happiness follows a pure thought like a shadow that never leaves.

Verse 2 repeats verse 1 almost word for word, with two changes. The evil thought becomes a pure thought. And the image changes. Muller renders it: if a man speaks or acts with a pure thought, happiness follows him, like a shadow that never leaves him.

Compare the two images. The wheel follows the ox with effort and grinding. The shadow follows a walker quietly and never falls away. One image is heavy, the other light. Readers note that the verse pairs match their images to their themes. The whole first chapter is built this way: twenty verses, mostly in pairs.

The pairing is a teaching method. A reader sees one path and its result, then the other path and its result, side by side. The structure invites comparison without a long argument. It also aids memory, as repetition does in the suttas. Many Buddhist children learn verses 1 and 2 together as a single lesson.

25.5

He Abused Me

Main ideaVerses 3 and 4 say hatred lasts in those who replay wrongs and ends in those who do not.

Verse 3 puts words in a hurt person’s mouth. In Muller’s translation: he abused me, he beat me, he defeated me, he robbed me. Then it says that in those who harbour such thoughts, hatred will never cease. Verse 4 is its twin: in those who do not harbour such thoughts, hatred will cease.

The key word is harbour. The verse does not say the wrongs did not happen. It speaks about holding on to them. A harbor keeps a ship safe and still. A mind that harbors a grievance keeps it safe too, and keeps it from sailing away. This is the first man in the chapter’s opening scene.

Buddhist teachers often connect these verses to and to the practice of letting go. Some readers raise a question: does this ask victims to stay silent about harm? Modern Buddhist teachers answer in different ways. Many say the verse is about the inner hold of hatred, not about giving up justice or safety.

25.6

An Old Rule

Main ideaVerse 5 states that hatred does not end by hatred but by love, which it calls an old rule.

Verse 5 is perhaps the most quoted line in the Dhammapada. Muller renders it: for hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule. The Pali word he translates as love is averena, which literally means by non-hatred.

Translators differ here. Muller chose love. Others write by non-enmity, by kindness or by , loving-kindness. Non-hatred is a negative word: it names the absence of something. Love is positive: it names a presence. The difference matters for what the verse asks. Is it asking a person to stop hating, or to start loving?

The commentary story tells of two women who harmed each other’s children through many lives, until the Buddha taught them this verse. The phrase old rule, sanantana dhamma, suggests a law that always holds. Buddhist teachers link the verse to peace work. It has been read aloud at events including a 1951 peace conference, where Sri Lanka’s J.R. Jayewardene quoted it.

The Chapter on the Mind

25.7

The Fletcher's Arrow

Main ideaChapter 3 compares training the mind to a fletcher straightening an arrow.

Chapter 3, verses 33 to 43, is the Citta Vagga, the chapter on thought or mind. Here the word is , another Pali word for mind, often linked with feeling and mood. The chapter opens with a craftsman. Muller renders it: as a fletcher makes straight his arrow, a wise man makes straight his trembling and unsteady thought.

A fletcher made arrows. He heated a shaft and bent it until it flew true. The image says the mind is like raw wood: bent, but workable. It needs skill and patience. The verse calls thought difficult to guard and difficult to hold back. It does not call it bad. It calls it unsteady.

Verse 34 adds a second image: the mind trembles like a fish taken from the water and thrown on dry ground. Buddhist teachers read the chapter as describing how minds behave and saying they can be trained. The chapter describes; it does not give step-by-step instructions. Those are found in meditation manuals and taught in practice communities.

25.8

A Tamed Mind

Main ideaVerses 35 to 37 say a tamed and guarded mind brings happiness.

Verse 35, in Muller’s words, says it is good to tame the mind, which is difficult to hold in and flighty. A tamed mind, it says, brings happiness. Verse 36 adds that the wise should guard their thoughts, for they are hard to see and very artful. Verse 37 says those who control the mind will be free from the bonds of Mara.

is a figure in Buddhist texts. The suttas present him as a tempter who tried to stop the Buddha’s awakening. Many Buddhist teachers also read Mara as a name for the forces of craving, fear and death. The Dhammapada often uses him this way, as the one whose bonds a trained mind escapes.

The verb tame, danta, is used for horses and elephants. Pali texts often compare mind training to training an animal, patient and steady. The chapter’s claims can be compared with other traditions. The Book of Proverbs, for instance, says one who rules his spirit is better than one who takes a city (Proverbs 16:32).

25.9

Mind as Friend or Enemy

Main ideaVerses 42 and 43 say an ill-directed mind does more harm than an enemy, and a well-directed mind more good than family.

The chapter ends with a strong pair. Verse 42 says that whatever harm a hater or an enemy may do, a wrongly directed mind will do greater harm. Verse 43 says that neither mother, father nor any relative can do as much good as a well-directed mind. It closes the chapter.

These verses rank the mind above outside forces. That can sound harsh. It seems to put all responsibility on the person. Buddhist teachers often read it more gently: outside events are real, but how the mind meets them shapes much of what follows. This is the second man in the opening scene.

The pair also closes the chapter as the twin verses began it: with as the source. A close reader notices how the Dhammapada builds its argument. It gives no long proof. It stacks images and pairs until the reader sees the pattern. This is a style, meant to teach through repetition and memorable lines.

Translations Side by Side

25.10

Muller and a Modern Version

Main ideaComparing Muller's 1881 wording with modern translations shows how translators shape a verse's meaning.

Muller worked in the 1870s, as one of the first Europeans to translate the Dhammapada into English. His English is formal and sometimes old-fashioned: harbour, listeth, unto. He also chose to read verse 1 as a claim about all that we are, a broad, almost philosophical line.

Modern translators often stay closer to the Pali shape. Many render verse 1 like this: mind precedes all mental states; mind is their chief; they are mind-made. Here, what the mind leads is mental states, dhamma in a narrow sense. That is a smaller claim than all that we are. Translators such as Narada Thera and Acharya Buddharakkhita read it this way.

Neither translation is simply right. Each makes choices about a word, dhamma, that has many meanings. A careful student names the translator, notes the choice, and asks what a reader of each would take away. The Pali stays the same. The opens one window on it at a time.

25.11

One Book, Many Readers

Main ideaTheravada monks, Western readers and modern teachers have each used the Dhammapada in their own ways.

In Theravada lands, the Dhammapada is a school text and a source of sermons. A monk giving a talk may recite a verse in Pali, then tell its commentary story. Laypeople may know dozens of verses by heart. In this setting the verses are part of a living practice of and moral training.

In nineteenth-century Europe and America, the Dhammapada was one of the first Buddhist texts people read. Muller’s version made it a classic. It became a favorite of writers seeking universal wisdom. Some of these readers set aside the Buddhist setting of the verses.

Modern teachers in Asian and American communities use the Dhammapada in classes, at retreats and in prisons. Some scholars caution against reading it as a set of quotes cut off from the tradition that kept it. A close reader holds both: the verses travel easily, and they come from a particular and community.

The story

The Question About Verse 160

A student reads one line and raises her hand, because it seems to contradict everything she learned last week.

Maya's class spent last week on anatta, the teaching of not-self. Her teacher explained that, as the suttas present it, the Buddha taught that no permanent, unchanging self can be found in body or mind. Maya wrote it in her notes and underlined it twice.

Today the class opens the Dhammapada to chapter 12, titled Self. Maya reads verse 160 in Muller's translation: self is the lord of self, who else could be the lord? She stops. She raises her hand. How can the same book say there is no self and also that self is the lord of self?

Her teacher does not answer right away. She asks the class to read the verses before and after 160. She asks them to look up the Pali word for self, atta, and see how the text uses it. She asks what a lord does in this verse. The class spends the period reading neighbors, not guessing.

By the end, the class has several possible answers, each held by some Buddhist teachers. This chapter reads the Dhammapada's chapters on the fool, the wise, the thousands, the self, anger and the Brahmana the way Maya's class did: one verse at a time, with its neighbors in view.

The Fool and the Wise

26.1

The Fool Who Knows

Main ideaChapter 5 defines a fool not by lack of schooling but by lack of self-knowledge and by harmful conduct.

Chapter 5 of the Dhammapada is the Bala Vagga, the chapter on the . The Pali word bala means a foolish or childish person. It does not mean someone who lacks school learning. In the Dhammapada, a fool is someone who acts in ways that bring harm and does not see it.

Verse 63 makes a sharp point. Muller renders it: the fool who knows his foolishness is wise at least so far. But a fool who thinks himself wise, he is called a fool indeed. So the worst fool is not the one who knows little. It is the one who does not know what he does not know.

Verse 64 adds an image. A fool may live near a wise person all his life and still not taste the truth, as a spoon does not taste the soup. Verse 65 gives the twin: an intelligent person near the wise for a moment tastes it, as the tongue tastes the soup. Buddhist teachers read the pair as about openness, not intelligence.

26.2

The Wise Like a Rock

Main ideaChapter 6 pictures the wise person as steady, like a rock the wind cannot shake.

Chapter 6, the Pandita Vagga, turns to the . It opens by praising anyone who points out one’s faults, like a guide to hidden treasure. Such a person, verse 76 says, should be followed. The wise person here is not someone who knows many facts. It is someone who welcomes correction.

Verse 81 gives the chapter’s best-known image. Muller renders it: as a solid rock is not shaken by the wind, wise people falter not amidst blame and praise. The word , upekkha, names this quality in other Buddhist texts. It is an evenness that neither praise nor blame can tip over.

Verse 82 adds another image: the wise become serene on hearing the teaching, like a deep, clear, still lake. The chapter pairs strength with calm. Buddhist teachers often read it together with chapter 5, since the fool and the wise are two sides of one portrait.

26.3

Better Than a Thousand

Main ideaChapter 8 says one useful word or one self-conquest is worth more than a thousand empty things or victories.

Chapter 8 is the Sahassa Vagga, the chapter on the . Its verses share a pattern: one of something good is better than a thousand of something empty. Verse 100 says one word of sense, which brings peace to the hearer, is better than a thousand senseless words.

Verse 103 is the chapter’s high point. Muller renders it: if one man conquer in battle a thousand times thousand men, and if another conquer himself, he is the greatest of conquerors. A million soldiers defeated is set against one person’s victory over himself, and the single victory wins.

The repeated form teaches by contrast. Readers notice that the chapter treats quality over quantity as a rule for speech, sacrifice and life itself. Verse 110 says one day of a virtuous, reflective life is better than a hundred years of wicked life. Buddhist teachers often use the chapter in talks about what truly counts.

Section 2

Self Is the Lord of Self

26.4

Reading Verse 160

Main ideaVerse 160 says the self is its own lord, a line that sits in a chapter about self-discipline.

Chapter 12 is the Atta Vagga, the chapter on self. Its verses speak of guarding oneself, training oneself before teaching others, and taking responsibility for one’s actions. Verse 160 sits in the middle. Muller renders it: self is the lord of self, who else could be the lord? With self well subdued, a man finds a lord such as few can find.

Read the neighbors. Verse 159 says one should first make oneself as one teaches others; only the well-trained can train others. Verse 165 says evil is done by oneself and purity is one’s own; no one can purify another. The chapter’s theme is responsibility. The self here is the person who acts and is responsible.

The Pali word is , self, the same root found in anatta, not-self. So the puzzle is real, and Buddhist teachers take it seriously. The chapter gives the reader the words; the next lesson shows how teachers have fit the two teachings together. Keep both in view.

26.5

Two Teachings, One Book

Main ideaBuddhist teachers explain verse 160 and not-self in several ways, most by separating everyday self-talk from a claim about a permanent self.

The teaching, found in suttas such as the Anattalakkhana Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 22.59), says that none of the five parts of a person, body, feeling, perception, formations or consciousness, is a permanent self. Theravada teachers read this as denying an unchanging, independent self. They do not read it as denying that people exist or act.

Many teachers explain verse 160 through the idea of . In everyday speech we say I, you and self. The Buddha, in the suttas, uses such words too. Verse 160 uses self in that ordinary way: the person who acts must train the person who acts. It makes no claim about a hidden, eternal soul.

Other readers draw different lines. Some scholars, such as Peter Harvey, argue that early texts deny a permanent self while still valuing a self that can be trained. A few modern writers argue for an even more positive reading of atta in early texts; many Buddhist teachers disagree. The debate is old and ongoing, and the Dhammapada gives both sides words to use.

26.6

Close Reading a Verse

Main ideaA close reader studies a verse's words, its neighbors, its translation and its readers before deciding what it means.

Maya’s class used four steps. First, read the verse slowly in one translation. Second, read its neighbors in the same chapter. Third, check a key word in the Pali and see how other translations render it. Fourth, find out how at least two groups of readers have understood it.

Applied to verse 160, the steps give a richer picture than the verse alone. The neighbors show a theme of responsibility. The Pali word atta links the verse to the anatta debate. Modern translators, such as Buddharakkhita, render it: one truly is the protector of oneself; who else could the protector be? Protector is gentler than lord.

This method works on any text, Buddhist or not. It keeps a reader from pulling one line out and building a case on it. It also keeps the reader honest about who is speaking. The verse says one thing. Teachers read it several ways. The student’s job is to report both accurately, and then to argue from .

Think it through
Lord or Protector?

Muller (1881): Self is the lord of self. Buddharakkhita (1985): One truly is the protector of oneself. The Pali word natha can mean lord, master, refuge or protector.

Section 3

Anger and Its Opposite

26.7

Holding Anger Back

Main ideaChapter 17 compares a person who checks rising anger to a driver who holds back a rolling chariot.

Chapter 17 is the Kodha Vagga, the chapter on . It opens by urging the reader to leave anger and pride behind. Verse 222 gives the image. Muller renders it as a man who holds back rising anger like a rolling chariot; him I call a real driver. Others, it says, are only holding the reins.

The image is exact. A chariot rolling downhill is hard to stop. Anyone can hold the reins while it runs. The real driver is the one who can stop it. The verse does not say anger never arises. It says the skill is in what happens next. Buddhist teachers call this .

Verse 223 turns to what replaces anger. Muller renders it: let a man overcome anger by love, let him overcome evil by good; let him overcome the greedy by liberality, the liar by truth. The verse pairs each harm with its opposite, as the twin verses did in chapter 1.

26.8

Blame Is Everywhere

Main ideaVerses 227 to 229 note that everyone is blamed by someone, so the wise look to conduct rather than reputation.

The anger chapter includes a surprising observation. Verse 227 says, in Muller’s rendering, that this is an old saying: people blame the one who sits silent, they blame the one who speaks much, and they blame the one who speaks little. There is no one on earth who is not blamed.

Verse 228 goes further: there never was, will not be, and is not now a person who is always blamed or always praised. Verse 229 then says that the one whom the discerning praise, day after day, because of blameless conduct, is worthy. The standard, then, is not public opinion. It is judged by the wise over time.

Readers connect these verses to anger in a practical way. Much anger comes from being blamed. If blame falls on everyone, it need not be carried like a wound. This links back to the rock of verse 81 and the harboring of verse 3. The Dhammapada often echoes itself across chapters.

Section 4

Worth by Conduct

26.9

Who Is a Brahmana?

Main ideaThe last chapter uses the word brahmana, a priestly class title, to mean a person made worthy by conduct, not birth.

The Dhammapada’s final chapter, 26, is the Brahmana Vagga. In ancient India, brahmins were a hereditary priestly class. They held high status by birth. The chapter takes that title and gives it a new meaning. In verse after verse, it says: him I call a who is free of anger, who is truthful, who harms no living being.

Verse 396 makes the argument directly. In substance it says: I do not call a man a brahmana because of his birth or his mother. The word keeps its honor, but the test for earning it changes. Worth comes from what a person does and is, not from the family they were born into.

Scholars note that the Buddha, as the suttas present him, often took over terms from the religious world around him and gave them new, ethical meanings. Verse 393 says a person is not a brahmana by matted hair or family, but by truth and righteousness. This does not attack brahmins as people; it redefines a title.

Not by Birth
The Buddha, as the verse presents him, says he does not call a person a brahmana because of birth or mother. He calls a brahmana one who owns nothing and clings to nothing.

26.10

Reading Across Chapters

Main ideaReading the Dhammapada as a whole shows repeated themes: the mind, restraint, conduct over status and quality over quantity.

A student who reads chapters 1 to 26 will notice echoes. The rock of verse 81 returns in the steady mind of chapter 17. The self-conquest of verse 103 returns in the self-lordship of verse 160. The mind that comes first in verse 1 returns in the trained mind of chapter 3. The book repeats itself on purpose, from new angles.

Four stand out. First, the mind shapes action and its results. Second, restraint is a strength, not a weakness. Third, worth comes from conduct, not status or birth. Fourth, one real good outweighs a great deal of empty show. These themes appear in other Pali texts too, which is one reason scholars call the Dhammapada a summary of early Buddhist ethics.

Readers from outside Buddhism have found parallels in their own scriptures, such as Proverbs or the Sermon on the Mount. Such comparison can be useful. It works best after reading the Dhammapada in its own terms first, with its Buddhist setting and words, like karma and nibbana, kept in view.
