Measuring, Time and Money

Measurement

How long is your desk? What time is lunch? How much is in your pocket? Every day you measure things. This unit shows you how. You will lay units end to end. You will read a ruler in inches and centimeters. You will make a graph of what you find.

Then you will learn the clock. The short hand tells the hour. The long hand counts minutes by fives. You will know when it is 3:00 and when it is 3:30. You will know the difference between a.m. and p.m.

Last, you will count coins. Pennies, nickels, dimes and quarters each have a value. You will add them up, buy things and give change. By the end, you can run a bake sale table all by yourself.

The big question

How do we use numbers to say how long, what time and how much?

How we figured it out

Wake up. The short hand is on the 7, the long hand on the 12.

8:30 a.m.

School starts. The long hand points to the 6: half past 8.

9:00 a.m.

Measure the hallway in shoes, then in feet with a tape.

10:15 a.m.

Make a bar graph of everyone's shoe lengths.

12:00 noon

Lunch. The clock hands both point straight up.

1:30 p.m.

Sort the bake sale coins: quarters, dimes, nickels, pennies.

2:00 p.m.

Count the coins. Big coins first: 25, 35, 45, 50.

2:45 p.m.

3:00 p.m.

6:00 p.m.

Dinner. Tell someone at home what time it is.

Big questionHow can we measure how long something is and show what we found?
The story

The Hallway in Shoes

A class measures the same hallway two ways and gets two very different numbers.

Ms. Ruiz's class had a question. How long is the hallway outside the room? Nobody knew. Amir had an idea. He took off one shoe. He set it on the floor by the wall. Then he set his other shoe in front of it, heel to toe.

Amir walked the hallway shoe by shoe. His friends counted with him. At the far wall he stopped. The count was 40 shoes. Then Nia tried it with her shoes. Her shoes were smaller. Her count was 50 shoes. The class was puzzled.

Ms. Ruiz brought out a long tape measure. It had numbers on it. Two students held the ends. They read the number at the far wall. The hallway was 30 feet long. Every student got 30 feet, no matter who held the tape.

Amir looked at the two numbers. Forty shoes. Fifty shoes. Thirty feet. The hallway had not changed at all. Only the unit had changed. That afternoon the class made a graph of everyone's shoe lengths.

Talk about itWhy did Amir and Nia get different numbers for the same hallway?

Comparing Lengths

7.1

Longer and Shorter

Main ideaTo compare two lengths, line up one end, then look at the other end.

Maya has a red crayon and a blue crayon. Which one is ? She puts them side by side. She lines up the left ends. Now she looks at the right ends. The red crayon sticks out more. So the red crayon is longer. The blue crayon is .

Lining up the ends matters. If one crayon starts ahead, you get fooled. A short crayon can look long. Always start both objects at the same line. Then compare the other ends.

Try it with a pencil and a marker. Line up the ends. Which one sticks out? That one is longer. If neither one sticks out, they are the same length.

Think it through
Two Pencils on a Desk

Picture two pencils on a desk. Their left ends touch the same line. The yellow pencil ends at the 4 on a ruler. The green pencil ends at the 6. Neither pencil has been moved.

Which pencil is longer? How do you know?

Words to know

shorter

Check yourself

1. Two ribbons start at the same line. The blue ribbon sticks out past the red one. Which ribbon is longer?

Why: The ends are lined up, so the ribbon that sticks out farther is longer. That is the blue one.

2. Why do you line up the ends before you compare?

Why: If one object starts ahead, it looks longer than it is. Lining up the ends makes it fair.

3. A pencil is longer than a crayon. The crayon is longer than an eraser. Which is shortest?

Why: Pencil is longer than crayon, and crayon is longer than eraser. So the eraser is the shortest of the three.
7.2

Putting Three in Order

Main ideaOrder lengths from shortest to longest by comparing two at a time.

Leo has three sticks. The brown stick is 5 cubes long. The gray stick is 8 cubes long. The white stick is 3 cubes long. He wants to line them up from shortest to longest.

Compare two at a time. 3 is less than 5, so white goes before brown. 5 is less than 8, so brown goes before gray. The is white, brown, gray. Shortest to longest: 3, 5, 8.

You can also order without numbers. Line up the ends. Find the shortest one. Set it first. Then find the next shortest. Keep going until all of them are in a row.

1. Three ropes are 4 feet, 9 feet and 6 feet long. Which order is shortest to longest?

2. A jump rope is 7 feet. A scarf is 3 feet. A ladder is 10 feet. Which is longest?

3. Sam's pencil is longer than Ana's. Ana's pencil is longer than Kim's. Whose pencil is in the middle?

Section 2

Measuring with Units

7.3

Units End to End

Main ideaMeasure by laying same-size units end to end with no gaps and no overlaps.

How long is the book? Rosa uses paper clips. She lays them in a line along the edge. Each clip touches the next. No . No overlap. She counts 6 clips. The book is 6 paper clips long.

The clips must all be the same size. Big and small clips mixed give a wrong count. Gaps make the count too small. Overlaps make it too big. Start at one end. Stop at the other end.

A different gives a different number. The same book is 6 big clips long. It is 9 small clips long. The book did not change. The unit changed. A smaller unit means more units.

Think it through
Two Ways to Lay Cubes

Look at a crayon box. Ben lays 4 cubes along it, end to end, with no gaps. Jo lays 4 cubes too, but she leaves spaces between them. Her cubes reach past the end of the box.

Whose measurement is right? Why?

Words to know
unit
the one thing you use over and over to measure, like a paper clip or a cube
gap
an empty space between two units that should be touching
Check yourself

1. Tia measures a shelf with cubes. She leaves gaps between the cubes. What happens to her count?

Why: Gaps take up space that no cube covers. Fewer cubes fit, so the count is too small.

2. A desk is 5 long shoes long. You measure it again with short shoes. What happens?

Why: Short shoes cover less space each. It takes more of them to reach the end of the desk.

3. Which rule is a good rule for measuring?

Why: Same-size units that touch end to end give a true count. Gaps and mixed sizes give wrong counts.
7.4

Inches and Feet

Main ideaAn inch is a small unit. A foot is 12 inches. Use a ruler to measure.

A has marks. Each mark is one . Put the 0 end at one end of the object. Read the number at the other end. A marker ends at the 7. The marker is 7 inches long.

A is 12 inches. Your math book is about 1 foot long. A door is about 7 feet tall. Use inches for small things. Use feet for big things, like a rug or a room.

Do not start at the 1 mark. Start at 0. If you start at 1, your number is one too big. A crayon that ends at 5 looks like 5 inches. But it is only 4 inches.

Check yourself

1. A pencil starts at 0 on a ruler and ends at the 6. How long is it?

2. How many inches are in 1 foot?

Why: One foot is 12 inches. A 12-inch ruler is exactly one foot long.

3. Which is best measured in feet?

Why: A rug is big. Feet are the bigger unit, so feet fit a rug. Clips and crayons use inches.
7.5

Centimeters and Meters

Main ideaA centimeter is smaller than an inch. A meter is 100 centimeters.

A is a small unit. It is about the width of your pinky finger. Many rulers show centimeters on one side. A paper clip is about 3 centimeters long.

A is 100 centimeters. A baseball bat is about 1 meter long. A door is about 2 meters tall. Use a meter stick for big things. Use a centimeter ruler for small things.

Measure a pencil twice. In inches it is 6. In centimeters it is 15. Centimeters are smaller, so you need more of them. Both numbers are right. They just use different units.

Check yourself

1. How many centimeters are in 1 meter?

Why: One meter is 100 centimeters. A meter stick has 100 centimeter marks.

2. A ribbon is 4 inches long. You measure it again in centimeters. What happens to the number?

3. Which tool is best for measuring a hallway?

Why: A hallway is long. A meter stick is the biggest tool here, so it takes the fewest moves.
7.6

Estimate, Then Measure

Main ideaAn estimate is a careful guess. Use something you know as a guide.

An is a careful guess. You know a paper clip is about 1 inch. A crayon looks about 4 clips long. So your estimate is about 4 inches. Then you measure. It is 4 inches. Good estimate!

Big things need a bigger guide. Your math book is about 1 foot. A desk looks like 2 books. Estimate: about 2 feet. Then measure it. If it is 2 feet, your guess was close.

Estimates do not have to be exact. Close is good. If you guess 20 feet and it is 3 feet, think again. Pick a better guide. Then guess again.

Showing Data

7.7

Picture Graphs and Bar Graphs

Main ideaA graph shows counts with pictures or bars so you can compare fast.

The class votes on a favorite pet. Dog gets 6 votes. Cat gets 4. Fish gets 2. A shows one picture for each vote. The dog row has 6 pictures. The cat row has 4.

A uses bars instead of pictures. A taller bar means more. The dog bar goes up to 6. The fish bar goes up to 2. You can see the winner fast. Dog wins.

Graphs answer questions. How many more dogs than cats? 6 − 4 = 2. How many votes in all? 6 + 4 + 2 = 12. Read the numbers off the graph. Then add or subtract.

Check yourself

1. A graph shows dog 6, cat 4, fish 2. How many more dogs than cats?

Why: 6 − 4 = 2. Adding gives 10, which is a different question.

2. Dog 6, cat 4, fish 2. How many votes in all?

Why: 6 + 4 = 10, then 10 + 2 = 12 votes in all.

Why: Bars grow with the count. The tallest bar stands for the biggest number.
7.8

Line Plots

Main ideaA line plot puts an X above each measurement on a number line.

The class measures pencils in inches. The lengths are 5, 6, 6, 7, 6, 5. A shows them. Draw a number line from 5 to 7. Put an X above the number for each pencil.

Above 5 there are 2 X’s. Above 6 there are 3 X’s. Above 7 there is 1 X. The tallest stack shows the most common length. That is 6 inches.

Count all the X’s to find how many pencils. 2 + 3 + 1 = 6 pencils. How many pencils are longer than 5 inches? Count the X’s above 6 and 7. 3 + 1 = 4.

One clock on the wall and one box of coins run the whole day in Room 12.

8.1

Hour and Half Hour

Main ideaThe short hand tells the hour. Long hand on 12 means o'clock; long hand on 6 means half past.

A clock has two hands. The short hand is the . The long hand is the . When the long hand points to 12, it is o’clock. Short hand on 3, long hand on 12: 3 o’clock. We write 3:00.

When the long hand points to 6, it is . The short hand sits between two numbers. It is between 3 and 4, so it is half past 3. We write 3:30. Thirty minutes is half an hour.

A digital clock shows only numbers. 3:00 means 3 o’clock. 3:30 means half past 3. Watch out: at 3:30 the short hand is past the 3, not on it. Use the number it just passed.

What Each Coin Is Worth

Main ideaA penny is 1 cent, a nickel 5 cents, a dime 10 cents, a quarter 25 cents.

A penny is worth 1 . A nickel is worth 5 cents. A dime is worth 10 cents. A quarter is worth 25 cents. We write cents with a ¢ sign. 5¢ means 5 cents.

Size can fool you. A dime is the smallest . But it is worth more than a penny or a nickel. A nickel is bigger than a dime but worth less. Look at the coin, not its size.

Two nickels make 10 cents, the same as one dime. 5 + 5 = 10. Five pennies make one nickel. Two dimes and a nickel make 25 cents. 10 + 10 + 5 = 25. That is the same as one quarter.

8.5

Counting Mixed Coins

Main ideaStart with the biggest coins. Count on by 25, 10, 5, then 1.

Jamal has a quarter, a dime and two pennies. Start with the biggest coin. Quarter: 25. with the dime: 35. Add a penny: 36. Add a penny: 37. He has 37 cents.

Sort first. Put quarters, then dimes, then nickels, then pennies. Two dimes and three nickels: 10, 20, then 25, 30, 35. That is 35 cents. Big coins first keeps the numbers easy.

8.6

Money Word Problems

Main idea100 cents make 1 dollar. Add or subtract cents to solve money problems.

A is 100 cents. Four quarters make a dollar: 25 + 25 + 25 + 25 = 100. We write one dollar as $1.00. We write 45 cents as 45¢ or as $0.45.

Mia buys a pencil for 30¢ and an eraser for 15¢. How much in all? 30 + 15 = 45. She pays 45¢. Kai has 50¢ and spends 20¢. How much is left? 50 − 20 = 30. He has 30¢ left.

Read the question twice. In all means add. Left means subtract. Draw the coins if it helps. Then check your work: 30 + 15 = 45, and 45 − 15 = 30. It matches.

8.7

Making Change by Counting Up

Main ideaTo make change, count up from the price to the money paid.

A cookie costs 35¢. Tom pays with two quarters, 50¢. How much does he get? Count up from 35. From 35 to 40 is a nickel. From 40 to 50 is a dime. 5 + 10 = 15. Tom gets 15¢ back.

Check with subtraction. 50 − 35 = 15. Same answer. Counting up is like climbing stairs. Start at the price. Stop at the money paid. The steps you took are the change.

Try another one. Juice is 60¢. Ana pays $1.00. Count up: 60 to 70 is 10. 70 to 100 is 30. 10 + 30 = 40. The change is 40¢. Check: 100 − 60 = 40. Yes.

Why: Count by fives to the 10: 10 fives is 50. It is 50 minutes past.

8. One quarter and two dimes. How many cents?

Why: 25 + 10 = 35, then 35 + 10 = 45 cents.

9. A pencil costs 30¢. A pen costs 45¢. How much for both?

Why: 30 + 45 = 75. Both together cost 75¢.

10. A snack costs 65¢. You pay with three quarters, 75¢. How much change do you get?

Why: Count up from 65 to 75. That is 10. Check: 75 − 65 = 10.
