Reasoning and Fallacies

Every room until now asked how something was said. This one asks whether it follows. An argument has parts — premises, a conclusion, and a warrant connecting them — and it can fail at any of the three while every sentence in it stays true. So the two things worth learning are the shape of a working argument and the standard ways one breaks. Two warnings come with that. Naming a fallacy is not refuting it: you have to show the exact step that fails. And spotting a bad argument does not make the conclusion false — bad arguments are given for true claims every day, which is why the honest move is to say the argument does not establish it, not that it is wrong. Five tabs — cards for the parts and the classic failures, hints with the three questions, a quiz built on arguments that are valid and unsound, and a workshop where you diagnose arguments and repair one.

Every sentence is true, and it still fails

Two premises and a conclusion. Both premises are true — the register really was checked twice — and I have no quarrel with either. So where is there anything to examine?

An argument has premises, a conclusion, and a warrant connecting them — and the warrant is almost never written down, which is why it is where most arguments break. So state the warrant out loud and test it. Two warnings: naming a fallacy is not showing it, and a failed argument leaves the conclusion open rather than false.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

The premises are true, so the argument is fine.

An argument is not a list of true statements — it is a movement from some to another, and the movement is a separate thing. Premises true, conclusion true, and the step between them can still not hold. That step is the warrant, and it is almost never on the page, so a reader who checks every sentence for truth will pass an argument that fails.

The question is not “is it true?” but “does it follow?”

⚠️ People often think…

That is a slippery slope. I have refuted it.

The name is a category, and whether the argument belongs in it is exactly what is in dispute. If the writer HAS given a reason for each link, it is a legitimate prediction and your label is false. Quote the specific step, state the warrant it needs, show that warrant is not available. The name is a shorthand for a demonstration you still owe.

Quote the step. The label alone earns nothing and can be wrong.

⚠️ People often think…

The argument was fallacious, so the conclusion is false.

That is the fallacy fallacy, and it is invalid. People give terrible reasons for true claims constantly — out of haste, or because they know something they cannot articulate. A failed argument tells you nothing about the claim, so the honest and more demanding sentence is: this argument does not establish it. You are still obliged to find out.

A failed argument leaves the question open, not settled against.

Premises, warrant, conclusion

1 Watch one

Diagnose: “Every successful shop on this street opens on Sundays, so Sunday opening causes success.”

  1. Premise: all the successful shops open on Sundays. Suppose it is true — we are not disputing it.
  2. Warrant, stated out loud: if every success shares a feature, that feature caused the success.
  3. Test it: what about the shops that opened on Sundays and closed anyway? They are not on the street to be counted. That is survivorship bias.
  4. Conclude honestly: the argument does not establish it. Sunday opening may help — but you would need the shops that failed, and nobody has them.

Helpful Hints

⚡ The one idea

An argument can fail while every sentence in it is true. The premises can be true, the conclusion can be true, and the step between them can still not hold — that step is the warrant, and it is almost never written down. So the analysis is not “is this true?” but “does this follow?”, and the answer lives in the join rather than in either end. Two consequences: naming a fallacy is not refuting it, and finding a bad argument does not make the conclusion false.

📐 The parts of an argument

PartWhat it isFails when
Premisesomething offered as already agreedit is false, or not actually agreed
Conclusionwhat the premises are meant to get you toit says more than the premises support
Warrantthe unwritten rule letting you crossit is not true, and nobody stated it

⚖️ Valid is not the same as true

An argument is VALID when the conclusion would have to follow IF the premises were true. It is SOUND when it is valid and the premises are actually true. So a valid argument can reach a false conclusion from false premises, and a true conclusion can sit on top of a hopeless argument. Keep the two questions separate: does it follow, and are the premises true. Most real disagreements are about the second, and most marks are awarded for noticing the first.

NameThe step that fails

False equivalencetwo unlike things weighed as equal

🚫 The fallacy fallacy

Finding a fallacy in an argument tells you the argument fails. It does not tell you the conclusion is false. People give terrible reasons for true claims constantly, and a student who writes “this is a slippery slope, therefore the policy is fine” has committed the very error they were pointing at. The honest sentence is: this argument does not establish the conclusion — which leaves the question open, where it belongs.
