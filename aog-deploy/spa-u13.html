The Present Tense

Present Tense

Most of what you say in a day happens in the present: I work, you eat, we live here, they are coming. In Spanish, each of those ideas lives inside one verb whose ending tells you who is acting. *Hablo* (I speak), hablas (you speak), hablamos (we speak). Once you see how stems and endings fit together, thousands of regular verbs open up at once.

This unit follows two families. One runs a taquería and uses regular -ar, -er and -ir verbs all week long. The other packs for a trip to Guadalajara and runs into the irregular verbs every speaker needs, like tengo, hago, voy and sé. Along the way you will learn when to use saber or conocer and how to talk about plans with ir a plus an infinitive.

By the end, you will be able to conjugate any regular verb, recognize vosotros and ustedes, use the most common irregular yo forms, and describe your week and your weekend plans in clear, correct Spanish. Heritage speakers will get names for patterns they already use; new learners will get a system they can trust.

The big question

How does the present tense let you describe your life, your habits and your plans in Spanish?

Regular -ar, -er and -ir Verbs

Regular Verbs
Big questionHow can one small ending on a verb tell you who is acting and when?
The story

A Week at Taquería Los Primos

One family, one small restaurant, and a whole week of verbs in the present tense.

Marisol is fifteen, and her family runs a small taquería on a busy corner. Every morning her father opens the door and says, "Abro a las siete" (I open at seven). Her mother answers from the kitchen, "Y yo cocino" (And I cook). Nobody says the word for "I" twice. The verb ending already tells everyone who is talking.

On Monday, Marisol's little brother Tomás asks, "¿Quién lava los platos?" (Who washes the dishes?). Marisol points at him and laughs: "Tú lavas los platos" (You wash the dishes). Tomás groans, but he knows the rule of the house. On school days, the kids work one hour after class, and then they do homework at a back table.

By Wednesday the regular customers arrive. Don Ernesto always orders the same thing. "Siempre como tacos al pastor" (I always eat tacos al pastor), he tells Marisol. A group of nurses from the hospital nearby comes in at noon. "Nosotras comemos aquí los miércoles" (We eat here on Wednesdays), one of them says, and they share a table by the window.

On Saturday, cousins visiting from Spain help out for the day. Marisol hears her cousin Lucía say, "¿Vosotros vivís cerca?" (Do you all live nearby?). In Mexico and in Chicago, Marisol would say "¿Ustedes viven cerca?" instead. Both forms are correct Spanish. They simply come from different places where Spanish is spoken.

On Sunday night the taquería is closed. The family sits together and eats dinner that someone else cooked for once. Marisol thinks about how many verbs she used this week: abrir, cocinar, lavar, comer, vivir. Each one changed its ending to fit the person. She realizes she has been conjugating verbs all week without even trying.

Talk about itIn the story, people rarely say yo, tú or nosotros. How did the listeners still know who was doing each action?
Section 1

Stems and Endings

25.1

What an Infinitive Is

Main ideaEvery Spanish verb has a base form called the infinitive, which ends in -ar, -er or -ir.

Picture a menu board at a taquería with a sign that says "Aquí puedes bien" (Here you can eat well). The word comer (to eat) is an infinitive. An infinitive is the base form of a verb, the form you find in a dictionary. In English, infinitives usually start with "to": to eat, to speak, to live.

Spanish infinitives do not need a separate word like "to." Instead, they end in one of three pairs of letters: -ar, -er or -ir. (to speak) ends in -ar. Comer (to eat) ends in -er. (to live) ends in -ir. These three groups are called the three verb families, and each family has its own set of endings.

When you learn a new verb, look at the last two letters first. They tell you which family the verb belongs to, and the family tells you which endings to use. Trabajar (to work) is an -ar verb. Beber (to drink) is an -er verb. Escribir (to write) is an -ir verb. Knowing the family is the first step to using any verb correctly.

Think it through
Sort the Verbs by Family

Here are six verbs from a taquería kitchen: cocinar (to cook), lavar (to wash), beber (to drink), abrir (to open), vender (to sell), servir (to serve). Look only at the last two letters. -ar: cocinar, lavar. -er: beber, vender. -ir: abrir, servir.

Which family do escuchar (to listen), leer (to read) and recibir (to receive) belong to?

Words to know
el infinitivo
the infinitive — the base form of a verb, ending in -ar, -er or -ir (hablar, comer, vivir)
hablar
to speak or talk — a regular -ar verb
comer
to eat — a regular -er verb
vivir
to live — a regular -ir verb
Check yourself

1. Which of these is an -er verb?

Why: Beber (to drink) ends in -er. Bailar and estudiar end in -ar, and escribir ends in -ir.

2. What does the infinitive vivir mean?

Why: Vivir means to live. Ver is to see, venir is to come and vender is to sell.

3. What do the last two letters of an infinitive tell you?

Why: The -ar, -er or -ir ending tells you the verb's family, and the family decides the endings you add.
25.2

Finding the Stem

Main ideaTo conjugate a regular verb, drop the -ar, -er or -ir and keep the stem.

Marisol writes on the kitchen whiteboard: "Yo cocino hoy" (I cook today). She started with cocinar (to cook). To get cocino, she took off the last two letters, -ar, and kept cocin-. That front part is called the stem. The stem carries the meaning of the verb: cocin- means cooking, no matter who does it.

Every regular verb works the same way. Hablar drops -ar and leaves habl-. Comer drops -er and leaves com-. Vivir drops -ir and leaves viv-. Then you add a new ending that matches the person doing the action. This process, changing the verb to match its subject, is called to conjugate a verb.

A common mistake is to keep the whole infinitive and add an ending, as in "hablaro." That form does not exist. Another mistake is to cut off too much, as in "hablo" becoming "hab-o." Cut only the last two letters. Then check: the stem plus the ending should sound like a real word, such as habl + o = hablo (I speak).

Think it through
Stem Surgery

trabajar → trabaj- + o = trabajo (I work). beber → beb- + o = bebo (I drink). escribir → escrib- + o = escribo (I write). The stem stays the same. Only the ending moves.

What is the stem of vender (to sell), and what is the yo form?

Words to know
la raíz
the stem — the part of the verb left after you drop -ar, -er or -ir (habl-, com-, viv-)
conjugar
to conjugate — to change a verb's ending to match who is doing the action
cocinar
to cook — a regular -ar verb: cocino, cocinas, cocina
Check yourself

1. What is the stem of escuchar (to listen)?

Why: Drop the last two letters, -ar, and escuch- is left.

2. Which form is correct for "I write" (escribir)?

Why: Drop -ir to get escrib-, then add -o for yo: escribo.

3. What does it mean to conjugate a verb?

Why: Conjugating means changing the verb's ending so it matches who is doing the action.
25.3

Subject Pronouns

Main ideaSpanish has subject pronouns for I, you, he, she, we, you all and they, including both vosotros and ustedes.

At the taquería counter, Marisol talks to many kinds of people. To her brother she says (you, informal). To an older customer like Don Ernesto she says usted (you, formal). These words are subject pronouns, the words that stand for the person doing the action. English has one "you," but Spanish has several.

Here is the full set. Yo (I). Tú (you, informal). Él (he), ella (she), usted (you, formal). Nosotros or nosotras (we). or vosotras (you all, informal, used mainly in Spain). Ellos or ellas (they), and (you all). Notice that él has an accent mark. Without it, el means "the."

In Latin America, including Mexico and most Spanish-speaking families in Chicago, ustedes is used for any group of "you all," friendly or formal. In most of Spain, people use vosotros for friends and family and ustedes for formal groups. You should recognize vosotros, but you can use ustedes with anyone and be understood everywhere.

Think it through
Who Is Who at the Counter

Marisol to her brother: "Tú lavas los platos." Marisol to Don Ernesto: "¿Usted quiere salsa verde?" Marisol to three nurses: "¿Ustedes comen aquí hoy?" Lucía, from Madrid, to her cousins: "¿Vosotros coméis aquí?"

Which pronoun would you use to speak to your principal, and which to speak to your best friend?

Words to know
tú
you (informal, one person) — for friends, family and kids; has an accent mark
usted
you (formal, one person) — for adults you respect; uses the same verb form as él and ella
vosotros
you all (informal) — used mainly in Spain; vosotras for an all-female group
ustedes
you all — used for any group in Latin America; formal groups in Spain
Check yourself

1. Which pronoun do you use for a group of friends in Mexico?

Why: In Latin America, ustedes is the pronoun for any group of "you all," including friends.

2. What is the difference between él and el?

Why: The accent mark matters: él is the pronoun he, and el is the article the.

3. Which pronoun fits a respected older neighbor you speak to directly?

Why: Usted is the formal "you" for one person, used to show respect.
Section 2

The Three Sets of Endings

25.4

Conjugating -ar Verbs

Main ideaRegular -ar verbs take the endings -o, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an.

Marisol’s father makes a schedule for who works the counter. "Yo trabajo el lunes, tú trabajas el martes, mamá trabaja el miércoles" (I work Monday, you work Tuesday, Mom works Wednesday). All three forms come from trabajar (to work). The stem trabaj- stays the same, and the ending changes: -o, -as, -a.

Here is hablar (to speak) in all six forms. Yo hablo. Tú hablas. Él, ella or usted habla. Nosotros hablamos. Vosotros habláis. Ellos, ellas or ustedes hablan. The endings are -o, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an. Notice that the vosotros ending, -áis, has an accent mark. The others do not.

English speakers often forget the -s on the tú form and say "tú habla." In English, "you speak" has no special ending, so the -s feels strange. But in Spanish, -s is a strong signal for tú in the present tense. Another mistake is using -o for everyone: "ella hablo." Remember that -o belongs only to yo.

Think it through
Hablar, Step by Step

hablar → habl-. yo habl + o = hablo. tú habl + as = hablas. él/ella/usted habl + a = habla. nosotros habl + amos = hablamos. vosotros habl + áis = habláis. ellos/ellas/ustedes habl + an = hablan.

Now try lavar (to wash). What are the tú and nosotros forms?

Words to know
trabajar
to work — a regular -ar verb: trabajo, trabajas, trabaja, trabajamos, trabajáis, trabajan
lavar
to wash — a regular -ar verb: lavo, lavas, lava
la terminación
the ending — the letters added to the stem to show the subject (-o, -as, -a…)
Check yourself

1. Choose the correct form: Nosotros ___ en la taquería. (trabajar)

Why: Nosotros takes -amos in -ar verbs: trabajamos.

2. Choose the correct form: Tú ___ los platos. (lavar)

Why: Tú takes -as: lavas. Lava is for él, ella or usted.

3. Which sentence is correct?

Why: Ella uses the -a ending: habla. The -o ending belongs only to yo.
25.5

Conjugating -er Verbs

Main ideaRegular -er verbs take the endings -o, -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en.

At lunch, Tomás come (eats) a quesadilla while his sister (drinks) agua de horchata. Both verbs are -er verbs: comer (to eat) and beber (to drink). Look at the endings. Come ends in -e, and bebe ends in -e. In -er verbs, the vowel a from the -ar endings switches to e.

Here is comer in all six forms. Yo como. Tú comes. Él, ella or usted come. Nosotros comemos. Vosotros coméis. Ellos, ellas or ustedes comen. The endings are -o, -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en. The yo form still ends in -o, just like -ar verbs. That part never changes in regular verbs.

A helpful trick is to think of the -er endings as the -ar endings with e in place of a. Hablas becomes comes. Hablamos becomes comemos. Hablan becomes comen. Learners sometimes mix the families and say "comas" or "coman" for the present. Those are real words, but they belong to a different verb form, so they are wrong here.

Think it through
Comer, Step by Step

comer → com-. yo com + o = como. tú com + es = comes. él/ella/usted com + e = come. nosotros com + emos = comemos. vosotros com + éis = coméis. ellos/ellas/ustedes com + en = comen.

Conjugate vender (to sell) for ella and for ellos.

Words to know
beber
to drink — a regular -er verb: bebo, bebes, bebe
vender
to sell — a regular -er verb: vendo, vendes, vende
aprender
to learn — a regular -er verb: aprendo, aprendes, aprende
Check yourself

1. Choose the correct form: Ellos ___ tacos los viernes. (vender)

Why: Ellos takes -en in -er verbs: venden. Vendan is not a present-tense form.

2. Choose the correct form: ¿Tú ___ agua o jugo? (beber)

Why: Tú takes -es in -er verbs: bebes.

3. How do the -er endings differ from the -ar endings?

Why: The -er endings use e where -ar endings use a: -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en. Yo still ends in -o.
25.6

Conjugating -ir Verbs

Main ideaRegular -ir verbs share most -er endings but use -imos and -ís for nosotros and vosotros.

Marisol’s family vive (lives) above the taquería. Her aunt’s family lives across town. "Nosotros vivimos aquí, y ellos viven en Pilsen" (We live here, and they live in Pilsen), Marisol explains. Vivir (to live) is an -ir verb, and its endings look almost exactly like the endings for comer.

Here is vivir in all six forms. Yo vivo. Tú vives. Él, ella or usted vive. Nosotros vivimos. Vosotros vivís. Ellos, ellas or ustedes viven. Compare it with comer: como, comes, come, comemos, coméis, comen. Only two forms are different. Nosotros uses -imos, and vosotros uses -ís.

The most common mistake is saying "vivemos" because comemos sounds so similar. Remember that the nosotros ending keeps the vowel of the infinitive: hablar gives hablamos, comer gives comemos, vivir gives vivimos. The same is true for vosotros: habláis, coméis, vivís. Other common -ir verbs are (to write) and abrir (to open).

Think it through
Comer and Vivir Side by Side

yo: como / vivo. tú: comes / vives. él: come / vive. nosotros: comemos / vivimos. vosotros: coméis / vivís. ellos: comen / viven. Four forms match exactly. The nosotros and vosotros forms keep the i from vivir.

What is the nosotros form of escribir? Say why it is not escribemos.

Words to know
escribir
to write — a regular -ir verb: escribo, escribes, escribe, escribimos
abrir
to open — a regular -ir verb: abro, abres, abre, abrimos
recibir
to receive — a regular -ir verb: recibo, recibes, recibe
Check yourself

1. Choose the correct form: Nosotros ___ en Chicago. (vivir)

Why: Nosotros in -ir verbs takes -imos: vivimos. Vivemos mixes in the -er ending.

2. Choose the correct form: Mi papá ___ la puerta a las siete. (abrir)

Why: Él (mi papá) takes -e in -ir verbs: abre.

3. Which two -ir forms are different from the -er forms?

Why: Only nosotros (-imos) and vosotros (-ís) differ; the other four -ir endings match -er.
25.7

Choosing the Right Ending

Main ideaFind the subject, find the verb family, then pick the one ending that fits both.

A customer asks, "¿Ustedes venden aguas frescas?" (Do you all sell fruit drinks?). To answer, Marisol makes two quick decisions. First, who is the (subject)? She is answering for the family, so the subject is nosotros. Second, which family is the verb? Vender is an -er verb. So the answer is "Sí, vendemos aguas frescas."

You can use this two-step check on any regular verb. Step one: find the subject and its person. Step two: find the verb family from the infinitive. The ending sits where the two meet. For ellas plus escribir, you get escriben. For tú plus cocinar, you get cocinas. For yo plus any regular verb, you get -o.

Remember that usted and ustedes use the third-person endings, the same as él and ellos, even though they mean "you." So you say "Usted habla muy bien" (You speak very well), not "usted hablas." This surprises many English speakers, because in English "you" never sounds like "he." In Spanish, usted borrows the él form of the verb.

Think it through
Two Questions, One Ending

Subject: ellas. Verb: escribir (-ir). Ending: -en → escriben. Subject: usted. Verb: trabajar (-ar). Ending: -a → trabaja. Subject: nosotros. Verb: aprender (-er). Ending: -emos → aprendemos.

Try it: subject ustedes, verb abrir. What form do you get?

Words to know
el sujeto
the subject — the person or thing doing the action of the verb
vender
to sell — a regular -er verb: vendemos (we sell), venden (they sell)
el agua fresca
a fruit drink made with water, fruit and sugar; uses el because agua starts with a stressed a (las aguas frescas)
Check yourself

1. Choose the correct form: Usted ___ muy bien. (cocinar)

Why: Usted uses the third-person singular ending, the same as él: cocina.

2. Choose the correct form: Ellas ___ cartas a su abuela. (escribir)

Why: Ellas plus an -ir verb takes -en: escriben.

3. A customer asks, "¿Ustedes venden aguas frescas?" How does the family answer?

Why: The family answers for themselves, so the subject is nosotros: vendemos.
Section 3

Using the Present Tense

25.8

Dropping the Subject Pronoun

Main ideaBecause the ending already shows the subject, Spanish usually leaves out yo, tú and nosotros.

When Marisol’s father unlocks the door, he says, "Abro a las siete," not "Yo abro a las siete." The -o ending on abro already means "I." Saying yo is not wrong, but most of the time native speakers leave it out. Spanish is sometimes called a pro-drop language because it can drop the subject pronoun.

English cannot do this. "Open at seven" sounds like a command, not a statement about yourself. That is why English speakers learning Spanish tend to put yo or tú in every sentence. It is correct grammar, but it can sound stiff or even a little pushy, as if you were stressing "I, and not someone else."

Speakers do keep the pronoun in three situations. They keep it for contrast: "Yo cocino y tú lavas" (I cook and you wash). They keep it for emphasis. And they keep it when the verb form could mean more than one person, since habla can mean he, she or you (usted). In that case, saying ella habla or usted habla makes it clear.

Think it through
Pronoun or No Pronoun?

Tomás: ¿Trabajas hoy? (Are you working today?) Marisol: Sí, trabajo hasta las cinco. (Yes, I work until five.) Tomás: Yo no. Yo estudio. (Not me. I study.) Marisol drops the pronoun. Tomás keeps yo to contrast himself with his sister.

Why does Tomás say yo but Marisol does not?

Words to know
el pronombre
the pronoun — a word that stands for a person, like yo, tú or ella
abrir
to open — abro (I open), a regular -ir verb
el contraste
contrast — a reason to keep the pronoun: Yo cocino y tú lavas.
Check yourself

1. Why can Spanish speakers say "Comemos a las dos" without nosotros?

Why: The ending -emos only fits nosotros, so the pronoun is not needed.

2. In which sentence is the pronoun most useful?

Why: Habla could mean he, she or you (usted), so usted makes the subject clear. The others have unique endings.

3. Which sentence uses yo for contrast?

Why: Yo and tú are kept to contrast two different people doing two different jobs.
25.9

Habits and Right Now

Main ideaThe Spanish present tense covers both what you do regularly and what you are doing now.

Don Ernesto says, "Como aquí todos los días" (I eat here every day). That is a habit. A minute later his phone rings, and he says, "No puedo hablar, como ahora" (I can’t talk, I’m eating now). Both times he uses como. The same Spanish (present tense) covers "I eat" and "I am eating."

English has two present forms, the simple present (I eat) and the progressive (I am eating). Spanish often uses just one. That is why "¿Qué haces?" can mean "What do you do?" or "What are you doing?" Context tells you which. Words like (now) point to right now, and todos los días (every day) points to a habit.

A very common mistake is to translate "I am eating" word for word as "Yo soy comiendo" or "Yo estoy como." Neither is correct. For most everyday sentences, the simple present is enough: "Como." Spanish does have a progressive form, estoy comiendo, but you will learn that later. For now, trust the simple present.

Think it through
Same Verb, Two Meanings

Tomás (texting): ¿Qué haces? Marisol: Lavo los platos. Ahora no puedo. (I'm washing the dishes. I can't right now.) Later, a new worker asks Marisol about her job. Marisol: Lavo los platos los lunes. (I wash the dishes on Mondays.)

Which word in each answer tells you whether it is a habit or right now?

Words to know
el presente
the present tense — the verb form for habits and for actions happening now
ahora
now — points to an action happening at this moment
todos los días
every day — points to a habit
Check yourself

1. How do you say "I am studying now" in simple Spanish?

Why: The simple present estudio can mean "I am studying"; the other choices mix verbs incorrectly or leave the verb unconjugated.

2. What does "Mi abuela vive con nosotros" mean?

Why: Vive is the present tense of vivir for ella: she lives.

3. Which phrase shows that an action is a habit?

Why: Todos los días (every day) signals a repeated habit; the others mean now.
25.10

Siempre, a Veces, Nunca

Main ideaFrequency words like siempre, a veces and nunca tell how often, and nunca goes before the verb.

Marisol takes orders and notices patterns. "Don Ernesto pide pastor" (Don Ernesto always orders pastor). "Las enfermeras comen aquí" (The nurses sometimes eat here). "Mi hermano lava los platos sin quejarse" (My brother never washes the dishes without complaining). These words tell how often something happens.

Siempre (always) and a veces (sometimes) are flexible. They can go before the verb, after it, or at the end: "A veces trabajo," "Trabajo a veces." Other useful ones are todos los días (every day), los lunes (on Mondays) and casi nunca (almost never). Notice that "los lunes" with los means every Monday, a repeated habit.

Nunca (never) usually goes right before the verb: "Nunca como carne" (I never eat meat). If you put it after the verb, Spanish needs no in front: "No como carne nunca." English speakers often say "Como nunca carne," following English word order. That sounds wrong. Put nunca first, or use no plus the verb plus nunca.

Think it through
How Often at the Taquería

Papá siempre abre a las siete. Mamá cocina todos los días. Tomás a veces lava los platos. Marisol casi nunca llega tarde. Nadie trabaja los domingos. (Nobody works on Sundays.)

Write two sentences about your own week: one with siempre and one with nunca.

Words to know
siempre
always — Siempre como tacos. (I always eat tacos.)
a veces
sometimes — A veces trabajo los sábados.
nunca
never — goes before the verb: Nunca bebo café. Or: No bebo café nunca.
Check yourself

1. Which sentence correctly says "I never drink coffee"?

Why: Nunca goes before the verb, and yo takes bebo. After the verb, nunca would need no: No bebo café nunca.

2. What does "A veces comemos en casa" mean?

Why: A veces means sometimes, and comemos is nosotros: we eat.

3. What does "los lunes" add to "Trabajo los lunes"?

Why: Los plus a day of the week shows a repeated habit: every Monday.

Yo-Irregulars and Ir a

Irregular Verbs
Big questionWhy do some of the most common Spanish verbs break the pattern, and how do you talk about plans?
The story

Packing for Guadalajara

A family trip to see relatives means plans, packing and a lot of irregular verbs.

Daniel is sixteen and lives in Little Village with his mom and his younger sister, Ana. In two weeks, they are flying to Guadalajara, in the Mexican state of Jalisco, to visit his grandmother. Daniel's mom calls it the trip of the year. Daniel calls it the week without his friends, but secretly he is excited.

At dinner his mom makes a list. "Yo hago las maletas el viernes" (I pack the suitcases on Friday), she says. "Tú traes los regalos para la familia" (You bring the gifts for the family). Daniel answers, "Yo traigo los regalos, pero no sé qué comprar" (I'll bring the gifts, but I don't know what to buy).

Ana has a question. "¿Conoces a los primos de Zapopan?" (Do you know the cousins from Zapopan?). Daniel shakes his head. "No los conozco. Sé sus nombres, nada más" (I don't know them. I know their names, nothing more). He has seen them only in photos on his grandmother's phone.

Then his mom reads the plan for the week out loud. "El sábado vamos a visitar a la abuela. El domingo vamos a ir al centro. El lunes, ustedes van a conocer a los primos" (On Saturday we're going to visit Grandma. On Sunday we're going to go downtown. On Monday, you two are going to meet the cousins).

Section 1

The Yo-Go Verbs

26.1

Verbs with -go in the Yo Form

Main ideaSome common verbs add a g in the yo form, like hago, pongo, salgo and traigo, but stay regular elsewhere.

Daniel’s mom says, "Yo hago las maletas" (I pack the suitcases, literally I make the suitcases). If hacer (to do, to make) were regular, the yo form would be "haco." But it is hago. Hacer belongs to a small group called the yo-go verbs, because the yo form ends in -go.

Four of the most useful yo-go verbs are hacer, (to put), (to leave, go out) and (to bring). Their yo forms are hago, pongo, salgo and traigo. Here is the good news: in the other forms, they are regular. Hacer goes hago, haces, hace, hacemos, hacéis, hacen. Salir goes salgo, sales, sale, salimos, salís, salen.

A common mistake is to keep the g in every form: "tú hagas" or "ella pongue." Only yo gets the g. Another mistake is to make yo regular: "yo salo" or "yo trao." Those forms sound natural to a learner, but no native speaker says them. Memorize the yo form and use regular endings everywhere else.

Find the four yo-go forms in Daniel's lines. Then say which forms in the dialogue are regular.

to put or place — yo pongo, tú pones, él pone
salir
to leave or go out — yo salgo, tú sales, él sale, nosotros salimos

to bring — yo traigo, tú traes, él trae

26.2

Tener, Venir and Decir

Main ideaTener, venir and decir have -go in the yo form and also change their stem in other forms.

Ana asks her brother, "¿Tienes el pasaporte?" (Do you have the passport?). Daniel checks his backpack. "Sí, tengo el pasaporte" (Yes, I have the passport). Tener (to have) is a yo-go verb, but it has an extra twist. In some forms, the e in the stem becomes ie: tienes, tiene, tienen.

Here is tener in full: tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen. (to come) follows the same shape: vengo, vienes, viene, venimos, venís, vienen. Notice that nosotros and vosotros keep the plain stem. You will learn more about this stem change, called the boot pattern, in the next unit.

(to say, to tell) is similar but changes e to i: digo, dices, dice, decimos, decís, dicen. English speakers often say "yo teno" or "tú tenes" because they expect a regular pattern. Practice these three verbs out loud until tengo, vengo and digo sound automatic, because you will use them every day.

Think it through

26.3

Conozco, Doy and Veo

Main ideaA few other verbs are irregular only in the yo form: conozco, sé, doy and veo.

Daniel says, "No conozco a los primos" (I don’t know the cousins). Conocer (to know, to be familiar with) is regular in every form except yo, where it adds a z: conozco. Many verbs ending in -cer or -cir after a vowel do the same, like parecer (parezco) and traducir (traduzco).

(to give) has the yo form doy, and the rest is simple: das, da, damos, dais, dan. Notice that dais has no accent because it is only one syllable. (to see) has the yo form veo, and then ves, ve, vemos, veis, ven. These verbs are short, so the irregular yo form stands out.

Saber (to know a fact) has the most surprising yo form of all: sé. It has an accent mark to tell it apart from se, a different little word. The rest of saber is regular: sabes, sabe, sabemos, sabéis, saben. Learners often write "yo sabo," which follows the pattern but is wrong. Say "No sé" (I don’t know) every time you want to practice.

Section 2

Saber or Conocer

26.4

Knowing Facts: Saber

Main ideaUse saber for facts, information and knowing how to do something.

Kevin asks Daniel, "¿Sabes cuándo sales?" (Do you know when you leave?). Daniel answers, "Sí, sé la fecha: el quince" (Yes, I know the date: the fifteenth). A date is a fact, a piece of information. When you know information, Spanish uses . English uses one verb, know, for many things. Spanish splits it into two.

Saber is also used before an infinitive to mean "to know how to." "Sé nadar" means "I know how to swim." "Mi abuela sabe cocinar birria" means "My grandmother knows how to cook birria." Notice that Spanish does not need a word for "how" in this meaning. English speakers often add cómo, but "sé cómo nadar" is not the usual way to say it.

Saber often comes before question words or si (whether, if). "No sé dónde está el pasaporte" (I don’t know where the passport is). "¿Sabes si llueve en Guadalajara?" (Do you know if it rains in Guadalajara?). If the thing you know could be written down as an (piece of information), saber is usually the right choice.

26.5

Knowing People and Places: Conocer

Main ideaUse conocer for being familiar with people, places and things, and add a before a person.

Ana asks, "¿Conoces a los primos?" (Do you know the cousins?). You cannot write a person down as a fact, so this is conocer. Conocer means to know in the sense of being acquainted or familiar. You conocer a person, a city, a restaurant or a song. "Conozco Chicago muy bien" (I know Chicago very well).

When the thing you know is a person, Spanish adds a small word, a, before the person. This is called the . "Conozco a tu mamá" (I know your mom). "No conocemos a los primos" (We don’t know the cousins). There is no English word for it, so English speakers often leave it out. With places, you do not use it: "Conozco Guadalajara."

Conocer can also mean to meet someone for the first time. "El lunes vamos a conocer a los primos" means "On Monday we are going to meet the cousins." So a quick test helps: if you mean acquainted with or meet, use conocer. If you mean information or how to do something, use saber.

26.6

Choosing Between Them

Main ideaAsk yourself: is it a fact or skill (saber), or a person, place or first meeting (conocer)?

Daniel’s grandmother calls on video. "¿Sabes mi dirección?" (Do you know my address?). "Sí, abuela, la sé" (Yes, Grandma, I know it). "¿Y conoces mi barrio?" (And do you know my neighborhood?). "No, no lo conozco" (No, I don’t know it). One conversation, both verbs, and each has its own job.

Here is a quick (test) you can run in your head. Can you finish the sentence with a fact, a question word or an infinitive? Then use saber: sé que, sé dónde, sé nadar. Is the thing a person, a place or something you have experienced, like a book or a song? Then use conocer: conozco a Ana, conozco Pilsen, conozco esa canción.

Heritage speakers usually choose correctly by ear, but it helps to name the rule when writing. New learners often choose saber for everything because it feels closer to "know." A good habit is to picture the object. If you can shake hands with it or walk around in it, you probably need conocer.

Section 3

Going and Plans

26.7

The Verb Ir

Main ideaIr (to go) is fully irregular: voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van.

Daniel tells his friend, "Voy a Guadalajara" (I’m going to Guadalajara). Voy comes from the infinitive (to go). Ir is so irregular that none of its present forms look like the infinitive. You cannot find a stem to drop. You just memorize it: voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van.

The good news is that once you learn voy, the rest follows an -ar pattern with the letter v: v-as, v-a, v-amos, v-ais, v-an. The vosotros form, vais, has no accent because it is one syllable. Ir is almost always followed by a (to) and a place. When a meets el, they join into al: "Vamos al centro" (We’re going downtown).

Learners sometimes say "yo vo" or "nosotros imos," trying to build forms from ir. Others forget the contraction and say "a el parque." Spanish always writes al for a + el. But a + la stays two words: "Voy a la playa" (I’m going to the beach). Only el combines.

26.8

Ir a + Infinitive

Main ideaTo talk about the near future, use a form of ir, then a, then an infinitive: Voy a comer.

Kevin texts, "¿Qué vas a hacer allá?" (What are you going to do there?). Daniel answers, "Voy a comer mucho" (I’m going to eat a lot). This pattern is called ir a + infinitive. It works just like "going to" in English, which makes it one of the easiest ways to talk about the (future).

The formula has three parts. First, conjugate ir for the subject: voy, vas, va, vamos, vais, van. Second, add a. Third, add the verb in its infinitive form, with no changes. "Ana va a visitar a la abuela." "Nosotros vamos a salir el viernes." "Ustedes van a conocer a los primos." Only ir changes; the second verb never does.

The two most common mistakes are forgetting a and conjugating the second verb. "Voy comer" is missing a. "Voy a como" conjugates the second verb, which is wrong. Check both parts each time. You can also add time words to show when: mañana (tomorrow), el fin de semana (the weekend), esta noche (tonight).

26.9

Planning a Weekend

Main ideaCombine ir a, days of the week and yo-irregulars to describe weekend plans clearly.

Before the trip, Daniel’s friends plan a weekend in Chicago. Kevin says, "El sábado voy a jugar fútbol en el parque. ¿Vienes?" (On Saturday I’m going to play soccer in the park. Are you coming?). Daniel says, "No puedo. Tengo que hacer la maleta" (I can’t. I have to pack). Tener que plus an infinitive means "to have to."

Plans often mix the present and ir a. The present can describe a fixed schedule: "El vuelo sale el viernes" (The flight leaves on Friday). Ir a describes what you intend: "Voy a dormir en el avión" (I’m going to sleep on the plane). Both are correct, and native speakers use them side by side in the same conversation.

Days of the week in Spanish are not capitalized: lunes, martes, miércoles, jueves, viernes, sábado, domingo. Use el for one day (el sábado, this Saturday) and los for a habit (los sábados, on Saturdays). Put it all together and you can say a lot: "El salgo con mi familia y vamos a comer en casa de mi tía."

26.10

Talking About a Trip

Main ideaA short trip description uses ir, ir a, the yo-go verbs and saber or conocer together.

On the plane, Daniel writes a short message to post when he lands. "Hoy voy a Guadalajara. Traigo regalos para mi abuela. No conozco a mis primos, pero voy a conocerlos mañana. No sé qué vamos a hacer, pero sé que vamos a comer bien." Every verb from this chapter is in those few lines.

Guadalajara is the capital of the state of Jalisco and one of the largest cities in Mexico. Jalisco is widely known as the home of mariachi music and of tequila, which is named after a town in the state. People from Guadalajara are often called tapatíos (people from Guadalajara). Many families in Chicago have roots in Jalisco and visit relatives there.

When you describe a trip, start with where you are going (voy a…), then what you bring or do (traigo, hago), then who or what you know (conozco, sé), and finish with plans (voy a + infinitive). This simple order helps you write a clear paragraph. It also lets you check each verb against the rule you learned for it.
