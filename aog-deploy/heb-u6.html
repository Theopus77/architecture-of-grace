6Unit

Kings and Prophets

Nevi'im

This unit follows the books of Samuel and Kings, in the part of the Tanakh called Nevi'im, the Prophets. It begins when the people of Israel ask for a king. You will meet Saul, the first king, who hid on the day he was chosen. You will meet David, a shepherd with a harp and a sling. And you will meet Solomon, who asked for wisdom and built the Temple in Jerusalem.

Then come the prophets. A prophet, or navi, is a messenger. Elijah faced a king on a mountain. Amos and Isaiah told rich people to be fair to the poor. Jonah ran away from his message and got swallowed by a fish. Jeremiah warned that Jerusalem would fall, and in 586 BCE it did. Jewish tradition still reads these stories every year: David in the Psalms, Elijah at the Seder, Jonah on Yom Kippur.

By the end of this unit you will be able to name the first three kings in order, tell the story of David and Goliath and of Nathan's lamb, explain what a prophet does, and say why Jewish communities read Jonah on the Day of Atonement. You will also be able to say what the text says and what its readers have made of it, without deciding for anyone what to believe.

The big question

What do the books of Samuel, Kings and the Prophets say about power, fairness and turning back?

The story

A Harp for a Troubled King

A shepherd boy walks into a palace with a harp, and the king cannot stop listening.

The king sat in his chair and stared at the wall. His name was Saul. He was the first king Israel ever had. He was tall and strong, but lately his mind was dark and heavy. His servants whispered. "Music might help," one said. "There is a boy in Bethlehem who plays the harp."

The boy's name was David. He was the youngest of eight brothers. He spent his days on the hills with his father's sheep. He knew how to sling a stone at a wolf. He also knew how to play a harp until the sheep lay down calm. Now a messenger stood at his door. "The king wants you," he said.

David walked into the palace with the harp on his back. He did not look at the gold or the guards. He sat down near the king and began to play. The notes were soft and slow. The king's shoulders dropped. He closed his eyes. For the first time in days, he could breathe.

The book of Samuel says Saul loved David and made him his armor-bearer. It also says that whenever the dark mood came back, David played, and the king felt better. Neither of them knew what the story would say next. One day the boy with the harp would wear the crown himself.

Jewish tradition still connects David with music. Many of the Psalms carry his name at the top. Some are sung in synagogues in Chicago every week. A song that once calmed one troubled king is still being sung.

Section 1

Israel Asks for a King

11.1

The People Want a King

Main ideaThe book of Samuel says the people asked for a king, and Samuel warned them what a king would cost.

For a long time, the book of Judges says, Israel had no king. Leaders called judges stepped up when trouble came. Then they went home. The last of these leaders was . He was a prophet and a judge. When Samuel grew old, the leaders of the people came to him with a request.

"Give us a king," they said, "like all the other nations." The book of , chapter 8, tells this scene. Samuel did not like the idea. The text says he prayed, and God told him to listen to the people but to warn them first.

Samuel gave a warning. A king would take their sons for his army. He would take their daughters as cooks and bakers. He would take the best of their fields and flocks. The people still said yes. Jewish tradition reads this as a story about what people give up when they want to be like everyone else.

11.2

Saul, the Tall, Shy King

Main ideaSaul, the first king, started strong but lost his way, and the text says Samuel told him the crown would pass to another.

The first king was . The text says he was taller than anyone else in Israel. He was also shy. When the people gathered to name him king, he hid among the baggage. They had to go and find him. Samuel poured oil on his head. This is called . It marked Saul as the one chosen to lead.

Saul won battles and gathered an army. But the book of Samuel says he made serious mistakes. Once he offered a sacrifice himself instead of waiting for Samuel. Another time he did not do what the prophet said after a battle. The text says Samuel told him God had chosen someone else to be king.

Saul stayed king for years after that. But the story says his mood turned dark and jealous. Readers have long asked why Saul failed. Some say he cared too much about what people thought. Others say the job was simply too big for one shy man.

11.5

David and Jonathan

Main ideaThe text says Saul's son Jonathan loved David like his own soul and protected him, even though David would be king instead of him.

Saul had a son named . He was a brave soldier and the next in line to be king. After David beat Goliath, the text says Jonathan’s soul was bound to David’s soul. He gave David his own robe, his sword and his bow. They made a , a promise to be loyal to each other.

Saul grew jealous of David. The people sang that Saul had killed thousands, but David tens of thousands. Saul tried to kill David more than once. Jonathan warned his friend. In 1 Samuel 20 they make a plan with arrows in a field. Jonathan shoots the arrows as a signal. David must run.

Jonathan chose his friend over his own chance to be king. Later, when Jonathan died in battle, David sang a sad song for him. Jewish tradition holds up this friendship as a model. The Mishnah, in Pirkei Avot, says a love that does not depend on getting something lasts, like the love of David and Jonathan.

King David in Jerusalem

11.6

A City for the King

Main ideaThe text says David became king of all Israel and made Jerusalem his capital, and Jewish tradition remembers it as the City of David.

After Saul died in battle, David became king. The text says he ruled first in Hebron, over the tribe of Judah, for seven years. Then all the tribes came to him. He became king of all Israel. He needed a capital city that did not belong to any one tribe.

David chose . The book of 2 Samuel says it was a hill city held by a people called the Jebusites. David captured it and made it his own. The text calls it the City of David. He brought the there, the gold box that held the tablets from Sinai. The text says he danced in front of it with all his might.

David wanted to build a house for the Ark, a temple. The prophet Nathan told him that his son would build it instead. Jewish tradition remembers David as the king who made Jerusalem the center. The city is named in Jewish prayers to this day.

Think it through
Why a New City?

Picture a map of the twelve tribes. Each tribe has its own land. David comes from Judah, in the south. The other tribes live in the north. If David rules from a city in Judah, the northern tribes may feel left out. Jerusalem sits on the border, and no tribe owned it yet. David makes it his own.

Why might a king choose a capital city that belongs to no single tribe? Can you think of a modern example?

11.8

David in the Psalms and in Prayer

Main ideaJewish tradition connects David with the Psalms, and some of those songs are said in synagogues every day.

The book of is in the Ketuvim, the Writings. It has 150 songs and poems. Many have a short heading. Seventy-three say "of David." Jewish tradition calls David the sweet singer of Israel. Historians say many people wrote the psalms over many years. Both agree the songs are old and loved.

One psalm of David, Psalm 145, is said three times a day in Jewish prayer. It is called , from its first word, "Happy." Psalm 23, "The LORD is my shepherd," is also read as David’s. A shepherd who became a king wrote about God as a shepherd. Many readers find that fitting.

David is named in the Jewish prayer book in other ways too. A prayer after meals names the "kingdom of David." A Shabbat song says, "David, king of Israel, lives on." For Jewish tradition, David is not only a king in an old story. He is part of the words said every week.

Solomon and the Split

11.9

Solomon Asks for Wisdom

Main ideaThe text says Solomon asked God for an understanding heart, and the story of two mothers shows his wisdom at work.

David’s son became king after him. The book of 1 Kings tells of a dream. God said to the young king, "Ask what I should give you." Solomon did not ask for gold or a long life. He asked for a wise heart. He wanted to judge the people well.

The text gives an example. Two women came to the king with one baby. Each said the baby was hers. There were no witnesses. Solomon called for a sword. "Cut the child in two," he said, "and give half to each." One woman said, "Yes, divide it." The other cried, "Give her the baby, only do not hurt him!"

Solomon gave the baby to the second woman. She was the real mother. She would rather lose him than see him hurt. The text says all Israel saw the king had . Jewish tradition also names Solomon as the author of Proverbs. That is the book of wise sayings.

11.10

The Temple in Jerusalem

Main ideaThe text says Solomon built the first Temple in Jerusalem, a stone house for the Ark, and it stood for about 400 years.

Solomon did what David could not. He built the . The book of 1 Kings, chapters 6 to 8, describes it. It was made of cut stone, lined with cedar wood from Lebanon and covered with gold inside. It took seven years to build. The Ark of the Covenant was placed in its inner room, called the Holy of Holies.

The Temple was the one place where the priests offered sacrifices. Three times a year, at Passover, Shavuot and Sukkot, people traveled to Jerusalem to bring gifts. The text says Solomon stood before the people on the day the Temple opened. He prayed that God would hear anyone who prayed toward this house, even a stranger from far away.

Historians call this the . The Tanakh says the Babylonians burned it in 586 BCE. A second Temple was built later and stood until 70 CE. Today, Jewish prayers still face toward Jerusalem. Many Jewish weddings end with breaking a glass, which some explain as remembering the Temple.

11.11

One Kingdom Becomes Two

Main ideaAfter Solomon died, the text says the kingdom split into Israel in the north and Judah in the south.

Solomon was famous for wisdom and wealth. But the book of 1 Kings says his building projects cost the people a lot. He used forced labor and heavy taxes. When Solomon died, his son became king. The northern tribes asked him to make their burden lighter. His young friends told him to be harsh instead. He told the people he would be harder than his father. The northern tribes walked away. They chose a man named as their own king.

The Prophets
Big questionWhat is a prophet in the Tanakh, and why do prophets so often say what people do not want to hear?
The story

A Boat Going the Wrong Way

A prophet gets a message he does not like, so he buys a ticket in the opposite direction.

Jonah had a job to do, and he did not want it. The book of Jonah says the word of the LORD came to him: get up, go to Nineveh, that great city, and tell them their wrongdoing has been noticed. Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, a strong enemy of Israel. It lay far to the east.

So Jonah went west. He walked down to the port at Joppa. He found a ship sailing to Tarshish, about as far away as a person could go. He paid the fare. He climbed aboard. He went down below the deck and fell fast asleep. The book says he was trying to get away from the LORD.

Then a great wind came. The waves rose. The sailors were afraid. They threw cargo into the sea. Each one cried to his own god. The captain found Jonah asleep. "How can you sleep?" he shouted. "Get up and call on your God!"

The sailors cast lots to find out who had brought the storm. The lot fell on Jonah. "Who are you?" they asked. "I am a Hebrew," he said. "I worship the LORD, who made the sea and the dry land." Then he told them: throw me into the sea, and it will be calm. They did not want to. They rowed hard. The storm grew worse.

At last they threw him in. The sea went still. And the text says a great fish swallowed Jonah. He was inside it three days and three nights. Every year on Yom Kippur, the Jewish Day of Atonement, this whole story is read aloud in the synagogue. The story of a prophet who ran away is the story people choose to hear on the day for turning back.

Section 1

What a Prophet Is

12.1

A Messenger, Not a Fortune Teller

Main ideaIn the Tanakh a prophet, a navi, is a person who brings a message from God, usually one that people do not want to hear.

The Hebrew word for prophet is . The second part of the Tanakh is called Nevi&#x27;im, the Prophets. A navi in the Tanakh is not mainly someone who tells the future. A navi is a messenger. The text often begins a prophet’s speech with the words, "Thus says the LORD."

The message was often hard. Prophets told kings they were wrong. They told rich people to stop cheating the poor. They warned that a city would fall if people did not change. Kings did not like this. Some prophets were put in prison. Jeremiah was thrown into a muddy pit.

Jewish tradition counts many prophets, men and women. Miriam, Deborah and Huldah are called prophets in the text. The prophets’ books in the Nevi’im come after the books of Samuel and Kings. In this chapter you will meet Elijah, Elisha, Amos, Isaiah, Jonah and Jeremiah.

Think it through
Two Kinds of Prophet

Some people think a prophet is like a fortune teller who says what will happen next year. In the Tanakh, a prophet is more like a messenger who knocks on the king's door with a letter. The letter often says: you are treating people badly, and if you do not change, there will be trouble. The prophet did not write the letter and often did not want to deliver it.

In your own words, what is the difference between a fortune teller and a messenger? Which one is a navi?

was a prophet in the northern kingdom of Israel. The king was Ahab, and the queen was Jezebel. The text says they built altars for , a god of the neighboring peoples. Elijah told Ahab there would be no rain for years. Then he went into hiding.

The Still Small Voice

Main ideaAfter his victory Elijah ran away afraid, and the text says God spoke to him not in wind, earthquake or fire, but in a still small voice.

After Mount Carmel, Queen Jezebel promised to kill Elijah. He ran for his life. He walked into the desert and sat under a broom tree. He said he had had enough. The text says an angel brought him bread and water. Then he walked forty days to , another name for Mount Sinai.

There Elijah hid in a cave. In 1 Kings 19 the text says a great wind tore the mountain, but the LORD was not in the wind. Then an earthquake, but the LORD was not in the earthquake. Then a fire, but the LORD was not in the fire. And after the fire, a still small voice.

Elijah wrapped his face in his cloak and came out. Readers have loved this scene for a long time. Many say it teaches that the most important things are often quiet. The text then tells Elijah to go back to work and to find a helper, Elisha. Later, 2 Kings 2 says Elijah was taken up to heaven in a whirlwind.

Elisha, Amos and Isaiah

12.4

Elisha Picks Up the Cloak

Main ideaThe text says Elisha took up Elijah's cloak and his work, and Jewish tradition still sets a place for Elijah at the Passover Seder.

was a farmer plowing with oxen when Elijah threw his cloak over him. That was the call. Elisha said goodbye to his family and followed. When Elijah was taken up in the whirlwind, his cloak fell. Elisha picked it up. The text says the other prophets saw that the spirit of Elijah now rested on Elisha.

The book of 2 Kings tells many Elisha stories. He helped a poor widow whose jar of oil kept pouring until every pot was full. He healed a foreign general named Naaman, who had a skin disease, by telling him to wash in the Jordan River seven times. He warned kings and fed hungry people during a famine.

Elijah did not disappear from Jewish life. The last prophet in the Tanakh, Malachi, says Elijah will come again before a great day. So at the Passover , many families fill a cup of wine for Elijah and open the door for him. At a baby boy’s circumcision, a chair is set for Elijah. He is the guest who is always expected.

12.5

Amos: Be Fair to the Poor

Main ideaAmos, a shepherd from the south, said that worship means nothing if people cheat the poor; the text says justice should flow like water.

was a shepherd and a keeper of fig trees from Tekoa, a village in Judah. The text says God sent him north to Israel, around 750 BCE. It was a rich time there. Big houses, fancy feasts, full markets. But Amos said the rich were cheating the poor, using false weights and selling people into slavery for the price of a pair of shoes.

Amos went to Bethel, a shrine in the north, and spoke. He said God did not want their festivals and songs. He wanted fairness. The book of Amos 5:24 says: let well up like water, and righteousness like a strong stream. A priest at Bethel told Amos to go home. Amos said he was not a professional prophet. He was just a shepherd who was sent.

Amos is one of the Twelve, the short prophetic books at the end of the Nevi’im. Many readers have used his words. In 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. quoted Amos 5:24 in his "I Have a Dream" speech. Jewish tradition reads Amos as a reminder that prayer and fair dealing belong together.

Main ideaIsaiah spoke in Jerusalem and said the same thing as Amos: care for the orphan and widow, and a day will come when swords become plows.

lived in Jerusalem, in the kingdom of Judah. He spoke a little after Amos. The text says he saw a vision of God in the Temple. Angels called, "Holy, holy, holy." He spoke to kings for about forty years. His book is the first of the big prophetic books in the Nevi’im.

Like Amos, Isaiah said offerings meant nothing without fairness. Isaiah 1:17 says to learn to do good and seek justice. Help the oppressed. Defend the . Plead for the . In his time, an orphan or a widow had no one to stand up for them. Isaiah said a city is judged by how it treats them.

Isaiah is also known for a picture of peace. Isaiah 2:4 says nations will beat their swords into plowshares. A plowshare is the blade that cuts soil. They will not learn war anymore. Those words are carved on a wall across from the United Nations in New York. Jewish readers hear Isaiah every year in the haftarah. That is the reading from the Prophets after the Torah portion.

Jonah and the Big Fish

12.7

A Prophet Runs Away

Main ideaThe book of Jonah says a prophet ran from his message, was swallowed by a great fish and prayed from inside it.

The book of is one of the Twelve. It is only four chapters long. It is different from the other prophets’ books. Instead of long speeches, it tells a story. Jonah is told to go to and warn it. Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, the empire that later destroyed the northern kingdom. Jonah goes the other way.

A storm hits the ship. The sailors throw Jonah into the sea, and it goes calm. The text says a great fish swallows him. Inside the fish, Jonah prays. His prayer in chapter 2 sounds like a psalm. He says he called out from the belly of the deep, and God heard him. After three days the fish spits him out onto dry land.

Readers of all ages have argued about the fish. Was it a whale? The Hebrew says only "a great fish." Jewish tradition usually reads the story for what it teaches, not for the zoology. The point, many say, is that you cannot run away from a job you are meant to do, and that a person can pray from anywhere, even from inside a fish.

12.8

Nineveh Says Sorry

Main ideaThe text says all of Nineveh, from the king down, turned back from evil, and Jonah was angry that God forgave them.

Jonah finally goes to Nineveh. He walks into the huge city and says just five words in Hebrew: in forty days, Nineveh will be overturned. Then something surprising happens. The people believe him. They stop eating. They put on rough cloth called sackcloth. Even the king gets off his throne and sits in ashes.

The king orders everyone, even the animals, to fast. He says, let everyone turn from their evil ways and from the violence in their hands. Who knows, he says, maybe God will turn back too. The text says God saw what they did, not what they said, and did not destroy the city. This is : turning back.

Jonah is furious. He sits outside the city and sulks. God makes a plant grow to shade him, then lets a worm kill it. Jonah is upset about the plant. The book ends with a question from God: you cared about a plant you did not grow; should I not care about a city of more than 120,000 people, and many animals too? Jonah never answers. The reader has to.

Think it through
The Last Question

The book of Jonah ends with God asking Jonah a question. Jonah was sad about a plant that grew in one night and died in one night. God asks: should I not care about Nineveh, a great city with more than 120,000 people who do not know their right hand from their left, and many animals? (Jonah 4:10-11, retold). The book stops there. Jonah does not answer.

Why do you think the book ends with a question and no answer? What would you say back if you were Jonah?

12.9

Jonah on Yom Kippur

Main ideaJewish communities read the whole book of Jonah on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, because it is a story about turning back.

is the Day of Atonement. It comes ten days after the Jewish New Year, Rosh Hashanah. Jewish tradition treats it as the most serious day of the year. Many Jewish adults fast from sunset to sunset. People spend the day in the synagogue, saying sorry for wrongs and asking to be forgiven.

In the afternoon service, the whole book of Jonah is read aloud as the haftarah. It is a strange choice at first. Jonah is a prophet who does everything wrong. But the people of Nineveh, who are not even Israelites, do everything right. They hear a warning, they turn back, and they are forgiven.

Rabbis over the centuries have explained the choice in many ways. Some say it shows that no one can run from God. Some say it shows that saying sorry really works, for anyone. Some point to the last line: God cares for every person and even the animals. On the day for teshuvah, Jewish tradition reads the story of a whole city that did it.

Think it through
Why This Story Today?

It is late afternoon on Yom Kippur in a Chicago synagogue. People have not eaten since last night. The reader opens the book of Jonah and chants it, all four chapters. The congregation hears about the ship, the fish, the city that turned back, and the plant. Then comes the last question, and the reader stops.

Imagine you are choosing one story to read on the day for saying sorry. Why would Jonah be a good choice? Why might it be a hard choice?

12.10

Jeremiah Warns the City

Main ideaJeremiah told Jerusalem that Babylon would come, and the text says people put him in a pit for saying it.

was a prophet in Jerusalem about 150 years after Isaiah. By then the northern kingdom was gone. Assyria had taken Samaria in 722 BCE. Now a new empire, , was growing in the east. Jeremiah said Babylon would take Jerusalem too, unless the people changed how they lived.

Nobody wanted to hear it. Jeremiah stood at the Temple gate and said the building would not save them. Only doing justice would. He wore a wooden yoke, the bar that goes over an ox’s neck, to show that Judah would serve Babylon. Priests and officials were angry. The text says they beat him and lowered him into a muddy pit.

Jeremiah’s book shows his feelings more than most. He says he wished he had never been born. He says the message burned inside him like a fire, and he could not hold it in. Jewish tradition also connects Jeremiah with the book of , the sad poems for the burned city.

12.11

Far from Home: The Exile

Main ideaIn 586 BCE Babylon burned the Temple and took many people away; the text says they wept by the rivers of Babylon and did not forget Jerusalem.

Jeremiah’s warning came true. The book of 2 Kings, chapter 25, says the Babylonian army broke through the walls of Jerusalem. They burned the Temple that Solomon had built. They burned the palace and the houses. Historians date this to 586 BCE. Many people were marched away to Babylon.

Living far from home under a foreign king is called . The Hebrew word is galut. Psalm 137 gives the feeling. It says the people sat down by the rivers of Babylon and wept when they remembered Zion, another name for Jerusalem. Their captors asked them to sing. They asked how they could sing the LORD’s song in a strange land.

The exile did not last forever. About fifty years later, a new king, Cyrus of Persia, let people go home and rebuild. But the exile changed Jewish life. Without a Temple, people gathered to pray and to read. Many historians say the synagogue and the habit of studying the text grew from this time. Every year on Tisha B’Av, Jewish communities fast and read Lamentations to remember the burned Temple.
