Devotion, Vision and the Upanishads

The Gita II and Upanishads

A warrior asks to see his friend as he truly is. A boy waits three nights at the door of Death. A father tells his son to break open a seed. These are scenes from the Bhagavad Gita and the Upanishads, and Hindus have returned to them for centuries in prayer, study and argument.

In this unit you will read the Gita's chapters on devotion (9 to 12) and its final teaching (18), then three Upanishads: the Katha, the Chandogya and the Isha. You will learn how devotion, awe and knowledge fit together in the Gita, and how the Upanishads teach through dialogues and simple images like salt, seeds and a chariot.

By the end you will be able to read a dialogue closely, name who asks and who answers, explain what image carries the idea, and describe how different Hindu schools have read the same words, such as tat tvam asi or Gita 18.66, without ranking them.

The big question

How do the Gita and the Upanishads teach about the self, God and devotion, and why do their readers understand them differently?

Devotion and the Universal Form

Gita 9–12, 18
Big questionHow does the Gita join love and awe, and how have its different readers understood its final teaching?
The story

Show Me Your Form

Arjuna asks to see his friend as he truly is, and then begs him to stop.

By chapter 11 of the Gita, Arjuna has listened to Krishna for a long time. He has heard about action, knowledge and meditation. He has heard Krishna say that all things rest in him. Now Arjuna asks for something more. If it is possible, he says, let me see your supreme form.

Krishna says that Arjuna cannot see it with ordinary eyes. So he gives him a divine eye. Then Sanjaya, the narrator, tries to describe what Arjuna saw. He says that if the light of a thousand suns rose at once in the sky, it might be like the brightness of that great being.

Arjuna sees many mouths and eyes, many arms, and all the worlds inside one body. He sees the warriors of both armies rushing into the mouths of this form, like rivers rushing to the sea. He is filled with wonder, and his hair stands on end. Then he is filled with fear.

At last Arjuna begs Krishna to show him the familiar form again, the friend with four arms, and then the gentle human form. Krishna does. Arjuna says that now his mind is calm. Krishna adds that this vision cannot be won by study or rituals alone, but by *bhakti*, devotion.

Readers have called chapter 11 the most dramatic scene in the Gita. In this chapter you will read it beside the gentler devotion of chapters 9 and 12, and the final teaching of chapter 18, and see how different schools read the same words.

Talk about itArjuna wants to see the whole truth, and then cannot bear it. Why might the poem show both his wish and his fear?
Section 1

A Leaf, a Flower, a Fruit

25.1

The Royal Secret

Main ideaGita 9 presents devotion as a path open to everyone, which Krishna calls the royal knowledge and royal secret.

Chapter 9 of the Gita has a grand title in many editions: the yoga of the royal knowledge and the royal secret. In it Krishna speaks of himself as the one in whom all beings rest, like the great wind that moves everywhere yet rests in space (9.6). He describes himself as father, mother and support of the world.

The chapter’s heart is , loving devotion. Krishna says that those who worship him with devotion are in him, and he is in them (9.29). The path is not hidden behind hard learning. It is a relationship of love and trust, which many Hindus practice through prayer, song and offering.

Vaishnava readers see chapter 9 as the Gita turning toward its center. Advaita readers, who hold that the self and Brahman are one, read devotion as a strong step toward knowledge. Both schools treasure the chapter, but they place it differently in the whole. Students should describe both without ranking them.

Gita 9.6 (paraphrase): as the mighty wind, moving everywhere, always rests in space, so all beings rest in me.

25.2

What Is Offered With Love

Main ideaGita 9.26 says Krishna accepts even a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water offered with devotion.

Gita 9.26 is one of the best-loved verses among Hindus. Krishna says that whoever offers him with devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, he accepts that offering of love from one whose heart is pure. The gift is small. The love is what matters.

This verse shapes daily , worship with offerings, in many Hindu homes. A family may offer a flower, a little water, or fruit before a , a sacred image, at a home shrine. Temples such as the Hindu Temple of Greater Chicago in Lemont receive similar offerings every day.

Readers point out that the verse makes worship open to the poor as well as the rich. It does not ask for costly rituals. The next verse, 9.27, widens it further: whatever you do, eat, offer or give, do it as an offering to me. Here bhakti and karma yoga meet, since all ordinary action becomes an offering.

25.3

No One Is Turned Away

Main ideaGita 9.30 to 9.32 say devotion is open to all, though some verses reflect the social views of their time.

Gita 9.30 says that even a person who has done great wrong, if they worship Krishna with undivided devotion, should be regarded as good, because they have resolved rightly. Verse 9.31 adds that Krishna’s devotee does not perish. Many readers find hope in these verses.

Verse 9.32 says that women, vaishyas and shudras, and even those of low birth, can reach the highest goal by taking refuge in Krishna. Many devotees read this as a strong statement that devotion is open to everyone, whatever their gender or birth. Bhakti saints later made the same point in song.

Critics, including many Hindu scholars today, point out that the verse also shows the social ranking of its time, since it names these groups as needing special assurance. Readers weigh both sides. A careful student notes what the verse opens up and what it takes for granted, and names who reads it which way.

The Universal Form

25.4

A Thousand Suns

Main ideaGita 11 shows Krishna's universal form through Sanjaya's words of light and wonder.

In Gita 11.8 Krishna gives Arjuna a divine eye, since ordinary eyes cannot see this form. Sanjaya then describes the sight to the blind king. Verse 11.12 says that if the light of a thousand suns rose at once in the sky, it might resemble the splendor of that great being.

The form is called the , the universal form. Arjuna sees the whole universe, with its many parts, gathered in the body of the God of gods (11.13). He sees many arms, bellies, mouths and eyes, with no beginning, middle or end. He bows his head and folds his hands.

Devotees read chapter 11 as a revelation of Krishna’s divine nature. Many Hindu homes and temples have paintings of the vishvarupa, and it is shown with deep reverence. Scholars often describe the chapter as a , a text in which a deity is said to appear to a human being.

25.5

Time, the Destroyer

Main ideaIn Gita 11.32 Krishna names himself as Time, and J. Robert Oppenheimer later recalled this verse in his own words.

Arjuna asks the fearsome form who it is. In 11.32 Krishna answers that he is Time, grown mighty, the destroyer of worlds, come here to sweep people away. Even without Arjuna, he says, none of the warriors in the opposing armies will survive. Verse 11.33 tells Arjuna to rise and be only the instrument.

The verse became famous in the United States through J. Robert Oppenheimer, the physicist who led the Los Alamos laboratory. In a 1965 television interview he recalled that, after the first atomic test in 1945, he thought of the line "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." That was his own wording, from his reading of the Gita in Sanskrit, not Arnold’s or Telang’s.

Readers debate what the verse means. Many Hindu commentators read , time, as the power that brings all things to their end, and Arjuna’s part as surrendering his ego. Others connect the verse to hard questions about responsibility in war. The same line carries very different weight for different readers.

25.6

From Awe Back to Love

Main ideaArjuna asks Krishna to return to his gentle form, and the chapter ends by praising devotion.

In Gita 11.41 to 11.44, Arjuna apologizes. He says that he used to call Krishna "friend" casually, joking with him at meals and at rest, not knowing his greatness. He asks forgiveness as a father forgives a son, a friend a friend, a lover the beloved.

Then in 11.45 and 11.46 he says he is glad to have seen what no one saw before, but his mind is shaken with fear. He asks to see the familiar four-armed form again. Krishna returns to that form and then to his gentle human shape, and Arjuna says his mind is restored (11.51).

The chapter ends in 11.54 and 11.55 by saying that this form can be known and seen only by undivided devotion. Many readers notice the movement: from friendship to awe and back to a love that now knows more. Devotional readers see this as the pattern of bhakti itself, closeness joined to reverence.

Section 3

The Devotee and the Final Word

25.7

Dear to Me

Main ideaGita 12.13 to 12.20 lists the qualities of a devotee whom Krishna calls dear to him.

Chapter 12 is short and much loved. In 12.13 to 12.20 Krishna describes the devotee who is dear to him. Such a person hates no being, is friendly and compassionate, free from possessiveness and ego, the same in pain and pleasure, and forgiving. It is a portrait, not a rule list.

The list goes on. The devotee is content, self-controlled and firm in resolve. The world is not troubled by this person, and this person is not troubled by the world. They are the same to friend and foe, in honor and dishonor, in heat and cold. Each group of verses ends with a refrain: that devotee is dear to me.

Readers often compare this list to the portrait of the sthitaprajna in chapter 2. Both describe steadiness. But chapter 12 adds warmth: friendliness, compassion and love. Many Hindu teachers use chapter 12 as a guide to character, and it is often memorized by students in Gita recitation contests.

25.8

Surrender: Gita 18.66

Main ideaGita 18.66 asks Arjuna to take refuge in Krishna alone, and schools read the verse in different ways.

Near the end of the Gita comes a verse many call its final word. In 18.66 Krishna says: giving up all dharmas, come to me alone for refuge; I will free you from all sins; do not grieve. The last phrase echoes Krishna’s first words in chapter 2, which told Arjuna not to grieve.

Readers disagree about "giving up all dharmas." In the Sri Vaishnava tradition of Ramanuja, the verse is called the charama shloka, "the last verse," and read as teaching complete , called prapatti, to God. In Shankara’s Advaita reading, giving up dharmas points to giving up the sense of being the doer, so that knowledge can arise.

Other readers, including Gandhi, read the verse as asking for inner surrender while still doing one’s work, since Arjuna does go on to fight. All three readings cite the same Sanskrit words. The verse is a good example of why commentaries matter: a short line can carry a whole school’s teaching.

Section 4

A Map of the Paths

25.9

Three Paths, One Poem

Main ideaMany readers map the Gita as teaching three paths: action, knowledge and devotion.

Teachers often sum up the Gita as three , or paths. Karma yoga is the path of action without attachment (Gita 2 to 6). Jnana yoga is the path of knowledge of the self (Gita 2, 4, 13). Bhakti yoga is the path of loving devotion (Gita 9 to 12). Chapter 18 draws them together.

This map is a helpful teaching tool, but it is a reader’s map, not a heading in the Sanskrit. Scholars note that the paths overlap in the text. Action is done as an offering, which is devotion. Devotion leads to knowledge. Knowledge makes action free.

Different schools draw the map differently. Advaita readers often put knowledge at the top. Vaishnava schools often place devotion at the center. Many modern teachers, like Vivekananda, stressed that different paths suit different people. A student can describe each map and notice what verses each one depends on.

25.10

How the Gita Ends

Main ideaGita 18 closes with Arjuna's doubt gone and Sanjaya's joy, and the Gita then returns to the epic's war.

After 18.66, Krishna asks in 18.72 whether Arjuna has heard with an attentive mind and whether his confusion is gone. In 18.73 Arjuna answers that his delusion is destroyed, his memory regained, his doubts gone, and that he will do as Krishna says. The student has become ready to act.

Then the narrator returns. Sanjaya tells the blind king that he thrills again and again as he remembers this wondrous dialogue and the form of Krishna. The Gita’s last verse, 18.78, says that where Krishna and Arjuna are, there will be fortune, victory and right conduct.

Right after the Gita, the Mahabharata goes back to the war. The battle lasts eighteen days, and nearly everyone dies. Readers notice this frame. Some see the Gita as teaching how to act in a tragic world. Others see its inner teaching as standing apart from the story around it.

Katha, Chandogya and Isha: "That Art Thou"

Upanishads
Big questionHow do the Upanishads use questions, teachers and simple images to teach about the self and Brahman?
The story

Inside the Seed

A father hands his son a fig from a great tree and tells him to break it open.

Shvetaketu has come home after twelve years of study. He is twenty-four, and he is proud of how much he has learned. His father, Uddalaka Aruni, asks him a question he cannot answer. Did you ask for the teaching by which the unheard becomes heard, and the unknown becomes known?

Over many days, the father teaches with things close at hand. One day he says: bring me a fruit from that banyan tree. Shvetaketu brings it. Break it, says his father. What do you see? Tiny seeds, sir. Break one of them. What do you see? Nothing at all, sir.

The father says that from this fine essence, which you cannot see, this great banyan tree stands. Then he says the words that close each lesson in this part of the Chandogya Upanishad. That fine essence is the self of all this. That is the real. That is the self. That art thou, Shvetaketu.

The Sanskrit is *tat tvam asi*. Hindu teachers have discussed those three words for well over a thousand years. Some read them as saying the self and Brahman are one. Others read them as saying the self depends on Brahman as a body depends on its soul. The debate is still alive.

In this chapter you will read three Upanishads closely: the Katha, where a boy questions Death himself, the Chandogya, and the short Isha. You will practice reading a dialogue: who asks, who answers, and what image carries the idea.

Talk about itThe father could have simply told his son the answer. Why might he have asked him to break open the seed instead?
Section 1

What the Upanishads Are

26.1

Texts at the End of the Veda

Main ideaThe Upanishads are the last layer of the Vedas, and many Hindus call them Vedanta, the end of the Veda.

The are Sanskrit texts that form the last layer of the Vedas. The earlier layers are the hymns, the ritual manuals and the forest texts. The Upanishads turn from ritual to questions about the self, the world and what is ultimately real. For this reason tradition calls them , the end or goal of the Veda.

Hindu tradition counts more than a hundred Upanishads, but about thirteen are called the principal or early ones. They include the Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya, Katha, Isha and Mundaka. Scholars usually date the oldest, the Brihadaranyaka and Chandogya, to about the 7th or 6th century BCE, and others such as the Katha to later centuries BCE.

Hindus who follow the Vedas regard the Upanishads as , "what was heard": revealed texts, not composed by human authors in the ordinary sense. Scholars describe how the texts were composed and passed down orally in different schools of Vedic priests. Both views are part of the story students should know.

26.2

How to Read a Dialogue

Main ideaUpanishads often teach through dialogues, so readers ask who asks, who answers, and what image carries the idea.

Many Upanishads are not lectures. They are conversations. A son asks his father. A wife asks her husband. A boy asks the god of death. A student sits near a teacher. The word upanishad is often explained as "sitting down near," though scholars debate the exact meaning.

When you read a , ask three questions. First, who asks and who answers? The asker’s situation shapes the teaching. Second, what does the teacher do: tell, show, test or tell a story? Third, what carries the idea: salt, a seed, a chariot, a river?

These questions help because the Upanishads rarely give a definition and stop. They show an idea working in a scene. A careful reader follows the scene, writes down the image, and then asks what the teacher wants the student to see. You will use these three questions all through this chapter.

Section 2

Nachiketa and Death

26.3

A Boy at Death's Door

Main ideaIn the Katha Upanishad, a boy named Nachiketa goes to Yama, the lord of death, and is granted three wishes.

The Katha Upanishad opens with a sacrifice. A man named Vajashravasa is giving away his possessions, but the cows he gives are old and dry. His young son Nachiketa is troubled and asks, "To whom will you give me?" He asks a second and third time. At last his angry father says, "To Death I give you."

Nachiketa goes to the house of , the lord of death, who is away. The boy waits three nights without food. When Yama returns, he feels he has wronged a guest, and offers three wishes, one for each night. This is how the Upanishad sets up its teaching.

Nachiketa’s first wish is that his father be calm and welcome him home. His second is to learn a fire ritual that leads to heaven. His third is the great question: when a person dies, some say he exists and some say he does not. I want to know this. The rest of the Upanishad is Yama’s answer.

Think it through
Three Wishes

Katha Upanishad 1.1 (summary): Wish 1: that his father be at peace and welcome him back. Wish 2: to learn the fire ritual that leads to heaven. Wish 3: to know what happens to a person after death.

How do the wishes move from near to far? Why might the Upanishad save the deepest question for last?

Words to know
Yama
the lord of death in Hindu texts, and Nachiketa's teacher in the Katha Upanishad
Nachiketa
the boy who questions Yama in the Katha Upanishad
boon
a wish granted as a gift; Yama grants Nachiketa three
Check yourself

1. Why does Nachiketa go to Yama?

Why: In Katha 1.1 his father, angered by his questions, gives him to Death.

2. Why does Yama grant three wishes?

Why: Yama makes up for three nights of an unwelcomed guest.

3. What is Nachiketa's third wish?

Why: The third wish asks about existence after death.
26.4

The Good and the Pleasant

Main ideaYama tests Nachiketa with riches, and praises him for choosing the good over the pleasant.

Yama does not answer at once. He says even the gods had doubts about this question, and he asks Nachiketa to choose another wish. He offers sons and grandsons, cattle, horses, gold, a long life and a great kingdom, even lovely companions with chariots and music.

Nachiketa refuses. These things last only until tomorrow, he says, and they wear out the senses. Wealth cannot make a person content. Keep your horses and your songs. The wish I choose is the one I asked. Yama is pleased. He has tested the student and found him ready.

In Katha 1.2 Yama teaches that there are two things, the good, , and the pleasant, . Both bind a person to action. The wise choose the good; the foolish choose the pleasant for the sake of comfort. Many Hindu teachers use this pair when they speak about choices young people face.

Think it through
Good or Pleasant?

Katha 1.2.1–2 (paraphrase): The good is one thing, the pleasant another. The wise one weighs them and chooses the good over the pleasant; the foolish one chooses the pleasant for the sake of getting and keeping.

Describe a choice a student might face between the good and the pleasant. Why does Yama test Nachiketa before teaching him?

Words to know
shreyas
the good: what truly benefits a person, in the Katha Upanishad
preyas
the pleasant: what gives quick pleasure
test
a trial by a teacher to see if a student is ready
Check yourself

1. What does Yama offer Nachiketa instead of an answer?

Why: In Katha 1.1 Yama offers many riches to turn Nachiketa aside.

2. What is shreyas?

Why: Shreyas is the good; preyas is the pleasant.

3. Why is Yama pleased with Nachiketa?

Why: By refusing riches, Nachiketa shows he is ready to learn.
26.5

The Chariot

Main ideaKatha Upanishad 1.3.3 to 1.3.4 pictures the self as the rider in a chariot drawn by the senses.

One of the most famous images in the Upanishads comes in Katha 1.3. Yama says: know the self as the rider in the chariot, and the body as the chariot. The intellect, , is the charioteer, and the mind is the reins. The senses are the horses, and the objects of the senses are the roads they run on.

Yama explains what the image means. A person without understanding, whose mind is never held firm, has senses like wild horses that do not obey the driver. A person with understanding, whose mind is held firm, has senses like good horses. Such a person reaches the end of the road.

Students may notice that the Gita also uses a chariot, with Krishna as Arjuna’s driver. Scholars often compare the two images. The Katha’s chariot is inside every person. Readers across Hindu schools use it to talk about self-control, and it is often drawn in Hindu children’s books.

From the source
The Rider in the Chariot
Know the Self to be sitting in the chariot, the body to be the chariot, the intellect (buddhi) the charioteer, and the mind the reins.
Katha Upanishad 1.3.3, tr. F. Max Müller, The Upanishads, part 2 (Sacred Books of the East 15, 1884)

Match each part of the chariot to a part of the person. Which part does the steering, and which part only rides?

Words to know
buddhi
the intellect or understanding; the charioteer in the Katha image
chariot image
Katha 1.3.3–4: self as rider, body as chariot, intellect as driver, mind as reins, senses as horses
self-control
the ability to steer one's actions; the Katha pictures it as a driver with firm reins
Check yourself

1. In the Katha chariot image, what are the horses?

Why: Katha 1.3.4 calls the senses the horses.

2. Who is the charioteer in Katha 1.3.3?

Why: The intellect drives; the mind is the reins; the self is the rider.

3. How does the Katha chariot differ from the Gita's chariot?

Section 3

Salt, Seed and Self

26.6

That Art Thou

Main ideaIn Chandogya Upanishad 6, Uddalaka teaches his son that the finest essence is the self of all, ending: tat tvam asi.

Chandogya Upanishad chapter 6 is a series of lessons from Uddalaka Aruni to his son Shvetaketu. Each lesson uses an everyday example: clay and pots, bees and honey, rivers and the sea. Each one ends with the same words. This refrain appears nine times, from 6.8.7 to 6.16.3.

The refrain says that the finest essence is the self of all that exists. It is the real. It is the self. That art thou, Shvetaketu. In Sanskrit the last phrase is . Tradition counts it as one of the four , the great sayings of the Upanishads.

What does it mean? Teachers of Advaita Vedanta read it as saying the self, atman, is identical with , ultimate reality. Teachers of other Vedanta schools read it as saying the self belongs to Brahman and depends on it, but is not the same. You will study these readings in depth in unit 15.

From the source
The Refrain
It is the True. It is the Self, and thou, O Svetaketu, art it.
Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7, tr. F. Max Müller, The Upanishads, part 1 (Sacred Books of the East 1, 1879)

Who speaks, and to whom? What does "it" refer to? Why might a teacher repeat this line at the end of each lesson?

Words to know
tat tvam asi
"That art thou"; the refrain of Chandogya Upanishad 6
mahavakya
"great saying"; four short Upanishad sayings tradition treats as summing up their teaching
Brahman
in the Upanishads, the ultimate reality behind all things
Check yourself

1. Who teaches Shvetaketu in Chandogya 6?

Why: Uddalaka teaches his son in Chandogya 6; Yama teaches in the Katha.

2. What is a mahavakya?

Why: Tat tvam asi is one of four mahavakyas.

3. How does Advaita Vedanta read tat tvam asi?

Why: Advaita reads the saying as identity of atman and Brahman; other schools read it differently.
26.7

The Banyan Seed

Main ideaIn Chandogya 6.12, a seed broken open shows nothing visible, yet from that fine essence a great tree grows.

In Chandogya 6.12, Uddalaka asks for a fruit of the nyagrodha, the banyan tree. He has his son break it and asks what he sees. Tiny seeds, the son says. Break one. What do you see? Nothing at all, sir. The seed’s inner core is too fine to see. The scene is short, but every step matters.

The father answers that from this fine essence which you do not see, this great banyan stands. Believe it, my dear, he says. Then comes the refrain: that fine essence is the self of all; that art thou. The lesson moves from the visible tree to the invisible source.

The banyan is a fitting choice. It is a huge tree whose branches send down roots, so one tree can look like a forest. The image teaches that something great can come from something too small to see. Readers note how the teacher lets the student discover the "nothing" himself before naming it.

From the source
Break It
Bring a fruit of that banyan tree. Break it. What do you see? These tiny seeds, sir. Break one of them. What do you see? Nothing at all, sir.
Chandogya Upanishad 6.12.1, paraphrased from F. Max Müller (Sacred Books of the East 1, 1879) (paraphrased)

Count the steps in this dialogue. Why does the teacher ask the student to break the seed himself?

Words to know
nyagrodha
the banyan tree; its fruit is broken open in Chandogya 6.12
essence
the finest inner nature of something; Chandogya 6 calls it the self of all
invisible
not able to be seen; the seed's core, and the essence it stands for
Check yourself

1. What does Shvetaketu see when he breaks a seed?

Why: In Chandogya 6.12 the seed's core is too fine to see.

2. What does the banyan lesson teach, according to the father?

Why: The visible tree comes from an invisible essence.

26.8

Salt in Water

Main ideaIn Chandogya 6.13, salt dissolved in water cannot be seen but can be tasted everywhere in it.

In the next lesson, Chandogya 6.13, Uddalaka tells his son to put a lump of salt in water and come back in the morning. In the morning he says: bring me the salt you put in the water. The son looks, but cannot find it, because it has dissolved. The lump seems simply gone.

Taste the water from the top, the father says. How is it? Salty. Taste from the middle. Salty. Taste from the bottom. Salty. The salt is everywhere in the water, though you cannot see it. So too, the father says, you do not see the real here, but it is here.

The image adds something the seed did not. The seed showed something too small to see. The salt shows something present everywhere at once, known by taste rather than sight. Many teachers use the salt lesson to explain how the Upanishads picture Brahman as present in all things.

Think it through
Seed and Salt Side by Side

Chandogya 6.12: a seed is broken, and its core is too fine to see, yet a banyan grows from it. Chandogya 6.13: salt vanishes in water, yet every sip tastes of it.

What does each image teach that the other does not? Why might a teacher use both?

Words to know
dissolve
to mix into a liquid until it can no longer be seen
pervade
to be present in every part of something
the real
sat; Chandogya 6's word for the being that underlies all things
Check yourself

1. Why can Shvetaketu not find the salt?

Why: The salt dissolved overnight.

2. How does he know the salt is still there?

Why: Tasting from top, middle and bottom shows it is present throughout.

3. What does the salt lesson add to the seed lesson?

Why: The salt pervades all the water, known by taste, not sight.
Section 4

The Isha Upanishad

26.9

Enjoy by Renouncing

Main ideaIsha Upanishad 1 says all this world is pervaded by the Lord, and one should enjoy by renouncing and not covet.

The Isha Upanishad is very short, only eighteen verses. It is found as the last chapter of the Vajasaneyi Samhita of the White Yajur Veda. It takes its name from its first word, isha, "the Lord." Many Hindus know its opening verse by heart. It is often chanted at the start of study.

Isha 1 says that all this, whatever moves in the moving world, is to be covered or pervaded by the Lord. Then it gives advice: enjoy through renouncing, and do not covet anyone’s wealth. The Sanskrit phrase tena tyaktena bhunjitha, "enjoy by what has been given up," puzzles and delights readers.

How can a person enjoy by renouncing? One reading: if the world belongs to the Lord, a person can use and enjoy things without grasping them as "mine." Gandhi said that if all the Upanishads were lost and only this verse remained, Hinduism would live on. He read it as a call to simple living and trusteeship.

From the source
The Opening Verse
All this, whatever moves in this moving world, is pervaded by the Lord. Enjoy it by letting go; do not covet anyone's wealth.
Isha Upanishad 1, paraphrased from F. Max Müller, The Upanishads, part 1 (Sacred Books of the East 1, 1879) (paraphrased)

How can someone enjoy something by letting go of it? Give one example from ordinary life.

Words to know
Isha
"the Lord"; the first word and name of the Isha Upanishad
renounce
to give up or let go of; Isha 1 says to enjoy by renouncing
covet
to want what belongs to someone else; Isha 1 says do not covet

26.10

Seeing All in the Self

Main ideaIsha 6 and 7 say that one who sees all beings in the self, and the self in all beings, feels no hatred or grief.

Isha verses 6 and 7 turn from the world to the one who sees it. A person who sees all beings in the self, and the self in all beings, does not shrink away from anything, or, as some translate, feels no hatred. When all beings have become one’s own self, what delusion or grief can there be for the one who sees that oneness?

These verses link the Upanishad’s big idea to how a person treats others. If the self is in all beings, then harming another is like harming oneself. Many Hindu teachers, and some Jain and Buddhist writers with their own reasons, connect this kind of seeing to compassion.

The Gita has a close parallel in 6.29, which describes the yogi who sees the self in all beings and all beings in the self. Readers compare such passages across texts, a practice called intertextual reading. It shows how ideas travel from the Upanishads into the Gita.

Think it through
Isha 6 and Gita 6.29

Isha 6 (paraphrase): one who sees all beings in the self, and the self in all beings, feels no hatred. Gita 6.29 (paraphrase): the yogi sees the self in all beings and all beings in the self, and sees the same everywhere.

What words are nearly the same? What is different about the setting of each passage?

Words to know
oneness
the state of being one; Isha 7 speaks of seeing all beings as one's own self
parallel passage
a passage in another text that says something similar
intertextual reading
reading one text beside another to see how ideas connect

Why: Gita 6.29 describes seeing the self in all beings and all beings in the self.

Why: Intertextual reading compares passages across texts.
