Show me one first

This one is nobody's answer — it is only here to show the moves before you touch yours. Watching it is instruction, never a trial, and it counts toward nothing.

Question 3 on the review sheet: what is the term for the division of power between the federal government and the state governments?
Two terms on this sheet sound almost the same, so sort them by DIRECTION. Federalism cuts up and down — Washington above, the states below. Separation of powers cuts sideways — three branches, all of them federal.
This question names the federal government and the state governments — two levels, not three branches. So the cut is up and down.
Federalism. Every question on this page starts the same way: name the room, or name the direction, before you read the four choices.

The cumulative review page, built straight from the class's own Constitution Review Sheet — every item carries its number from that sheet, and the number shows on screen, on the hint, on the printed worksheet and in the rehearsal results, so a student who misses one knows exactly which line to go read. Set 1 is the plan and its parts (the Articles of Confederation's failure, the Preamble, federalism, the republic, the Virginia Plan, separation of powers, the three-fourths ratification wall, and the federal/state/shared sort). Set 2 is Congress and the presidency (535, the census, the ages and residency, the once-a-year floor, the Cabinet, the presidential qualifications, four years and the ten-year outside edge, and 538/270/the House deciding). Set 3 is the courts, the checks and the amendments (nine and life, what begins at the Supreme Court, the district courts, appointment and confirmation, judicial review and Marbury, impeachment's two rooms, the veto and its two-thirds answer, and the five amendments the sheet names). Items 1-5 are recall; 6-8 are the plan at work. The probe row below still measures only the practice walk, exactly as on every other page. ⚠ ONE THING TO SETTLE BEFORE THE QUIZ. The class study guide states that 2/3 is needed in BOTH houses to pass a bill. Two-thirds is the number for OVERRIDING a veto; an ordinary bill passes each house by a simple majority, which is what h14 teaches and what the Constitution says. Rather than teach either one over the top of the other, this page leaves that single question out of its items — the override and its two-thirds are asked directly in Set 3, where they are not in doubt. Worth a word with the department before the test.
