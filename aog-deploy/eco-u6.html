Trade and Government

Open your backpack and read the tags. Your things came from all over the world. Now walk outside. The road, the streetlight and the library down the block were not bought by any one person. We paid for them together.

This unit is about how people work together. First you will learn why people and countries trade. You will see what Illinois sells and buys, and how Chicago moves goods. Then you will learn about taxes, public goods and the jobs people do.

By the end, you will be able to tell an import from an export. You can figure out a sales tax. You can sort public and private goods. You can also make a plan for a job you wonder about.

The big question

How do trade, taxes and work connect us to our community and the world?

Why People Trade

11.1

Nobody Makes Everything

Main ideaPeople trade because nobody can make everything they need by themselves.

Think about your breakfast. Maybe you ate cereal with milk. A farmer grew the grain. A dairy farmer got the milk. A factory made the box. A truck driver brought it to the store. You did not do any of those jobs yourself.

means giving something to get something else. Long ago, people traded goods for goods. Today we usually trade money for goods. You give the store $4, and the store gives you a box of cereal (example price). Both sides get something they want.

Nobody has the time, tools or skill to make everything. So people make some things and trade for the rest. A family, a town and a whole country all do this. That is why trade happens everywhere.

11.2

Doing What You Do Best

Main ideaWhen people focus on what they do best and trade, they can both end up with more.

Sam and Rosa are making snacks for a class party. In one hour, Sam can bake 20 cookies or make 5 fruit cups. Rosa can bake 10 cookies or make 10 fruit cups. These numbers are an example.

If each works half the hour on each snack, Sam makes 10 cookies and 2 or 3 fruit cups. Rosa makes 5 cookies and 5 fruit cups. Together that is about 15 cookies and 7 fruit cups. Now let Sam bake for the whole hour and Rosa make fruit cups. Together they have 20 cookies and 10 fruit cups. That is more of both!

Focusing on one job you do well is called . After they specialize, Sam and Rosa trade so each has both snacks. Countries do the same thing. Each makes what it does best, then trades.

11.3

Imports and Exports

Main ideaExports are things a place sells to other places; imports are things it buys from them.

Illinois farmers grow lots of soybeans. Some of those soybeans are sold to other countries and shipped away. Goods that leave a country to be sold somewhere else are called .

Now think of a banana at a Chicago store. Bananas do not grow in Illinois winters. They are grown in warm countries and shipped here. Goods that come into a country from another country are called .

Here is a trick to remember. Exports EXIT. Imports come IN. The same soybean can be an export for the United States and an import for the country that buys it. Every trade between countries has both sides.

Why: An export for the seller is an import for the buyer. The soybeans come into Japan.

Illinois and the World

11.4

What Illinois Sells

Main ideaIllinois sells crops like corn and soybeans and machines like tractors to places all over the world.

Drive across Illinois in summer and you see fields of corn and soybeans for miles. Illinois is one of the top states in the country for growing both. Farmers sell much of the harvest to other states and other countries.

Illinois also has factories. Workers there build machines, like tractors, engines and equipment for building roads. Other factories make medicines, chemicals and foods. Many of these goods are exported.

Why does Illinois sell these things? It has rich, dark soil and plenty of rain for crops. It has skilled factory workers. It has good ways to ship goods. A place’s help decide what it makes and sells.

11.5

What Illinois Buys

Main ideaIllinois imports goods it cannot make well or cheaply, like fruit in winter, phones and clothing.

In January, Illinois fields are frozen. But the store still has strawberries and grapes. Many of them are grown in warmer places, like Mexico or Chile, and shipped north. Those are imports.

Illinois also imports phones, laptops, toys and clothing. Many are made in countries in Asia and Latin America. Some parts, like car parts, come from Canada and Mexico. Canada and Mexico are big trading partners because they are close by.

A is a place you buy from and sell to. Illinois buys what other places make well. It sells what it makes well. This way people here get more kinds of goods than they could make alone.

11.6

Chicago Moves the Goods

Main ideaChicago's trains, airport and port help move goods between Illinois and the world.

Goods cannot trade themselves. They have to be moved. Chicago is one of the busiest places in the country for moving goods. Many railroad lines meet there. Trains carry grain, cars and big metal boxes called containers.

O’Hare International Airport is one of the busiest airports in the country. Planes there carry people. They also carry cargo, like medicine, flowers and electronics that need to arrive fast.

The Port of Chicago sits on Lake Michigan. Ships can travel from the Great Lakes to the Atlantic Ocean. Barges on rivers can reach the Mississippi River. Roads, rails, planes and ships are part of a place’s .

11.8

Depending on Each Other

Main ideaTrade makes people and countries depend on each other, which brings more goods but also some risk.

When Sam bakes and Rosa makes fruit cups, they need each other. Sam counts on Rosa for fruit. Rosa counts on Sam for cookies. Needing each other like this is called .

Countries are interdependent too. Illinois sells soybeans to other countries. It buys phones and clothes from them. When trade works well, everyone gets more goods at lower prices than if each made everything alone.

But there is a risk. If a storm closes a port, goods can get stuck. Stores might run short, and prices can go up. Some businesses buy from more than one place, so one problem does not stop everything.

11.9

Fair Rules for Trade

Main ideaTrade is fair when both sides agree freely and follow honest rules; people disagree about how much to limit it.

A fair trade is one both sides choose freely. Nobody is tricked or forced. Labels tell the truth. If a toy says it is safe, it must be safe. Honest rules help buyers trust sellers.

Trade can be unfair too. A seller might lie about a product. A factory might treat workers badly or unsafely. Countries make rules and agreements to try to stop these problems.

Some countries put a on imports. A tariff is a tax on goods that come in from another country. Some people say tariffs protect jobs at home. Others say tariffs make things cost more for buyers. People still disagree about this.

From the source

It is the maxim of every prudent master of a family, never to attempt to make at home what it will cost him more to make than to buy.
Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, 1776

Words to know

fair trade

Check yourself

2. Which trade is fair?

3. Why do people disagree about tariffs?

The story

Three Things Nobody Bought Alone

A pothole, a library card and a fire truck all have the same secret.

On Monday, Mrs. Kim's car hit a deep pothole on Oak Street. Thunk! By Friday, a city crew had filled it with fresh black asphalt. Mrs. Kim did not get a bill. Neither did anyone else who drives on Oak Street.

On Wednesday, her son Daniel got his first library card. He checked out three books about sharks. The librarian stamped the due date. Daniel asked, "How much do I owe?" The librarian laughed. "Nothing today. Just bring them back."

On Thursday night, a fire truck raced past their house with its siren on. A kitchen fire down the block was put out in minutes. Nobody on the block had to buy a fire truck. Nobody had to hire the firefighters that night.

At dinner, Daniel asked, "Who paid for the road, the library and the fire truck?" His mom thought about it. "We did," she said. "All of us did, together." Daniel frowned. "But I never paid anything." His mom smiled. "Every time we buy something, part of the money goes to pay for them."

What Taxes Are

12.1

Money We Pay Together

Main ideaA tax is money people and businesses must pay to the government to pay for shared services.

Daniel buys a toy that costs $20. At the counter, the total says $21. Where did the extra $1 go? That $1 is a sales tax. In this example, the tax is 5% of the price. 5% of $20 is $1.

A is money people and businesses must pay to the government. The government uses that money to pay for things everyone shares, like roads and schools. Paying taxes is required by law.

There are different kinds of taxes. A is added when you buy things. An income tax is taken from the money people earn at work. A property tax is paid by people who own homes and land.

What Taxes Pay For

Main ideaTax money pays for schools, roads, parks, police, firefighters, libraries and more.

Look around your school. The building, the desks, the buses and your teacher’s pay come mostly from taxes. In many places, a big share of local property taxes pays for schools.

Taxes also pay for roads and bridges, parks and playgrounds, police officers and firefighters, and public libraries. Money from the national government helps pay for the military and for help for older people.

Government has a , just like a family. A budget is a plan for how to spend money. There is only so much tax money, so leaders must choose. More money for parks can mean less money for roads. Every choice has a trade-off.

12.3

Who Decides on Taxes

Main ideaLeaders chosen by voters decide on taxes at the city, state and national levels.

Who decides how much tax we pay? In the United States, leaders chosen by voters make those choices. That way, the people have a say. It is one way a community makes shared choices.

There are three main levels. A city or county council decides many local taxes. The Illinois state lawmakers in Springfield decide state taxes. Congress in Washington, D.C., decides national taxes, and the President signs or rejects the laws.

The U.S. gives Congress the power to collect taxes. If voters do not like how taxes are spent, they can vote for different leaders. They can also go to public meetings and speak up.

Businesses would have a hard time selling public goods. People could use them without paying! So the government usually provides them and pays with taxes. Streetlights and national defense are examples of public goods.

Main ideaWhat people earn depends on their skills, their education and how many people want that work done.

Why do some jobs pay more than others? Think about skills. A is something you have learned to do well. Fixing an airplane engine takes many skills. Few people can do it, so the pay tends to be higher.

Demand matters too. If many businesses need nurses, and there are not enough nurses, businesses offer more pay to hire them. If lots of people want a job and few are needed, pay tends to be lower.

The money paid for work is called a . Say one job pays $15 an hour and another pays $30 an hour (example wages). In an 8-hour day, the first earns $120 and the second earns $240.

Planning for a Job You Want

Main ideaYou can plan for a career by learning what a job needs and building those skills step by step.

A is the kind of work a person does over many years. You do not have to pick one now. But you can start exploring. What do you like to do? What are you good at? What helps you learn?

Pick a job you wonder about, like a vet. Find out what a vet does each day. Find out what training a vet needs. Vets go to college and then vet school. You could start by reading about animals and caring for a pet.

Make a simple plan with steps. Step 1: learn about the job. Step 2: build one skill it needs. Step 3: talk to someone who does it. Plans can change, and that is fine. Every skill you build can help in many jobs.

Maya's Career Plan

Maya loves drawing and building with blocks. She wonders about being an architect. She finds out architects draw plans for buildings and study math and design in college. She joins the art club and practices measuring.

What is one more step Maya could take? Make a three-step plan for a job you wonder about.
