Capstone

Every sport leaves paper behind. A newspaper notice announces a game. A club prints its rules. A league writes a charter that says who may join. A governor general buys a trophy and names trustees. Years later, a museum decides which objects to keep and which names to honor. Historians read all of these to learn what happened and who was left out.

This unit follows one sport, ice hockey, from painted Dutch canals and Mi'kmaq-carved sticks to the National Hockey League, the Colored Hockey League of Nova Scotia and the first women's Olympic tournament. Then it turns to objects and places: a stolen World Cup trophy, a gold medal made mostly of silver, a home plate marker in a Chicago parking lot, and the halls of fame and research groups that keep the record.

By the end, you can trace one sport through its documents and dates. You can tell a primary source from a later legend. You can explain how trophies, ballpark plans and archives shape memory. You can build an argument about a sport's past and name the record behind every claim.

One Sport and Its Paper: Ice Hockey

Capstone
Big questionWhat can the written record of one sport, from a newspaper notice to a league charter, show about who was let in and who was kept out?
The story

A Notice in the Morning Paper

In March 1875 a Montreal newspaper told readers about a game on indoor ice, and a paper trail began.

On March 3, 1875, the Montreal Gazette told its readers about a game to be played that evening at the Victoria Skating Rink. The rink was an indoor building with a sheet of natural ice. A young engineer named James Creighton had gathered two teams of nine players each. Skaters in Montreal already knew stick games on frozen rivers and ponds.

The notice promised that spectators could watch in safety. The players would use a flat wooden disc instead of a ball, so it would not fly into the crowd. The next day the paper reported on the game. Historians still read that report closely, because it is one of the earliest printed accounts of an organized game of hockey indoors.

Over the next fifty years the paper stack grew. Montreal clubs printed a short set of rules in 1877. A governor general bought a silver bowl in 1892 and wrote conditions for winning it. Owners met in a Montreal hotel in 1917 and founded a new league. Each document added a rule, a trophy or a membership list.

Each paper also answered a quiet question: who may play here? For decades the answer on most rosters was white men and boys. Black players in Nova Scotia formed their own league, women organized their own teams, and the paper record shows when those doors opened.

Section 1

Before the Rule Book

33.1

Stick Games on Frozen Water

Main ideaPeople played stick-and-ball games on ice in Europe and North America long before anyone wrote down hockey's rules.

Dutch painters of the early 1600s left some of the oldest pictures of games on ice. Hendrick Avercamp painted crowded winter scenes on frozen canals. In several of them, people swing curved sticks at a ball. The Dutch game was called , and it was also played on land. The paintings show a pastime, not a league, with no fixed teams or lines.

In Britain and Ireland, people played field games with sticks and a ball under names such as bandy, shinty and hurling. When British soldiers and settlers came to Canada, they carried these games onto frozen rivers and lakes. On the ice, the games had no agreed size of team and no written rules. Each town and garrison played its own way.

The Mi’kmaq people of Nova Scotia played a stick-and-ball game of their own. In the 1800s, Mi’kmaq carvers made many of the hardwood hockey sticks sold in Canada, often from hornbeam trees. Their sticks were shipped to clubs across the country. This trade connects the sport’s early equipment to Indigenous craft workers, whose names seldom appear in club records.

33.2

Birthplace Claims and Evidence

Main ideaSeveral towns claim to be the birthplace of hockey, but historians separate those legends from the earliest printed records.

Windsor, Nova Scotia, says that boys at King’s College School played hockey on a pond in the early 1800s. The town points to a passage in the writings of Thomas Chandler Haliburton, a Nova Scotia author who attended that school. Kingston, Ontario, and Montreal have made their own claims. Each town has a museum, a plaque or a local story to support it.

A is a statement that a sport began in one place. Such claims help towns attract visitors and take pride in a game. Historians ask a harder question: what written record, made at the time, shows the game being played? A memory written down decades later is weaker evidence than a newspaper printed the next morning.

By that test, the Montreal game of March 3, 1875, stands out. It was announced ahead of time and described afterward in print. That does not mean nobody played hockey earlier. It means 1875 is the earliest date that historians can tie to a public, organized indoor game with a behind it.

33.3

The Victoria Rink, 1875

Main ideaAn indoor rink in Montreal gave hockey a fixed room, a crowd and a newspaper audience in 1875.

The Victoria Skating Rink opened in Montreal in 1862. It was a long brick building with a roof over natural ice. Members paid to skate there, and the rink held carnivals and costume balls. A roof meant the ice was protected from snow, and the walls marked the edges of the playing space.

On March 3, 1875, James Creighton brought two teams of nine players to the rink. Creighton had grown up in Halifax, Nova Scotia, and moved to Montreal as a young man. The players used a flat wooden instead of a ball. The Montreal Gazette said this choice would keep the object on the ice and protect the people watching.

The rink changed what hockey could become. A game in a building could be scheduled, advertised and watched by paying spectators. In 1883 Montreal’s Winter Carnival held a hockey tournament, and in 1886 clubs formed the Amateur Hockey Association of Canada. A room, a crowd and a came together, and written rules soon followed.

Rules, a Trophy and a League

33.4

The Montreal Rules, 1877

Main ideaIn 1877 Montreal players printed a short set of hockey rules in the newspaper, borrowing from older field games.

In February 1877 the Montreal Gazette printed seven rules for hockey. Students and club members in the city had drawn them up. Several rules were adapted from field hockey and rugby, which the players already knew. Printing the rules in a newspaper meant that anyone in Montreal could read the same text.

A written lets strangers play together. Before 1877, a team from one town might argue with another about what was allowed. After the rules were printed, a captain could point to a sentence in the paper. McGill University students formed a hockey club in 1877, and they played under the new text.

The rules kept changing for decades. Team size fell from nine to seven by the 1880s, and later to six. Leagues added a to enforce the rules and a goal judge to rule on scores. Each change was voted on and printed, so historians can date many of them.

33.5

Lord Stanley's Bowl

Main ideaThe Stanley Cup began in 1892 as a governor general's challenge trophy for amateur clubs and later became the prize of one professional league.

Frederick Arthur Stanley, Lord Stanley of Preston, was Canada’s governor general from 1888 to 1893. His children played hockey in Ottawa, and his daughter Isobel was photographed on the ice around 1890. In 1892 Lord Stanley bought a silver bowl in London. He gave it as a for Canada’s best amateur hockey club.

Stanley named two to hold the bowl and settle disputes. The champion club had to accept challenges from other clubs that qualified. The Montreal Hockey Club, part of the Montreal Amateur Athletic Association, was named the first holder in 1893. Stanley went home to England that year and never saw a Cup game.

Over time, professional teams took over. The Seattle Metropolitans of the Pacific Coast league became the first American team to win the Cup, in 1917. By 1926 only National Hockey League teams played for it. Winners’ names are engraved on bands added to the base, so the object itself became a record.

33.6

A New League in a Hotel

Main ideaThe National Hockey League was founded in a Montreal hotel in 1917, partly to shut one owner out of the old league.

By the early 1900s, hockey players could be paid openly. The International Professional Hockey League, based in Michigan’s Upper Peninsula and nearby towns, played from 1904 to 1907. In Canada the National Hockey Association began in 1909. Its owners quarreled often, especially with Eddie Livingstone, who owned a Toronto club.

On November 26, 1917, several owners met at the Windsor Hotel in Montreal. Instead of expelling Livingstone, they formed a new league without him: the National Hockey League. The first NHL season had four clubs, and one, the Montreal Wanderers, dropped out after its arena burned in January 1918. The league’s founding shows that a can be written to exclude as well as to organize.

The NHL grew in the 1920s by selling , the rights to own a team in a city. The Boston Bruins joined in 1924 as the first American club. In 1926 the Chicago Black Hawks, the Detroit club and the New York Rangers joined. The Black Hawks, owned by Frederic McLaughlin, won their first Stanley Cup in 1934.

Who the Rosters Left Out

33.7

A Black League in Nova Scotia

Main ideaBlack players in Nova Scotia built their own hockey league in 1895, decades before the NHL had a Black player.

Black communities in Nova Scotia date back to the late 1700s, when Black Loyalists arrived after the American Revolution. In Halifax and nearby towns, Black churches were centers of community life. In 1895, Black players and community leaders formed the Colored Hockey League of the Maritimes. Its teams came from towns around Halifax and Dartmouth.

The league played for decades, drawing crowds to games on outdoor and rink ice. White clubs and associations did not admit these teams, so the league created its own schedule and championships. Historians George and Darril Fosty told its story in their 2004 book Black Ice. Much of what is known comes from old newspapers and family memories gathered in that research.

The NHL’s first Black player came much later. Willie O’Ree of Fredericton, New Brunswick, first played for the Boston Bruins in 1958. The gap between 1895 and 1958 shows that an unwritten can shape a sport even when no rule names race. The Colored Hockey League is one of the institutions that kept playing during those years.

33.8

Women on the Ice

Main ideaWomen played hockey from the 1890s, but world championships, Olympic events and college titles for women came only from 1990 on.

Around 1890, Isobel Stanley, the governor general’s daughter, was photographed playing hockey outdoors in Ottawa. By the 1890s, women’s teams played at Canadian universities and in towns. They often played in long skirts. In the 1930s, the Preston Rivulettes of Ontario won many Canadian women’s titles.

International bodies were slow to add women. The International Ice Hockey Federation held its first Women’s World Championship in Ottawa in 1990, and Canada won. Women’s hockey joined the Olympic program at Nagano, Japan, in 1998. The United States won that first Olympic gold medal, beating Canada in the final.

In American colleges, of 1972 pushed schools to add women’s teams. The NCAA held its first national championship in women’s ice hockey in 2001, won by the University of Minnesota Duluth. In 1992 goalie Manon Rhéaume played in an NHL preseason game for the Tampa Bay Lightning, the first woman to do so.

A World Game with Written Rules

33.9

Paris, 1908, and the Olympics

Main ideaEuropean nations founded an international hockey federation in 1908, and hockey entered the Olympic Games in 1920.

On May 15, 1908, delegates met in Paris and founded the Ligue Internationale de Hockey sur Glace. Today it is called the International Ice Hockey Federation. Its first members were European, including France, Belgium, Switzerland, Great Britain and Bohemia. Canada and the United States joined in 1920.

Ice hockey first appeared at the Olympics in 1920 at Antwerp, Belgium, during the Summer Games. Canada sent the Winnipeg Falcons, a club made up mostly of players from Icelandic immigrant families. The Falcons won the gold medal. When the first Winter Olympics were held at Chamonix, France, in 1924, hockey moved there.

International hockey needed a shared rule book, because North American and European rinks and rules differed. The wrote rules for its tournaments. For decades the Olympics admitted only , so NHL players stayed home. NHL players first took part in the Olympic tournament in 1998.

33.10

Injuries and the Rule Book

Main ideaA goalie's mask in 1959 and a player's death in 1968 pushed hockey toward rules requiring head protection.

For decades most NHL goalies played without masks. On November 1, 1959, Montreal Canadiens goalie Jacques Plante was cut in the face by a shot during a game in New York. He returned wearing a mask he had made for practice. His coach had resisted, but Plante kept wearing it, and masks slowly became normal for goalies.

On January 13, 1968, Bill Masterton of the Minnesota North Stars hit his head on the ice during a game, and he died two days later. He was the first NHL player to die from an injury suffered in a game. The league later named a trophy for him. His death led many to call for , but the league did not require them at once.

In 1979 the NHL adopted a rule that players entering the league after that year must wear helmets. Players already in the league could choose, under a . The last player to play without a helmet, Craig MacTavish, retired in 1997. The rule shows how leagues often change slowly, protecting new players while exempting veterans.

The story

A Letter from Denver

In 1907 a baseball commission trusted one old man's letter, and a legend got a museum.

In 1905, sporting-goods maker Albert Spalding set up a commission to decide where baseball came from. He had argued with the writer Henry Chadwick, who said the game grew from the English children's game of rounders. Spalding wanted an American origin. The commission was led by Abraham G. Mills, a former president of the National League.

The commission asked the public for memories. A mining engineer in Denver named Abner Graves wrote back. He said that in Cooperstown, New York, around 1839, a young man named Abner Doubleday had drawn a diagram of a new game. Graves was a boy at the time, and he wrote his letter more than sixty years later.

On December 30, 1907, the Mills Commission issued its report. It named Doubleday as baseball's inventor and Cooperstown as the place. Doubleday had died in 1893. He had become a Union general in the Civil War, and his own papers never mentioned baseball. In 1839 he was a cadet at West Point.

Historians have long rejected the story. Yet in 1939 the National Baseball Hall of Fame and Museum opened in Cooperstown, timed to a claimed hundredth birthday of the game. A single late letter helped decide where the sport's most famous museum stands.

34.1

Older Papers About Baseball

Main ideaWritten records older than 1839 show that games called baseball existed before the Doubleday story says the game was invented.

In 2004 a historian in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, drew attention to a town bylaw from 1791. It banned playing games such as "baseball" within eighty yards of the new meeting house, to protect its windows. The bylaw was written almost fifty years before 1839. It shows that a game with that name was already common enough to regulate.

The English novelist Jane Austen mentioned "base ball" in Northanger Abbey, written in the late 1790s and published in 1817. Her heroine enjoys the game as a girl. English children’s books of the 1700s also printed rhymes about a game called base-ball. These sources point to a family of bat-and-base games brought to North America from England.

Historians call the Doubleday story an , a story that gives a sport a single inventor and a single birthday. The real history is a slow change, from folk games to club rules such as the Knickerbocker rules of 1845. The Pittsfield bylaw is a , a town document that recorded a problem, not a sport’s triumph.

34.2

A List of Olympic Victors

Main ideaThe famous date 776 BCE for the first Olympic Games comes from a list of winners compiled by Hippias of Elis around 400 BCE.

Around 400 BCE, a scholar named Hippias of Elis compiled a list of Olympic victors. Elis was the city-state that ran the festival at Olympia. Hippias counted backward through the winners of the footrace called the . His list began with a runner named Koroibos of Elis, dated to what we call 776 BCE.

Later Greek writers used this list to count time itself. They dated events by , the four-year spans between festivals. The philosopher Aristotle also worked on a list of victors. Much later, Christian and Roman writers copied these lists, and the 776 BCE date passed into modern textbooks.

Modern historians treat the early part of the list with care. Hippias wrote more than three hundred years after the first names on it. He may have used local memories, temple records or guesses. Archaeology shows that people used the site at Olympia before 776 BCE, so the date marks the start of a list, not proof of the first games.

34.3

William Webb Ellis and a Plaque

Main ideaThe story that William Webb Ellis invented rugby in 1823 rests on a letter written more than fifty years later, yet the World Cup trophy bears his name.

A plaque at Rugby School in England says that in 1823 a pupil named William Webb Ellis picked up the ball and ran with it, in disregard of the football rules of his time. The story first appeared in 1876, in a letter by Matthew Bloxam, a former pupil. Bloxam had left the school before 1823 and did not see the event himself.

In 1895 the Old Rugbeian Society, a club of former pupils, looked into the story. It found no one who could confirm it. Webb Ellis had died in 1872, in France, after a career as a clergyman. He never claimed to have started a new game. Still, the society accepted the tale, and the school honored him.

The story shows how an institution can adopt a legend. When the first was held in 1987, its trophy was called the Webb Ellis Cup. Historians describe rugby’s real history as a slow split among school football codes, leading to the Rugby Football Union in 1871. The is a record of what people later believed.

34.4

A Trophy Stolen Twice

Main ideaThe first soccer World Cup trophy was stolen in 1966 and found by a dog, then stolen again in 1983 and never recovered.

The first FIFA World Cup trophy was a gilded statue of a winged figure. It was later named for Jules Rimet, the FIFA president who pushed for the tournament. In March 1966, months before England hosted the World Cup, the trophy was stolen from a public exhibition in London. A week later, a dog named Pickles found it wrapped in newspaper under a hedge in south London.

FIFA’s rules said that a nation winning the trophy three times would keep it for good. Brazil won its third title in 1970 in Mexico and took the Jules Rimet Trophy home permanently. It was displayed at the offices of the Brazilian football confederation in Rio de Janeiro. In December 1983 thieves stole it there, and it has never been found.

FIFA commissioned a new trophy, designed by the Italian artist Silvio Gazzaniga, first awarded in 1974. Under newer rules, winners do not keep the original; they receive a . The two thefts show that a trophy is more than a prize. It is an , an object that carries a nation’s memory, and losing it cannot be undone.

34.5

What a Gold Medal Is Made Of

Main ideaOlympic medals changed from silver for winners in 1896 to solid gold by 1904 and to gold-plated silver after 1912.

At the first modern Olympics in Athens in 1896, winners received a silver medal and an olive branch. Runners-up received a bronze or copper medal. Third place received nothing official. The now-familiar order of gold, silver and bronze was first used at the 1904 Games in St. Louis.

The 1912 Games in Stockholm were the last to award medals of . After that, the cost of gold made such medals too expensive. Modern rules from the International Olympic Committee say a gold medal must be mostly silver and with at least six grams of pure gold. Each host city designs its own medal within those rules.

A medal’s metal tells a story about money and meaning. The word gold stays in the name even though the object is mostly silver. Collectors and museums prize medals from 1912 and earlier. Some athletes have sold their medals, and some Olympic medals have been reissued after officials changed results.

Two Typed Pages at Auction

Main ideaJames Naismith's original typed rules for basketball sold at auction in 2010 and now sit on display at the University of Kansas.

James Naismith’s original rules for basketball fit on two typed pages from December 1891. The pages stayed with his family for decades. In December 2010 the auction house Sotheby’s sold them in New York. David Booth, an investor who had studied at the University of Kansas, paid about 4.3 million dollars.

Booth gave the pages to the University of Kansas, where Naismith had coached and taught from 1898. The university built a new home for them, the DeBruce Center, which opened in 2016 next to Allen Fieldhouse. The pages are shown behind glass with controlled light. An moved a family keepsake into public view.

The sale raised questions that historians and collectors still discuss. Should key sports documents belong to families, private buyers or public museums? A , the record of who owned an object and when, helps buyers trust that a document is real. The Naismith pages had a clear line of ownership from the inventor to his family.

34.7

A Home Plate in a Parking Lot

Main ideaChicago's first Comiskey Park stood from 1910 to 1991, and a marker in a parking lot now shows where its home plate was.

Comiskey Park opened on Chicago’s South Side on July 1, 1910. White Sox owner Charles Comiskey built it of steel and concrete. The architect was Zachary Taylor Davis, who later designed the park now called Wrigley Field. Concrete stands replaced wooden ones, which often burned.

The park hosted the first Major League All-Star Game in 1933. It also hosted the East-West All-Star Game of the Negro Leagues from 1933 on. The White Sox played their last game there on September 30, 1990. A new park opened across 35th Street in 1991, and the old one was torn down that year.

Today the site of the old park is a parking lot. A marble painted into the pavement shows where home plate stood. The architect’s , the drawing of where the park sat on its block, helps historians match old photographs to the ground. A marker turns an empty lot into a record of a building.

34.8

Lights at Wrigley, 1988

Main ideaWrigley Field played only day games until 1988, when the city allowed a limited number of night games.

Other major league parks added lights for night games starting in 1935, in Cincinnati. The Cubs’ owner, Philip K. Wrigley, planned to add lights in 1941. After the attack on Pearl Harbor that December, he gave the steel and wiring to the war effort. For decades afterward, the Cubs played every home game in daylight.

Wrigley Field sits in a crowded neighborhood of homes and apartments. Neighbors worried that night games would bring noise, traffic and late crowds. In the 1980s, state and city rules restricted night games there. In 1988 the Chicago City Council approved lights, with a limit on how many night games the Cubs could play each season.

The first night game at Wrigley began on August 8, 1988, but rain stopped it before it became official. The first completed night game came the next evening, August 9. The fight over lights shows that a ballpark is part of a city’s , the rules about how land in each area may be used. A can shape a team’s schedule.

Camden Yards Looks Backward

Main ideaOriole Park at Camden Yards, opened in 1992, used an old warehouse and an old-style plan, and many cities copied it.

From the 1960s to the 1980s, many cities built round, concrete stadiums for both baseball and football. The Orioles’ new park in Baltimore went the other way. Oriole Park at Camden Yards opened on April 6, 1992. It was built for baseball only, on the site of an old railroad yard downtown.

The design kept the long brick warehouse of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad beyond right field. The park used steel and brick to look like ballparks of the early 1900s. The Maryland Stadium Authority, a state body, paid for and owned it. The architecture firm HOK Sport led the design.

Camden Yards began a wave of , new parks built to look old. Cleveland, Denver, San Francisco and Pittsburgh soon opened parks in the same style. The plan shows that design copies design. It also shows , keeping an old building and giving it a new job.

Main ideaEach sport's hall of fame chose a town tied to a story about its beginning, from Cooperstown to Canton.

34.11

Researchers Build the Record

Main ideaGroups of researchers, such as SABR and Retrosheet, and libraries such as the LA84 Foundation's, gather and check the records sports history depends on.

In August 1971 a small group of baseball researchers met in Cooperstown. Led by L. Robert Davids, they founded the Society for American Baseball Research, known as . Members, many of them unpaid, dig through old newspapers and box scores. They correct records, write biographies and publish research journals.

In 1989 David Smith founded Retrosheet, a project to collect the of old major league games. Volunteers type up scorebooks and newspaper accounts so that the record of each game can be checked. Their work has helped fix errors in official statistics. The data is free for anyone to use.

Some archives came from the Games themselves. The 1984 Los Angeles Olympics ended with a surplus, and part of it created a foundation in Southern California, now called the LA84 Foundation. Its library holds books, magazines and Olympic reports open to researchers. An like this keeps paper that clubs and leagues might throw away.
