Festivals, Arts and Everyday Life

Living Culture

Every group of people in the world has special days. They light lamps, dance in parades, cook favorite foods and remember loved ones. They also play games, sing songs and tell stories. These things make up a big part of a people's culture, or way of life.

In this unit you will visit Carnival in Brazil, Lunar New Year in China and Korea, Diwali in India and Día de los Muertos in Mexico. You will hear mariachi and flamenco, play chess and soccer, and meet Anansi and the Monkey King. You will also see festivals right here in Chicago.

By the end, you will be able to explain why people celebrate. You will compare two traditions from different places. You will also trace how music, games and stories travel and blend into something new.

The big question

What can the ways people celebrate, play and tell stories teach us about what they care about?

The story

The Sugar Skull

Lucía thinks a skull will be scary. Her grandmother shows her it is full of love.

It is the end of October in Pilsen, a Mexican American neighborhood in Chicago. Lucía sits at her grandmother's kitchen table. In front of her is a small white skull. It is made of sugar. Lucía frowns. Why would anyone want a skull?

Her grandmother, Abuela Rosa, smiles. She hands Lucía a tube of pink icing. "We decorate it," she says. "Flowers for the eyes. A big smile. Then we write a name on the forehead." Lucía asks whose name. "Your grandfather's," Abuela says softly.

Lucía draws blue petals around the eyes. She adds yellow dots and a curly smile. Abuela writes "Tomás" across the top. The skull no longer looks scary. It looks cheerful, like Grandpa Tomás did when he laughed.

In the living room, Abuela has built a table with photos, candles and orange marigolds. She sets the skull next to Grandpa's picture. She adds a cup of his favorite hot chocolate. "This is our ofrenda," she says. "On Día de los Muertos, we remember the people we love."

Lucía looks at the photo and the bright skull. She feels a little sad. She also feels close to her grandfather. Abuela squeezes her hand. "Remembering is a way of loving," she says. Lucía nods. Next year, she wants to make one herself.

Carnival in Brazil

Main ideaCarnival in Brazil is a huge festival of music, dancing and costumes held just before Lent.

Picture a street in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Drums pound. Thousands of dancers wear feathers and sequins. Giant floats roll by. People on the sidewalks sing and dance too. This is , one of the biggest parties on Earth.

Carnival comes just before Lent. Lent is a season of about forty days in the Christian calendar. Many Christians give something up during Lent. So people celebrate with joy before it starts. Carnival lasts several days in February or March.

In Rio, groups called practice all year. They build floats and sew costumes. Then they parade and dance to samba music. Samba grew from the music of Afro-Brazilian communities. Judges pick a winning school. Other Brazilian cities have their own Carnival styles.

Lunar New Year in China

Main ideaIn China, the Spring Festival welcomes the new year of the moon calendar with family, food and the color red.

Mei’s family cleans every corner of their home in Beijing. They hang red paper on the doors. Red stands for good luck. Tomorrow is the . It is China’s biggest holiday. Many people call it Lunar New Year.

The date comes from a . A lunar calendar follows the moon. So the holiday moves each year. It falls in January or February. Each year is also named for one of twelve animals, like the dragon or the rabbit.

Families travel far to be together. On New Year’s Eve they share a big dinner. In the north, many families fold dumplings. Children get red envelopes with money inside. Lion dances and fireworks fill the streets. The festival ends about fifteen days later with the Lantern Festival.

Seollal in Korea

Main ideaKorea celebrates Lunar New Year as Seollal, with bows of respect to elders and a bowl of rice cake soup.

Ji-woo puts on a bright . A hanbok is traditional Korean clothing. Today is , the Korean Lunar New Year. Ji-woo and her brother kneel before their grandparents. They bow all the way to the floor.

This deep bow is called sebae. It shows respect for elders. After the bow, the grandparents give good wishes for the year. They often give the children a little money too. Many families also honor their ancestors on this day.

Then everyone eats tteokguk. It is a soup with thin slices of rice cake. Many Koreans say eating it makes you one year older. China and Korea usually share the same holiday date. Yet each country celebrates in its own way.

Main ideaDiwali is a festival of lights celebrated in India and around the world by Hindus, Sikhs and Jains.

At dusk in Jaipur, India, Priya sets small clay lamps along the steps of her home. Each lamp is called a . Soon hundreds of little flames glow down the street. It is , the festival of lights.

Diwali comes in October or November. It lasts about five days. Hindus, Sikhs and Jains all celebrate it, each with their own stories. Many Hindus remember the god Rama returning home. People lit lamps to welcome him. The lights stand for good winning over evil.

Día de los Muertos in Mexico

Main ideaOn Día de los Muertos, families in Mexico remember loved ones who have died, with altars, flowers and food.

Remember Lucía’s sugar skull? She was getting ready for . The name means Day of the Dead. It is celebrated on November 1 and 2. It began in Mexico and blends Indigenous and Catholic traditions.

Families build an . An ofrenda is an altar with photos of loved ones who have died. They add candles, the person’s favorite foods and sweet bread called pan de muerto. Orange marigolds light the way with their color and smell.

Many families visit the cemetery. They clean the graves and decorate them with flowers. Some stay late, sharing food and stories. The day is not meant to be scary. It is a way to say that the people we love are still remembered.

Main ideaOktoberfest in Munich began with a royal wedding in 1810 and grew into a giant fair of food, music and rides.

In 1810, Crown Prince Ludwig of Bavaria married Princess Therese. The city of Munich threw a party in a big field. There was a horse race. Everyone was invited. People liked it so much that they did it again the next year.

That party became . Today it starts in late September and ends in early October. Millions of visitors come. They ride a Ferris wheel and roller coasters. They eat big soft pretzels and roast chicken. Brass bands play in huge tents.

Many people wear from Bavaria. Men may wear leather shorts called lederhosen. Women may wear a dress and apron called a dirndl. Other cities, including some in the United States, now hold their own Oktoberfests.

Cherry Blossom Season in Japan

Main ideaIn spring, people in Japan gather under blooming cherry trees for hanami, a custom of enjoying the flowers.

In early spring, the cherry trees in a Tokyo park burst into pale pink. Families spread blue mats on the grass. Friends share rice balls and tea. Petals drift down like snow. This custom is called , which means flower viewing.

Cherry blossoms are called . They bloom for only a week or two. Then the petals fall. Many people in Japan see this as a reminder. Beautiful things do not last forever, so enjoy them now.

The flowers open first in the warm south and later in the cool north. TV news shows maps of where the trees are blooming. People have enjoyed hanami for more than a thousand years. In 1912, Tokyo gave cherry trees to Washington, D.C., as a gift of friendship.

Independence Days

Main ideaMany countries celebrate the day they became free to govern themselves.

On July 4, fireworks burst over towns across the United States. The day marks 1776. That year, leaders approved the Declaration of Independence. It said the colonies were free from Britain. A day like this is called an .

Other nations have their own. Mexico celebrates on September 16. In 1810, a priest named Miguel Hidalgo rang a church bell and called for freedom. Brazil celebrates on September 7, from 1822. India celebrates on August 15, from 1947.

These days share some things. There are flags, parades and speeches. Families picnic and watch fireworks. Each one helps a country remember its . It also tells people they belong to one nation.

Four Reasons to Celebrate

Main ideaMost festivals celebrate the harvest, history, family or faith, and many celebrate more than one.

Look back at the festivals in this chapter. Why do people hold them? Most have one of four reasons. The first is the . The harvest is when crops are gathered. In Korea, the fall holiday Chuseok gives thanks for the harvest.

The second reason is history. Independence Days remember big events in a nation’s past. The third reason is family. Lunar New Year brings families home. Día de los Muertos keeps loved ones in memory.

The fourth reason is . Faith means religious belief. Diwali and the season before Lent come from religious calendars. Many festivals mix reasons. Diwali is about faith, family and a fresh start. Knowing the reasons helps you understand a festival from the inside.

The story

One Ball, Five Countries

A single soccer ball travels the world and joins five different games.

The ball starts in a small factory in Sialkot, Pakistan. Many of the world's hand-stitched soccer balls have been made in this city. A worker pulls the last stitch tight. The ball is round and white with black patches. It goes into a box and onto a ship.

In Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, a boy named Davi gets the ball for his birthday. He plays barefoot on the beach with his cousins. They juggle it with their knees and heads. When Davi's family moves away, he gives the ball to his best friend's older brother.

The brother takes it to Lagos, Nigeria, where he goes to college. Kids on a dusty lot use two sandals for a goal. They shout in Yoruba and English. The ball gets scuffed and gray. A visiting teacher from England admires their skill. She is given the ball as a thank-you gift.

In a park in London, her nephew kicks it with friends after school. Later, he moves with his family to Seoul, South Korea. There the ball rolls across a school field. Children cheer in Korean. Last of all, it flies to Chicago, where a girl named Amara plays in Humboldt Park.

The ball is worn out now. Its seams are loose. But it has heard many languages. Every child who kicked it knew the same rules. Nobody needed a translator to play. Amara holds the old ball and wonders where it will go next.

Mariachi from Mexico

Main ideaMariachi is a kind of Mexican music played by a group with violins, trumpets and guitars.

A wedding party dances in Guadalajara, Mexico. Suddenly, a group of musicians walks in. They wear matching suits with silver buttons and wide hats. Trumpets ring out. Violins sing. This is a band.

Mariachi music comes from western Mexico, especially the state of Jalisco. A band often has violins, trumpets and several kinds of guitar. One is the guitarrón, a very large guitar that plays the low notes. The musicians sing too.

Mariachi bands play at birthdays, weddings and holidays. In 2011, UNESCO added mariachi to its list of important living traditions. UNESCO is part of the United Nations. Today mariachi bands play all over the world, including in Chicago.

Main ideaFlamenco is a music and dance from southern Spain, shaped strongly by the Roma people of Andalusia.

In Seville, Spain, a dancer stands very still. A guitarist begins to play. A singer lets out a long, deep cry. Then the dancer’s heels strike the floor, fast as rain. People around her clap in sharp rhythms. This is .

Flamenco comes from Andalusia, in southern Spain. The Roma people, called Gitanos in Spain, did much to create it. Their music mixed with other traditions of the region. Flamenco has three main parts: singing, guitar and dance.

Flamenco songs can be joyful or full of sorrow. Hand clapping, called palmas, keeps the . Dancers stamp their feet to add sound. In 2010, UNESCO named flamenco an important living tradition too.

The Sitar and the Talking Drum

Main ideaThe Indian sitar and the West African talking drum are instruments with special sounds and long histories.

The is a stringed instrument from India. It has a long wooden neck and a round body, often made from a gourd. Some strings are plucked. Others ring on their own, making a shimmering sound. Ravi Shankar made the sitar famous around the world in the 1960s.

The comes from West Africa. It is shaped like an hourglass. Cords run from one drumhead to the other. A drummer holds it under one arm. Squeezing the cords makes the sound go higher. Letting go makes it lower.

Many West African languages, like Yoruba, are tonal. That means a word’s meaning changes with high or low sound. A skilled drummer can copy those sounds. Listeners can recognize names and messages. The drum can almost talk.

Think it through
Squeeze and Listen

A drummer in Nigeria taps the talking drum. At first the tone is low. He squeezes the cords under his arm. The next taps rise higher. He lets go and they fall. High, low, high, high, low. The pattern matches the tones of someone's name.

Soccer Around the World

Main ideaSoccer, called football in most countries, is the world's most popular sport and is easy to play almost anywhere.

Chess from India and Persia

Main ideaChess began in India as chaturanga, grew in Persia, and spread around the world.

Two players sit over a board of 64 squares. They move kings, queens, knights and pawns. This game is . It is played in parks, schools and online in nearly every country. But where did it start?

Most historians trace chess to India about 1,400 years ago. There it was called . The pieces stood for parts of an army: foot soldiers, horses, elephants and chariots. Chaturanga then traveled to Persia, today’s Iran. There it was called shatranj.

From Persia, the game spread to Arab lands and then to Europe. Along the way, pieces changed. The Persian adviser piece became the queen. Our word checkmate comes from the Persian shah mat. It means the king is helpless.

The Brothers Grimm

Main ideaJacob and Wilhelm Grimm collected German folktales and published them in 1812.

In the early 1800s, two brothers in Germany went looking for stories. Their names were Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm. They listened to neighbors, friends and storytellers. They wrote the tales down carefully.

In 1812, they published their first book of . It included Hansel and Gretel, Little Red Riding Hood and Rapunzel. Many of the stories were darker than the cartoon versions you may know.

How Culture Travels and Blends

Main ideaMusic, games and stories spread when people move, trade and share, and they often blend into something new.

Think about everything in this chapter. Chess moved from India to Persia to Europe. Soccer sailed from England to Brazil. Anansi crossed the ocean to Jamaica. Culture travels when people move, trade and share.

When cultures meet, they often . Blend means to mix into something new. Flamenco blended Roma music with other sounds of Spain. K-pop blended Korean songs with American hip-hop. The new thing belongs to both.

Blending does not erase where something came from. Chaturanga became chess, but it is still India’s gift. Knowing a thing’s roots is a way to show . It honors the people who made it first.
