The story

Sweet Tea for a Tiny Statue

Children line up in a temple full of flowers to pour tea over a very small statue.

It is a spring morning in a Japanese American temple. The room smells of flowers. Near the front is a tiny roof made of blossoms. Under the roof stands a small statue of a baby. The baby points one hand up and one hand down.

A girl named Emi waits in line with her little brother. Her grandmother holds their coats. When Emi reaches the front, she picks up a small wooden ladle. She dips it into a bowl of sweet tea.

Emi pours the tea gently over the statue. Her brother does the same. Their grandmother bows her head. She tells them this day is called Hanamatsuri, the Flower Festival.

Buddhists in Japan keep this day to remember the birth of the Buddha. Their stories say he was born in a garden full of flowers. After the pouring, the children drink a cup of the sweet tea. Then everyone shares lunch.

Remembering the Buddha

13.1

Vesak: Three Events, One Day

Main ideaMany Buddhists keep Vesak to remember the Buddha's birth, awakening and death all on one day.

In Sri Lanka, Thailand and many other places, a big holiday comes in spring. It is called . Some people spell it Wesak. It falls on the full moon of a month in April or May.

Many Theravada Buddhists teach that three things happened on this same full moon. The Buddha was born. He reached awakening under a tree. Many years later, he died. So Vesak remembers all three.

On Vesak, families visit the temple. They bring flowers and light lamps. Many take the five promises called for the day. Streets fill with lanterns. Some people give free food to anyone who walks by.

13.2

Bathing the Baby Buddha

Main ideaIn Japan and other places, Buddhists remember the Buddha's birth by pouring sweet tea over a small statue.

In Japan, the Buddha’s birthday is often kept on April 8. The day is called , which means Flower Festival. Temples build a small shrine covered in flowers.

Inside stands a statue of the baby Buddha. Buddhist stories say that right after he was born, he took seven steps. He pointed up to the sky and down to the earth. The statue shows him that way.

People pour sweet tea over the statue with a ladle. Many say this remembers a story that the heavens sent gentle rain to wash the new baby. Korean, Chinese and Taiwanese Buddhists also bathe the baby Buddha on his birthday.

13.3

Different Lands, Different Calendars

Main ideaBuddhists in different countries keep the Buddha's birthday on different dates.

Not every Buddhist keeps the Buddha’s birthday on the same day. Many Theravada countries use a moon calendar. So Vesak moves a little each year. Some use the sun instead.

In Japan, most temples use April 8 on the modern calendar. In Korea, China and Taiwan, many keep the eighth day of the fourth moon month. That day falls in April or May.

This does not mean one group is right. Each community follows its own . A calendar counts months by the moon. Families in Illinois may keep the day of the country their family came from.

Uposatha: Moon Days

Main ideaUposatha days are moon days when many Buddhists visit the temple and keep extra promises.

Some Buddhist holy days come every week or two. They are called days. They follow the phases of the moon, like the full moon and the new moon.

On these days, many Theravada laypeople go to the temple. A is a Buddhist who is not a monk or nun. They listen to a talk and give food. Some keep eight promises instead of five for that day.

Monks meet on the full moon and new moon days too. The Vinaya, the book of rules for monks and nuns, says they gather to recite their rules together. It is a way to keep the community honest.

13.5

Kathina: Robes for Monks

Main ideaAt Kathina, laypeople give cloth for robes to monks after the rainy season retreat.

In the rainy season, many Theravada monks stay in one temple for about three months. This is the rains . The Vinaya tells the story of why: walking in the rain could crush young plants and small creatures.

When the retreat ends, families hold a festival called . They bring cloth for new robes. They also bring food, soap and other things monks need.

People often walk in a happy line, carrying the gifts. For many Buddhists, giving is a way to make merit. Merit is the good that Buddhists believe comes from good actions.

The Vinaya tells that people complained when monks walked about in the rains, treading on green plants and small living things. So the Buddha set a rule that monks should stay in one place for the rainy season.
Vinaya Pitaka, Mahavagga III.1; see Rhys Davids and Oldenberg, Vinaya Texts, Sacred Books of the East 13 (1882) (paraphrased)

13.6

Lanterns, Flowers and Gifts

Main ideaLamps, flowers and incense are common offerings, and Buddhists give them meaning.

At many festivals, people bring to the shrine. Common ones are flowers, candles or lamps, incense and food. These gifts show respect for the Buddha. Each one is placed with care on the shrine table.

Buddhists often explain what each gift means. A lamp can stand for wisdom that lights the way. Fresh flowers wilt in a few days, so many teachers say they remind people that all things change.

Some offerings are also shared. Food given to the temple often feeds monks and guests. In this way, a festival is also a time for a whole to eat together.

Remembering Family and New Beginnings

13.7

Obon: Welcoming Ancestors

Main ideaAt Obon, Japanese Buddhists honor family members who have died, with lanterns and dancing.

In summer, many Japanese and Japanese American Buddhists keep . It is a time to remember , the family members who lived before us. Many families keep it in July or August.

Families clean graves and set out food. Many light lanterns. At temples, people join in circle dances called Bon Odori. Buddhist temples in Chicago and across America hold Obon festivals, and neighbors are welcome.

Obon connects to a story. A student of the Buddha named Moggallana, called Mokuren in Japan, saw that his mother was suffering after death. The Buddha taught him to make offerings to monks to help her. The story says she was freed, and he danced for joy.

13.8

Losar: Tibetan New Year

Main ideaLosar is the Tibetan New Year, a time for cleaning, prayer flags, family and fresh starts.

Tibetan Buddhists keep a new year festival called . It follows a Tibetan moon calendar. It usually comes in February or March. Families look forward to it all winter.

Before Losar, families clean the house to sweep out the old year. They make special foods and fried pastries. Many hang new . These are colored cloth flags printed with prayers and blessings.

On Losar, people visit monasteries and make offerings. They greet family and friends and wish them a good year. Tibetan families in America keep Losar too, often at a community center or temple.

Main ideaBuddhist festivals follow the seasons and help communities share memory, giving and joy.

Put the festivals together, and you see a whole . Losar begins the year for Tibetans. The Buddha’s birthday comes in spring. The rains retreat and Kathina follow the wet season.

Obon comes in summer. Uposatha moon days come all year round. Each festival fits a place and its weather. Some festivals are big and noisy. Others are quiet and simple. All of them help people remember.

Many Buddhists say festivals help them remember the Buddha’s teachings. They also bring families together. A child may learn the stories best by joining the festival year after year, a tradition passed down.

The story

A New Temple in a New City

A family leaves a camp in 1944 and helps build a temple in Chicago.

During World War II, the U.S. government forced Japanese Americans on the West Coast to leave their homes. Families were sent to camps behind barbed wire. Many of these families were Buddhist.

In the camps, people still met for Buddhist services. They sat in plain wooden barracks. They chanted and listened to talks. A priest helped them keep their faith and their hope.

Some families were allowed to leave the camps if they moved away from the West Coast. Many came to Chicago. They found jobs and small apartments. But they missed their temple.

In 1944, a Buddhist priest named Gyomay Kubose and a group of Japanese Americans started a temple in Chicago. Today it is called the Buddhist Temple of Chicago. It still welcomes people every week.

14.6

The Alms Round

Main ideaIn parts of Asia, monks walk each morning to receive food, and families feel glad to give.

In Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Myanmar, many monks walk out early each morning. They walk quietly in a line, carrying . This is the alms round.

Families wait by the road. They place rice and other food in the bowls. Often they kneel or bow. The monks may chant a short blessing in return. No one hurries.

Monks do not ask for food. They simply walk. Buddhists say the round gives laypeople a chance to practice every day. Some temples in America hold a version of this on special days.
