Torah: Genesis and Exodus

Torah

The Torah, the first five books of the Tanakh, begins with two long books that most readers know at least in pieces: Genesis (Bereshit) and Exodus (Shemot). Genesis moves from the creation of the world to one family and its promises. Exodus follows that family, now a people, out of slavery, to a mountain, and into the building of a tent for God to dwell among them. Together they set up nearly everything the rest of the Tanakh assumes.

In this unit you will read these books the way Jewish tradition reads them, as Torah studied week by week, and the way historians and literary readers read them, as texts from the ancient Near East with a shape, a style and a history. You will meet Rashi and the midrash, the Epic of Gilgamesh and the Code of Hammurabi, the Passover Haggadah and the spiritual "Go Down, Moses." You will not be asked to decide whether the stories are true; you will be asked to say what they say and how their readers have read them.

By the end you will be able to name the parts of Genesis and Exodus in order, explain key terms such as covenant, parashah, Akedah, Aseret HaDibrot and Mishkan, quote passages in the JPS 1917 translation, compare a biblical story with a neighboring ancient text, and describe how the same verses are read differently by Jewish and Christian readers and by historians.

The big question

How do Genesis and Exodus turn the story of a world, a family and a rescue into the covenant that the rest of the Tanakh builds on?

Genesis: From Creation to Joseph

Bereshit
Big questionHow does the book of Genesis tie a creation story, a flood, and one family's promises into a single thread?
The story

The Walk Up the Mountain

A father and a son climb a hill with wood, fire and a knife, and the son asks a question that the text never fully answers.

Early in the morning, an old man saddles a donkey. The book of Genesis says his name is Abraham. He cuts wood, wakes two servants and his son Isaac, and sets out for a place called Moriah. The text gives no speech, no argument, no tears. It says only that he rose early and went.

On the third day, Abraham sees the place far off. He tells the servants to wait. He puts the wood on Isaac's back and carries the fire and the knife himself. Genesis 22 says the two of them walked on together. Then Isaac speaks. He says, "Father." Abraham answers, "Here am I, my son."

Isaac asks the question that readers have turned over for more than two thousand years. He sees the fire and the wood. Where is the lamb for the offering? Abraham answers that God will see to the lamb. The text says again that the two of them walked on together. It does not say what either of them was thinking.

Jewish tradition calls this story the *Akedah*, the binding of Isaac. It is read in synagogues on Rosh Hashanah, the Jewish New Year. The rabbis of the midrash filled its silences with stories. Modern readers, Jewish and not, have argued about what it teaches and whether Abraham was right to go.

This chapter walks through the whole book of Genesis, called *Bereshit* in Hebrew, from the first words about creation to the last words about Joseph in Egypt. The book moves from the whole world to one family. Along the way it keeps returning to one idea: a promise, and the people who carry it.

Talk about itGenesis 22 never says what Isaac was thinking. Why might a storyteller leave that out on purpose, and what does the reader have to do because of it?
Section 1

The World Before the Family

17.1

Two Ways to Tell a Beginning

Main ideaGenesis opens with two accounts of creation, and readers have long noticed that they tell the story in different orders and with different names for God.

The Tanakh begins with the word , "in the beginning." That word is also the Hebrew name of the whole first book. In English the book is called Genesis, from a Greek word for origin. The first chapter counts off seven days. Light comes first, then sky and sea, land and plants, sun and moon, fish and birds, land animals, and last of all human beings. On the seventh day God rests. Jewish tradition connects that rest to Shabbat, the weekly day of rest.

Then, at Genesis 2:4, the story seems to begin again. This second account is told in a different style. It names God differently, using the four-letter name that JPS 1917 prints as "the LORD." It is not built on days. Here a man is formed from dust before any plants grow, then a garden is planted, then animals are made, and then a woman is formed from the man’s side. The order is not the same as chapter 1.

Readers have handled the two accounts in different ways. Rashi, the great medieval commentator, reads the second story as filling in the details of the first. Many modern scholars read them as two separate traditions placed side by side by an editor. Jewish tradition holds both chapters as Torah. In this course we describe the readings; we do not rule on which is right.

One thing both accounts share is parallelism of a kind. Ideas are repeated in patterns: "and God saw that it was good" returns like a refrain, and the days pair up, with light on day one and the lights of the sky on day four. Noticing these patterns is the first step of reading Genesis as literature, and Jewish readers have done it for centuries.

17.2

The Garden and the First Brothers

Main ideaGenesis 2 through 4 tells of a garden, a choice, and the first murder, and Jewish readers have drawn lessons about responsibility from each scene.

Genesis 2 places the first humans in a garden called Eden. The text says they may eat from every tree but one, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. A talking serpent tells the woman that eating it will open their eyes. She eats, and gives some to the man. When God asks what happened, the man blames the woman and the woman blames the serpent. They are sent out of the garden.

Jewish tradition does not usually read this scene as a story of "original sin" in the way many Christian readers do. The rabbis of the midrash and later commentators tend to read it as a story about choice and its results. The characters are given the power to decide, and they must live with what they chose. Christian tradition, especially after Augustine, reads it as the moment sin entered all human nature. Both readings begin from the same Hebrew words.

In Genesis 4 the first two brothers, Cain and Abel, bring offerings. The text says God accepts Abel’s and not Cain’s, without saying why. Cain, angry, kills his brother in a field. When God asks where Abel is, Cain answers with a question that has become famous: "Am I my brother’s keeper?" The text then says the voice of Abel’s blood cries out from the ground.

The Mishnah, the early rabbinic law code, draws on the word "blood" in that verse. It notes that the Hebrew word is plural, "bloods," and reads it to mean Abel’s blood and the blood of all the descendants he would never have. From this the Mishnah teaches that whoever destroys one life is as if he destroyed a whole world. That is a : a reading that fills a gap in the text with meaning.

17.3

Noah's Flood and Gilgamesh's Flood

Main ideaGenesis tells of a great flood, and an older Mesopotamian poem tells one too; comparing them shows what is shared and what Genesis says differently.

Genesis 6 through 9 tells of a flood that covers the earth. The text says the world had filled with violence, and God decides to wash it away. One man, Noah, is told to build an ark, a huge wooden boat, and to bring his family and pairs of every animal aboard. Rain falls for forty days. When the water goes down, Noah sends out a raven and then a dove. The dove comes back with an olive leaf. God then makes a with Noah and sets a rainbow in the sky as its sign.

In 1872 a scholar named George Smith, working at the British Museum in London, read a broken clay tablet from ancient Nineveh and found a flood story on it. It is part of the Epic of Gilgamesh, a long poem from Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Its hero, Utnapishtim, is warned by a god, builds a boat, saves animals, and sends out birds when the water goes down. The tablet is far older than any copy of Genesis we have.

The two stories share a shape: a warning, a boat, animals, birds, a mountain, an offering. They also differ. In Gilgamesh, many gods argue, and the flood comes because humans are too noisy. In Genesis, one God acts, and the reason is human violence. Genesis ends with a promise never to flood the earth again. Scholars use the older story to understand the world Genesis grew up in. Jewish tradition reads Genesis as Torah regardless of what its neighbors told.

For this course, the point is not to decide which story is "true." It is to see that Genesis was written in a world that already told flood stories, and to notice how the Torah’s telling stresses justice, one God, and a covenant. Those are the threads that run through the rest of the book.

17.4

Babel and the Shape of Genesis

Main ideaThe tower of Babel ends the first part of Genesis, and the book's own structure shows a shift from the whole world to one family.

Genesis 11 tells of a city where all people speak one language. They decide to build a tower with its top in the heavens, to make a name for themselves. The text says God comes down, mixes their language so they cannot understand each other, and scatters them. The city is called Babel. The Hebrew plays on the word for "mixed up." The name also points to Babylon, the great city of Mesopotamia whose tall stepped temples, called ziggurats, were famous.

With Babel, the first eleven chapters of Genesis come to a close. Scholars often call these chapters the primeval history, meaning the story of the earliest world. They move fast and cover the whole human race. Then, at the end of chapter 11, the camera narrows. A family in the city of Ur is named. One of its sons is Abram. From chapter 12 to chapter 50, Genesis follows that one family and no other.

Jewish tradition divides Genesis into twelve weekly readings, each called a . Each takes its name from one of its first words. The first, Bereshit, covers creation to Noah. Lech Lecha, "go forth," begins Abraham’s story. The last, Vayechi, "and he lived," ends with Joseph’s death in Egypt. Synagogues around the world read these portions in the same weeks each year, finishing the whole Torah in a year.

A useful way to hold the book in your head is by its own repeated phrase. Ten times Genesis says "these are the generations of" someone: the heavens and the earth, Adam, Noah, the sons of Noah, Shem, Terah, Ishmael, Isaac, Esau and Jacob. The Hebrew word is . Many readers use these headings as the book’s table of contents.

17.5

Abraham, Sarah and the Call

Main ideaGenesis 12 begins with a command to go and a promise of land, people and blessing, and this covenant is the thread the rest of the Tanakh follows.

Genesis 12 opens with a command and a promise. The text says God tells Abram to leave his country, his family and his father’s house, and to go to a land that God will show him. In return, God promises to make him a great nation, to bless him, and to make him a blessing. Abram goes. He takes his wife Sarai, his nephew Lot, and their household, and they travel to Canaan, the land that later becomes Israel.

The promise is repeated and made more exact in later chapters. In Genesis 15, the text says God shows Abram the stars and promises descendants as many as the stars. In Genesis 17, God changes his name to Abraham and Sarai’s to Sarah, and gives the sign of the covenant: circumcision, the that Jewish families still keep for baby boys on the eighth day. The word for this bond is covenant, and it comes up again and again.

Abraham and Sarah are old and have no children. Sarah laughs when she hears she will have a son. The son is born and named Isaac, which plays on the Hebrew word for laughter. Jewish tradition calls Abraham, Isaac and Jacob the , the fathers, and Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel and Leah the , the mothers. Many Jewish prayers begin by naming them.

Midrash adds stories the text does not tell. The most famous says that young Abram smashed the idols in his father’s shop and blamed the largest one, to show his father that idols cannot act. This story is not in Genesis. It is in a collection called Genesis Rabbah, from the early centuries of the Common Era. Knowing which parts are text and which are midrash is a basic skill in reading the Tanakh.

17.6

Hagar and Ishmael

Main ideaGenesis gives Hagar, Sarah's Egyptian servant, her own encounter with God and her own promise, and readers across traditions have treated her story with care.

Before Isaac is born, Sarah gives her Egyptian servant Hagar to Abraham so that a child can be born through her. That was a practice known in the ancient Near East. Hagar becomes pregnant and, the text says, looks down on Sarah. Sarah treats her harshly, and Hagar runs away into the wilderness. Genesis 16 says a messenger of God finds her by a spring. The messenger tells her to go back, and promises that her son will father a great people.

Hagar names God with a name of her own, one that JPS 1917 renders as "Thou art a God of seeing." Readers often note that she is the only person in the Tanakh who gives God a name of her own. Her son is called Ishmael, "God hears." Years later, after Isaac is born, Sarah asks Abraham to send Hagar and Ishmael away. Abraham is troubled. The text says God tells him to listen to Sarah, and promises that Ishmael too will become a nation.

In Genesis 21, Hagar and the boy run out of water in the wilderness. Hagar sets the child under a bush. She sits at a distance so she will not see him die. The text says God hears the boy’s voice, and Hagar sees a well. This chapter is read in synagogues on the first day of Rosh Hashanah, before the Akedah is read on the second. The two readings are paired: two sons of Abraham, each nearly lost, each spared.

Islamic tradition honors Hagar and Ishmael as ancestors of the Arab peoples and of the Prophet Muhammad. The Quran tells of them in its own way. Jewish and Christian readers have often noticed how much sympathy the Genesis text shows for a servant woman treated unfairly. Describing all these readings, without ranking them, is how this course handles a shared story.

17.7

The Akedah: Reading the Silence

Main ideaGenesis 22 tells the binding of Isaac in very few words, and Jewish tradition has read its gaps through midrash, liturgy and argument for two thousand years.

Genesis 22 begins by saying God tested Abraham. God tells him to take his son Isaac to the land of Moriah and offer him there. Abraham rises early and goes. On the mountain he builds an altar, binds Isaac, and lifts the knife. A messenger of God calls out and stops him. Abraham sees a ram caught in a thicket and offers it instead. The promise of blessing is repeated. Then Abraham returns to his servants. The text does not say that Isaac returned with him.

The Hebrew word for "binding" gives the story its name, the . Nineteen verses carry an enormous weight. Almost nothing about feelings is stated. The rabbis of the midrash noticed this and filled the gaps. One midrash has Abraham arguing with God about the contradiction between this command and the promise. Another has Isaac asking to be bound tightly so that he will not flinch. These are not in the text; they are readings of it.

Rashi reads the command "take thy son, thine only son, whom thou lovest, even Isaac" as a slow conversation. Abraham says he has two sons; God says the only one; Abraham says each is the only son of his mother; God says the one you love; Abraham says he loves both; and only then does God say Isaac. Rashi’s point is that the text piles up words to show the command being given gently, one step at a time.

The Akedah is read on the second day of Rosh Hashanah, and the shofar, a ram’s horn, is blown on that holiday. Tradition connects the horn to the ram in the thicket. Jewish thinkers of the modern era have read the story in opposite ways: as the highest picture of trust, or as a warning that a voice commanding harm to a child must be questioned. Christian tradition often reads Isaac carrying the wood as a picture of Jesus carrying the cross. Each reading begins from the same nineteen verses.

17.8

Rebekah, Jacob and Esau

Main ideaGenesis 25 to 28 tells how the younger twin, Jacob, gets the birthright and the blessing, and readers have long debated how the text judges him.

Isaac marries Rebekah, whom Abraham’s servant finds at a well in Genesis 24. Rebekah is barren for years, then carries twins who struggle inside her. The text says God tells her that two nations are in her womb and that the elder will serve the younger. The first twin is born red and hairy and named Esau. The second comes out holding his brother’s heel and is named Jacob, a name that plays on the word for heel.

Esau grows up a hunter; Jacob stays near the tents. One day Esau comes in from the field starving and sells his birthright, the older son’s share, to Jacob for a bowl of red lentil stew. Later, when Isaac is old and blind, Rebekah helps Jacob dress in Esau’s clothes and goatskins, and Jacob receives the blessing meant for Esau. Esau’s cry when he learns the truth is one of the most painful lines in Genesis. Jacob flees to his uncle Laban.

Readers have judged Jacob very differently. Some midrashim make Esau a villain so that Jacob’s actions look justified. Other readers, ancient and modern, note that the text itself never praises the trick. Jacob will later be deceived by Laban, who swaps a bride in the dark, just as Jacob deceived his father in the dark. Many readers see the text quietly balancing the scales.

On the road to Laban, Jacob sleeps with a stone for a pillow and dreams of a ladder set on the earth with its top in heaven and messengers of God going up and down. The text says God repeats the promise given to Abraham. Jacob names the place Beth-El, "house of God." This scene is read in synagogues in the parashah called Vayetze, "and he went out."

Jacob's Family and Joseph

17.9

Leah, Rachel and a Night of Wrestling

Main ideaJacob's two wives and their children become the twelve tribes, and a night of wrestling gives Jacob and the whole people the name Israel.

In Laban’s house Jacob works seven years for Rachel, is given Leah instead, and then works seven more for Rachel. Genesis 29 and 30 tell of the two sisters and their servants Bilhah and Zilpah, and of the children born to each. Leah, the text says, was unloved, and names her sons for her hope that her husband will love her. Rachel is barren for years before Joseph is born. Between them, twelve sons and a daughter, Dinah, are born.

Those twelve sons become the twelve of Israel in the rest of the Tanakh: Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher, Issachar, Zebulun, Joseph and Benjamin. Later, Joseph’s two sons Ephraim and Manasseh count as tribes in his place. Jewish parents bless their sons on Friday nights with the words Jacob speaks over those two grandsons in Genesis 48.

After twenty years, Jacob leaves Laban and heads home, afraid of Esau. The night before they meet, Genesis 32 says he is left alone by the Jabbok stream and a man wrestles with him until dawn. The stranger cannot beat him, touches his hip and leaves him limping, and then gives him a new name: . The text explains it as "he strives with God." Jacob names the place Peniel, "face of God," and says he has seen God face to face.

Who was the stranger? The text calls him a man. Hosea 12 calls him an angel. Some midrashim say he was Esau’s guardian angel. Some modern readers see Jacob wrestling with his own past. The name Israel is then given to Jacob’s descendants, and the Tanakh calls them "the children of Israel." The name itself, tradition notes, means struggle. The next morning Esau runs to meet Jacob, embraces him, and weeps.

Main ideaThe Joseph story in Genesis 37–50 reads like a short novel, with dreams, a pit, a prison, a palace, and a scene where brothers finally recognize each other.

They hate him, throw him into a pit, and sell him to traders headed for Egypt. They dip the coat in goat’s blood and let their father believe Joseph is dead. Many readers note the goat: Jacob once used goatskins to deceive his own father.

In Egypt, Joseph rises in the house of an official, is falsely accused by the official’s wife, and lands in prison. There he interprets the dreams of two of Pharaoh’s servants. When Pharaoh has dreams of seven fat cows and seven thin ones, Joseph is called from prison to explain them. He predicts seven years of plenty and seven of famine, and Pharaoh puts him in charge of storing grain. Joseph, sold as a slave, becomes the second most powerful man in Egypt.

Scholars often call these chapters a , a short novel, because the story is long, has one plot, and moves toward a single climax. The climax comes when famine drives the brothers to Egypt to buy grain. They bow before Joseph, as in the dreams, and do not know him. He tests them, hides a silver cup in Benjamin’s sack, and waits to see whether they will abandon this brother too. Judah steps forward and offers himself in Benjamin’s place.

Then Joseph can hold back no longer. Genesis 45 says he sends everyone out, weeps so loudly the Egyptians hear, and says, "I am Joseph; doth my father yet live?" The brothers cannot answer. This is the payoff of the whole story. Jacob’s family moves to Egypt, which sets up the book of Exodus. Genesis ends with Joseph’s death and his request that his bones one day be carried back to the land.

17.11

What Archaeology Can and Cannot Say

Main ideaHistorians place Genesis in the world of the ancient Near East and can describe that world, but they cannot confirm or deny the lives of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.

Genesis is set in a real region: Mesopotamia, Canaan and Egypt. Archaeologists have dug up cities the book names, such as Ur and Haran, and thousands of clay tablets from the second millennium BCE. Those tablets show customs like the ones in Genesis, such as a childless wife giving a servant to her husband so that an heir can be born. The Code of Hammurabi, a Babylonian law collection from c. 1750 BCE, sets out laws about slaves, marriage and injury that later readers compare with the Torah’s laws.

What archaeology cannot do is find Abraham. No inscription names him, Isaac or Jacob. Nomadic families leave few traces, and the events are placed long before any writing about Israel survives. The oldest object outside the Bible that names "Israel" at all is an Egyptian stone called the Merneptah Stele, from c. 1208 BCE, and it says nothing about the ancestors. Historians therefore describe the stories as set in a real world, without being able to test them as events.

Scholars also study when Genesis was written down. Most date the written book, in the form we have, to sometime between c. 1000 and c. 500 BCE, with older stories and poems inside it. The oldest surviving copies of Genesis are fragments among the Dead Sea Scrolls, from c. 200 BCE onward. The full Hebrew text used today is preserved in the Masoretic manuscripts of c. 900 to 1000 CE. Jewish tradition holds that the Torah was given to Moses; historians describe the text’s growth. This course reports both.

So a careful answer to "Did it happen?" sounds like this. The world of Genesis is real and well known. Its customs and laws fit the second and first millennia BCE. Its people cannot be found or ruled out by digging. What a reader makes of that depends on the reader, and Jewish tradition has never made the value of the Torah depend on a spade.

One Song, Two Shores

A song of freedom sung at the edge of a sea, and the same story sung in a field in 1850 by people who had never seen that sea.

The book of Exodus says the people stood on the far shore with the water behind them. Hours before, they had been trapped: Pharaoh's chariots at their backs, the sea in front. In Torah scrolls this song is written differently from the rest of the text, in a pattern of short blocks with spaces, like bricks in a wall. Now move nearly three thousand years forward, to a plantation in the American South around 1850. People who have been enslaved gather at night. They cannot read the Hebrew, and most cannot read at all. But they know the story, because preachers have told it. They sing: when Israel was in Egypt's land, let my people go. The song is called "Go Down, Moses," and its Moses is anyone who leads people out.

Harriet Tubman, who guided enslaved people north to freedom, was called Moses by those she helped. When the song was first printed in 1861, it was because a chaplain heard Black refugees from slavery singing it at a Union fort in Virginia. The story of Exodus had become their story, told in their words.

This chapter follows Exodus, called *Shemot* in Hebrew, from slavery to the sea, from the sea to the mountain, and from the mountain to the building of a tent for God to dwell among the people. It also follows the story out of the book: into the Passover Seder, where Jewish families retell it every spring, and into American history, where it became a song of freedom.

Main ideaExodus opens with the descendants of Jacob enslaved in Egypt and with five women whose choices save the baby who will become Moses.

The second book of the Torah begins with a list of names, and its Hebrew title, , means "names." The English title, Exodus, is Greek for "going out." The book picks up where Genesis left off. Jacob’s family has grown into a people in Egypt. A new Pharaoh, the text says, did not know Joseph. A woman from the tribe of Levi hides her baby three months. Then she puts him in a basket of reeds sealed with pitch. She sets it among the rushes at the river’s edge. His sister watches from a distance.

Pharaoh’s daughter comes to bathe, sees the basket, and takes pity on the crying child. The sister steps forward and offers to find a Hebrew nurse, and brings the baby’s own mother. Main ideaExodus 7 through 12 tells of ten plagues and a night of departure, and Jewish families still mark that night each year with the Passover Seder.

Sinai and the Ten Sayings

Main ideaAt Sinai, Exodus says the people hear ten sayings, and Jewish, Catholic and Protestant traditions count the same words in three different ways.

In the third month after leaving Egypt, Exodus 19 says the people camp at the foot of Mount Sinai. God tells Moses that if the people keep the covenant they will be a kingdom of priests and a holy nation. The people answer that they will do all that the LORD has spoken. Three days later, the text says, there is thunder, lightning and a thick cloud. A loud horn sounds, and the mountain smokes. Then the words of Exodus 20 are spoken.

Jewish tradition calls these words , the Ten Sayings or Ten Words, rather than ten commandments. It counts them starting from "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt." That first saying is a statement, not an order. The second in the Jewish count joins "no other gods" with the ban on images. Catholic and Lutheran tradition treat the opening statement as an introduction. They join the first two and split the last saying about coveting into two. Most Protestant traditions keep "no other gods" and "no images" as separate commands one and two.

So the same verses give three lists. This is a good example of how a text and its readers work together. The Hebrew text has no numbers in it; each tradition supplies its own. The Sayings appear twice in the Torah, in Exodus 20 and again in Deuteronomy 5, with small differences. The Exodus version says to remember the Sabbath because God rested at creation; the Deuteronomy version says to keep it because you were slaves in Egypt.

Jewish tradition links the giving of the Torah to the holiday of , seven weeks after Passover. Exodus 19 and 20 are read in the synagogue, and many people stay up all night studying. In many synagogues the congregation stands when the Ten Sayings are read. The Talmud also records a caution. The rabbis removed the Sayings from the daily prayers. They did not want anyone to think these ten mattered and the rest of the Torah did not.

18.6

The Covenant Code

Main ideaExodus 21 through 23 is a collection of laws for daily life, and rabbinic tradition reads even its harshest-sounding lines in a specific way.

After the Ten Sayings, the Torah does not go straight back to story. Exodus 21 to 23 is a block of laws that scholars call the , because Exodus 24 calls it the book of the covenant. The laws are mostly case laws: if this happens, then do that. If an ox gores a person, if a fire spreads to a neighbor’s field, if someone borrows an animal and it dies. Many of the cases are like ones in the Code of Hammurabi, which is centuries older, and scholars compare them line by line.

The Covenant Code includes laws about slavery, which the text takes for granted as a practice of its world. A Hebrew slave must be freed in the seventh year. Killing a slave is punished. A slave injured by a master goes free. These laws limit a practice rather than end it, and readers from the rabbis to modern historians have noted both the limits and what the text does not do. Describing that honestly is part of reading the book.

One line in Exodus 21 has become famous: eye for eye, tooth for tooth. Rabbinic tradition, in the Talmud’s tractate Bava Kamma, reads this as payment for injury, not as injury in return, and Jewish law has followed that reading for two thousand years. Some readers point out that even the plain sense sets a limit: one eye, not a life. The words are the same; the readings differ, and the Talmud’s is the one Jewish practice adopted.

The code also returns again and again to the weak. Do not wrong a stranger, it says, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt. Do not afflict the widow or the orphan. If you take a poor man’s cloak as a pledge, return it by sunset, because it is his only covering. Jewish tradition counts the command to love or not wrong the stranger as the most repeated command in the Torah. The reason given each time is memory: you know what it felt like.

The Golden Calf and the Thirteen Attributes

Main ideaWhile Moses is on the mountain, the people make a golden calf, and the story that follows gives Jewish prayer its most repeated words about mercy.

Moses goes up the mountain for forty days to receive the tablets. Exodus 32 says the people grow afraid that he is not coming back. On the mountain God tells Moses what is happening and offers to destroy the people and start over with him. Moses argues back: what will the Egyptians say, and what about the promise to Abraham?

Moses comes down, sees the dancing, and smashes the tablets at the foot of the mountain. He burns the calf, grinds it to powder, scatters it on water and makes the people drink it. The Levites carry out a punishment, and a plague follows. Then Moses goes back up to plead for the people, and asks to see God’s glory. The text says no one can see God’s face and live, but God will pass by and proclaim the name.

Exodus 34:6–7 gives that proclamation. Jewish tradition counts thirteen of mercy in it. Among them: merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in kindness and truth, keeping kindness for thousands, forgiving sin. The rabbis of the Talmud, in tractate Rosh Hashanah, say that God taught Moses these words as a prayer for forgiveness. They are recited on fast days, on Yom Kippur, and when the ark is opened on festivals.

The tradition also pairs the calf with the calendar. According to the rabbis, Moses came down with the second set of tablets on the tenth of Tishrei. That day is Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement. So the day of forgiveness in the Jewish year is tied to the story of the worst failure at the mountain. Commentators differ on how guilty the people were and how guilty Aaron was; Rashi, for instance, reads Aaron as stalling for time. The text itself lets Aaron’s weak excuse stand without comment.

18.8

Why Half the Book Is a Tent

Main ideaThirteen chapters of Exodus describe a portable sanctuary in detail, and readers have asked what that emphasis is meant to show.

A student reading Exodus straight through often gets a surprise around chapter 25. The action stops. For seven chapters, God gives Moses instructions for a tent sanctuary, the , meaning "dwelling place." The instructions cover everything in it. There is an ark of acacia wood covered in gold, a table, a lampstand with seven branches, and an altar. There are curtains, poles, and the clothing of the priests. Then, after the calf story, six more chapters describe the people building it, almost word for word the same.

Why so much detail? Readers give several answers. The text’s own answer is in Exodus 25:8: make me a sanctuary that I may dwell among them. The point of leaving Egypt was not just freedom but a people with God’s presence in their midst. Some commentators note that the Mishkan story mirrors the creation story. Both are done in stages. Both end with blessing and rest. The Torah uses similar words for finishing each. A people that could not make a calf could make a home.

The workers are named. Bezalel of the tribe of Judah and Oholiab of Dan are called wise-hearted. The text says Bezalel was filled with the spirit of God in skill and craft. Every person is asked to bring gifts, and Exodus 36 says they brought so much that Moses had to tell them to stop. Many readers see a deliberate contrast with the gold earrings collected for the calf.

The Mishkan is the ancestor of the later Temple in Jerusalem and, in a looser way, of the synagogue. The ark that held the tablets gives its name to the , the cabinet where Torah scrolls are kept today. The seven-branched is still a symbol of the Jewish people. When the book ends, the cloud of God’s presence fills the tent, and the people know it is time to move when the cloud lifts. Exodus closes not with a battle but with a building.

18.9

The Haggadah: A Book That Retells a Book

Main ideaPassover turns the Exodus story into something Jewish families do each year, and the Haggadah has grown over centuries as the script for that night.

The Torah commands that the story of leaving Egypt be told to the next generation, and Jewish tradition built a whole evening around that command. The Seder follows the Haggadah. Its oldest parts come from the Mishnah, edited c. 200 CE. Its full form took shape in the early Middle Ages. The Mishnah, in tractate Pesachim, already lists the four cups, the questions, and the rule that one begins with shame and ends with praise.

The center of the Haggadah is not a reading of Exodus. It is a rabbinic commentary on four verses from Deuteronomy 26 that sum up the story, with verses from Exodus brought in as proof. The Haggadah quotes Deuteronomy’s line that "a wandering Aramean was my father." Then it walks through the going down to Egypt, the oppression, the cry, and the rescue. Moses is barely mentioned at all. Many commentators say this is on purpose, so that the credit goes to God and not to a human leader.

The line from the Mishnah that shapes the whole night is this: in every generation a person must see himself as if he personally went out of Egypt. That is why the Seder is done with the body. People lean to one side like free people at a banquet. They taste salt water for tears. They eat bitter herbs, and a sweet paste that stands for mortar. They open the door. Children ask questions because the Torah says the telling begins with a child’s question.

The Haggadah is one of the most printed Jewish books. There are thousands of editions with art, commentary and translation. They run from illuminated medieval manuscripts to modern versions that add readings about freedom today. Because the Seder is held at home, not in a synagogue, families often make their own. In the Chicago area, as across the United States, Jewish communities hold public Seders as well. Many invite neighbors of other faiths as guests.

18.10

Go Down, Moses

Main ideaEnslaved African Americans took the Exodus story as their own, and its words became spirituals, sermons and a language of freedom in American history.

Enslaved Africans in the American South heard the Bible mostly by ear, in sermons and songs. Of all its stories, the one they made most their own was Exodus. Slaveholders often preferred verses telling servants to obey. The enslaved sang about a God who heard the cry of slaves and brought them out. In their songs, Egypt was the South and Pharaoh was the master. The Jordan was the Ohio River or death. Canaan was the North or heaven.

The best known of these is "Go Down, Moses." Its verses retell Exodus: Israel in Egypt’s land, oppressed so hard they could not stand. The refrain is let my people go. That is Moses’s demand to Pharaoh in Exodus 5 and after. The song was first printed in 1861. A chaplain had heard it sung by Black people who had escaped slavery and reached Fort Monroe, a Union army post in Virginia. The Fisk Jubilee Singers carried it across the United States and Europe in the 1870s.

Harriet Tubman escaped slavery in Maryland in 1849 and returned many times to lead others north. The people she guided, and the press, called her Moses. Frederick Douglass and later Martin Luther King Jr. drew on Exodus in their speeches. In his final speech in 1968, King said he had been to the mountaintop and seen the promised land. That picture comes from the end of Deuteronomy, where Moses sees the land he will not enter.

Historians describe this as the Exodus story crossing from one people to another. Jewish readers have noticed and honored it; some Haggadot include "Go Down, Moses" or readings from the civil rights movement. The story that Exodus tells about one nation became, in America, a way for another people to name their own bondage and their own hope. The words belong to the Torah; the song belongs to those who sang it.

Think it through
One Refrain, Two Books

In Exodus, Moses stands before Pharaoh and repeats a demand: let my people go, that they may serve me. In "Go Down, Moses," the refrain is the same four words, sung by people in bondage about themselves. Between the verses, the song tells Moses to go down to Egypt's land and tell old Pharaoh to let my people go. The singers were not in Egypt, and their Pharaoh was not a king of the Nile.

18.11

Exodus and the Historians

Main ideaHistorians can describe the Egypt of the story and note what is missing from the record, but they cannot settle whether the exodus happened as the Torah tells it.

What does the evidence outside the Bible say? Egypt kept many records, and none of them mentions Israelites in slavery, ten plagues, or an army lost in the sea. Historians point out that ancient kings did not record their defeats, and that a group of workers leaving would not have made the royal lists. Still, silence is silence, and it must be reported. The Torah gives no Pharaoh’s name, which makes the story hard to pin to a date.

There are hints. Exodus 1:11 says the Israelites built the store city of Raamses. A city by that name was built by Ramesses II, who ruled Egypt c. 1279 to 1213 BCE. The Merneptah Stele, from c. 1208 BCE, names "Israel" as a people in Canaan by then. Many scholars who think some real memory lies behind the story place it in the thirteenth century BCE. Others think the story took shape much later, in the time of the kings or the exile, and grew from smaller memories of people escaping Egypt.

Jewish tradition itself does not depend on the digging. The Torah is read as Torah; the story is retold at every Seder; and the rabbis teach that the point is what the story asks of a people. Some modern Jewish thinkers say that even if the details cannot be recovered, the story is true in the sense that it has shaped a people’s life and law for millennia. Others hold firmly that the events happened as written. This course reports the range of views and takes no side.

For a student, the best answer is careful. The world of the story, with brick-making, store cities, a great river, and a powerful king, is Egypt as it was. The exodus itself has no outside witness, for or against. The book’s own claim is a religious one: that a God who freed slaves asks that people to remember what slavery felt like. That claim can be described, quoted and studied, whatever a reader decides about the sea.
