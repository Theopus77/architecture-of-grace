THE ROOMS (architectureofgrace.com/the-studio) — where the reverbs come from

Each file is the sound of a real space answering one sharp sound: a balloon
popped, a click, or a slow sine sweep worked back into a click. A reverb
plays your music through it, so it sounds as if it were played there.
These are called impulse responses.

Every one comes from Freesound (freesound.org) and was given to everyone
under Creative Commons 0 (CC0 1.0 Universal, public domain):
  https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/
We read the licence on each sound's own page. Nothing here needs
permission. We name the people who made them because we are grateful.

What we changed (music-handoff/tools/rooms/rooms.py; the page's "high
quality preview" of each sound, MP3 128 kbps, is what was used):
  - Each file is 44,100 samples a second, 16-bit WAV. Stereo recordings
    stay stereo (left and right as recorded); the spring is mono.
  - The DC offset is taken out.
  - The quiet before the first sound is trimmed, and that first, direct
    sound (3 ms) is taken out, so the reverb adds the room, not a second
    copy of the music. The room's first echoes keep their time.
  - The tank and the spring ring longer than a plate and a spring reverb
    do (the spring's recording ends in hiss), so they were made to die
    away sooner, like a plate (about 2 s) and a spring (about 1.3 s).
  - Each tail ends where it reaches the recording's own noise, with a
    gentle fade, and no longer than 1 to 4 seconds.
  - Every file was brought to the same loudness (the same energy), so
    changing rooms does not change the level.
  - On the pages (aog-realrooms.js), each room takes the place of the
    made-up reverb that was there before, evened to it: the same short
    pause before the first echoes, and as loud for music (pink noise
    through it, measured as a loudness meter hears it). So no song gets
    wetter.
  - The decay times above were measured on the finished files (the
    Schroeder backward integral, from -5 dB to -35 dB).
