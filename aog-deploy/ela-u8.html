Writing

Writing

Daniela wants a longer recess. Theo wants you to feel the snow fort fall in. Priya wants you to understand the Great Chicago Fire. Three writers, three jobs: an opinion, a story, an explanation. All three start with the same problem. The idea is clear in the writer's head and messy on the page.

This unit is about closing that gap. You will learn to build an opinion paragraph from a claim, reasons, examples and a closing. You will learn to zoom in on one small moment and make a reader see it, and to organize true facts so a reader can learn from them. Then you will learn what real writers know: the first draft is only the start, and revising, editing and reading aloud are where the writing gets good.

By the end, you will be able to plan a piece, draft it, make it stronger, make it correct, and hand it to a real reader, whether that reader is a friend, a class or the principal.

The big question

How does a writer turn an idea in their head into words a reader can trust?

The steps
Choose

Pick the job: an opinion to argue, a moment to tell, or a topic to explain.

Plan

Map it in short phrases: claim and reasons, or the moment's beats, or groups of facts.

Draft

Write the whole thing fast and rough. Its only job is to exist.

Read

Read the entire draft once without a pencil and ask what it needs next.

Revise

Add what is missing, cut what is extra, move what is misplaced, replace what is vague.

Feedback

Give it to a peer. Hear one thing that worked and one honest question.

Strengthen

Swap weak verbs and vague nouns for strong, exact ones; vary sentence length.

Edit

One rule per pass: capitals, commas in a series, quotation marks, apostrophes, spelling.

Aloud

Read it out loud with a finger on the words. Mark every stumble, then fix it.

Final

Type a clean copy with a title, your name and the date. Keep the old drafts.

Share

Give it to a real reader. A message is not finished until someone receives it.

The Paragraph: a Claim and Reasons

Opinion Writing
Big questionHow do you make a reader believe what you think?
The story

Twenty Minutes Is Not Enough

A fourth grader thinks recess should be longer. Thinking it is easy. Proving it takes work.

Daniela's school in Chicago gives fourth graders twenty minutes of recess. By the time the class walks outside, ten minutes are gone. She wants recess to be longer. On Monday her teacher, Mr. Beck, says the class will write opinions about a school question. Daniela knows her question right away.

Her first draft is one sentence: "Recess should be longer because it is fun." Mr. Beck reads it and asks, "Would the principal change the schedule because it is fun?" Daniela thinks about it. No. Fun is true, but it is not a reason a principal would care about.

So she looks for better reasons. She asks the gym teacher, who says kids need at least sixty minutes of movement a day. She counts how many minutes the class really gets outside: ten. She notices that after recess, the room is quieter and math goes faster. Now she has three reasons, and each one has a fact behind it.

Her second draft has a topic sentence, three reasons, an example for each and a closing line. She even adds a sentence about the other side: "Some people say longer recess means less learning time. But a calm class learns more in forty minutes than a wiggly class learns in fifty."

Mr. Beck reads it twice. "Now this is an argument," he says. "Who should read it?" Daniela smiles. "The principal." She writes it again as a letter, and on Friday she puts it in the office mailbox herself.

Talk about itWhy was "because it is fun" a weak reason? What made Daniela's new reasons stronger?
Section 1

Building the Claim

17.1

The Topic Sentence Takes a Side

Main ideaA topic sentence tells your reader exactly what you believe in one clear sentence.

Read these two sentences. "Recess is a part of the school day." "Recess should be twenty minutes longer." The first one is just true. Nobody would argue with it. The second one takes a side. Someone could disagree. That second sentence is a . A claim is an opinion you are ready to defend.

In an opinion paragraph, the claim goes first. It is the , the sentence that tells the reader what the whole paragraph is about. A good topic sentence is short, clear and bold. It does not say "I kind of think maybe." It says "Our school should start a garden."

Try this test on your own topic sentence. Could someone argue against it? If yes, it is a claim. If no, it is only a fact. "Dogs are animals" is a fact. "Dogs make better pets than cats" is a claim. Facts belong later, as proof. The claim comes first.

17.2

Reasons Hold the Claim Up

Main ideaA reason answers the question why, and a strong reason is one your reader would care about.

A claim by itself is like a table top with no legs. It falls over. "Recess should be longer." Why? The answers to why are the , and they are the legs. Daniela’s first reason was "because it is fun." That is a real reason, but it is weak. A principal does not plan the day around fun.

A strong reason is one your reader would care about. Daniela’s reader was the principal. Principals care about learning, health and safety. So her strong reasons were: kids need sixty minutes of movement a day, the class only gets ten minutes outside, and a class that has moved learns faster. Each reason is something the principal already wants.

Aim for two or three reasons. One reason is thin. Six reasons make a list, not a paragraph. Pick the strongest and put them in order. Many writers save the best reason for last, so it is the one the reader remembers.

17.3

Examples Make Reasons Real

Main ideaAn example is a specific fact or moment that shows a reason is true.

Here is a reason: "Our class learns better after recess." It sounds good, but a reader might shrug. Now add this: "Last Tuesday, after twenty minutes outside, we finished the math page in ten minutes. On Wednesday, with no recess, it took twenty-five." Now the reader can see it. That is an .

An example is a specific fact, number, story or moment. Specific means exact: not "a lot of kids" but "eighteen of twenty-four kids." Not "one time" but "last Tuesday." Examples come from things you saw, counted, read or asked someone about. Daniela asked the gym teacher and counted minutes with a clock.

Each reason should get at least one example. Write the reason, then write the example right after it. The reason is the rule; the example is the proof. Together they make a pair the reader can trust.

Section 2

Holding It Together

17.4

Linking Words Connect Ideas

Main ideaLinking words like because, for example, also and therefore show the reader how your sentences fit together.

Read this. "Recess should be longer. Kids need to move. The class only gets ten minutes outside." The ideas are there, but the sentences sit like bricks with no mortar. Now read it again. "Recess should be longer because kids need to move. For example, our class only gets ten minutes outside." The words because and for example are the mortar.

These are . Each one tells the reader what kind of idea is coming. Because and since introduce a reason. For example and for instance introduce an example. Also, another and in addition add one more reason. Therefore, so and that is why introduce a result or a closing.

Use linking words on purpose, not everywhere. One per new idea is enough. If every sentence starts with also, the paragraph sounds like a list. Mix them, and your reader can follow the path from claim to reasons to closing without getting lost.

From the source
One Paragraph, Linked
Our class should keep a pet fish because caring for an animal teaches responsibility. For example, someone must feed it every single day, even on Fridays. Also, watching a fish calms people down. Therefore, a fish would help us learn and help us focus.

17.5

The Closing Sentence

Main ideaA closing sentence restates the claim in new words and leaves the reader with something to remember.

A paragraph without a closing sentence feels like a song that stops in the middle. The reader waits for more. The is the last sentence, and it does two jobs. First, it says the claim again in new words. Second, it leaves the reader with a feeling or a thought to carry away.

Daniela’s claim was "Recess should be twenty minutes longer." Her closing sentence was "Give us those twenty minutes, and you will get a calmer, healthier, smarter fourth grade." Same idea, new words, and a picture of what the reader gains. Notice that she did not just copy her topic sentence.

Two ways to close. You can the claim with a linking word: "That is why our school needs a garden." Or you can call the reader to act: "Plant the garden this spring." Do not add a brand new reason at the end. New reasons belong in the middle; the end is for landing.

17.6

Plan Before You Draft

Main ideaA short plan, made before you write, keeps your paragraph in order and saves you from starting over.

Picture a builder who starts nailing boards before drawing a plan. The house comes out crooked. Writers who start drafting with no plan get crooked paragraphs: a reason with no example, or a closing that forgets the claim. So before you draft, make a . It takes five minutes and saves twenty.

A plan for an opinion paragraph can be a simple box chart. Top box: the claim. Under it, two or three boxes, one per reason. Under each reason, a line for its example. Bottom box: the closing. Daniela filled hers in with short phrases, not full sentences: "60 min movement, gym teacher said."

When the plan is full, look at it before you write. Is any reason weak? Cut it. Does a reason have no example? Find one or cut it. Is the best reason last? Move it. Fixing the plan is easy. Fixing a finished paragraph is hard. Then : turn each box into one or two full sentences.

Section 3

Real Opinions for Real Readers

17.7

Your Opinion About a Book

Main ideaWhen you write an opinion about a book, your reasons come from what actually happens in the book.

"This book was good" is not a claim anyone can use. Good how? For whom? A real book opinion sounds like this: "Third graders who like animals should read this book." Or: "The ending of this book is not fair to the main character." Now there is something to argue.

For a book opinion, your examples come from inside the book. A reason might be "The main character is brave." The example is a moment: "When the barn catches fire, she runs back in for the calf." Point to a page or a scene. Readers trust you more when you show that you really read it.

You can also give the of your opinion a job. Say who should read the book and why. Say whether you would read it again. Say what you wished the author had done. Those are all claims. Back each one with a scene, and you have a book a friend could actually use.

From the source
A Book Opinion, Short
Kids who like scary stories should read The Lighthouse Key. The main reason is that the author makes you wait. For example, in chapter four the door creaks for a whole page before anyone opens it. Also, the ending answers every question but one, which makes you want a second book. Read it with the lights on.

17.8

Considering the Other Side

Main ideaNaming what the other side thinks, and answering it, makes your opinion stronger, not weaker.

Daniela wrote: "Some people say longer recess means less learning time. But a calm class learns more in forty minutes than a wiggly class learns in fifty." She named the , the opinion that disagrees with hers. Then she answered it. This is one of the strongest moves an opinion writer can make.

Why does it help? Because your reader is already thinking of the other side. The principal was going to think "but learning time" the moment she read the claim. If Daniela says it first, the principal sees that Daniela thought hard. If Daniela hides it, the principal thinks Daniela never noticed.

The shape is simple. "Some people think ____. But ____." Give the other side a fair sentence, not a silly one. Then answer it with a reason or an example. Put it near the end, just before your closing. One other-side sentence and one answer is enough for a paragraph.

2. Why does naming the other side make your opinion stronger?

17.9

A Letter to the Principal

Main ideaA letter turns your opinion paragraph into a message for a real person, with a greeting, a polite tone and a request.

An opinion paragraph in a notebook changes nothing. The same paragraph in a letter to the principal might change the schedule. A letter has a real , the person who will read it. When you know your audience, you choose your reasons, your words and your tone for that one person.

A friendly letter has parts. The date at the top. A greeting: "Dear Dr. Alvarez,". The body, which is your opinion paragraph. A closing word like "Sincerely," and your name. Daniela added one more thing at the end of the body: a clear request. "Please consider adding twenty minutes to fourth-grade recess this spring."

Tone matters. Tone is how your words sound to the reader. "Recess is way too short and it is not fair" sounds like complaining. "Our class would learn more with twenty more minutes outside" sounds like a person with a plan. Polite and specific beats loud every time. Then proofread it, sign it and send it.

Narrative and Informative
Big questionHow do you make a true thing come alive on the page, whether it is a moment or a fact?
The story

The Snow Day, Twice

Theo writes about a snow day. The first draft is true. The second draft is true and alive.

In January a storm dropped eleven inches of snow on Chicago, and school closed. On Monday, Ms. Okafor asked her fourth graders to write a true story about the snow day. Theo wrote fast. "I woke up. It was a snow day. I played outside with my brother. It was fun. Then I came in and had hot chocolate. The end."

Ms. Okafor read it and said, "Every word of this is true. And I cannot see any of it." Theo looked at the page. She was right. It could have been anyone's snow day. "Pick one part," she said. "The smallest part that mattered. Then slow down and show me."

Theo picked the moment the snow fort fell in. He closed his eyes and remembered. The wall had been taller than his brother, Eli. Eli had pushed one more block onto the top. There had been a soft whump, and then Eli's boots sticking out of a white pile, and Eli's muffled voice: "I'm okay! I'm okay!"

His second draft started in the middle of the action. "Eli lifted the last block over his head. 'Don't,' I said. He did." He added the whump. He added the cold that got inside his gloves. He added what Eli's voice sounded like from under the snow. He ended with the two of them lying in the wreck of the fort, laughing so hard they could not stand up.

When Theo read it aloud, two kids laughed at the whump. Ms. Okafor taped both drafts to the wall side by side. "Same day," she said. "Same boy. One of these is a list. The other one is a story."

Talk about itTheo's first draft was true. Why did his teacher say she could not see it? What did he change?
Section 1

Writing a Small Moment

18.1

Pick One Small Moment

Main ideaA strong true story zooms in on one small moment instead of listing a whole day.

Theo’s first draft covered a whole day in six sentences. Wake up, play, come in, drink cocoa. That is a list, and a list is not a story. A story needs a camera that stops and holds still. Ms. Okafor told him to find the smallest part that mattered. He found ten seconds: the fort falling in.

Writers call this a . It is one event that took a few minutes, or even a few seconds. The bus pulling away without you. The moment your dog first saw snow. The second the cake fell over. Small moments make better stories because you can slow down and show every detail.

To find yours, start with a big memory. Then ask: what is the one part I would tell a friend first? That is the moment. Everything else is background. You can mention the rest in a sentence, but the moment gets the whole page.

18.2

A Beginning That Hooks

Main ideaStart inside the action, with a line of talk, a sound or a picture, so the reader has to keep going.

Compare two first lines. "I woke up. It was a snow day." And: "Eli lifted the last block over his head. ’Don’t,’ I said. He did." The first one could begin a thousand stories. The second one makes you ask what happens next. That pull is called a .

Three kinds of hooks work well for a small moment. Start with action: someone is already doing something. Start with talk: a line of , the words a person says. Start with a sound or a picture: "Whump. Then silence, then boots." Any of these drops the reader into the moment instead of walking them to it.

What does not work? Starting at the start of the day. Starting with "I am going to tell you about." Starting with the weather unless the weather is the story. Try this: write your moment, then cut the first two sentences. Often the real hook was hiding in sentence three.

18.3

Talk and the Five Senses

Main ideaDialogue and sensory details let the reader hear, see and feel the moment instead of being told about it.

"It was fun" tells. "Eli’s boots stuck out of the pile and his muffled voice said, ’I’m okay! I’m okay!’" shows. The difference is . Theo’s second draft gave the reader things to hear, see and feel. Those are called : details that use the five senses of sight, sound, touch, smell and taste.

You do not need all five in every paragraph. Pick the two or three that were strongest in the moment. For the fort, it was sound (whump, muffled voice) and touch (cold inside the gloves). Ask yourself: what did I hear? What did my hands feel? What could I smell? Write the honest answer.

Dialogue is another way to show. When Eli says "I’m okay!" twice, we learn he is scared and also fine, and we learn it faster than if Theo explained it. Keep dialogue short and real. People do not give speeches in a snow fort. Two or three lines of talk in a small moment is usually plenty.

18.4

An Ending That Satisfies

Main ideaA good ending lands on the feeling of the moment; it does not just stop or say the end.

Theo’s first draft ended with "The end." His second ended with the brothers lying in the wreck of the fort, laughing too hard to stand up. The first ending stops. The second one lands. It leaves the reader with the feeling the whole story was building toward.

Three ways to land a small moment. End with a picture: the last thing you saw. End with a line of talk: the last thing someone said. End with a thought: what you knew or felt right then, in one honest sentence. "We never rebuilt the fort. We did not need to." Any of these gives the story a place to rest.

What to avoid. Do not race to bedtime: "Then we went in and ate and went to sleep." Do not explain the lesson: "And that is why you should listen to your brother." Trust the reader. If you showed the moment well, they already feel it. Stop one sentence sooner than you think you should.

Main ideaA how-to lists what you need, then gives numbered steps in the exact order a reader must do them.

Next comes a list of what you need: one sheet of paper, a flat table. Then the , numbered. Each step is one action, starting with a verb: "1. Fold the paper in half the long way. 2. Open it flat again." Words like first, next, then, after that and finally help the reader keep their place.

Main ideaWhen you write about a text, make a point and then prove it with words or details from the text itself.

The pattern has three parts. Make your point. Give the evidence, either a short quote in quotation marks or a detail in your own words. Choose evidence that really fits. Not every detail proves every point. If your point is that the hare is careless, the fact that he is fast does not help. The fact that he naps in the middle of a race does. Pick the detail that makes your reader nod.

A Draft Is Only a Start

19.1

Drafting Is Not Finishing

Main ideaA first draft is for getting ideas down; the real work of writing happens in the drafts that follow.

Mr. Nguyen’s first draft took ninety seconds and looked like a mess. That is normal. A is a version of a piece of writing, and the first one is only for getting the idea onto the page before it escapes. Nobody expects it to be good. Its only job is to exist.

Many students stop after one draft and hand it in. That is like a builder who nails up the boards and never comes back to paint. The has more steps: plan, draft, , edit, share. Revising and editing are where a rough paragraph becomes one a reader can trust.

Here is the mindset. When you finish a draft, do not ask "Am I done?" Ask "What does this need next?" Sometimes the answer is bigger ideas. Sometimes it is stronger words. Sometimes it is just a missing period. Every draft is a step, and each step has its own job.

19.2

Revising: Add, Cut, Move, Replace

Main ideaRevising changes what a piece says, using four moves: add, cut, move and replace.

Look at what happened between draft one and draft two. "It was bad" disappeared. The open gate and the crying sister appeared. The finding at the park moved to the end where it belonged. Those changes are revising: changing the ideas, details and order, not the spelling. Revising happens with a big view of the whole piece.

There are four revising moves. : put in a detail, a reason or an example the reader needs. : take out what is boring, repeated or off topic. Move: put a sentence where it makes more sense, like the best reason last. : swap a vague idea for a specific one, like "it was bad" for "my sister cried."

To revise, read the whole draft once without a pencil. Then ask four questions. What is missing? What is not needed? What is in the wrong place? What is vague? Each question matches one move. Mark the spot, make the change, and read the whole thing again. One good revising pass beats ten drafts of fiddling.

19.6

Main ideaEditing fixes the rules: capitals at sentence starts and on names, and commas between items in a list.

Main ideaQuotation marks go around the exact words a person says, with a comma or end mark inside them.

Mr. Nguyen’s sister yelled something in the yard. On the page it looks like this: "Biscuit!" she yelled. "Where are you?" The marks around her words are . They tell the reader: these are the exact words that came out of her mouth. Words outside the marks, like she yelled, are the writer’s.

Three rules make dialogue clean. First, the quotation marks wrap only the spoken words. Second, the punctuation at the end of the spoken words goes inside the closing mark: "I’m okay," he said. Notice the comma, not a period, when the sentence keeps going with he said. Third, a new speaker gets a new line. When the sister stops and Mom starts, start a new paragraph.

The is the part like she yelled or he said. It can come before or after the words. "We’ll find him," Mom said. Or: Mom said, "We’ll find him." Either way there is a comma between the tag and the words. If a question mark or exclamation point ends the speech, it replaces the comma: "Where?" she asked.

Apostrophes

Main ideaAn apostrophe shows a missing letter in a contraction or shows that something belongs to someone.

Look at the small mark in don’t and in Biscuit’s. It is an , and it does two different jobs. In don’t, it stands in for a missing letter: do not squeezed into one word, with the o gone. Words like that are : can’t, I’m, she’s, we’ll, isn’t. The apostrophe goes exactly where the missing letters were.

In Biscuit’s collar, the apostrophe shows that the collar belongs to Biscuit. That is a . Add an apostrophe and s to a name or a singular noun. My sister’s voice. The dog’s bowl. A plural noun that already ends in s is different. Put the apostrophe after the s. The dogs’ bowls means bowls for more than one dog.

"The dog wagged it is tail" is wrong, so no apostrophe. And plain plurals never get one: two dogs, three parks, no apostrophe at all.

Spelling Strategies

Main ideaGood spellers use strategies: word parts, patterns, a personal word list and a dictionary check.

"Evrywhere" sat in Mr. Nguyen’s draft for three versions. He was not a bad speller; he was a fast drafter, and that is fine. Spelling gets fixed in editing. But you need strategies, ways to catch and fix words, because you will not always have a computer to underline them in red.

Strategy one: break the word into parts. Every-where. Un-believe-able. If you can spell the parts, you can spell the word. Strategy two: know the patterns. When a word ends in a silent e, drop the e before adding ing: hope, hoping. For words that end in y after a consonant, change y to i before adding es or ed: cry, cried. Patterns cover thousands of words.

Strategy three: keep a , the five or ten words you get wrong again and again. Check your draft against it every time. Strategy four: when you are not sure, circle the word and keep writing. Look it up in a dictionary or ask at the end. Never let one hard word stop the whole draft.

Section 4

Finishing and Sharing

19.10

Read It Aloud

Main ideaReading your draft out loud lets your ears catch mistakes your eyes skip over.

Your eyes cheat. They know what you meant to write, so they read the missing word as if it were there. Your ears do not cheat. Read this out loud: "We searched the yard the alley the park." You will run out of breath and hear the missing commas. Read a run-on aloud and you will hear where the period was supposed to be.

Reading catches four things. Missing or doubled words. Sentences that go on too long. Sentences that all sound the same. And places where the meaning is unclear, because you will stumble there. When you stumble, mark it. Do not fix it yet. Finish the read, then go back to every mark.

Do it slowly, with your finger on the words. That way you read what is on the page, not what is in your head. Better yet, have a partner read your draft aloud to you. They do not know what you meant, so they read exactly what is there. Where they stumble is where your reader will stumble.

Think it through
Your Ears Will Catch It

Read this draft sentence out loud, slowly: "We looked for for Biscuit for two hours and my sister was crying and then Mom said the park and we ran and there he was by the swings eating a hot dog someone dropped."

What did your ears catch that your eyes might have skipped? Where would you put a period?

Words to know
aloud

run-on
two or more sentences jammed together without the right punctuation
Check yourself

1. Why do your eyes miss mistakes in your own draft?

Why: Your brain fills in what you meant. Your ears hear what is really there.

2. What should you do when you stumble while reading aloud?

Why: Mark the stumbles, finish the read, then go back. Stopping each time breaks the flow.

3. Why is a partner a good reader for your draft?

Why: A partner does not know your intentions, so their stumbles show real problems.
19.11

Type It and Share It

Main ideaTyping a final copy and sharing it with a real reader is the last step of the process, and it is the reason for all the rest.

When the revising and editing are done, make a clean copy. Most classes type it. Typing has real benefits: the spelling checker underlines words to look at, you can move a sentence without erasing, and a reader can read it easily. But the checker is not your editor. It misses there for their and it’s for its. Your own editing passes still come first.

A needs a title, your name and the date. Use one plain font, and put a space between paragraphs or indent the first line. Print it or save it with a clear file name, like snow-day-story-final. Keep your earlier drafts too. They show how far the piece came, and sometimes a cut sentence turns out to be worth putting back.

Then it. Read it to the class. Post it on the wall. Put it in a class book, or send it to the person it was for. Writing is a message. A message that never reaches a reader is not finished. Mr. Nguyen’s four drafts stayed on the wall all year. Every student could see what a first draft looks like and what it can become.

From the source

A word is dead / When it is said, / Some say. / I say it just / Begins to live / That day.
Emily Dickinson, short poem published after her death in 1894

Dickinson says a word begins to live when it is said. How is sharing your writing like saying a word out loud?

Write it

Mr. Nguyen said, "Good writing is mostly rewriting." Do you agree? Write an opinion paragraph that makes a claim, gives two reasons with examples from this unit, and answers the other side.

  • Claim: take a clear side in one sentence. Do not say kind of or maybe.
  • Evidence: use what happened to Theo's snow-day drafts, Mr. Nguyen's four drafts on the wall, or Daniela's two versions.
  • Reasoning: explain how each example proves your reason. Point, evidence, explain.
  • The other side: some writers say a first draft is enough if it is true. Name that view fairly and answer it.
  • Close by restating your claim in new words, then read the whole paragraph aloud before you hand it in.
