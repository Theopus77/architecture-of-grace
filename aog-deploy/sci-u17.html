Biology: Genetics and Evolution

Life Science

A child has her father's chin and her grandmother's laugh. A field of corn stands in rows of plants that are nearly identical. A strain of bacteria in a hospital shrugs off a drug that killed its ancestors ten years ago. All three are the same story told at different speeds. Something inside cells carries instructions. It copies them, hands them on, and once in a while changes them. This unit is about that something, and about what it does over time.

The first chapter goes inside the cell. You will see the shape of DNA and how a cell copies it. You will see how a gene becomes a protein, and how a change of one base can change a life. You will follow Gregor Mendel's peas to learn why traits skip a generation. You will follow Queen Victoria's family to watch a gene ride down an X chromosome. The chapter ends with the tools that let scientists read a whole genome and edit a single base, and the questions those tools raise.

The second chapter steps back to the whole history of life. The evidence comes from rock layers, from bones, from embryos and from DNA itself. All of it points the same way. You will meet the fish with a neck. You will meet the finches whose beaks changed in one year of drought, and the moths that turned dark with factory smoke. By the end you should be able to explain, with evidence, how living things became so varied and why they are still changing.

The big question

How do living things pass on their traits, and how does that inheritance, over long stretches of time, produce the variety of life on Earth?

The story

The Picture That Showed the Shape of DNA

In 1952 a single X-ray photograph, taken in a basement lab in London, gave away the secret of the molecule of life.

Picture a thin strand of DNA pulled out of a cell, wet and stretched like a fine thread. Now imagine firing a beam of X-rays through it. The rays bounce off the atoms inside and land on a piece of film. They do not make a photo of the molecule. They make a pattern of spots and smears. If you know how to read the pattern, it tells you the shape of the thing the rays passed through.

In May 1952, at King's College in London, the chemist Rosalind Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling took such a picture. They labeled it Photo 51. It showed a bold X made of dark smudges. Franklin knew what an X like that meant. It is the signature of a helix, a shape like a spiral staircase. From the spacing of the smudges she could measure the size of the steps and the width of the whole spiral.

Franklin worked slowly and carefully. She wanted the measurements right before she said what the shape was. Meanwhile, at Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick were building models out of metal plates and wire, trying shapes until one fit the facts. In January 1953, Franklin's colleague Maurice Wilkins showed Watson Photo 51. Franklin did not know he had done it. Watson later wrote that his pulse raced when he saw it.

Within weeks Watson and Crick had a model that fit everything: two strands winding around each other, with paired bases inside like rungs on a twisted ladder. Their short paper appeared in the journal Nature on April 25, 1953. Franklin's own paper, with the measurements from Photo 51, appeared in the same issue. But hers was printed as if it merely supported their idea, not as the evidence that made it possible.

Franklin died of cancer in 1958, at the age of 37. In 1962 the Nobel Prize went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins. The prize is never given to someone who has died, so her name was not on it. Today scientists argue about how much credit she was owed. What no one argues about is the picture. The X on Photo 51 was real, the measurements were right, and they showed the shape that every living thing uses to store its instructions.

The Shape of the Molecule

38.1

A Twisted Ladder

Main ideaDNA is a double helix: two backbones with paired bases inside, and the pairing rule lets it store information.

Every cell that has a nucleus keeps its instructions in a molecule called . If you could unwind the DNA from one human cell and lay it out straight, it would be about 2 meters long. Yet it fits inside a nucleus far too small to see without a microscope. It fits because it is a very thin thread, about 2 nanometers wide, coiled and folded again and again.

The shape of the thread is a . Think of a ladder. Now twist the ladder into a spiral. The two rails of the ladder are long chains of sugar and phosphate. The rungs are made of pairs of smaller molecules called . There are only four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). Each rung is one base from one rail reaching across to one base on the other.

The bases do not pair at random. A always pairs with T, and G always pairs with C. This is the rule. It works because of shape and chemistry. A and T fit together with two weak hydrogen bonds. G and C fit with three. A pair of A with G would be too wide; a pair of C with T would be too narrow. The ladder only stays even when the rule is followed.

The order of the bases along one rail is the information. A stretch reading ATTGCA means something different from ATTGCC. Human DNA holds about 3 billion base pairs. Because of the pairing rule, if you know one rail you can write the other. That simple fact, as you will see, is how a cell copies its instructions.

38.2

Copying the Code

Main ideaBefore a cell divides, it unzips its DNA and builds a new partner strand for each half, following the pairing rule.

A cell in your skin divides. Each of the two new cells needs a complete set of DNA. That means the whole 3 billion base pairs must be copied first, with almost no mistakes. The double helix makes this possible. Because each strand is the mirror of the other, each strand can serve as a template, or pattern, for building a new one.

starts when an enzyme called helicase unzips the helix. It breaks the weak hydrogen bonds between the base pairs and pulls the two strands apart. Now each old strand has its bases exposed. A second enzyme, , moves along each old strand. It grabs free bases floating in the cell and snaps them into place, always following the rule: A across from T, G across from C.

When it is done, there are two double helices where there was one. Each contains one old strand and one new strand. Scientists call this copying, because half of each new molecule is conserved from the original. In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl proved it by growing bacteria in food with heavy nitrogen, then switching to light nitrogen and weighing the DNA after each round of division.

DNA polymerase also checks its work. If it places a wrong base, it usually backs up and fixes it. Even so, a few errors slip through, roughly one for every billion bases copied. Those rare errors are called , and later in this chapter you will see that they are the raw material of change.

Section 2

From Gene to Protein

38.3

Writing the Message

Main ideaA gene is copied into a strand of messenger RNA that can leave the nucleus and carry the instructions to the cell's protein-building machines.

DNA stays in the nucleus, but the work of the cell happens outside it. Proteins do that work: they build structures, speed up reactions and carry signals. So the cell needs a way to send the instructions for a protein out of the nucleus without sending the DNA itself. It does this with a messenger.

A is a stretch of DNA that holds the instructions for one protein. To read it, an enzyme called RNA polymerase unzips just that stretch and builds a single strand of alongside it. RNA is a close cousin of DNA. It has one strand, not two, and it uses a base called uracil (U) in place of thymine (T). So where DNA reads ATG, the RNA copy reads UAC. This copying step is called , the way a clerk transcribes a document.

In cells with a nucleus, the first RNA copy is edited before it leaves. Some stretches, called introns, are cut out. The remaining pieces are joined. The finished strand, called or mRNA, slips through a pore in the nuclear membrane into the cytoplasm. There it is picked up by a ribosome, the machine that will turn its message into a protein.

Not every gene is transcribed all the time. A cell in your eye and a cell in your liver have the same DNA, but each turns on a different set of genes. That control is what makes the two cells different. A gene that is never read is like a recipe in a closed book: present, but not in use.

38.4

Reading the Message

Main ideaA ribosome reads mRNA three bases at a time, and each three-base codon tells it which amino acid to add to a growing protein.

The mRNA has reached a ribosome. Now the message must be turned into a protein. A protein is a chain of smaller units called . There are 20 kinds. The order of amino acids in the chain decides how it folds, and the fold decides what it can do. The job of is to read the bases of the mRNA and string the right amino acids together in the right order.

The ribosome reads the mRNA in groups of three bases. Each group is a . With four bases there are 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 possible codons, more than enough to name 20 amino acids. Most amino acids have several codons. One codon, AUG, means ’start’ and also stands for the amino acid methionine. Three codons, UAA, UAG and UGA, mean ’stop’. This code is nearly the same in bacteria, oak trees and people, which is strong evidence that all life shares an ancestor.

Small molecules called transfer RNA, or tRNA, do the matching. Each tRNA has three bases that fit one codon on one end and carries the matching amino acid on the other. As the ribosome slides along the mRNA, the right tRNA docks at each codon and hands over its amino acid. The chain grows one unit at a time until a stop codon arrives. Then the finished protein is released to fold up and get to work.

The whole route, DNA to RNA to protein, is sometimes called the central dogma of biology. The word dogma is a bit strong, because scientists have found exceptions. Some viruses copy RNA back into DNA. But for the genes in your own cells, the path runs one way, and every protein in your body was built this way.

38.5

When the Code Changes

Main ideaA mutation changes the sequence of bases; its effect can be nothing, a small change, or a broken protein, depending on where and how it happens.

In the 1940s doctors studying sickle cell disease found that the red blood cells of patients bend into a curved shape when oxygen is low. The bent cells jam in small blood vessels and cause pain. In 1949 Linus Pauling’s team showed that the hemoglobin protein itself was different. Years later the cause was traced to a single base. In one gene, an A had been swapped for a T. That changed one codon, which changed one amino acid, which changed the shape of the whole protein.

A like that swaps one base for another. Some substitutions are silent: the new codon names the same amino acid, so the protein is unchanged. Others change one amino acid, as in sickle cell. Still others create a stop codon in the middle of a gene, cutting the protein short. An or adds or removes bases. If the number is not a multiple of three, every codon after it is read wrong. This is a , and it usually wrecks the protein.

Where do mutations come from? Some are copying errors that slip past proofreading. Others are caused by damage: ultraviolet light from the sun, certain chemicals in tobacco smoke, or radiation. The cell repairs most damage, but not all. Most mutations land in stretches of DNA that do not code for anything and have no effect. A few are harmful. Rarely, one is helpful.

Which cells the mutation is in matters. A mutation in a skin cell affects only that cell and its descendants in your body. A mutation in a sperm or egg cell can be passed to a child and to every cell in that child. Only those inherited mutations feed into the story of evolution, which the next chapter tells.

Passing Genes On

38.6

Shuffling the Deck

Main ideaMeiosis makes sex cells with half the usual chromosomes, and crossing over and independent assortment mix the genes so every cell is different.

Your DNA is packed into 46 , in 23 pairs. One of each pair came from your mother, the other from your father. A sperm or egg carries only 23, one from each pair. When they join, the count returns to 46. The special kind of cell division that halves the count is called .

Meiosis begins like ordinary division: the DNA is copied. Then the cell divides twice. In the first division, the paired chromosomes line up side by side and are pulled to opposite ends. In the second, the copies separate. One cell becomes four, each with a single set of 23. Because it happens only in sex cells, meiosis is the bridge between generations.

Two things during meiosis make offspring different from each other. First, while paired chromosomes lie side by side, they swap pieces. This is . A chromosome that was purely your father’s comes out with stretches from your mother’s copy. Second, when the pairs line up, which member goes left and which goes right is random for each pair. This is . With 23 pairs there are 2 to the 23rd power, more than 8 million, possible combinations from one parent alone.

Add crossing over, and then the random meeting of one sperm and one egg, and the number of possible children from two parents is beyond counting. Brothers and sisters share parents but not the same shuffle. Identical twins are the exception: they come from one fertilized egg that split, so they carry the same combination.

38.7

Mendel's Peas

Main ideaGregor Mendel showed that traits are carried by paired factors, one from each parent, and that some versions hide others.

In a monastery garden in what is now the Czech Republic, a monk named Gregor Mendel spent eight years crossing pea plants. He chose peas because they have clear traits, like round or wrinkled seeds and tall or short stems, and because he could control which plant pollinated which. Between 1856 and 1863 he grew and counted thousands of plants. He published his results in 1866. Almost no one noticed until 1900.

Here is what he found. Cross a plant with round seeds and a plant with wrinkled seeds, and every offspring has round seeds. The wrinkled seems to vanish. But let those offspring pollinate themselves, and wrinkled seeds come back in about one plant in four. Mendel counted 5,474 round to 1,850 wrinkled, close to a 3:1 ratio. He got the same ratio for six other traits.

Mendel explained it with factors that we now call , versions of a gene. Each plant carries two alleles for seed shape, one from each parent. The round allele is : if a plant has even one, its seeds are round. The wrinkled allele is : seeds are wrinkled only when both alleles are wrinkled. When a plant makes pollen or eggs, the two alleles separate, and each sex cell gets one. This matches what you learned about meiosis, though Mendel never saw a chromosome.

A Punnett square shows the possibilities. Write one parent’s alleles across the top, the other’s down the side, and fill in the boxes. For two parents that each carry one round allele (R) and one wrinkled allele (r), the boxes are RR, Rr, Rr and rr. Three of four have at least one R and are round. One is rr and is wrinkled. The 3:1 ratio falls out of the square.

38.8

Beyond Simple Dominance

Main ideaMany traits do not follow a simple dominant-recessive pattern: alleles can blend, both can show, or a gene can ride on the X chromosome.

Cross a red snapdragon with a white one and the offspring are pink. Neither allele hides the other. This is . One red allele makes half as much red pigment as two, so the flower is a lighter color. Cross two pink plants, and you get red, pink and white in a 1:2:1 ratio, which shows the alleles never blended. They just shared the work.

Sometimes both alleles show fully at once. Human blood type is the classic case. The gene for the ABO blood group has three alleles. The A allele and the B allele each put a different marker on red blood cells. A person with one of each has both markers and is type AB. This is . The third allele, O, puts no marker and is recessive to both. So type O people carry two O alleles.

Some genes sit on the X chromosome. Women have two X chromosomes; men have one X and one Y. A recessive allele on the X is usually masked in a woman by her other X. A man has no second X, so if his one X carries it, the trait shows. These are traits. Red-green color blindness works this way. It affects about 1 in 12 men but only about 1 in 200 women. A colorblind man’s sons never inherit it from him, because sons get his Y; his daughters get his X and become carriers.

Most human traits are more tangled still. Height, skin color and the risk of many diseases depend on many genes at once, plus food, sunlight and other conditions. That is why children are not simple averages of their parents and why a Punnett square cannot predict how tall you will be.

38.9

Reading a Family Tree

Main ideaA pedigree charts a trait across generations, and its pattern reveals whether an allele is dominant, recessive or sex-linked.

Geneticists cannot cross people the way Mendel crossed peas. Instead they draw a : a family tree that marks who has a trait. Squares stand for males and circles for females. A horizontal line joins parents; a vertical line leads down to their children. A shaded shape means the person shows the trait. Half-shading or a dot can mark a , someone who has one copy of a recessive allele but does not show it.

The pattern tells the story. If two unaffected parents have an affected child, the trait must be recessive: both parents were carriers. If a trait appears in every generation and every affected child has an affected parent, it is likely dominant. If it shows up mostly in males and skips generations through unaffected mothers, it is probably recessive and on the X chromosome.

The most famous pedigree in history is Queen Victoria’s. She carried an allele for hemophilia, a disease in which blood does not clot properly, on one of her X chromosomes. She had no symptoms. But one of her sons had the disease, and two of her daughters were carriers. Through royal marriages the allele reached the ruling families of Spain, Germany and Russia. The pattern, affected sons and carrier daughters, is exactly what a sex-linked recessive trait produces.

Pedigrees have practical uses today. A genetic counselor can use one, along with a DNA test, to tell a couple the odds that a child will inherit a condition that runs in the family. The chart cannot say what will happen to one particular child. It can only say what fraction of many such children would be expected to inherit it.

Reading and Editing the Code

38.10

Sequencing a Genome

Main ideaScientists can now read the full order of bases in an organism's DNA, and doing so for humans changed medicine and how we see ourselves.

A is the complete set of DNA in one organism. In 1990 a large team of scientists in the United States and other countries set out to read all 3 billion base pairs of the human genome. This was the Human Genome Project. It took 13 years and about 3 billion dollars, and it finished in April 2003. Today a person’s genome can be in a day or two for a few hundred dollars.

The results held surprises. Scientists had guessed humans would have around 100,000 genes. The count came in at only about 20,000 protein-coding genes, similar to a mouse and fewer than some plants. Only about 1 to 2 percent of the genome codes for protein. Much of the rest controls when and where genes are turned on. And any two people are about 99.9 percent identical in their DNA. All human variety fits in the other 0.1 percent.

Reading genomes changed medicine. Doctors can test for the alleles behind cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease and many cancers. Some drugs are now matched to a patient’s genes. Comparing genomes across species also became a powerful tool for tracing evolution, because closely related species have more similar sequences. You will use that evidence in the next chapter.

Reading your own code raises questions. Who should see it? Could an employer or an insurer use it against you? In the United States a 2008 federal law bars health insurers and most employers from using genetic information to discriminate. But privacy of DNA stored in company databases remains an open issue.

38.11

Editing With CRISPR

Main ideaCRISPR lets scientists cut DNA at a chosen spot and change it, which brings real cures and hard questions about how far to go.

Bacteria get infected by viruses, and some bacteria keep a memory of past infections. They store short pieces of viral DNA in their own genome, in a region called . When the same virus returns, the bacterium makes an RNA copy of the stored piece. That RNA guides a protein called Cas9 to the matching spot in the virus’s DNA, and Cas9 cuts it. It is an immune system made of DNA.

In 2012 Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that this system could be aimed anywhere. Give Cas9 a guide RNA that matches any sequence you choose, and it will cut there. The cell then repairs the cut, and scientists can supply a new piece of DNA for it to use as a patch. This is . For this work the two shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020.

The first cures came fast. Sickle cell disease is caused by one changed base, as you saw. In December 2023 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved a treatment that edits a patient’s own blood-forming cells and returns them to the body. Researchers are also editing crops for drought tolerance and disease resistance. Illinois, where more than 90 percent of corn acres are planted with genetically engineered varieties, is watching closely.

The hard questions come with the power. Editing a patient’s blood cells changes one person. Editing an embryo would change every cell of a person not yet born, and their children too. In 2018 a scientist in China announced he had edited the genes of twin girls before birth. Scientists worldwide condemned it, and he was sent to prison. Most countries now ban editing embryos that will be carried to birth. Who decides which conditions to edit, who can afford it, and what counts as a disease rather than a difference are questions the science alone cannot answer.

Evolution and Its Evidence

Evolution
Big questionWhat evidence shows that living things change over time, and what forces drive that change?
The story

The Fish With a Neck

Three scientists used a geology map to predict where a missing fossil should be, then went to the Arctic and found it.

Fish do not have necks. Their skulls are bolted to their shoulders by a row of bones, so a fish turns its whole body to look sideways. Land animals with four limbs, from frogs to people, have a gap there, and can swing the head alone. Fossils show that fish came first and four-limbed animals came later. If that is true, then somewhere in the rocks there should be a creature in between: part fish, part land animal, with a neck.

In the late 1990s, Neil Shubin, Ted Daeschler and Farish Jenkins decided to look for it on purpose. They reasoned like detectives. The first four-limbed animals appear in rocks about 365 million years old. Fish with strong, lobed fins appear in rocks about 385 million years old. So the in-between creature should lie in rocks from about 375 million years ago, laid down by shallow fresh water, and those rocks should be exposed at the surface where no one had dug before.

A geology textbook had a map of exactly such rocks on Ellesmere Island, in the far north of Canada, about 1,000 kilometers from the North Pole. The team went in 1999. They found almost nothing. They went back in 2000, 2002 and 2004. Summers there are short and cold, and polar bears are a real danger. In July 2004 a crew member noticed the snout of a flat-headed animal sticking out of a cliff.

The fossil was a fish, with scales and fins and gills. But its head was flat like a crocodile's, with eyes on top. It had ribs sturdy enough to hold up a body. Inside its front fin were bones that matched a shoulder, an elbow and a wrist. And its skull was free of the shoulder bones. It had a neck. The team named it Tiktaalik, from an Inuktitut word for a large freshwater fish, and published it in 2006.

Tiktaalik was not a guess that got lucky. It was a prediction: this kind of animal, in rocks of this age, in this kind of place. The prediction came true. That is what makes a scientific idea strong. Evolution had said what should be found and where, decades before anyone had found it. This chapter is about the evidence that lets scientists make predictions like that.

Reading the Record

39.1

Fossils and Deep Time

Main ideaRock layers and radioactive dating let scientists put fossils in order and show that life has changed over billions of years.

In 1669 the Danish scientist Nicolas Steno pointed out something that now seems obvious. Layers of sediment settle one on top of another, so in undisturbed rock, deeper layers are older. Walk down into a canyon and you walk back in time. The in each layer, the preserved remains or traces of living things, give a snapshot of life at that time.

Steno’s rule gives the order but not the age in years. That came in the twentieth century, when scientists learned to use as a clock. Some atoms break down at a steady rate that nothing speeds up or slows down. Measure how much of the original element is left in a volcanic rock layer, and you can compute when it formed. Fossils between two dated layers are bracketed in time. By this method, the Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, and the oldest known fossils are microbes about 3.5 billion years old.

The record tells a consistent story. Simple single-celled life appears first. Animals with hard parts show up about 540 million years ago. Fish come before amphibians, amphibians before reptiles, reptiles before mammals and birds. No one has ever found a rabbit in rocks from the age of trilobites. And where the record is good, it shows like Tiktaalik, and like Archaeopteryx, a feathered animal with teeth and a bony tail, found in Germany in 1861.

Illinois has its own window on deep time. The Mazon Creek beds near Braidwood preserve soft-bodied animals and plants from about 309 million years ago, when this region was a warm coastal swamp. The state fossil, the Tully monster, comes from these beds and is so strange that scientists still argue about what group it belongs to.

39.2

Same Bones, Different Jobs

Main ideaLiving things share body plans, leftover structures and early stages of development that make sense only if they descend from common ancestors.

Lay out the front limbs of a human, a cat, a whale and a bat. A human arm is for grasping, a cat’s leg for running, a whale’s flipper for swimming and a bat’s wing for flying. Yet all four have the same bones in the same order: one upper bone, two lower bones, a cluster of wrist bones, then the finger bones. The whale’s flipper even has finger bones inside it. These are : same plan, different job. The simplest explanation is that all four inherited the plan from one ancestor.

Compare this to a bird’s wing and a butterfly’s wing. Both are for flying, but one is built of bone and feathers and the other of thin membrane held by veins. They look alike only on the outside. These are : same job, different plan. They evolved separately. Homology points to shared ancestry; analogy points to shared problems.

Some structures have no job at all. Whales have small pelvic bones buried in muscle, far from any leg. Some snakes have tiny hind-limb bones. Humans have a tailbone made of fused vertebrae and, in some people, a small muscle that in other mammals moves the ears. These are leftovers, shrunken but not gone, from ancestors that used them.

Early development tells the same story. Embryos of fish, chickens, and humans all pass through a stage with a tail and a row of pouches in the neck. In fish the pouches become gills. In humans they become parts of the jaw, ear and throat. A word of caution: the nineteenth-century biologist Ernst Haeckel drew these embryos to look more alike than they are, and his drawings were used in textbooks for a century. The real photos still show the shared stages. The exaggeration was the problem, not the evidence.

39.3

The Evidence in DNA

Main ideaThe more closely two species are related, the more similar their DNA, and shared broken genes show descent from the same ancestor.

Darwin never saw a gene. If he had, he would have found his strongest evidence. Every living thing uses DNA, the same four bases, and almost the same codon table. That alone is hard to explain unless all life descends from one early ancestor. But the sequences say much more. When two species split from a shared ancestor, their DNA starts out alike and slowly gathers different mutations. The longer since the split, the more differences pile up.

So scientists can build family trees from DNA. Humans and chimpanzees differ in only about 1 to 2 percent of their aligned DNA. Humans and mice differ more; humans and fish more still. The trees built from DNA match, in almost every branch, the trees built from bones and fossils decades earlier. Two independent lines of evidence, one from rocks and one from molecules, agree. That agreement is what makes scientists confident.

The most telling evidence is in broken genes. Most mammals make their own vitamin C. Humans, chimpanzees and other primates cannot; we must eat it or get scurvy. The reason is a gene called GULO. Primates still carry it, but it is wrecked by mutations and no longer works. And the same wrecking mutations appear in the same spots in humans and chimps. A designer starting fresh would not include a broken gene. An ancestor that broke it and passed the ruin to all its descendants would.

DNA also records change within a species. Every person alive carries mutations that trace back through their mother’s line and their father’s line. By comparing them, scientists have traced human ancestry to Africa roughly 200,000 to 300,000 years ago, in agreement with the oldest Homo sapiens fossils, found in Morocco and Ethiopia.

How Selection Works

39.4

Darwin's Idea

Main ideaDarwin proposed that living things change because individuals with helpful traits leave more offspring, so those traits spread through a population.

In 1831 a young Charles Darwin boarded the ship HMS Beagle as its naturalist. For five years he collected plants, animals and fossils around the world. In the Galápagos Islands, off the coast of Ecuador, he collected small birds that he did not think much of at the time. Back in England, an expert told him they were all finches, different species on different islands, with beaks shaped for different foods. Darwin began to wonder whether one kind of finch had reached the islands and changed.

He also read the economist Thomas Malthus, who argued that people breed faster than food supplies grow. Darwin saw that this is true of every living thing. A pair of elephants, the slowest breeders known, could have millions of descendants in a few centuries if all survived. They do not. Most offspring die before they reproduce. Darwin asked: which ones survive? And he answered: on average, the ones best suited to their surroundings.

That is . Farmers already knew they could breed cattle for more milk or dogs for herding by choosing which animals to mate. Darwin argued that nature does the same, without any choosing. Individuals vary. Some variations help. Those individuals live longer and leave more young, who inherit the variation. Over many generations, the helpful variation becomes common. Given enough time, a population can change so much that it becomes a new species.

Darwin sat on the idea for 20 years, gathering evidence. In 1858 he received a letter from Alfred Russel Wallace, working in what is now Indonesia, who had reached the same idea on his own. Their papers were read together at a meeting in London that July. Darwin’s book, On the Origin of Species, appeared in November 1859 and sold out its first printing at once.

39.5

Four Conditions

Main ideaNatural selection happens whenever there is variation, the variation is inherited, more are born than survive, and some variants survive better than others.

Natural selection is not a mysterious force. It is what must happen when four ordinary conditions are met. First, individuals in a vary. No two rabbits run at exactly the same speed. Second, some of that variation is : it is passed from parent to offspring through genes. Third, more offspring are born than can possibly survive, so there is competition for food, space and mates. Fourth, individuals with some variations survive and reproduce more than others.

Put those together and the outcome is automatic. Faster rabbits escape foxes more often. They live to have more babies, who inherit faster legs. Each generation, the average rabbit is a little faster. No rabbit tried to become faster. No rabbit changed during its life. The population changed because the mix of alleles in it shifted. Individuals do not evolve; populations do.

A common mistake is to think that living things change because they need to. A giraffe does not grow a longer neck by stretching, and it cannot pass on a stretched neck. Selection can only work on variation that already exists, from mutation and the shuffling of meiosis. If no rabbit in a population carries an allele for faster legs, selection cannot make one. Mutation writes the options; selection picks among them.

Selection also has no goal. It does not aim for perfection or for humans. It only favors whatever works better right now, in this place. A trait that helps in a drought may hurt in a flood. That is why living things are full of compromises and leftover parts, and why they keep changing as their surroundings change.

39.6

Fitness and Adaptation

Main ideaIn biology, fitness means how many offspring an organism leaves, and an adaptation is any heritable trait that raises it.

In everyday speech, the fittest is the strongest or the fastest. In biology, means one thing only: how many offspring an individual leaves, compared with others in the population. A small, slow, well-camouflaged moth that has 200 babies is fitter than a big, strong one that is eaten before it breeds. Fitness is about reproduction, and survival only matters because you must survive long enough to reproduce.

An is a heritable trait that raises fitness in a particular place. The thick fur of an Arctic fox is an adaptation to cold. The long tongue of a hummingbird is an adaptation for reaching nectar. The bitter taste of some plants is an adaptation against being eaten. Adaptations look designed for their jobs because selection kept the versions that did the jobs best.

But adaptations fit the past, not the future. A trait is an adaptation only in the surroundings where it was selected. Move the Arctic fox to Texas and its fur becomes a burden. When surroundings change quickly, as when humans clear a forest or a new disease arrives, yesterday’s adaptations may fail. Populations then either change fast enough, move, or die out.

Selection is not the only thing that shifts allele frequencies. In small populations, chance alone can change them; this is called genetic drift. A storm that kills half of a hundred beetles changes the mix of alleles for reasons that have nothing to do with fitness. Migration also matters: when individuals move between populations, they carry alleles with them. Evolution is the combined result of all these processes acting on the variation that mutation supplies.

Evolution You Can Watch

39.7

Finches in a Drought

Main ideaOn one Galápagos island, a single drought shifted finch beak size in one generation, and scientists measured it happening.

Darwin guessed that evolution was far too slow to see. He was wrong. In 1973 Peter and Rosemary Grant began catching, measuring and banding every medium ground finch on Daphne Major, a small volcanic island in the Galápagos. They measured wings, legs and, above all, beaks. Beak depth varied from bird to bird by a millimeter or so, and it was heritable: deep-beaked parents had deep-beaked chicks.

In 1977 the rains failed. Almost no seeds were produced. The finches ate through the small, soft seeds first. What remained were large, hard seeds that only a deep, strong beak could crack. The population fell from roughly 1,200 birds to fewer than 200. The survivors were not a random sample. On average, they had deeper beaks than the birds that died.

The next year the survivors bred, and their chicks had beaks measurably deeper than the generation before the drought, by roughly half a millimeter on average. That is natural selection, measured in the wild in a single year. Then in the 1980s came years of heavy rain and a flood of small seeds. Selection reversed, and beaks grew smaller again. The Grants have documented these swings for more than forty years.

Their work shows several things at once. Selection can be fast. It can go in either direction. It responds to conditions, not to a goal. And it requires variation that is already there: if every finch had the same beak, the drought would have killed at random and nothing would have shifted.

Moths, Bugs and Bacteria

Main ideaPeppered moths, crop pests and disease bacteria all show populations changing fast under selection caused by humans.

Before 1848 nearly every peppered moth in England was pale and speckled, which hid it on lichen-covered tree bark. That year a dark form was recorded near Manchester. As coal smoke killed the lichen and blackened the trees, dark moths became harder for birds to see, and pale ones easier. By 1895 about 98 percent of the moths around Manchester were dark. When clean-air laws cut the smoke in the second half of the twentieth century, the trees lightened and the pale form came back. Experiments releasing moths of both colors and watching birds hunt them confirmed the cause.

Farmers see the same thing. Spray a field with a pesticide and most insects die. But if a few carry a mutation that lets them survive, those few breed, and the next generation is full of resistant insects. This happened with DDT in the 1940s and 1950s and has happened with pesticide after pesticide since. In Illinois and Iowa, some western corn rootworms have evolved resistance to corn engineered to make its own insecticide. The spray did not create the resistance; it selected for it.

The most urgent case is . Bacteria divide every twenty minutes or so, so selection works on them at high speed. Alexander Fleming, who discovered penicillin, warned in 1945 that using too little of it could breed resistant germs. Today some infections resist nearly every antibiotic we have. Each time a person stops taking a prescription early or a farm feeds antibiotics to healthy animals, it is a selection experiment that the bacteria win.

These examples answer a common objection: that evolution has never been observed. It is observed constantly, wherever a population faces strong, steady selection. The lesson for medicine and farming is to change the selection: rotate drugs, use them only when needed, and plant refuges of unmodified crops so that susceptible insects survive to dilute the resistant ones.

39.9

When Nothing Changes

Main ideaThe Hardy-Weinberg rule describes a population where allele frequencies stay constant, which lets scientists detect when evolution is happening.

In 1908 a mathematician named Godfrey Hardy and a doctor named Wilhelm Weinberg separately answered a puzzle. If a dominant allele always shows, why does it not take over the population? Hardy showed with simple algebra that it does not. Left alone, allele frequencies stay the same generation after generation. Dominance changes how a trait looks, not how common the allele is.

Here is the idea. Call the frequency of one allele p and the other q, so that p + q = 1. If mating is random, the fraction of individuals with two copies of the first allele is p × p, the fraction with two copies of the second is q × q, and the fraction with one of each is 2 × p × q. If nothing disturbs the population, those fractions repeat every generation. This is the .

The rule holds only if five things are true: no mutation, no migration in or out, a very large population, random mating, and no selection. Real populations rarely meet all five. That is the point. The rule describes what a population looks like when it is not evolving. When scientists count alleles in a real population and the numbers do not fit, something on the list is happening, and they can go looking for which one.

The rule also has a practical use. Suppose 1 person in 10,000 is born with a recessive genetic condition. That number is q × q, so q is 1 in 100. Then the carriers, 2 × p × q, are about 2 in 100, or 1 person in 50. A condition that seems rare can be carried by a surprising number of people who never show it.

39.10

How Species Form

Main ideaA new species forms when two populations stop exchanging genes and drift apart until they can no longer interbreed.

A is a group of organisms that can breed with each other and produce fertile offspring. Horses and donkeys can mate, but their mules are sterile, so horses and donkeys are separate species. The line is not always sharp, especially for bacteria and plants, but the idea works for most animals.

usually starts with a barrier. A river changes course and splits a population of mice. A few lizards raft on a log to an island. A mountain range rises. Once the two groups no longer exchange genes, they evolve separately. Different mutations appear on each side. Selection favors different traits in different places. Drift shifts allele frequencies at random. After enough generations, if the groups meet again, they may not recognize each other as mates, or their offspring may not survive.

The Galápagos finches are the textbook case. One ancestral finch reached the islands from the mainland. Different islands offered different foods, and populations on each island were selected for beaks suited to those foods. Today there are more than a dozen species, each adapted to a diet: seed-crushers, insect-eaters, cactus-flower feeders, even one that pecks at other birds and drinks their blood. This branching from one ancestor into many is called .

Speciation does not always need a physical barrier. In the 1800s, in the Hudson River valley of New York, some apple maggot flies that had always laid eggs on hawthorn fruit began laying them on the newly planted apples. Apples ripen weeks earlier than hawthorns, so the two groups of flies now emerge at different times and rarely meet. Their genes are already diverging. It is speciation caught in the act, in an orchard.

Think it through
Two Sides of a Canyon

Squirrels live on both rims of the Grand Canyon. The canyon opened millions of years ago, and squirrels do not cross it. On the north rim the squirrels have white tails and white bellies; on the south rim they have gray tails and dark bellies. They look like close relatives.

What happened to the gene flow between the two populations when the canyon opened? What would you need to test to decide whether they are now separate species?

Why: Once gene flow stops, the populations gather different mutations and are selected in different ways.

39.11

Extinction

Main ideaSpecies go extinct constantly, and a few times in history most of life vanished at once, opening the way for survivors to diversify.

More than 99 percent of all species that have ever lived are gone. is the normal end of a species, just as death is the normal end of an individual. Usually it is slow and local: surroundings change, a competitor arrives, a food source vanishes, and a species that cannot adapt or move dwindles to nothing. This steady loss is called background extinction.

A few times, the fossil record shows something else: a thin band of rock above which most species are simply missing. These are . Scientists count five major ones. The largest, about 252 million years ago, wiped out roughly nine of every ten marine species. The most famous, 66 million years ago, ended the non-bird dinosaurs. In 1980 Luis and Walter Alvarez found that the rock layer from that moment, all over the world, is rich in iridium, an element rare on Earth but common in asteroids. A crater of the right age, about 150 kilometers wide, was later identified near Chicxulub, Mexico.

Mass extinctions clear the stage. For 150 million years, mammals lived in the shadow of dinosaurs, most of them no bigger than a cat. Within a few million years of the asteroid, mammals had radiated into whales, bats, horses and primates. The survivors of a catastrophe are not necessarily the best adapted; they may simply be small, or able to burrow, or lucky. What they inherit is an emptied world.

Extinctions caused by people are recorded in living memory. The passenger pigeon once flew over Illinois in flocks that darkened the sky for hours; the last one, named Martha, died in the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914. Many biologists argue that the current rate of extinction, driven by habitat loss, hunting and climate change, is far above the background rate. Whether it becomes a sixth mass extinction depends on choices being made now.

Think it through
The Thin Gray Line

In a road cut in Italy, a geologist points to a layer of clay about a centimeter thick. Below it, the limestone is full of tiny fossil shells of many kinds. Above it, the same limestone holds only a few kinds, and they are much smaller. Chemical tests show the clay is rich in iridium.

What two separate observations here point to a sudden catastrophe? Why was the iridium so important to the explanation?

mass extinction

iridium

Check yourself

1. What evidence linked the extinction of the dinosaurs to an asteroid?

Why: Iridium is rare on Earth and common in asteroids. Its worldwide spike marked the impact.

2. How did the extinction of the dinosaurs affect mammals?

Why: With the dominant animals gone, mammals radiated into the empty roles.

3. Which best describes background extinction?

Why: Background extinction is the ordinary, ongoing rate, unlike the spikes of mass extinctions.
39.12

The Tree of Life

Main ideaAll living things can be arranged on one branching tree of descent, and DNA has redrawn its deepest branches.

In 1837, two years after visiting the Galápagos, Darwin sketched a branching diagram in a notebook. Above it he wrote two words: ’I think.’ The sketch showed species splitting from common ancestors like the limbs of a tree. Before Darwin, naturalists like Carl Linnaeus had grouped living things by similarity, in nested boxes. Species sat inside genera, genera inside families, and so on. Darwin explained why the boxes nest. Similar species share a recent ancestor. The bigger boxes share older ones.

A diagram of these relationships is a . Each branch point is a common ancestor. Two species that join at a recent branch point are close relatives. Reading the tree, you find surprises. Birds are a branch of the dinosaurs. Whales are a branch of the hoofed mammals. Fungi are closer to animals than to plants. The tree is a hypothesis. It is tested every time a new fossil or genome is compared with it.

The deepest branches were redrawn in 1977. Carl Woese compared the RNA of ribosomes across many organisms. He found that some microbes, then lumped in with bacteria, were as different from bacteria as bacteria are from us. He named them . Life, it turned out, has three main : Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. The last includes every plant, animal, fungus and protist. Humans are one twig on the eukaryote branch.

At the root of the tree is a single ancestor. All living things descend from it, starting roughly 3.5 to 4 billion years ago. The evidence is the shared machinery: the same DNA, the same code, the same ribosomes, the same basic energy chemistry in every cell. Look at a redwood, a mushroom, a bacterium in your gut, or your own hand. You are looking at branches of one tree.

Check yourself

1. On a phylogenetic tree, what does a branch point stand for?

Why: Each split marks the ancestor from which the branches beyond it descend.

2. What did Carl Woese discover by comparing ribosomal RNA?

Why: Archaea had been mistaken for bacteria. Their RNA showed a deep, separate branch.

3. Which is the strongest evidence that all living things share one ancestor?

Why: Shared molecular machinery across every domain is best explained by inheritance from one root.

Evolution and Its Evidence

0 / 8

1. The team that found Tiktaalik chose Ellesmere Island because:

Why: They predicted a fish-to-land transition in 375-million-year-old freshwater rocks and looked where such rocks lay open.

2. A whale's flipper contains finger bones. This is best explained by:

Why: The bones are homologous with land mammals' limbs, inherited from a common ancestor.

3. Which condition is NOT required for natural selection?

Why: Selection has no goal and needs no intent. It only needs heritable variation and unequal reproduction.

4. During the 1977 drought, finches with deeper beaks survived because:

Why: Soft seeds vanished. Deep beaks could crack the hard seeds that were left.

5. Why does a population that does not fit Hardy-Weinberg numbers interest scientists?

Why: The rule is the baseline for no change. A mismatch signals selection, drift, migration or mutation.

6. The dark form of the peppered moth declined after clean-air laws because:

Why: With soot gone, bark lightened and pale moths regained the camouflage advantage.

7. What does the iridium layer found around the world mark?

Why: Iridium is rare on Earth but common in asteroids. Its worldwide spike dates the impact.

8. Humans and chimpanzees share the same broken vitamin C gene. What does this show?

Why: Identical breaks in the same places are the mark of a shared ancestor that passed the ruin to both.

Why: A gene is first transcribed into mRNA, then the mRNA is translated into protein.

Why: Two is not a multiple of three, so every codon downstream is read out of frame.

Why: Crossing over and independent assortment during meiosis shuffle alleles into new combinations.

5. A cross between two Rr pea plants gives what ratio of round to wrinkled seeds?

Why: The Punnett square gives RR, Rr, Rr, rr: three round to one wrinkled.

Why: Men have one X, so one recessive allele shows. Women need it on both X chromosomes.

7. In a pedigree, two unshaded parents have a shaded child. The trait is:

Why: Only a recessive allele can be carried unseen by both parents and appear in a child.

8. What does CRISPR-Cas9 use to find its target in DNA?

Why: The guide RNA pairs with the matching DNA sequence and directs Cas9 to cut there.

9. Why did the Tiktaalik team look in rocks about 375 million years old?

Why: Lobe-finned fish came before 385 million years ago and land animals after 365 million, so the transition should fall between.

10. The forelimbs of a human, cat, whale and bat share the same bones. This is evidence of:

Why: Same plan with different uses is homology, inherited from one ancestor.

11. Which statement about natural selection is correct?

Why: Selection acts on existing heritable variation and shifts the population; individuals do not change their genes.

12. After the 1977 drought on Daphne Major, the next generation of finches had:

Why: Deep-beaked birds survived and bred, and beak depth is heritable.

Why: Spraying selects for resistance alleles already present in a few individuals.

Why: Without gene flow, mutation, selection and drift push the two groups apart.
