The Wider Economy

Every day, the whole country makes, buys and sells an enormous amount of stuff. Bakers bake. Truckers drive. Nurses care for patients. The story

Grandma and the Nickel Candy Bar

A candy bar once cost about a nickel. Does that mean Grandma was richer than we are?

Maya's grandmother likes to tell a story. When she was a girl around 1950, a candy bar cost about a nickel. She could walk to the corner store with a dime and buy two. Maya looked at the price tag at the gas station. The same kind of bar cost well over a dollar. Maya's first thought was simple. Grandma had it easy.

Then Maya asked a second question. How much money did people earn back then? Grandma laughed. Her father worked in a Chicago factory. His pay looked tiny next to what Maya's mom earns today. A nickel was not small change to him. Prices were lower, but paychecks were lower too.

Maya's teacher said economists have a name for this. When prices across the whole economy rise over time, that is inflation. To compare two years fairly, you cannot just compare prices. You must compare prices with what people earn. You also have to ask what money can buy, like cars, phones and medicine that did not exist in 1950.

This chapter zooms out from one store to the whole country. You will learn how we measure everything the country makes. You will see how prices, jobs and taxes fit together. You will also meet the Federal Reserve, which watches prices and jobs across the nation. By the end, you can answer Maya's question yourself.

Talk about itWas Grandma richer or poorer than us? What two things would you need to know to decide?
Section 1

Measuring the Whole Economy

19.1

GDP: The Size of the Economy

Main ideaGDP is the total value of all final goods and services made in a country in one year.

Picture a tiny island with one bakery and one bike shop. In one year the bakery sells 1,000 loaves at $3 each (example prices). That is $3,000. The bike shop sells 20 bikes at $200 each. That is $4,000. Add them up. The island made $7,000 worth of goods that year.

That total is the island’s gross domestic product, or GDP. GDP is the market value of all final goods and services made inside a country in one year. Final means sold to the last user. We count the bread, not the flour the baker bought. Counting the flour too would count it twice.

The United States works the same way, just much bigger. Economists add up spending on houses, haircuts, trucks, doctor visits and much more. The Bureau of Economic Analysis, a federal agency, measures U.S. GDP. A bigger GDP usually means more goods, more services and more jobs.

3. Which agency measures U.S. GDP?

Why: The Bureau of Economic Analysis, part of the Commerce Department, measures GDP.
19.2

Growth, Recession and the Cycle

Main ideaThe economy grows most years, but sometimes it shrinks for a while; that pattern is called the business cycle.

Think of a town where factories hire more workers each year. Stores are busy. People buy new cars. This is growth. If GDP is $100 billion one year and $103 billion the next (example numbers), it grew by $3 billion. That is 3% growth.

Sometimes spending slows. Stores sell less, so they order less from factories. Factories cut hours and lay off workers. Those workers spend less, and the slowdown spreads. A widespread drop in economic activity that lasts more than a few months is called a recession.

Over many years, the economy moves up and down in waves. Economists call this the business cycle. It has an expansion, when things grow, then a peak, then a contraction, and then a low point called a trough. After the trough, growth starts again. A common mistake is thinking every dip is a recession. A short, small wobble usually is not.

Think it through

Words to know
recession

business cycle

expansion

Check yourself

1. GDP goes from $200 billion to $210 billion (example numbers). What is the growth rate?

Why: $10 billion / $200 billion = 0.05, which is 5%.

2. What comes right after the peak in the business cycle?

Why: After the top, or peak, activity falls. That falling part is the contraction.

3. Which event best signals a recession?

Why: A recession is broad and lasts more than a few months.
19.3

Real GDP versus Nominal GDP

Main ideaReal GDP removes the effect of rising prices, so it shows whether we really made more.

Go back to the island. Last year it made 1,000 loaves at $3. This year it made the same 1,000 loaves, but the price rose to $4 (example prices). GDP in dollars went from $3,000 to $4,000. Did the island make more bread? No. It made the same bread. Only the price changed.

GDP measured in current prices is called nominal GDP. It can rise just because prices rose. Real GDP uses the prices of one fixed year for every year. Using last year’s $3 price, this year’s real GDP is still $3,000. Real GDP shows the true change in how much was made.

When news reports say the economy grew 2%, they almost always mean real GDP. Mixing the two is a common mistake. If nominal GDP rose 5% and prices rose 3%, real output grew only about 2%.

3. Why do economists prefer real GDP to compare years?

Why: Real GDP takes out inflation, so it tracks real output.
Section 2

Prices and Jobs

19.4

Inflation and the Dollar

Main ideaInflation is a rise in the general level of prices, and it means each dollar buys less.

Imagine a snack basket: a sandwich, an apple and a juice. Last year it cost $10. This year the same basket costs $10.50 (example prices). Prices rose $0.50 on $10. That is 5%. When prices across the economy go up like this, it is called inflation.

Inflation shrinks what a dollar can buy. That buying power is called purchasing power. If your allowance stays at $10 but the basket costs $10.50, you can no longer buy the whole basket. Your dollars did not change. What they can buy did.

Inflation happens for different reasons. Sometimes people and businesses want to buy more than the economy can make, so prices get pushed up. Sometimes costs like fuel jump, and businesses pass them on. The Federal Reserve aims for inflation of about 2% a year over time. A little inflation is normal. Fast inflation makes planning hard.

How many candy bars could each worker buy with one hour of pay? Who is better off in candy bars?

Words to know
inflation
a rise in the general level of prices across the economy over time
purchasing power
how much a set amount of money can buy
Check yourself

1. A basket rises from $20 to $21 (example prices). What is the inflation rate?

Why: $1 / $20 = 0.05, which is 5%.

2. Your pay stays the same while prices rise 4%. What happens to your purchasing power?

Why: The same dollars buy fewer goods, so purchasing power falls.

3. About what inflation rate does the Federal Reserve aim for over time?

Why: The Fed's stated goal is inflation of 2% over the longer run.
19.5

How Prices Are Measured

Main ideaThe Consumer Price Index tracks the cost of a fixed basket of things households buy.

No one can check every price in the country. So the Bureau of Labor Statistics, or BLS, uses a sample. Each month it checks prices on a large basket of goods and services, like food, rent, gas, clothes and doctor visits. The result is the Consumer Price Index, or CPI.

The CPI starts from a base period set to 100. Suppose the basket’s index is 100 in the base year and 110 later (example numbers). Prices rose 10% since the base. To find inflation between any two years, use percent change. From 110 to 121 is a rise of 11. Divide 11 by 110. That is 10%.

The CPI is not perfect. People change what they buy when prices change. New products appear. Quality gets better. Still, the CPI is the most used price measure. Governments use it to adjust things like Social Security payments so they keep up with prices.

Why: (108 - 100) / 100 = 0.08, which is 8%.

2. Which agency publishes the CPI?

3. An index goes from 150 to 165. What is the percent change?

19.6

Who Counts as Unemployed

Main ideaA person is unemployed only if they have no job, can work, and are looking for work.

Picture a street with 10 adults. Six have jobs. Two have no job and are sending out applications. One is retired. One is a full-time college student who is not looking for work. Who is unemployed? Only the two who are looking.

The labor force is everyone who is working or looking for work. On this street, that is 6 + 2 = 8 people. The unemployment rate is the unemployed divided by the labor force, times 100. Here that is 2 / 8 x 100 = 25%. The retiree and the student are not in the labor force at all.

A common mistake is dividing by all adults. That would give 2 / 10 = 20%, which is wrong. The BLS finds the real rate each month with a large survey of households. The rate was about 25% in the worst year of the Great Depression and about 10% in late 2009.

Words to know
labor force
all people who have a job or are actively looking for one
unemployment rate
the unemployed divided by the labor force, times 100
unemployed
having no job, being able to work, and actively looking for work
Check yourself

1. A town has 90 people working and 10 looking for work. What is the unemployment rate?

Why: Labor force = 90 + 10 = 100. 10 / 100 x 100 = 10%.

2. A retired teacher is not looking for work. How is she counted?

Why: She is not working and not looking, so she is outside the labor force.

3. A town has 45 employed, 5 unemployed and 50 retired people. What is the unemployment rate?

Why: Labor force = 45 + 5 = 50. 5 / 50 x 100 = 10%. Retirees are not in it.

Taxes, Budgets and the Fed

19.7

Three Kinds of Taxes

Main ideaGovernments collect money mostly through taxes on income, on sales and on property.

Jordan earns $500 from a summer job. Some of it goes to income tax, a tax on what people earn. Then Jordan buys $40 of shoes. The store adds a sales tax, a tax on things you buy. At a rate of 6% (example rate), that adds $2.40. The shoes cost $42.40.

Jordan’s family owns a house. Each year they pay property tax, a tax based on the value of land and buildings. Property taxes pay for much of the local public schools in Illinois, along with state money. Your town and county use them for roads, parks and police too.

Different levels of government lean on different taxes. The federal government collects mostly income and payroll taxes. States use income and sales taxes. Local governments use property taxes the most. Each tax makes something a little costlier, so every tax has trade-offs.

19.8

Where Federal Money Goes

Main ideaMost federal spending goes to a few big items: Social Security, health programs, defense and interest on debt.

Imagine the federal government spends $100 (a pretend round number). Where would it go? In recent years, about $21 went to Social Security. About $26 went to health programs such as Medicare and Medicaid. About $13 went to national defense. About $13 went to interest on money the government borrowed.

That leaves about $27 for everything else. Everything else includes highways, food aid, veterans’ benefits, science, national parks and much more. When the government spends more than it collects in taxes in a year, the gap is a deficit. It borrows to cover it, and the total borrowed over time is the debt.

Congress and the President decide the budget. This is called fiscal policy: choices about government spending and taxes. People disagree about how big the budget should be. Some say more spending helps people and builds roads and schools. Others say lower spending and taxes leave more for families and businesses. Both sides weigh trade-offs.

19.9

Who Pays What Share

Main ideaA tax can take a bigger share, the same share or a smaller share of income as income rises.

Two neighbors each buy $1,000 of goods and pay a 5% sales tax (example rate). Both pay $50. One neighbor earns $20,000 a year. The other earns $100,000. For the first, $50 is 0.25% of income. For the second, it is 0.05%. The same tax takes a bigger share from the lower income.

A tax that takes a larger share from lower incomes is called regressive. A tax that takes a larger share from higher incomes is progressive. The U.S. federal income tax is progressive: rates rise in steps as income rises. A tax that takes the same share from everyone is proportional, sometimes called a flat tax.

People argue about which kind is fairest. Some say those who earn more can afford to pay a larger share. Others say everyone should pay the same share, and that high rates discourage work and investment. Economists study how each design affects work, saving and fairness.

Check yourself

1. A tax takes 10% from a $20,000 income and 25% from a $200,000 income. What kind is it?

Why: The share rises as income rises, which makes it progressive.

2. Everyone pays 4% of income (example rate). How much does someone earning $50,000 pay?

19.10

The Federal Reserve

Main ideaThe Federal Reserve watches prices and jobs and sets a key interest rate to keep the economy steady.

Think of the economy like a car on a long road. Too fast, and prices rise too quickly. Too slow, and people lose jobs. The Federal Reserve, often called the Fed, is the nation’s central bank. Congress created it with the Federal Reserve Act of 1913.

Congress gave the Fed two main goals, often called the dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices. The Fed has a board in Washington, D.C., and twelve regional Reserve Banks. One is the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, which serves Illinois and nearby states.

The Fed’s main tool is a key interest rate that banks charge each other for overnight loans. When the Fed raises it, loans for cars, homes and businesses tend to cost more, so spending slows and inflation can cool. When the Fed lowers it, borrowing gets cheaper and spending can pick up. This is monetary policy. It is separate from fiscal policy, which is spending and taxes.

Global Trade and Development

The World Economy
Big questionWhy do countries trade, and why are some countries richer than others?
The story

One Phone, Many Countries

The phone in your pocket has traveled farther than most people do in a year.

Luis got a new phone for his birthday. On the back, in tiny letters, it said it was designed in one country and assembled in another. Luis bought it at a store in Chicago. He wondered how many countries had touched this one small thing before it reached his hand.

His older sister looked it up. The design and software work happened in one place. The chips came from factories in other countries. Some of the glass, the battery and the metals came from still other places. The parts met in a large factory, where workers put the phone together. Then ships and planes carried it across the ocean.

Luis asked why one country did not just make the whole thing. His sister had an answer. Each place does the part it does best, at the lowest cost for what it gives up. Trading lets everyone get more than they could make alone. But trade also brings hard questions about jobs, prices and fairness.

This chapter follows goods and money across borders. You will learn why countries trade, what tariffs do, and how one currency is traded for another. You will see why some countries grow richer. You will also see how Chicago became a place where the world's trade meets.

Section 1

Why Countries Trade

20.1

Imports and Exports

Main ideaAn export is something sold to another country; an import is something bought from another country.

An Illinois farmer grows soybeans. A buyer in another country purchases a shipload. For the United States, those soybeans are an export, a good or service sold to people in another country. Illinois is one of the top soybean-growing states, so soybeans are a big Illinois export.

Now think of a banana at a Chicago grocery. Bananas grow in warm places, so most of ours come from other countries. For the United States, that banana is an import, a good or service bought from another country. Services count too. When a tourist from abroad stays in a Chicago hotel, that is a U.S. service export.

Every import for one country is an export for another. Trade happens because people want goods they cannot make well at home, or cannot make as cheaply. The U.S. both imports and exports trillions of dollars of goods and services each year.

20.2

The Trade Balance

Main ideaThe trade balance is exports minus imports; a negative balance is a trade deficit.

Picture a small country that sells $50 billion of goods to the world and buys $70 billion (example numbers). Its trade balance is exports minus imports: $50 billion - $70 billion = -$20 billion. When the balance is negative, the country has a trade deficit. When it is positive, it has a trade surplus.

The United States has run a trade deficit for many years. That means Americans buy more from other countries than they sell to them. In GDP, this shows up as net exports, the NX in C + I + G + NX. A negative NX pulls measured GDP down a little.

A common mistake is to think a trade deficit is like losing a game. It is not that simple. Buyers still got goods they wanted in return for their money, and money spent abroad often comes back as investment. Economists disagree about when a deficit is a worry. Some point to lost factory jobs. Others say it reflects strong spending and foreign investment.

20.3

Comparative Advantage

Main ideaTwo countries both gain when each makes what it gives up the least to make, then trades.

Imagine two countries, Northland and Southland. In a day, a Northland worker can make 6 shirts or 3 radios. A Southland worker can make 2 shirts or 2 radios (example numbers). Northland is better at both. Does trade still help? Yes. Look at what each gives up.

In Northland, 1 radio costs 2 shirts, because making it means giving up 2 shirts. In Southland, 1 radio costs only 1 shirt. So Southland has the lower opportunity cost for radios. For shirts, Northland gives up half a radio per shirt, while Southland gives up a whole radio. Northland has the lower cost for shirts.

A comparative advantage means making a good at a lower opportunity cost than someone else. Northland should make shirts and Southland radios. If they trade at, say, 1.5 shirts for 1 radio, both get radios or shirts more cheaply than making them at home. A common mistake is to think only the country that is better at everything should make anything.

From the source
Adam Smith on Buying Instead of Making
It is the maxim of every prudent master of a family, never to attempt to make at home what it will cost him more to make than to buy.
Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, Book IV, Chapter 2, 1776

How does a family's choice to buy shoes instead of making them match the idea of comparative advantage?

Words to know
comparative advantage
being able to make a good at a lower opportunity cost than someone else
absolute advantage
being able to make more of a good than someone else with the same resources
opportunity cost
the next-best thing you give up when you make a choice
Check yourself

1. In one day, Ana can bake 10 pies or 5 cakes. What is her opportunity cost of 1 cake?

Why: 10 pies / 5 cakes = 2 pies given up for each cake.

2. Country A gives up 3 apples per orange. Country B gives up 1 apple per orange. Who has the comparative advantage in oranges?

Why: Country B gives up less (1 apple) for each orange.

3. Northland is better at making both shirts and radios. Can trade still help both countries?

Why: Gains come from comparative advantage, not absolute advantage.
Section 2

Tariffs and Money Across Borders

20.4

Tariffs and Who Pays

Main ideaA tariff is a tax on imports; much of its cost usually shows up in higher prices for buyers at home.

A bike made in another country sells in the U.S. for $200 (example price). The government puts a 25% tariff on imported bikes. A tariff is a tax on imports. It is paid to the U.S. government by the company that brings the bike in. That is $50 per bike.

Who really bears that cost? The importer may raise the price toward $250. Then American buyers pay more. Some of the cost may be absorbed by the foreign maker or the store. Studies of recent U.S. tariffs found that much of the cost was passed to U.S. buyers. A common mistake is to think the foreign country simply pays the tax.

Tariffs have trade-offs. They can protect jobs at American bike makers, who now face less competition. They can raise money for the government. But buyers pay higher prices, and other countries may answer with tariffs on U.S. exports, such as Illinois soybeans. Economists debate when, if ever, the protection is worth the cost.

Think it through
A Tariff on Steel

The U.S. puts a tariff on imported steel. A steel mill in Indiana hires 100 more workers. A Chicago company that makes car parts from steel now pays more for its steel.

20.5

Exchange Rates on a Trip

Main ideaAn exchange rate is the price of one country's money in another country's money.

Sofia’s family visits Mexico. In Mexico, prices are in pesos, not dollars. At the bank, suppose 1 U.S. dollar trades for 20 pesos (example rate). That number is the exchange rate, the price of one currency in terms of another. Currency is simply a country’s money.

Sofia sees a T-shirt for 300 pesos. How many dollars is that? Divide by the rate: 300 / 20 = $15. She changes $100 at the bank. She gets 100 x 20 = 2,000 pesos. Always ask which way the rate goes. Multiply dollars by 20 to get pesos. Divide pesos by 20 to get dollars.

Exchange rates change every day. Traders buy and sell currencies, so the price moves with supply and demand. If more people want dollars, the dollar’s price rises. That is why a trip can cost more one year and less the next, even when prices in the other country stay the same.

the kind of money used in a country, like the dollar, the peso or the euro
Check yourself

Why: 500 / 20 = $25.

Why: Currencies are traded like goods, so their prices move with supply and demand.
20.6

A Strong or a Weak Dollar

Main ideaWhen the dollar gets stronger, imports and trips abroad get cheaper for Americans, and U.S. exports cost more for others.

Say the rate moves from 1 dollar = 20 pesos to 1 dollar = 25 pesos (example rates). Each dollar now buys more pesos. The dollar has become stronger. Sofia’s 300-peso T-shirt now costs 300 / 25 = $12 instead of $15. Trips and imports from Mexico got cheaper for Americans.

Now flip it. A Mexican family wants to buy a U.S. product that costs $100. At 20 pesos per dollar, that is 2,000 pesos. At 25, it is 2,500 pesos. The strong dollar made U.S. goods more expensive for them. So a strong dollar tends to help U.S. shoppers and travelers but can hurt U.S. exporters.

A weaker dollar does the opposite. U.S. exports get cheaper for other countries, so farmers and factories may sell more abroad. But imports and foreign trips cost Americans more. A common mistake is to think a strong dollar is simply good. Whether it helps depends on whether you are buying from abroad or selling to it.

Think it through
Who Cheers?

The dollar gets much weaker against the euro. Three people read the news: an Illinois farmer who sells corn to Europe, a family planning a trip to Italy, and a store that sells German cars.

Who is happy about the weaker dollar and who is not? Explain for each.

Words to know
appreciation
when a currency becomes worth more compared with another currency
depreciation
when a currency becomes worth less compared with another currency
Check yourself

1. The rate goes from 1 dollar = 20 pesos to 1 dollar = 25 pesos. The dollar has:

Why: Each dollar buys more pesos, so it rose in value.

2. A weaker dollar most likely helps which group?

Why: A weaker dollar makes U.S. goods cheaper for foreign buyers.

3. At 1 dollar = 25 pesos (example rate), how many pesos does a $100 U.S. item cost?

Why: $100 x 25 = 2,500 pesos.

Supply Chains: One Phone

Main ideaA supply chain is every step and place that goods pass through, from raw materials to the buyer.

Follow a phone backward. Before the store, it rode in a truck. Before that, a ship or plane. Before that, a factory put it together. Before that, other factories made the chips, the screen and the battery. Before that, mines dug up metals. All these steps together make a supply chain.

Companies spread the steps across countries to lower costs and use special skills. One place may have expert chip makers. Another may have many trained assembly workers. Another may have the mines. Using each place for what it does best is comparative advantage at work. Linking the world’s economies this way is called globalization.

Long supply chains have a weak spot. If one step stops, everything waits. During the COVID-19 pandemic, closed factories and crowded ports delayed many goods, and some prices rose. Since then, some companies have moved steps closer to home or found more than one supplier. That can add safety but also add cost.

Think it through

Words to know
supply chain
all the steps, people and places that turn raw materials into a product and get it to the buyer
globalization
the growing links among the world's economies through trade, travel, money and ideas
Check yourself

1. Which is the best example of a supply chain step?

Why: A supply chain covers the steps that make and deliver the product.

2. Why do companies spread phone-making steps across many countries?

Why: Each step goes where it can be done at lower cost, an idea based on comparative advantage.

3. What is one risk of a long supply chain?

Why: Every step depends on the one before it.
20.8

Why Some Countries Are Richer

Main ideaCountries grow richer mostly by making more per worker, helped by education, health, good institutions and trade.

Picture two farms. On one, a family digs by hand and grows enough to eat. On the other, a trained farmer uses a tractor, good seed and weather data, and grows food for hundreds of people. The second farm has much higher productivity: more output per worker per hour.

Productivity is the main reason some countries are richer. It grows with human capital, the skills and health of workers, and with physical capital, like tools, machines and roads. It also grows with new technology. Economists often compare countries using GDP per person, which is GDP divided by population.

Institutions matter too. Institutions are the rules of the game: fair courts, protected property, honest government and stable money. When people trust that they can keep what they earn, they build and invest. Trade helps by opening bigger markets and new ideas. No single recipe fits every country, and economists still debate which steps matter most.

GDP per person

Check yourself

Why: $800,000,000,000 / 40,000,000 = $20,000.

2. A country spends more on schools and job training. Which part of growth does this build?

Why: Education builds workers' skills, which is human capital.

3. Why do fair courts and protected property help a country grow?

Why: Good institutions give people reasons to save, invest and start businesses.
20.9

Chicago: Where Trade Meets

Main ideaChicago grew as a trade hub because railroads, waterways, farms and markets all met there.

Look at a map of the Midwest. Chicago sits where the Great Lakes come close to rivers that lead to the Mississippi. In the 1800s, canals and then railroads linked the city to farms to the west and cities to the east. Grain, lumber and livestock poured in. Chicago grew fast.

In 1848, traders founded the Chicago Board of Trade. It became a place to buy and sell grain, and later contracts for grain to be delivered in the future. Farmers and buyers used it to set prices and manage risk. It is now part of CME Group, and its prices are watched around the world.

Today Chicago is still a crossroads. Many major railroads meet there, and O’Hare Airport handles large amounts of air cargo. Illinois farms send soybeans and corn abroad, and Illinois factories export machinery. When world trade rises or falls, Chicago feels it quickly.

20.10

The Economic Way of Thinking

Main ideaEconomists look at choices, costs, incentives and trade-offs to understand people, markets and nations.

You started this course with wants and needs. Now you can see the whole world economy. Through it all, a few ideas kept coming back. Resources are scarce, so every choice has an opportunity cost. People respond to incentives, like prices, wages and taxes.

Trade helps both sides when each does what it gives up the least to do. Markets use prices to share information. Governments set rules, collect taxes and provide some goods. The Fed tries to keep prices steady and jobs plentiful. Each step has trade-offs, and wise choices name them.

Try it on a real question. Should your town build a new park? Ask what it costs, what else that money could do, who gains, who pays, and how people’s behavior might change. That set of questions is the economic way of thinking. You can use it at the store, at work and when you vote.

From the source
David Ricardo on Free Trade
Under a system of perfectly free commerce, each country naturally devotes its capital and labour to such employments as are most beneficial to each.
David Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, Chapter 7, 1817

What does Ricardo think countries will do if trade is free? What might someone who disagrees say?

Words to know
incentive
something that encourages people to act in a certain way, like a reward or a cost
trade-off
giving up some of one thing to get more of another
Check yourself

1. A city spends $2 million on a park instead of fixing roads. What is the opportunity cost?

Why: Opportunity cost is the next-best use given up, here the road repairs.

2. A store puts snacks on sale and sells twice as many. Which idea does this show?

Why: A lower price is an incentive that changes behavior.

3. Which question best fits the economic way of thinking?

Why: Economic thinking weighs costs, benefits and trade-offs.
