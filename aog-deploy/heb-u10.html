Torah: Leviticus to Deuteronomy, and the Former Prophets

Torah and Nevi'im

The first two books of the Torah tell of a family and a rescue. The last three turn that rescued people into a community with a way of life. Leviticus sets out offerings, purity and holiness. Numbers counts the tribes and follows them through forty years in the wilderness. Deuteronomy gives Moses's last speeches, ending with his death within sight of the land. Jewish tradition reads these books as Vayikra, Bamidbar and Devarim, and draws from them the Shema, Yom Kippur, tzedakah and the 613 commandments.

Then the story crosses the Jordan. The books Jewish tradition calls the Former Prophets, Joshua, Judges, Samuel and Kings, tell one long history: the land taken and settled, the judges, the first kings, David's Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple, a kingdom split in two, prophets who confront kings, and two empires, Assyria and Babylon, that end it. The text judges every king by the standard Deuteronomy set.

In this unit you will read laws and stories side by side and see how each shapes the other. You will meet the commentators and the rabbis who have read these books for two thousand years, and the historians and archaeologists who set the text beside stones and clay from the ground. You will be able to explain what a passage says, how Jewish tradition has read it, and what the outside evidence adds.

The big question

How do law, story and history work together in the Torah's last books and the Former Prophets to explain who Israel is and why its kingdom fell?

The story

Five Sisters at the Tent Door

Their father died with no sons, and the law says his land will go to someone else. So the sisters go to Moses.

The camp in the wilderness is quiet in the early morning. Smoke rises from the altar in front of the Tent of Meeting. Moses stands near the entrance with Eleazar the priest and the leaders of the tribes. Then five women walk up together. The book of Numbers gives their names: Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah and Tirzah. They are the daughters of a man named Zelophehad, from the tribe of Manasseh.

Their father has died. He had no sons. Under the way land was passed down at the time, a family's share went from father to son. With no son, the share of Zelophehad would pass out of his family. The sisters have thought this through. They say their father was not part of the rebellion of Korah. He simply died, as people do. Why, they ask, should his name disappear from his family just because he had no son? Give us a holding of land among our father's brothers.

The text says Moses does not answer right away. He brings their case before God. And the answer, as Numbers 27 tells it, is that the daughters of Zelophehad speak rightly. They are to receive their father's share. Then the text goes further. It states a general rule for every family in Israel: if a man dies with no son, his inheritance passes to his daughter.

This small scene shows something about the last three books of the Torah. They are full of laws, but the laws are not handed down in a vacuum. They come out of stories: a census, a rebellion, a plague, a question from five sisters. The rabbis of the Talmud praised the daughters of Zelophehad as wise and as careful readers of the law. Modern readers see in the story a community working out what fairness means.

In this chapter you will read Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy the way Jewish tradition reads them: as *Vayikra*, *Bamidbar* and *Devarim*, three books that turn a rescued people into a community. You will meet offerings and the Day of Atonement, spies and a talking donkey, the Shema and the last words of Moses. Watch for how a story and a law sit side by side.

Section 1

Vayikra: Offerings and Holiness

19.1

The Book That Begins With a Call

Main ideaLeviticus, called Vayikra in Hebrew, is the Torah's book of priests, offerings and holiness, set at the Tent of Meeting.

The third book of the Torah opens with the words "And He called." In Hebrew that is . Jewish tradition names the book by that first word. In English it is called Leviticus, from the tribe of Levi. The priests who served at the Tent came from that tribe. Most of the book is God speaking to Moses at the . That was the portable sanctuary built at the end of Exodus.

Leviticus has little story. Instead it describes offerings brought to the altar: grain, oil, and animals such as sheep, goats, cattle and birds. The text names a purpose for each. Some give thanks. Some mark a festival. Some make up for a wrong done by mistake. The Hebrew word for an offering is , from a root meaning "to come near." Jewish tradition reads the offerings as a way of drawing near to God.

Modern readers ask what these chapters meant to the people who first heard them. Historians note that offerings were common across the ancient Near East. Leviticus gives its own order and meaning to the practice. The Temple in Jerusalem was destroyed in 70 CE. After that, the rabbis taught that prayer, study and acts of kindness take the place of offerings. Jewish communities still read Leviticus every year in the Torah cycle.

Young children in traditional Jewish schools once began their Torah study with Leviticus, not Genesis. A midrash explains: let the pure come and study what is pure. Whether or not a reader shares that view, the choice shows how central this book has been to Jewish life.

19.2

The Day of Atonement

Main ideaLeviticus 16 describes a yearly day when the high priest made atonement for the whole people; Jewish tradition keeps it as Yom Kippur.

Leviticus 16 sets out one day each year unlike any other. On the tenth day of the seventh month, the high priest was to enter the innermost room of the sanctuary, the Holy of Holies. He did this on no other day. He brought offerings for himself and for the people. The text calls this making , setting right what had gone wrong between the people and God.

The chapter also describes two goats. Lots were cast over them. One goat was offered at the altar. On the head of the other the high priest confessed the sins of the people, and then the goat was sent away into the wilderness. The Hebrew text says it was sent "to Azazel," a word whose meaning readers have long debated. The English word "scapegoat" comes from an early translation of this passage.

Jewish tradition keeps this day as , the Day of Atonement. Since there is no Temple, there are no offerings. Instead, Jews fast from evening to evening, pray, and confess wrongs together. The rabbis taught that Yom Kippur atones for wrongs against God, but wrongs against another person require first making peace with that person. The day comes ten days after Rosh Hashanah, the new year, and closes a season of , returning or repentance.

For many Jews in Chicago and across Illinois, Yom Kippur is the most widely kept day of the year. Even families who rarely attend a synagogue often fast on it. Reading Leviticus 16 shows where the day began. It also shows how far its practice has traveled from the Tent in the wilderness.

19.3

Be Holy: Leviticus 19

Main ideaLeviticus 19 ties holiness to everyday fairness, and includes the command to love your neighbor as yourself.

Chapters 17 to 26 of Leviticus have a shared theme, and scholars since the 1800s have called them the . The name comes from a line repeated through them: be holy, because God is holy. Chapter 19 is the heart of it. It moves quickly from one command to the next, and many of them are about ordinary life, not the altar.

The chapter says to leave the corners of a field unharvested so the poor and the stranger can gather food. It says not to hold back a worker’s wages overnight. It says not to curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind. It says to use honest weights and measures. Then, in verse 18, it says: love your neighbor as yourself. Rabbi Akiva, a sage of the second century CE, is recorded in the midrash as calling this a great principle of the Torah.

Readers have asked who counts as a neighbor. In the chapter itself, the same command appears again in verse 34 about the stranger who lives among you. Love him as yourself, it says, because you were strangers in Egypt. Jewish commentators such as Ramban read verse 18 as a call to want good for another person as you want it for yourself. Christian readers know the verse from the Gospels, where it is quoted alongside the Shema.

The word holy, in Hebrew , means set apart. Leviticus 19 connects being set apart with how a person treats a worker, a stranger and a neighbor. That link between holiness and fairness matters to readers. It is one reason some communities read this chapter in the synagogue on the afternoon of Yom Kippur.

Section 2

Bamidbar: Forty Years in the Wilderness

19.4

Counting the Camp

Main ideaNumbers, called Bamidbar, opens with a census of the tribes and a plan for the camp around the Tent of Meeting.

The fourth book of the Torah is called in Hebrew, "in the wilderness," from its opening line. Its English name, Numbers, comes from the counting that fills its first chapters. The text says the count took place in the second year after leaving Egypt. Moses and Aaron counted every man of fighting age, tribe by tribe. The total given is 603,550. The were not included; they were counted separately for service at the Tent.

The book then arranges the camp. The Tent of Meeting stands in the middle. The Levites camp closest around it. The other twelve tribes camp in four groups of three, one group on each side. When the cloud that the text says rested on the Tent lifted, the people packed up and marched in that same order. The picture is of a whole people moving together around a center.

Historians read these numbers with care. A census of over 600,000 men would mean a nation of two million or more. By most estimates that is far larger than the whole population of the region at the time. Some scholars suggest the Hebrew word for "thousand" may once have meant a unit or clan. Jewish tradition has often read the count another way: each person mattered enough to be counted by name and tribe.

Numbers also contains the blessing the priests were to say over the people, in chapter 6. Jewish families still say these words over their children on Friday evening. It is also one of the oldest passages of the Torah found on any object. Two tiny silver scrolls from Jerusalem carry it. They are dated to around the 600s BCE.

19.5

Twelve Spies and Forty Years

Main ideaNumbers 13–14 tells how the people refused to enter the land after the spies' report, and the text says a generation wandered forty years as a result.

Numbers 13 says Moses sent twelve men, one from each tribe, to scout the land of Canaan. They were gone forty days. They came back with a cluster of grapes so large that two men carried it on a pole. The land, they agreed, was good. But ten of the spies said its cities were walled and its people were giants. "We were in our own sight as grasshoppers," they said. Only two, Caleb and Joshua, urged the people to go up.

The people wept all night. They talked of choosing a new leader and going back to Egypt. The text says God’s response was that this generation would not enter the land. They would wander in the wilderness forty years, one year for each day the spies had scouted. Their children would go in instead. Of the adults who left Egypt, only Caleb and Joshua would live to enter.

Jewish tradition remembers this night. The Talmud connects it with the . On that date, tradition holds, both Temples were later destroyed, and it became a day of mourning. The midrash turns the spies’ words over. They were not lying about the walls, the rabbis say, but they lost trust. Modern readers note that the story explains two things: why the wilderness period is forty years, and why Moses’s generation does not enter the land.

The story also shows a pattern in Numbers. The people complain, about water, about food, about leaders. Moses is worn down. In one scene he asks God to let him die rather than carry the people alone. The book does not hide the strain of leading.

19.6

Balaam and the Talking Donkey

Main ideaIn Numbers 22–24 a foreign seer hired to curse Israel blesses it instead, and his donkey sees what he cannot.

As the people camped near Moab, its king, Balak, grew afraid. Numbers 22 says he sent for , a seer from far away who was known for powerful blessings and curses. Balak wanted Israel cursed. Balaam, the text says, was told by God not to curse them, but after more messengers came, he saddled his donkey and set out anyway.

On the road the donkey stopped three times. It saw an angel with a drawn sword blocking the way. Balaam, who saw nothing, struck the animal each time. Then the text says the LORD opened the donkey’s mouth, and it asked Balaam why he had beaten it. Only then did Balaam’s eyes open to see the angel. The famous seer had been blind to what a donkey could see.

Balaam went on to Balak. But each time he opened his mouth to curse, blessing came out instead. One of his lines, "How goodly are thy tents, O Jacob," is sung in synagogues today at the start of morning prayers. Jewish tradition treats Balaam with suspicion because he wanted to curse. Yet it kept his words. The midrash lists the donkey’s mouth among things created at the very end of the six days of creation.

In 1967, archaeologists at Deir Alla in Jordan found plaster fragments with an ink text. It is dated to around the 800s BCE. It mentions a seer named Balaam son of Beor. He is one of the few figures in the Torah named in a text found outside the Bible. Historians debate what the inscription shows. But it makes clear that Balaam was a known name in the region.

19.7

The Daughters of Zelophehad

Main ideaNumbers 27 records five sisters asking for their father's land and the law changing in response; the rabbis praised them as wise.

The story that opened this chapter comes in Numbers 27. Zelophehad of the tribe of Manasseh died with five daughters and no sons. Under the rules of the time, a family’s land passed to sons. The daughters, Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah and Tirzah, came to the Tent of Meeting and put their case to Moses, Eleazar the priest and the leaders. They asked for a holding among their father’s brothers.

Moses, the text says, brought the case before God. The answer was that the daughters spoke rightly, and a general rule followed. If a man dies with no son, his inheritance passes to his daughter. If he has no daughter, it passes to his brothers, then to his father’s brothers, then to the nearest relative. The story is one of a few places where the Torah shows a law being made because someone asked.

The story has a second part in Numbers 36. Leaders of Manasseh worried that if the daughters married men from other tribes, the land would leave the tribe. A further rule was given: in such a case, the daughters should marry within their tribe. The text says they did. Readers have noted that the sisters gained land, but within limits set by the tribal system of the time.

The rabbis of the Talmud call the daughters of Zelophehad wise, skilled at interpreting, and righteous. Rashi, the medieval commentator, notes when they came forward. It was a moment when others wanted to go back to Egypt, and the sisters asked for land instead. Modern Jewish readers, including many women scholars, return to this story. They use it when they discuss how the tradition has changed and can change.

Devarim: The Words of Moses

19.8

Moses Speaks and the Shema

Main ideaDeuteronomy is framed as Moses's speeches before his death, and its sixth chapter holds the Shema, the central line of Jewish prayer.

The fifth book of the Torah is called , "words," from its opening: "These are the words which Moses spoke." The English name Deuteronomy means "second law," because the book retells much of what came before. The setting is the plains of Moab, across the Jordan from the land. The people are the children of those who left Egypt. Moses, now old, speaks to them in a series of long addresses.

The best-known passage comes in chapter 6. It begins "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one," and goes on: love the LORD your God with all your heart, soul and might. Jewish tradition calls this the , from its first word, "hear." Jews say it morning and evening. Many say it as the last words at night and, tradition holds, at the end of life.

The passage also says to teach these words to your children. It says to bind them as a sign on your hand and between your eyes. It says to write them on the doorposts of your house. Rabbinic tradition reads these lines as the source of two objects. Tefillin are small boxes with Torah passages, worn at weekday morning prayer. A is a small case on the doorpost of a Jewish home. A visitor to many homes in Chicago’s Jewish neighborhoods will see a mezuzah beside the door.

Scholars note that Deuteronomy’s style is different from the earlier books. It speaks in the second person. It repeats key phrases. It argues with the listener. Many scholars date the core of the book to the 600s BCE. They tie it to a scroll found in the Temple under King Josiah, a story told in the next chapter. Jewish tradition holds that Moses spoke the book. Both readings agree that it is a book of persuasion.

19.9

The Stranger, the Poor and the Field

Main ideaDeuteronomy commands care for the stranger, the widow, the orphan and the poor, grounding it in the memory of Egypt; Jewish tradition calls such giving tzedakah.

Deuteronomy returns again and again to four groups: the stranger, the orphan, the widow and the Levite, who had no land of his own. The book says to leave forgotten sheaves, fallen olives and leftover grapes for them. It says to bring a tithe, a tenth of the harvest, every third year for them to eat. It says to lend to a poor neighbor and, every seventh year, to release the debt.

The reason the book gives is memory. Deuteronomy 10:19 says to love the stranger, because you were strangers in the land of Egypt. Deuteronomy 24 repeats it: remember that you were a slave in Egypt. The command to care for the outsider is tied to the people’s own story. Scholars count the stranger, in Hebrew , as one of the most repeated concerns in the book.

Jewish tradition calls giving to those in need . The word comes from tzedek, justice, not from a word for charity or pity. In that reading, giving is not a favor but something owed. Deuteronomy 16:20 says "Justice, justice shalt thou follow." Rabbinic readers asked why the word is doubled and gave many answers, including that the means must be just as well as the goal.

A tzedakah box sits in many Jewish homes and classrooms today. Giving before Shabbat and before holidays is a common practice. Jewish federations in Chicago and across the country run food and housing programs. Their leaders often describe that work in these terms.

19.10

Blessings, Curses and a Mountain Grave

Main ideaDeuteronomy ends with a choice between blessing and curse, a song, and the death of Moses within sight of the land he does not enter.

Near its end, Deuteronomy sets out two paths. Chapter 27 has the tribes stand on two mountains, Gerizim and Ebal, with the Levites calling out curses on those who break the law. Chapter 28 lists blessings for keeping the covenant and long curses for breaking it: drought, defeat, exile. Chapter 30 sums it up: I have set before you life and death, the blessing and the curse; choose life.

The book then turns to Moses himself. He is one hundred and twenty years old. He hands leadership to Joshua. He teaches the people a long song. He blesses each tribe. 19.11

Counting the Mitzvot

Main ideaRabbinic tradition counts 613 commandments in the Torah, and Jewish thinkers have organized and explained them in many ways.

Read through Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy and you will meet hundreds of commands. Jewish tradition calls each one a , a commandment; the plural is mitzvot. The Talmud records a teaching of Rabbi Simlai, from the 200s CE, that the Torah holds of them. There are 365 "do not" commands, one for each day of the solar year. There are 248 "do" commands, said to match the parts of the body as the rabbis counted them.

The number 613 is a teaching, not a list in the Torah itself. Scholars over the centuries did not agree on exactly which commands to count. In the 1100s wrote a Book of the Commandments that set out his list of 613 with reasons. Other rabbis made different lists. Many of the 613 concern the Temple and its offerings. They cannot be kept today, since the Temple no longer stands.

Traditional Jews often speak of mitzvot in daily life: lighting Shabbat candles, giving tzedakah, honoring parents, saying a blessing over food. In everyday speech a mitzvah can also mean a good deed. Jewish thinkers have asked why the commands exist. Maimonides argued that each has a reason a person can understand. Others held that some are kept simply because the Torah gives them.

Rabbi Simlai’s teaching in the Talmud goes on. Later prophets, he says, summed up the commands in fewer words. David gave eleven principles, Isaiah six, Micah three, and Habakkuk one. The point, as the rabbis read it, is that the many commands share a core.

Main ideaJewish tradition reads the Torah's laws as halakhah, a way of walking through life, and reads the whole Torah aloud in a yearly cycle.

Historians and literary readers approach these laws differently, comparing them with other ancient law collections and asking how they took shape. Jewish tradition asks how to live them. This course holds both questions side by side: what the text says, and what it has meant to the people who have kept reading it.

20.1

Four Books, One Story

Main ideaJoshua, Judges, Samuel and Kings read as one connected history, and many scholars think they were shaped by editors who measured every king against Deuteronomy.

The Tanakh’s second part, Nevi’im, the Prophets, opens with four books that read like one story: Joshua, Judges, Samuel and Kings. Jewish tradition calls them the . They begin just after Moses’s death. They end with Jerusalem in ruins and its king a prisoner in Babylon. In Jewish counting, Samuel is one book and Kings is one book. So the Former Prophets are four books, not six.

Read together, the books share a way of judging. A king who keeps the covenant and worships only in Jerusalem is called good; one who sets up other altars is called evil. The good and bad outcomes match the blessings and curses of Deuteronomy. In 1943 the German scholar Martin Noth argued that one editor or school, working during or after the exile, had shaped these books into a single work. He called it the .

Not every scholar agrees on the details, but the name stuck. Historians read the Former Prophets as a work with a point of view. Its writers had seen the kingdom fall and wanted to explain why. Jewish tradition reads the same books as prophecy. Following a passage in the Talmud, it attributes Joshua to Joshua, Judges and Samuel to the prophet Samuel, and Kings to Jeremiah.

Both readings agree on something important: these books are not neutral records. They select, they judge, and they teach. This chapter reads them as story and as history, and sets them beside evidence found in the ground.

20.2

Crossing In: Text and Spade

Main ideaThe book of Joshua tells of a swift conquest, while archaeology points to a slower settlement of small villages; readers hold the two accounts side by side.

The book of Joshua tells of the people crossing the Jordan on dry ground, marching around Jericho until its walls fall, and taking city after city in a few campaigns. Chapters 13 to 21 then divide the land among the tribes. The book’s own last chapters say the work was not finished; Judges 1 lists cities the tribes did not take. So even within the text, the picture is mixed.

Archaeologists have dug at many of the sites Joshua names. At Jericho, excavations in the 1950s led by Kathleen Kenyon found that the city’s great walls had fallen centuries before the time most readers assign to Joshua, and the site was small or empty in the period usually proposed. Hazor, by contrast, shows a large destruction in the 1200s BCE. Some cities show no trace of attack at all.

What the ground does show is a wave of new small villages in the central hill country around 1200 BCE. They have simple houses, storage pits and few pig bones. Many historians read these as the first Israelite settlements. The Merneptah Stele from Egypt, dated to about 1208 BCE, names "Israel" as a people in Canaan, the oldest mention of the name outside the Bible.

How to hold the text and the evidence together is a real debate. Some readers see a memory of conquest reshaped over time; others see a story written to teach later generations; traditional Jewish readers hold to the account as given. This course does not decide the question. It asks you to know what the text says, what the ground shows, and how different readers put them together.

Main ideaThe book of Judges tells a repeating cycle of turning away, oppression, crying out and rescue, and ends by saying there was no king.

The book of Judges covers the time between Joshua and the first king. Its leaders, the , were not judges in a courtroom. They were rescuers who rose up in a crisis, led for a time, and died. The book gives the pattern in chapter 2: the people turn to other gods, an enemy oppresses them, they cry out, God raises a deliverer, the land has rest, and then it all begins again.

The stories are vivid. Deborah, a prophetess, directs Barak in battle and sings a victory song that scholars count among the oldest poems in the Tanakh. Jewish tradition reads the judges as flawed people through whom, the text says, God still acted. The rabbis discussed Jephthah’s vow at length as a case of a promise that should not have been kept. The story of Deborah is read as the , the prophetic reading, alongside the Song at the Sea in Exodus.

Main ideaFirst Samuel tells how the people asked the prophet Samuel for a king, and how Saul, the first king, rose and fell.

Prophets and Empires

20.8

Elijah and Elisha

Main ideaElijah and Elisha, prophets in the northern kingdom, confront kings and the worship of Baal, and Elijah lives on in Jewish practice as the guest who will return.

Elijah appears in 1 Kings 17 without introduction, telling King Ahab there will be no rain. Ahab’s queen, Jezebel, had brought the worship of , a Canaanite storm god, into the royal court. On Mount Carmel, the text says, Elijah challenged Baal’s prophets to a contest: two altars, and the god who answered with fire would be God. The people, watching, cried out that the LORD is God.

Then Elijah fled from Jezebel into the wilderness and came to Horeb, the mountain of Moses. First Kings 19 says a great wind, an earthquake and a fire passed by. But the LORD was not in them. After the fire came "a still small voice." Readers have long asked what that means. Some take it as a lesson that God is found in quiet rather than in spectacle. Others read it as a rebuke to a prophet who wanted fire.

Elijah did not die, the text says. He was taken up in a whirlwind with a chariot of fire, and his cloak fell to his student . Elisha’s stories fill 2 Kings 2 to 13. He feeds a hundred men with twenty loaves. He heals the foreign general Naaman of a skin disease by having him wash in the Jordan. He advises kings in war. Where Elijah was fierce and alone, Elisha moved among ordinary people.

Because Elijah did not die, Jewish tradition holds that he will return before the final redemption, following the last verses of the prophet Malachi. A cup of wine is poured for Elijah at the Passover seder and the door is opened for him. A chair is set for him at every brit milah, a boy’s circumcision. Christian tradition also honors Elijah, and the Gospels ask whether John the Baptist was Elijah come again.

20.9

Assyria: One Kingdom Falls, One Survives

Main ideaThe Assyrian empire destroyed the northern kingdom in 722 BCE and besieged Jerusalem in 701 BCE, and the book of Kings and Assyrian records both tell of it.

In the 700s BCE the empire of , based in what is now northern Iraq, spread west with a large army. Its kings demanded tribute, and when a small kingdom rebelled, they crushed it and deported its people. The northern kingdom of Israel rebelled. Second Kings 17 says the Assyrian king besieged Samaria for three years and took it, and carried Israel away to Assyria. Historians date the fall to 722 BCE.

The book of Kings pauses to explain why. In a long passage it says the north fell because its people worshiped other gods and would not listen to the prophets. The deported Israelites were settled far away and did not return as a people; later legends called them the ten lost tribes. Assyria moved other peoples into Samaria, and the text says a mixed population grew there, the ancestors, some hold, of the Samaritans.

Judah survived, but barely. Its king rebelled too. In 701 BCE the Assyrian king Sennacherib marched on Judah, took its fortified towns, and surrounded Jerusalem. Second Kings 18 and 19 tell the story. An Assyrian officer shouts threats at the wall in Hebrew. Hezekiah prays in the Temple. The prophet Isaiah promises the city will stand. Then the Assyrian army leaves after, the text says, a great loss in one night.

Sennacherib’s own account survives on clay prisms. It boasts of taking forty-six of Hezekiah’s walled towns and shutting him up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage." It does not claim to have taken the city. Both sources agree that Jerusalem was not captured; they differ on why. Hezekiah’s tunnel, cut through rock to bring water inside the walls, can still be walked in Jerusalem today.

20.10

Josiah and the Found Scroll

Main ideaSecond Kings 22–23 tells how a scroll found in the Temple led King Josiah to reform worship, and scholars connect that scroll with Deuteronomy.

The story that opened this chapter comes in 2 Kings 22. Josiah became king of Judah as a boy of eight, after his father was killed. In his eighteenth year, about 622 BCE, workers repairing the Temple found a scroll. The high priest Hilkiah gave it to the scribe Shaphan, who read it to the king. Josiah tore his clothes and sent to the prophetess , who confirmed that its warnings were real.

Chapter 23 lists what Josiah did next. He read the scroll to all the people and renewed the covenant. He removed the objects of other gods from the Temple. He shut down the shrines outside Jerusalem, so that worship would happen in one place. He destroyed the altar at Bethel that Jeroboam had built. And he kept a Passover, the text says, greater than any since the days of the judges.

Readers noticed long ago that these acts match the demands of Deuteronomy: one place of worship, no other gods, a Passover kept by all. In 1805 the German scholar W. M. L. de Wette argued that the found scroll was an early form of Deuteronomy, and that the book took its shape around Josiah’s time. Many historians today accept some version of this. Jewish tradition holds that the scroll was the Torah of Moses, lost or hidden during the reigns of bad kings.

The book of Kings praises Josiah more highly than any other king. Yet the story ends in grief. In 609 BCE Josiah went out to meet Pharaoh Neco at Megiddo and was killed. Within a generation Judah fell. The text does not soften the blow: the best king could not undo what earlier kings had done.

Section 4

The Fall and the Evidence

20.11

Babylon Burns Jerusalem

Main ideaSecond Kings ends with Babylon capturing Jerusalem, burning the Temple in 586 BCE and deporting Judah, and Jewish tradition mourns it each year on the ninth of Av.

Assyria fell to a new power, , at the end of the 600s BCE. Judah was caught between Babylon and Egypt. Second Kings 24 says the Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem in 597 BCE, carried off its young king Jehoiachin with the Temple’s treasures and thousands of its leading people, and set Zedekiah on the throne. A Babylonian clay tablet, the Babylonian Chronicle, records the capture of the city in that same year.

Zedekiah rebelled. Nebuchadnezzar’s army came back and besieged Jerusalem for about a year and a half. Second Kings 25 tells of famine inside the walls, the wall broken through, the king caught as he fled, his sons killed before him and his eyes put out. Then a Babylonian officer burned the house of the LORD, the palace and every great house, broke down the walls, and deported most of those who were left. Historians date the destruction to 586 BCE.

The book of Kings does not end there. Its last four verses jump ahead to the thirty-seventh year of Jehoiachin’s exile, when a new Babylonian king freed him from prison and gave him a seat at the royal table. Readers argue about this quiet ending. Some see a small sign of hope for David’s line; others see only a record. The story continues in other books: Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Ezra and Nehemiah.

Jewish tradition holds that both Temples fell on the ninth of Av: the First to Babylon, and the Second to Rome in 70 CE. On that day, Tisha B&#x27;Av, Jews fast, sit on the floor, and read the book of Lamentations, whose poems grieve the burned city. The fall of Jerusalem in 586 BCE is one of the fixed points of the Tanakh’s history. Everything after it is shaped by exile and return.

2 Kings 25:8–12, paraphrased from the Jewish Publication Society translation, 1917 (paraphrased)

20.12

Stones That Speak: Outside Evidence

Main ideaInscriptions such as the Tel Dan stele and Sennacherib's prism name people and events from the book of Kings, and historians use them to test and date the story.

In 1993 and 1994, excavators at Tel Dan in northern Israel found pieces of a broken stone slab. It carries an in Aramaic. A king of Aram set it up, probably in the 800s BCE. It boasts of victories over the king of Israel and the king of the "House of David." It is the earliest known mention of David outside the Bible. Scholars debate how to read some damaged lines, but most accept the phrase.

Sennacherib’s account of his 701 BCE campaign survives on several six-sided clay prisms written in cuneiform. One is in the British Museum in London. Another is in Chicago, at the University of Chicago’s museum of the ancient Near East, which acquired it around 1920. Students in Illinois can stand in front of an object that names Hezekiah, king of Judah, in the words of the man who besieged him.

Other objects fill in the picture. The Mesha Stele from Moab, from around 840 BCE, names King Omri of Israel. Assyrian reliefs in the British Museum show the siege of the Judean city of Lachish, which 2 Kings mentions in one line. The Siloam inscription, found in Hezekiah’s tunnel, describes the moment two teams of diggers met underground. The Babylonian Chronicle dates the capture of Jerusalem in 597 BCE.

None of these objects tells the whole story. Each was made to serve its maker: a king’s boast, a victory list, a workers’ note. Historians read them the way they read the book of Kings. They ask who wrote this, for whom, and why. Together the text and the stones place the Former Prophets in a real world of kings, sieges and clay. They do not settle every question the text raises.
