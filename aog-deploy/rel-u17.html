Islam: The Qur'an and the Five Pillars

Traditions

About one in four people on earth is Muslim, by common estimates close to two billion people, from Indonesia and Pakistan to Nigeria, Turkey and the neighborhoods of Chicago. Many of them begin each day with a prayer said before sunrise, facing a small stone building in a valley in western Arabia. This unit asks where that practice comes from and what it means to the people who keep it.

You will start in seventh-century Mecca and Medina, with the life of Muhammad as Muslims understand it and the book Muslims call the Qur'an, God's word recited in Arabic. You will study the Five Pillars that shape a Muslim's day, year and life. Then you will follow the tradition outward: the Prophet's example in the hadith, the law scholars built from it, the split between Sunni and Shia, the inner path of the Sufis, and a golden age of learning that stretched from Baghdad to Córdoba to Timbuktu.

By the end you will be able to explain the core beliefs and practices of Islam in its own terms, read a passage of the Qur'an or a hadith and say what its author and audience are doing, describe the diversity among Muslims without flattening it, and write about Islam the way a scholar of religion does: attributing each belief, comparing without ranking, and citing the sources.

The big question

How do a book, a Prophet's example and five simple practices shape the lives of nearly two billion people, and why do Muslims live them out in so many different ways?

The story

Ramadan in Room 214

For one month each year, a Chicago high school runs on two clocks at once: the bell schedule, and the sun.

It is the second week of Ramadan, and the lunchroom at a high school on Chicago's Northwest Side is louder than usual, because about thirty students are not in it. They are in Room 214, a science lab that a teacher opened at lunch so that fasting students would have somewhere to sit that did not smell like pizza. Some are doing homework. Two are asleep with their heads on their arms. A junior named Amina is explaining to a friend who is not Muslim why she woke up at 4:15 that morning to eat.

The friend has questions, and Amina does not mind them. No, not even water, from the first light before dawn until the sun goes down. Yes, every day for about a month. No, not little kids, not people who are sick, not travelers, not women who are pregnant or nursing; the Qur'an itself excuses the sick and travelers, and scholars extended that mercy to others. The point, she says, is not to suffer. The point is to feel what hunger is, to remember people who feel it every day, and to spend a month paying closer attention to God.

The gym teacher has quietly stopped requiring the mile run during Ramadan. The lunch ladies, who saw the empty tables the first day, now pack extra fruit into bags that the fasting students take home for later. At 7:40 in the evening, in a dozen kitchens across the neighborhood, the same thing happens: someone checks a phone app or a printed schedule, hears the call to sunset prayer, and breaks the fast with a date and a glass of water, the way Muslims believe the Prophet Muhammad did.

On the last Friday of the month, the school's Muslim Student Association hosts an iftar, the evening meal that ends the fast, in the cafeteria. Two hundred people come, including the principal, the gym teacher and Amina's friend. Before anyone eats, a senior stands and recites a few verses of the Qur'an in Arabic, in a slow, rising chant. Almost no one in the room speaks Arabic, but everyone is quiet. Then the trays come out, and it is, after all, a high school cafeteria.

A month like this is a good place to start studying Islam, because so much of the tradition is visible in it: a text recited aloud, a practice timed by the sun, a community that eats together, and a set of rules that bend for the sick and the young. This chapter follows those threads back to their sources: the Arabia of the seventh century, the man Muslims call the Prophet, the book they call the Qur'an, and the five practices, the pillars, that hold the whole structure up.

Arabia and the Messenger

33.1

Arabia Before Islam

Main ideaSeventh-century Arabia was a land of trading towns and tribes, with many gods, a famous shrine at Mecca, and neighbors who were Jewish and Christian.

Picture Mecca around the year 600. It sits in a dry valley in western Arabia, on a caravan route that carries spices, leather and cloth between Yemen in the south and Syria in the north. In the middle of town stands a cube-shaped stone building called the . Arabs from across the peninsula travel to it each year, and during the pilgrimage months fighting stops so that they can come safely. Inside and around the Kaaba, historians say, stood images of many tribal gods.

Most Arabs at this time were organized by , a large group of related families that protected its members and avenged them when they were wronged. There was no king and no central government. Loyalty, generosity and courage were prized; poetry was the great art, recited from memory at fairs and markets. Muslims later called this period the , a word usually translated as the age of ignorance, meaning the time before the Qur’an’s guidance arrived.

Arabia was not cut off from the wider world. Jewish tribes had lived in the oasis town of Yathrib, later called Medina, for centuries. Christian communities existed in Yemen, in the north near Syria and among some Arab tribes. The two great empires of the day, the Christian Byzantine Empire and the Zoroastrian Persian Empire, fought long wars just beyond Arabia’s borders. Stories of Abraham, Moses and Jesus were known in Mecca before Islam began.

Islamic tradition also remembers a few Meccans called who rejected the tribal gods and worshiped one God without belonging to a Jewish or Christian community. Muslims believe that the Kaaba itself was first built by Abraham and his son Ishmael as a house for the one God, and that Meccans had forgotten this over time. Whether or not a student accepts that account, it explains why the Kaaba matters so much in the rest of this story.

33.2

The First Revelation

Main ideaMuslims believe that in about 610 the angel Gabriel brought Muhammad the first words of the Qur'an in a cave near Mecca, beginning twenty-three years of revelation.

Islamic tradition tells the story this way. was born in Mecca around 570 into the Quraysh tribe. His father died before his birth and his mother when he was about six, so he was raised by his grandfather and then his uncle, Abu Talib. He worked in the caravan trade and earned a reputation for honesty; people called him al-Amin, the trustworthy. At about twenty-five he married Khadija, a wealthy merchant widow who employed him, and they had several children.

Muhammad had a habit of going alone to a cave on Mount Hira, outside Mecca, to think and pray. Muslims believe that one night in about 610, when he was about forty, the angel (Jibril in Arabic) appeared to him and commanded, Recite. Muhammad answered that he could not recite. The command came again, and then the first words of what became the Qur’an: verses that speak of a Lord who created humans and taught them by the pen. Muslims call this night Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power.

According to the tradition, Muhammad came home shaking and asked Khadija to cover him. She comforted him, and she became the first person to believe that the message was from God. Over the next twenty-three years, Muslims hold, Gabriel brought further in pieces, sometimes a few verses at a time, until Muhammad’s death in 632. Muhammad recited each passage to his followers, who memorized it and wrote parts down on whatever was at hand.

Muslims do not believe that Muhammad wrote the Qur’an or that he was divine. They believe he was a , a human messenger chosen to deliver God’s words, in the same line as Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus, and that he was the last of them. That is why Muslims often add the words peace be upon him after his name, and why a student of religion writes Muslims believe when describing these events, rather than stating them as settled history.

33.3

Mecca, Medina and the Hijra

Main ideaRejected in Mecca, Muhammad and his followers moved to Medina in 622, the hijra that starts the Muslim calendar, and built the first Muslim community there.

For about a dozen years Muhammad preached in Mecca. His message, that there is one God, that the idols at the Kaaba are false, that the rich must care for the poor and that everyone will be judged, won a small following: family members, some young people, some enslaved people and poor Meccans. Mecca’s leading families opposed him. The shrine and its pilgrims were their business, and a preacher attacking the tribal gods threatened both their beliefs and their income. Some early Muslims were beaten; a few were killed.

In 622, after Khadija and Abu Talib had died and the danger had grown, Muhammad accepted an invitation from the town of Yathrib, about 200 miles north, where feuding tribes wanted an outside judge. He and his followers moved there in small groups. This move is the , meaning migration. It matters so much that the Islamic calendar counts years from it: the year 622 CE is year 1 AH, from the Latin for in the year of the hijra. Yathrib became known as Medina, from Madinat al-Nabi, the city of the Prophet.

In Muhammad was no longer only a preacher but the leader of a community, the , the body of all Muslims. He built the first mosque, set up rules for how Muslims, Jews and others in the town would live together, and led the community through several battles with Mecca, including Badr in 624 and Uhud in 625. The revelations from these years, Muslims note, deal more often with law, family and community than the shorter, urgent Meccan passages.

In 630 Muhammad returned to Mecca at the head of a large force, and the city surrendered with little fighting. According to tradition he cleared the Kaaba of its idols and rededicated it to the one God, while granting a general pardon to most of his former enemies. He died in Medina in 632 and is buried there. By then most of Arabia’s tribes had accepted Islam. The question of who would lead the umma after him is taken up in the next chapter.

The Book

33.4

How the Qur'an Is Arranged

Main ideaThe Qur'an has 114 suras arranged roughly from longest to shortest, not in the order revealed, and Muslims believe its Arabic text is God's own speech.

Open a printed Qur’an and the first thing you meet is not the first revelation from Mount Hira. It is a short prayer of seven verses called al-Fatiha, the Opening. After it comes the longest chapter in the book, and the chapters then generally grow shorter until the last ones are only a few lines. Each chapter is a , and each verse is an , a word that also means a sign. There are 114 suras and, by the usual count, 6,236 ayahs.

The order is not chronological. Muslims believe the passages were revealed over twenty-three years, and scholars sort suras into ones, usually short and intense, warning of judgment and calling people to the one God, and ones, usually longer, with laws about marriage, inheritance, contracts and the community. A single sura can contain verses from both periods. Each sura has a name taken from a word in it: the Cow, the Bee, the Light, the Night.

For daily reading, the Qur’an is also divided into thirty roughly equal parts called juz&#x27;. A person who reads one juz’ each night finishes the whole book in a month, which is exactly what many Muslims do during Ramadan. Muslims believe the text was fixed in writing under the third caliph, Uthman, in the 650s, when official copies were sent to the main cities. The Arabic text used today is, Muslims hold, unchanged since then.

Two features surprise students who expect a book like the Bible. First, the Qur’an is mostly not a narrative; it speaks directly, often as God addressing the listener, and returns again and again to the same themes. Second, it assumes you already know the stories of Adam, Noah, Abraham, Joseph, Moses and Jesus, and refers to them briefly to make a point. The Qur’an calls Jews and Christians People of the Book, communities that also received scriptures.

33.5

Recitation and Translation

Main ideaMuslims experience the Qur'an first as sound recited in Arabic; translations are considered interpretations of its meaning, not the Qur'an itself.

The word Qur&#x27;an means recitation. Before it was a book it was a sound, and for most Muslims it still is. Children learn to recite suras long before they can read them, and a person who has memorized the whole text is honored with the title , meaning guardian. There are set styles of melodic recitation called tajwid, with rules for how long each vowel is held and where to breathe. Famous reciters have followings the way singers do, and recitation contests draw thousands of listeners.

This is why the Arabic matters so much. Muslims believe the Qur’an is God’s speech in the very words revealed to Muhammad, so the Arabic text is the Qur’an and a translation is not. An English version is usually titled something like The Meaning of the Glorious Qur’an to make this point. Muslims who do not speak Arabic, and that is most of the world’s Muslims, still pray in Arabic and often study the original alongside a translation in their own language.

For a student in a public school, translations are what we have, and it helps to know a little about them. The first English versions were made by non-Muslims, and some were hostile. J. M. Rodwell’s translation (1861) and E. H. Palmer’s (1880) are now in the public domain and were used by scholars for decades. Twentieth-century translations by Muslims, such as Marmaduke Pickthall’s (1930) and Abdullah Yusuf Ali’s (1934), are widely read today, along with many newer versions. Rodwell even rearranged the suras into what he thought was their historical order.

Comparing two translations of one verse is a good habit for this course, as unit 13 taught. A single Arabic word may be rendered God or Allah, the Merciful or the Compassionate, recite or read. None of these choices is neutral. When this course quotes the Qur’an in paraphrase, it is offering one careful reading of the meaning, and a Muslim reader would remind you that the real thing is the Arabic.

33.6

Tawhid: The Oneness of God

Main ideaTawhid, the absolute oneness of God, is the center of Islamic belief, and it shapes what Muslims say about prophets, scripture, angels and judgment.

Sura 112 is one of the shortest chapters in the Qur’an, four lines that many Muslims recite every day. It says that God is one, eternal and absolute, that God does not beget and was not begotten, and that nothing is like God. Muslims call the teaching packed into those lines , the oneness of God. It is the first half of the Muslim declaration of faith, and everything else in Islam is meant to follow from it.

The opposite of tawhid is , associating anything else with God: worshiping idols, as Meccans did before Islam, but also treating wealth, power or any person as if it were divine. The Qur’an treats shirk as the gravest error. This is also why Muslims do not accept the Christian teaching that Jesus is the Son of God or part of a Trinity, even though the Qur’an honors Jesus as a prophet born of the Virgin Mary who worked miracles. Muslims and Christians agree on much about Jesus and differ here.

From tawhid flow the other core beliefs that Muslim teachers list. Muslims believe in angels, God’s messengers, including Gabriel. They believe God sent scriptures before the Qur’an, the Torah to Moses, the Psalms to David, the Gospel to Jesus, but that those texts were altered over time, which the Qur’an corrects. They believe in a line of prophets ending with Muhammad. And they believe in a Day of Judgment, when each person is raised and answers for their deeds, followed by paradise or hell.

Muslims speak of God with ninety-nine names drawn from the Qur’an: the Merciful, the Compassionate, the Just, the Forgiving, the Creator, the Light. You may see them on prayer beads or on the walls of a mosque. The word is simply Arabic for God, and Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews use it too. Understanding tawhid explains a great deal about Muslim practice, from the ban on images in worship to the fact that prayer is addressed to God alone.

The Five Pillars

33.7

Shahada and Salat

Main ideaThe first two pillars are the declaration of faith, which makes a person a Muslim, and the five daily prayers that structure every day.

Muslim teachers sum up the practice of Islam in five duties called the , an image of posts holding up a roof. A well-known hadith, a report of the Prophet’s words, lists them: testimony of faith, prayer, charity, fasting in Ramadan and pilgrimage. The first is the , the declaration: There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the messenger of God. Saying it sincerely, usually in Arabic and often before witnesses, is how a person becomes a Muslim. It is whispered into a newborn’s ear and, if possible, is the last thing a dying person says.

The second pillar is , ritual prayer five times a day: before sunrise, at midday, in the afternoon, just after sunset and at night. The times shift with the sun, so they change through the year. In a Chicago June the last prayer falls after 10 p.m. Before praying, a Muslim performs , washing the hands, mouth, nose, face, arms, head and feet. Then, facing the , the direction of the Kaaba in Mecca, the worshiper recites al-Fatiha and other verses while standing, bowing, prostrating with the forehead on the ground and sitting, in a set sequence.

Salat can be done anywhere clean, on a mat at home, in an office, in an airport, but praying together is preferred, and the midday prayer on Friday, jumu’ah, is a congregational prayer at the mosque with a sermon. The call to prayer, the adhan, is chanted from the mosque in Arabic and begins Allahu akbar, God is greater. In Muslim-majority countries it sounds from loudspeakers five times a day. In Chicago it is more often an app notification.

Notice what these two pillars do. The shahada names the two things a Muslim must affirm, one God and one final messenger, and nothing more. There is no long creed to sign. Salat then takes that belief and puts it into the body five times a day, at the same times and in the same direction as more than a billion other people. Muslims often describe salat as a conversation with God and as a reset that pulls them out of ordinary business.

33.8

Zakat and the Ramadan Fast

Main ideaZakat, a yearly share of wealth given to those in need, and sawm, the dawn-to-sunset fast of Ramadan, turn belief into discipline and care for others.

The third pillar is , which means purification. Once a year, Muslims who have savings above a set minimum give a fixed share, traditionally 2.5 percent of that wealth, to people in need. The Qur’an names who may receive it: the poor, the needy, those in debt, travelers, those who collect it and others. Zakat is not the same as ordinary charity, which Muslims call sadaqa and give freely; zakat is a duty, and in some countries it is collected like a tax. Many Muslims calculate it during Ramadan.

The fourth pillar is , fasting during , the ninth month of the Islamic calendar, the month in which Muslims believe the Qur’an was first revealed. From the first light before dawn until sunset, adults do not eat, drink, smoke or have sexual relations. The Qur’an lists exceptions: the sick, travelers, and by extension pregnant and nursing women and the elderly, who make up the days later or feed the poor instead. Children are not required to fast, though many start with partial days.

Because the Islamic calendar follows the moon, its year is about eleven days shorter than the solar year, so Ramadan moves earlier each year and circles through all the seasons in about thirty-three years. A Chicago Ramadan in December means a fast of about ten and a half hours; one in June means nearly seventeen. Each day ends with the , the fast-breaking meal, often begun with dates and water. The pre-dawn meal is called suhoor.

Muslims describe Ramadan as much more than not eating. Mosques are full for extra night prayers, during which the whole Qur’an is recited over the month. People try to give up gossip and anger, read more, and give more. The last ten nights include Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, when Muslims believe the first revelation came and prayer is said to be especially valuable. The month ends with a festival, Eid al-Fitr, which the next section describes.

The Hajj

Main ideaThe hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca required once in a lifetime for those who are able, gathers millions of Muslims to retrace the steps of Abraham, Hagar and Muhammad.

The fifth pillar is the , the pilgrimage to Mecca during the last month of the Islamic calendar, Dhu al-Hijjah. 34.1

Hadith and Sunnah

Main ideaThe Sunnah is the Prophet's example, known through hadith reports that scholars collected and tested, and it is the second source of Islamic guidance after the Qur'an.

The Qur’an tells Muslims many times to pray, but it does not spell out every movement of the prayer or how many times to bow. Muslims learned those details by watching Muhammad. The Qur’an itself calls the Messenger of God a fine example to follow. His way of living, speaking and deciding is called the , meaning the beaten path or custom. For most Muslims it is the second source of guidance after the Qur’an.

The Sunnah is known through the , reports of what the Prophet said, did or approved. Each hadith has two parts. The is the chain of people who passed it on: one person heard it from a second, who heard it from a third, who heard the Prophet. The matn is the text of the report itself. After Muhammad’s death, thousands of reports circulated, and some were invented to support one side in a dispute.

So scholars built a science for testing reports. They studied every person in each chain. Was he or she honest? Did they have a good memory? Could they really have met the person they quoted? A report judged fully reliable is , meaning sound; others are graded good or weak. In the 800s, al-Bukhari (died 870) and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (died 875) compiled the collections Sunni Muslims trust most, each called a Sahih. Shia Muslims rely on their own collections, which give special weight to reports passed down through the Prophet’s family.

Hadith shape ordinary life: how to wash before prayer, what to say after a sneeze, eating with the right hand, and the teaching that even a smile for another person counts as charity. Keep one distinction clear. Muslims believe the Qur’an is the word of God. Hadith are human reports about the Prophet, and scholars have always weighed them, sometimes reaching different conclusions about the same report.

The Question of Succession

Main ideaWhen Muhammad died in 632, Muslims disagreed about who should lead; Sunnis accept the chosen caliphs, while Shia believe leadership belonged to Ali and the Prophet's family.

Medina, June 632. The Prophet has died. He left no surviving son and, Sunnis hold, named no successor. A group of leading Muslims met and chose Abu Bakr, Muhammad’s close friend and father-in-law, as , from the Arabic khalifa, successor or deputy. The caliph led the community in politics, war and prayer, but he did not receive revelation. Muslims believe prophecy ended with Muhammad.

The name comes from ahl al-sunna, the people of the Sunnah and of the community. Sunnis honor the first four caliphs, Abu Bakr (632 to 634), Umar (634 to 644), Uthman (644 to 656) and Ali (656 to 661), as the Rightly Guided Caliphs. In Sunni thinking, the community should be led by a qualified Muslim whom it accepts. Later caliphs, such as the Umayyads in Damascus and the Abbasids in Baghdad, ruled great empires.

The name comes from Shi’at Ali, the party of Ali. Ali was Muhammad’s cousin, one of the first to accept Islam, and the husband of the Prophet’s daughter Fatima. Shia Muslims believe the Prophet named Ali as his successor at a place called Ghadir Khumm, on the way home from his last pilgrimage. Sunnis accept a report of that event but read its key word as friend, not ruler. For Shia, rightful leadership belongs to a line of descended from Ali and Fatima.

Today, by common estimates, about 85 to 90 percent of the world’s Muslims are Sunni and about 10 to 15 percent are Shia. Shia are the majority in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Bahrain, with large communities in Lebanon, Pakistan and India. The two share the Qur’an, the Five Pillars and the hajj, where they pray side by side. The split began as a question of leadership, though over centuries each developed its own law, holidays and practices.

Sufism: The Inner Path

Main ideaSufism is the mystical side of Islam, in which Muslims seek direct experience of God's love through remembrance, poetry and a master's guidance.

In Konya, Turkey, men in tall felt hats and long white skirts turn slowly to the music of a reed flute, the right palm raised toward heaven and the left turned toward earth. They belong to the Mevlevi order, founded by followers of the poet Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207 to 1273), who is buried in Konya. For them the turning, called sema, is a form of prayer. Rumi’s poems, often in loose English versions, are widely read in the United States today.

, tasawwuf in Arabic, is the mystical path within Islam: the effort to know God directly, from the heart, and not only through outward rules. Most Sufis have kept the law carefully and seen it as the doorway to the inner path. The name probably comes from suf, wool, the rough cloth early ascetics wore. An early Sufi, Rabi’a al-Adawiyya of Basra (died 801), is remembered for teaching that God should be loved for God’s own sake.

The central Sufi practice is , remembrance: repeating the names of God or a phrase such as la ilaha illa Allah, there is no god but God, alone or in a circle, often in rhythm with the breath. Sufis join a , an order or path, and learn from a , a master who guides disciples. Orders such as the Qadiriyya, Naqshbandiyya and Chishtiyya spread across three continents, and Sufi teachers helped spread Islam in South Asia, Africa and Southeast Asia. There are both Sunni and Shia Sufis.

Al-Ghazali (died 1111), a leading scholar in Baghdad, left his teaching post during a personal crisis and later wrote that Sufi experience and the law belong together; many Muslims accepted his view. Not all Muslims embrace Sufism. Some reform movements, including Salafi movements and the Wahhabi movement in Saudi Arabia, criticize practices such as visiting saints’ tombs, which they see as risking shirk. Sufis reply that they honor saints but worship God alone. A student of religion describes both views.

A Golden Age of Learning

34.7

Baghdad and the House of Wisdom

Main ideaIn Abbasid Baghdad, scholars of many faiths translated Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic, preserving them and building on them.

In 762, the caliph al-Mansur founded a new capital on the Tigris River and laid it out as a great circle: Baghdad. Within a century it was one of the largest cities in the world, a crossroads of trade from China to Spain. Paper, a Chinese invention, was being made in Baghdad by the late 700s. It was far cheaper than parchment, so books became cheaper and more plentiful, and booksellers’ shops lined whole streets.

The caliphs, who ruled from Baghdad, sponsored a that lasted about two centuries. Scholars translated the works of Aristotle, Euclid, Ptolemy, Galen and Hippocrates from Greek, often through Syriac, along with books from Persian and Sanskrit. Many translators were Christians, such as the physician Hunayn ibn Ishaq (died 873), who with his students translated much of Galen’s medicine. Jewish, Sabian and Muslim scholars worked alongside them. The caliph al-Ma’mun (ruled 813 to 833) was an especially famous patron.

Al-Ma’mun’s court is linked with the , Bayt al-Hikma in Arabic, a royal library where scholars read, copied and discussed books. Historians debate how large and organized it was; some describe it mainly as a library rather than a university. What is clear is that scholars in Baghdad did not only preserve the Greek works. They checked them, argued with them and corrected them.

Why did this happen? Historians give several reasons: the Qur’an’s repeated call to reflect on the signs of God in nature; practical needs such as calendars, prayer times, the qibla, inheritance math and medicine; and the prestige that learning brought a ruler. The golden age is usually dated from the 700s to the 1200s. In 1258 the Mongols sacked Baghdad and killed the last Abbasid caliph there, but learning continued in Cairo, Samarkand, Istanbul and Mughal India.

Algebra, Stars and Medicine

Main ideaScholars writing in Arabic advanced mathematics, astronomy, optics and medicine, and words like algebra and algorithm still carry their traces.

When you solve x + 5 = 12 by subtracting 5 from both sides, you are using a method explained by al-Khwarizmi (about 780 to 850), a scholar in Baghdad. Around 820 he wrote a book on calculating by al-jabr, restoring, and al-muqabala, balancing. Al-jabr became our word . His name, written in Latin as Algoritmi, gave us . Another of his books spread the Indian system of place-value numerals with zero; Europeans later called them Arabic numerals, though they began in India.

Astronomy had religious uses. Muslims needed to set the times of the five prayers, find the qibla, and sight the new moon that begins each month of the lunar calendar. Astronomers built observatories, such as those at Maragha in Iran (1259) and Samarkand (1420s), improved Ptolemy’s tables, and perfected the , a brass instrument for measuring the positions of the sun and stars. Many star names are Arabic, including Aldebaran, Betelgeuse, Altair and Vega.

Cities such as Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo built hospitals, called , with separate wards for different illnesses; many treated patients without charge. The physician al-Razi (died about 925) wrote one of the earliest clear accounts telling smallpox from measles. Ibn Sina (980 to 1037) wrote the Canon of Medicine, which was taught in European universities for centuries. In Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham (died about 1040) used experiments to argue that we see because light enters the eye.

These scholars were Arabs, Persians, Central Asians and others; some were Christian or Jewish, like the physician and philosopher Maimonides. Their books were later translated into Latin in Spain and Sicily, where Ibn Sina became Avicenna and al-Razi became Rhazes. Historians describe this as a bridge from ancient Greek science to the European Renaissance, and they stress that it was original work, not only storage for Greek ideas.

Córdoba and Timbuktu

Main ideaLearning flourished far from Baghdad, in al-Andalus in Spain and in the West African city of Timbuktu, where manuscripts are still kept.

In 711, Muslim armies crossed from North Africa into Spain, and the Muslim-ruled lands there became known as . In 756, Abd al-Rahman, an Umayyad prince who had escaped the Abbasids, set up his rule at Córdoba. Its Great Mosque, begun in 785, is famous for its forest of red-and-white striped arches. By the 900s Córdoba was one of the largest cities in Europe, and the library of the caliph al-Hakam II was said to hold hundreds of thousands of books.

Jews and Christians in al-Andalus lived as , protected communities who paid a special tax and had fewer rights than Muslims, but kept their own worship and courts. Many historians use the Spanish word , living together, for the exchange among the three faiths; others stress its limits and periods of persecution. Both are part of the record. The Muslim philosopher Ibn Rushd (1126 to 1198) and the Jewish philosopher Maimonides (1138 to 1204), who wrote mostly in Arabic, were both born in Córdoba. Castile conquered the city in 1236, and the mosque became a cathedral.

Timbuktu stands at the edge of the Sahara near the Niger River. Under the Mali and later the Songhay empires, it became a market where gold, salt and books were traded. Mali’s ruler Mansa Musa made the hajj in 1324 and passed through Cairo with so much gold that writers described it for generations. He brought scholars home with him, and the mud-brick Djinguereyber mosque, begun in his time, still stands. Teachers at mosques such as Sankore taught Maliki law, Arabic grammar, astronomy and medicine.

Timbuktu’s families collected books for centuries: Qur’ans, law, astronomy, poetry and business letters, some in African languages written in Arabic script. In 2012, armed groups occupied the city. Librarians and families smuggled hundreds of thousands of manuscripts south to Bamako; a few thousand were burned, but most were saved. Timbuktu is a reminder that the golden age of Islamic learning was African as well as Arab and Persian.

The Art of the Written Word

Main ideaBecause Muslims revere the Qur'an as God's word, calligraphy became the most honored Islamic art, joined by geometric pattern and the arabesque.

Look up inside the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed around 691, and you see bands of gold Arabic letters, among the earliest dated inscriptions of Qur’anic verses. Islamic religious art usually avoids images of living beings, and the written word takes their place. , beautiful writing, became the most honored art of all, because it gives visible form to words Muslims believe God spoke.

Calligraphers developed many styles. is angular and was used in early copies of the Qur’an. is rounded and easy to read, and it became the standard hand for copying books. Thuluth is large and flowing, often used on mosque walls. Calligraphers trained for years under a master, cut their own reed pens, and measured each letter in dots made by the pen’s tip. Ottoman sultans signed decrees with a tughra, an elaborate calligraphic emblem of the ruler’s name.

Two other elements appear everywhere. Geometric patterns start from circles and grow into stars and polygons that seem to repeat without end; many Muslims see in them the order and unity of creation, though artists’ own intentions varied. The is a pattern of winding vines and leaves. You can see all three at the Alhambra in Granada, in the tiled mosques of Isfahan, and at the Taj Mahal in Agra, built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan in the 1600s as a tomb for his wife, with Qur’an verses inlaid in stone.

Not all Islamic art avoids figures. Persian, Turkish and Mughal painters illustrated books of history and poetry with detailed scenes of people and animals, and some even depicted the Prophet, often with his face veiled. Views on images vary by place, period and community; avoidance is strongest in mosques and in copies of the Qur’an. Today calligraphy is still thriving, from traditional masters in Istanbul to young artists painting Arabic letters on city walls.
