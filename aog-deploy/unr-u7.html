Angels and the Angel of the LORD

Messengers

Angels appear on greeting cards, tree ornaments and paintings. Most of those pictures show sweet babies with tiny wings. The Bible describes something very different. Its messengers are often strong and bright. People who meet them are told, "Do not be afraid."

In this unit you will read what the Bible says about angels, with Michael Heiser as your guide. Heiser was a scholar who read the Bible in Hebrew and Greek. You will learn the words for messengers, cherubim and seraphim. You will meet Michael, Gabriel and Raphael. Then you will meet a puzzling figure. He is called the Angel of the LORD, and he speaks as if he were God.

By the end, you will be able to explain what the word angel means. You will be able to retell the stories of Hagar, Moses, Gideon and Samson's parents. You will be able to describe Heiser's reading of the Angel of the LORD. You will also know at least one other way readers understand him.

The big question

What does the Bible say angels are and do, and who is the Angel of the LORD who speaks as God?

Angels
Big questionWhat does the Bible mean by the word angel, and what do the angels in its stories do?
The story

The Angel on the Tree

A small ornament with chubby cheeks starts a big question about what the Bible really says.

Marcus hung the last ornament on his grandmother's tree. It was a tiny angel. It had round pink cheeks, curly hair and two little wings. "Angels are so cute," Marcus said. His grandmother smiled. She sat down with her Bible and opened it to the Gospel of Luke.

She read about shepherds out in the fields at night. An angel of the Lord suddenly stood near them. A bright light shone all around. Luke says the shepherds were terrified. The very first thing the angel said to them was, "Do not be afraid."

Marcus looked at the ornament again. "Why would anyone be scared of this?" he asked. His grandmother laughed. She said the angels in Bible stories do not look like babies. When they show up, people often fall to the ground or shake. In the Book of Daniel, a messenger comes, and Daniel falls on his face.

"So where did baby angels come from?" Marcus asked. His grandmother said that painters in Italy, about five hundred years ago, loved to paint little children with wings. People liked those pictures very much. Over time, many people began to picture all angels that way.

Marcus wanted to know more. He found out about a scholar named Michael Heiser. Heiser studied the Bible in its old languages, Hebrew and Greek. He wrote a whole book called Angels (2018) about what the Bible says about them. Marcus decided to start there.

Section 1

Messengers and Their Names

13.1

Angel Means Messenger

Main ideaThe Hebrew word mal'ak and the Greek word angelos both mean messenger, so the word angel names a job.

In Genesis 32, Jacob is traveling home. He is nervous about meeting his brother Esau. So he sends some people ahead with a message. The Hebrew word for these people is mal&#x27;ak. It means messenger. These messengers were ordinary men from Jacob’s own camp.

The same word shows up just two verses earlier. In Genesis 32:1, messengers of God meet Jacob on the road. Those are heavenly messengers. Hebrew uses one word for both groups. A messenger is anyone who is sent with a message, whoever sends him.

Long ago, the Hebrew Bible was put into Greek. Then mal’ak became . The English word angel comes from angelos. Michael Heiser stressed this point in his book Angels (2018). He said angel names a job. It tells you what someone does, not what kind of being they are.

Think it through
Two Kinds of Messengers

Genesis 32:1 — Jacob goes on his way, and messengers of God meet him. Genesis 32:3 — Jacob sends messengers ahead to his brother Esau in the land of Edom. In Hebrew, both verses use the same word: mal'ak.

Both groups are called mal'ak. What clues in each verse tell you which messengers are heavenly and which are human?

13.2

Many Names for One Family

Main ideaThe Bible also calls heavenly beings sons of God, holy ones and the host of heaven; Heiser said these names describe what they are.

If angel is a job, who does the job? The Bible uses several names for the beings of the unseen world. In Job 38:7, God says the shouted for joy when the earth was made. That is a family word. It pictures heavenly beings who belong to God’s household.

Psalm 89:5–7 calls them the . They gather in a great meeting around God. In 1 Kings 22:19, the prophet Micaiah sees the LORD on his throne. All the stands beside him. Host is an old word for a very large army or crowd.

Heiser thought these names describe what the beings are. They are spirit beings who live in God’s presence. Mal’ak describes one thing some of them do, which is carry messages. In his view, a son of God could be sent with a message. Then he would be doing the job of an angel.

13.3

Cherubim Guard Holy Places

Main ideaIn the Bible, cherubim are powerful guardians of holy places, like the garden of Eden and the ark of the covenant.

At the end of Genesis 3, Adam and Eve must leave the garden of Eden. God places on the east side of the garden. A flaming sword turns every way. Their job is to guard the path to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24). Cherubim is the plural of cherub.

Cherubim show up again in Exodus 25. God tells Moses to make a gold-covered box called the . On its cover sit two gold cherubim. They face each other, and their wings spread over the top. God says he will speak to Moses from between them (Exodus 25:22).

Ezekiel 10 describes cherubim with four faces and many eyes. They are not babies at all. Heiser noted that in the Bible, cherubim guard God’s throne and holy places. Scholars also point out that ancient kings in Assyria set huge winged guardian statues at their palace gates.

13.4

Seraphim Around the Throne

Main ideaIn Isaiah 6, seraphim with six wings stand above God's throne and call out that God is holy.

Isaiah 6 begins in the year King Uzziah died. The prophet Isaiah has a vision of the Lord on a high throne. Above the throne stand . Each one has six wings. Two cover its face, two cover its feet, and with two it flies.

The seraphim call to one another, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD." The doorposts shake, and the house fills with smoke. Isaiah feels he is not clean enough to be there. A seraph touches his lips with a hot coal from the altar. It tells him his guilt is taken away. Then Isaiah says, "Here I am. Send me."

The Hebrew word saraph seems to be linked to burning. The same word is used for the fiery serpents in Numbers 21:6. So some scholars, Heiser among them, think the seraphim may have looked like shining serpents. Other readers picture them in other ways. The text itself tells us only their wings and their words.

Angels With Names

13.5

Michael the Great Prince

Main ideaMichael is named in Daniel, Jude and Revelation; Daniel calls him a great prince who stands up for Daniel's people.

Only a few angels in the Bible have names. One is Michael. His name means "Who is like God?" Michael first appears in the Book of Daniel. In Daniel 10:13, a heavenly messenger tells Daniel that Michael came to help him.

Daniel 12:1 calls Michael "the great prince" who stands up for Daniel’s people. A here means a leader or ruler. Heiser read these verses beside Deuteronomy 32:8. He thought they show heavenly beings who had charge over nations, with Michael caring for Israel.

Michael appears in the New Testament too. The letter of Jude, verse 9, calls him "the ." That means a chief messenger, a leader among angels. Revelation 12:7 pictures Michael leading the angels on God’s side in a struggle in heaven.

13.6

Gabriel Brings News

Main ideaGabriel explains visions to Daniel and brings news of coming births to Zechariah and to Mary.

The other angel named in the Hebrew Bible is Gabriel. In Daniel 8:16, a voice tells Gabriel to help Daniel understand a vision. In Daniel 9:21, Gabriel comes to Daniel again in the evening. He comes to give Daniel wisdom and understanding.

Gabriel appears again in Luke 1. First he comes to Zechariah, a priest, inside the temple. He says Zechariah and his wife Elizabeth will have a son, John. He introduces himself: "I am Gabriel. I stand in the presence of God" (Luke 1:19).

Six months later, Gabriel is sent to Nazareth. He brings news to a young woman named Mary. He tells her she will have a son named Jesus. To Zechariah and to Mary, Gabriel’s first words are the same: "Do not be afraid." His job fits the word mal’ak. He news and explains it.

13.7

Raphael in the Book of Tobit

Main ideaRaphael is named in the Book of Tobit, which is in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles but not in the Hebrew Bible or Protestant Old Testament.

A third named angel, Raphael, appears in the Book of Tobit. Tobit tells of a Jewish family living far from home in Nineveh. The son, Tobias, must make a long trip. A kind stranger offers to guide him. The stranger says his name is Azarias. A dog comes along too.

On the trip, the guide helps the family in many ways, and the story ends with healing. At the end, the guide tells them who he really is. "I am Raphael," he says, one of seven holy angels who stand before the Lord (Tobit 12:15). The name Raphael means "God heals."

Not every Bible has Tobit. It is in the Old Testament of Catholic and Orthodox Bibles. It is not in the Hebrew Bible. Protestants place it in a separate group called the , outside their . The book of 1 Enoch also names Raphael, alongside Michael and Gabriel.

Section 3

Clearing Up Mix-Ups

13.8

Where Baby Angels Came From

Main ideaChubby baby angels come from art made long after the Bible; Bible angels often look like grown men and make people afraid.

About five hundred years ago, painters in Italy liked to fill their pictures with little winged children. In art, these are often called . Around 1512, the painter Raphael put two small winged children at the bottom of a famous painting. People loved them. Over time, baby angels became a common picture.

The Bible describes angels very differently. In Genesis 18, three visitors come to Abraham. The text calls them men. In Genesis 19, two of them are called angels. Hebrews 13:2 says some people have welcomed angels as guests without knowing it. So angels in the Bible often look like grown people.

When angels show their glory, people are often afraid. That is why so many visits start with "Do not be afraid" (Luke 1:13; 1:30; 2:10). Heiser wanted readers to see this. Bible angels are strong and bright, not cute. Baby angels are art, not Bible description.

13.9

People Do Not Become Angels

Main ideaThe Bible never says people become angels when they die; angels and people are different kinds of beings.

Many people call a loved one who has died "an angel." It is a loving way to talk. But Heiser, like many Bible scholars, pointed out that no verse says people turn into angels. In the Bible, angels and people are two different kinds of beings.

In Matthew 22:30, Jesus is asked a tricky question about marriage. He says that in the , people will not marry. They will be like the angels in heaven. Being like angels in one way is not the same as becoming angels.

Hebrews 12:22–23 gives a picture of a great gathering. It lists angels in one group. It lists the spirits of good people in another group. Christians hold many beliefs about what happens after death. But the Bible’s own words keep angels and people separate.

What Angels Do

13.10

Sent to Help

Main ideaHebrews 1:14 describes angels as spirits who serve God and are sent to help people.

Hebrews 1:14 sums up what angels do. It calls them spirits who serve. It says God sends them to help people who will receive salvation. Heiser’s book Angels (2018) stresses this. Angels are of God, and they do what God sends them to do.

Bible stories show this help in plain ways. In 1 Kings 19:5–7, the prophet Elijah is worn out and asleep under a bush. An angel touches him and says, "Get up and eat." Bread and water are waiting. In Acts 12:7–10, an angel wakes Peter in prison. His chains fall off, and a gate opens by itself.

Angels in these stories point people to God, not to themselves. In Revelation 22:8–9, John bows down to an angel. The angel tells him not to. "I am a with you," the angel says. "Worship God."

Eyes Opened at Dothan

Main ideaIn 2 Kings 6, Elisha's servant is shown horses and chariots of fire, a picture of the unseen world Heiser wrote about.

The Angel of the LORD

God Made Visible
Big questionWho is the Angel of the LORD who speaks as God, and how have different readers understood him?
The story

A Stranger Under the Oak

A young farmer hiding his wheat hears himself called a mighty warrior.

The Book of Judges tells of a hard time in Israel. Raiders from Midian swept in at every harvest. They took the crops and the animals. So people hid their food wherever they could. A young man named Gideon had some wheat. He did not dare work it out in the open.

Gideon took his wheat down into a winepress. A winepress was a low stone pit for squeezing grapes. It was an odd place to beat wheat, but it was hidden. Nearby stood an oak tree in the town of Ophrah. It belonged to Gideon's father, Joash (Judges 6:11).

A stranger was sitting under that oak. The text calls him the angel of the LORD. He looked at Gideon, crouched down in the pit, and spoke. "The LORD is with you, you mighty warrior" (Judges 6:12). Gideon did not feel mighty at all.

Gideon answered with honest questions. If the LORD was with them, why was life so hard? Then something strange happens in the story. The text stops saying the angel spoke. It says, "The LORD turned to him" and told him to go and save Israel (Judges 6:14).

Readers have wondered about this moment for a very long time. Who was the stranger under the oak? Was he a messenger speaking for God, or was he God himself, seen in a form Gideon could look at? This chapter follows that question through several Bible stories.

14.1

Hagar at the Spring

Main ideaIn Genesis 16, the angel of the LORD makes promises in God's own voice, and Hagar names the one who spoke to her God.

Hagar was an Egyptian servant in the household of Abram and Sarai. Sarai treated her harshly, and Hagar ran away into the desert. The found her by a spring of water on the road to Shur (Genesis 16:7). He asked where she was going.

The angel told Hagar to go back. Then he said, "I will greatly multiply your descendants." He did not say God will do it. He said I will. He told her to name her son Ishmael, which means "God hears." The LORD, he said, had heard her pain.

Genesis 16:13 then says that Hagar named "the LORD who spoke to her." She called him "the God who sees me." So the text calls the speaker both the angel of the LORD and the LORD. Heiser saw this as an important clue. Many other readers see it too.

14.2

A Voice From Heaven to Abraham

Main ideaIn Genesis 22, the angel of the LORD calls to Abraham and says you have not held back your son from me.

Genesis 22 tells a hard story. The text says God tested Abraham. God asked him to offer his son Isaac on a mountain. Readers in every tradition have found this story difficult. Jewish tradition calls it the , which means the binding.

Abraham went to the mountain. Before he could harm Isaac, the angel of the LORD called to him from heaven. "Abraham, Abraham!" the angel said. "Do not lay your hand on the boy" (Genesis 22:11–12). Then Abraham saw a ram caught in a bush and offered it instead.

Look closely at the angel’s words. He says, "Now I know that you fear God. You have not held back your son from me." From me, not from God who sent me. In verse 16 the angel adds, "By myself I have sworn, says the LORD." Heiser pointed to verses like these. The messenger talks as God talks.

14.3

The Bush That Did Not Burn Up

Main ideaIn Exodus 3, the angel of the LORD appears in a flame, and then God speaks from the same bush.

Moses was taking care of sheep near Mount Horeb. Exodus 3:2 says the angel of the LORD appeared to him in a flame of fire. The fire came from the middle of a bush. The bush was burning, but it did not burn up. Moses turned aside to look.

Now read verse 4. "God called to him out of the middle of the bush." The angel appeared in the bush, and God spoke from the bush. God told Moses to take off his sandals, because the ground was . God said, "I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob."

Moses hid his face. He was afraid to look at God (Exodus 3:6). Heiser read this as one more place where the angel and God are the same figure. Many Jewish readers explain it as God speaking through his messenger. Both readings start from the same words in the text.

Gideon and Samson's Parents

14.4

The LORD Turned to Him

Main ideaIn Judges 6, the text first says the angel of the LORD spoke to Gideon and then says the LORD spoke to him.

Judges 6 opens during hard years. Raiders from Midian kept taking Israel’s crops. Gideon was beating out wheat in a winepress to hide it. The angel of the LORD sat under an oak nearby. He said, "The LORD is with you, you mighty warrior" (Judges 6:12).

Gideon asked a bold question. If the LORD is with us, why is all this happening? Then verse 14 says, "The LORD turned to him." The LORD told Gideon to go in his strength and Israel. The writer moves from the angel to the LORD without a pause.

Gideon said his family was the weakest in his tribe. He said he was the youngest in his family. The answer came: "I will be with you" (Judges 6:16). Heiser noted how the text slides between the two names. To him, this showed the angel and the LORD as one.

14.5

Fire From the Rock

Main ideaGideon's offering burns up when the angel touches it, and Gideon names his altar The LORD Is Peace.

Gideon asked for a sign. He brought out a meal of goat meat and flat bread. The angel of God told him to set it on a rock. The angel touched the food with the tip of his staff. Fire came up from the rock and burned it all. Then the angel was gone (Judges 6:19–21).

Gideon was afraid. "I have seen the angel of the LORD face to face!" he cried. In the Bible, seeing God was thought to be very dangerous. But the LORD said to him, "Peace to you. Do not be afraid. You will not die" (Judges 6:23).

Gideon built an there. He named it "The LORD Is Peace" (Judges 6:24). The story ends calmly. Gideon is worried because he saw the angel. Heiser and other readers note that the worry makes most sense if seeing the angel was like seeing God.

Main ideaIn Judges 13, the angel who visits Samson's parents will not give his name, and they say they have seen God.

Judges 13 tells of a couple from the town of Zorah. The husband was Manoah. His wife is not named. They had no children. The angel of the LORD came to her and said she would have a son. That son grew up to be Samson.

Manoah asked the visitor his name. The angel answered, "Why do you ask my name? It is " (Judges 13:18). Some translations say secret. Manoah offered a young goat on a rock. As the flame went up, the angel went up in the flame. Then Manoah knew he was the angel of the LORD.

Manoah said, "We will surely die, for we have seen God." His wife answered calmly. If the LORD meant to harm us, she said, he would not have accepted our gift or told us all this (Judges 13:22–23). Heiser pointed out that Manoah says they saw God, not just a messenger.

Section 3

Heiser's Reading

14.7

My Name Is in Him

Main ideaIn Exodus 23:20–21, God says his name is in the angel he sends ahead of Israel.

In Exodus 23, Israel is getting ready to travel to a new land. God says, "I am sending an angel ahead of you." The angel will guard them on the way. He will bring them to the place God has prepared (Exodus 23:20).

Then God gives a warning. Listen to him, and do not rebel against him. "He will not pardon your wrongdoing, for my is in him" (Exodus 23:21). In the Bible, a name is more than a label. It stands for who someone is.

Heiser thought this verse was very important. In the Hebrew Bible, forgiving wrongdoing belongs to God. And God’s name was in this angel. For Heiser, that meant the angel carried God’s own presence. Other readers say the angel simply carried God’s , the way an ambassador speaks for a king.

14.8

God Unseen and God Seen

Main ideaHeiser argued that the Angel of the LORD was God in a form people could see, a second figure he called a second Yahweh.

Some verses say no one can see God. In Exodus 33:20, God tells Moses, "No one may see me and live." Yet Jacob says in Genesis 32:30, "I have seen God face to face." Hagar, Gideon and Manoah say much the same. How can both be true?

Heiser offered an answer in The Unseen Realm (2015). He said the Hebrew Bible shows God in two ways. One is God who is unseen, in heaven. The other is God who is seen, often as the Angel of the LORD. Heiser called this the idea. is the personal name of God in Hebrew.

Many English Bibles print that name as LORD in small capital letters. Many Jewish readers do not say the name aloud, out of respect. Heiser said Christians later linked the seen God with Jesus. He believed that link grew out of these Hebrew stories. Other readers disagree, as the next lessons show.

Think it through
Two Verses Side by Side

Exodus 33:20: God tells Moses, You cannot see my face, for no one may see me and live. Genesis 32:30: After wrestling all night, Jacob names the place Peniel. He says, I have seen God face to face, and my life was spared.

These two verses seem to pull in different directions. How would Heiser's two Yahwehs reading explain both? How might a reader who disagrees explain them?

14.9

The Word and the Name

Main ideaHeiser also pointed to verses where the Word of the LORD and the Name of the LORD seem to act like a person.

Heiser noticed that the angel is not the only clue. Genesis 15:1 says "the came to Abram in a vision." A few verses later, this Word brings Abram outside and tells him to count the stars (Genesis 15:5). Heiser read this Word as someone Abram could see.

In 1 Samuel 3:10, the LORD "came and stood" and called the boy Samuel. Verse 21 says the LORD showed himself to Samuel "by the word of the LORD." Heiser read these as more pictures of God being seen. Other readers take word here to mean a message.

The Name works the same way. Deuteronomy 12:11 speaks of the place where God will make his name dwell. And in Exodus 23:21, God’s name is in the angel. In Genesis 48:15–16, Jacob blesses his grandsons in the name of God and the angel who rescued him. Heiser noted that the verb bless there is singular.

Think it through

Section 4

Other Ways of Reading

14.10

A Messenger Speaking for God

Main ideaJewish readers usually read the Angel of the LORD as a messenger who speaks with God's authority, the way an agent stands for the one who sent him.

Jewish readers have usually read these stories another way. They see the angel of the LORD as a messenger. A messenger may speak in the first person for the one who sent him. A royal herald could read a king’s letter aloud and say I, meaning the king.

The Mishnah is a collection of Jewish teaching written down about 200 CE. It states a rule about an . An agent is someone sent to act for another person. The Mishnah says a person’s agent is like the person himself (Mishnah Berakhot 5:5). Readers use this idea to explain why an angel can say I will for God.

Some ancient Jewish teachers argued about a teaching called . Later rabbis rejected it. They held that there is one God, with no second power beside him. Heiser studied these old arguments. He thought they showed that some Jewish readers once saw two figures in these stories.

From the source
An Agent Is Like the Sender
A person's agent is like the person himself.
Mishnah Berakhot 5:5, c. 200 CE, paraphrased in simple words (paraphrased)

How could this rule explain an angel who says I will when he makes God's promise to Hagar?

Why: Jewish readers usually see the angel as a messenger or agent speaking for God, not as God himself.

2. According to Mishnah Berakhot 5:5, what is a person's agent like?

3. What did later rabbis decide about two powers in heaven?

Why: Later rabbis rejected the two powers teaching and held to one God with no second power.
14.11

Christians and the Angel

Main ideaMany early Christians linked the Angel of the LORD with Jesus, while others, like Augustine, thought God spoke through created angels.

About 155 CE, a Christian writer named Justin Martyr wrote a long debate. It is called Dialogue with Trypho. Justin talked about the one who appeared to Abraham, Jacob and Moses. He argued that this was the one Christians call the Son of God. Many early Christian writers read the stories that way.

The Gospel of John says, "No one has ever seen God" (John 1:18). It says that God the only Son has made him known. John 1:14 says the became flesh and lived among people. Heiser pointed to these verses. He thought John was building on the Hebrew stories of God seen.

Not all Christians agree. Augustine, a North African bishop writing about 400 CE, took a careful view. He thought God may have spoken through created angels in some of these stories. So readers differ within traditions too. Each reading starts from the same Bible passages.
