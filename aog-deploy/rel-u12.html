Indigenous Traditions
Big questionHow do traditions without one written book pass on what they hold sacred, and how have they survived being pushed aside?
The story

The Ceremony Comes Back Outside

A ceremony that was against the rules in 1900 is held in the open a century later.

It is a summer morning in the year 2000 on a reservation in the northern Plains. Families park their trucks at the edge of a wide field. Children carry folding chairs. An elder walks slowly to the center, where people have built a circle for a ceremony. Singers gather around a large drum. Nobody is hiding. A local newspaper has even printed the date.

The elder is in her eighties. She remembers her grandmother's stories about a very different time. Around 1900, U.S. government rules banned many Native American ceremonies. Agents on reservations could punish people for dancing or for holding religious gatherings. Some families kept the ceremonies alive in secret, far from the agency buildings. Others lost them.

Her grandmother was also sent away to a boarding school. There, teachers cut her hair and punished her for speaking her own language. She was taught that her family's prayers were wrong. When she came home years later, she had to learn her traditions again from the old people who still remembered.

Now the drum begins. The elder listens to the songs her grandmother once sang in a whisper. In 1978, Congress passed a law saying that the United States would protect Native American religious freedom. Laws alone do not bring a tradition back. People do that, by teaching, remembering and gathering. Today, this ceremony is held in the open.

Sacred Words Carried by Voice

23.1

Scripture Without a Book

Main ideaIn many Indigenous traditions, sacred teachings are passed on by voice, song and ceremony instead of a written book.

On a winter night in a Great Lakes community, an Ojibwe elder begins a story. Children sit close. In many Ojibwe families, some sacred stories are told only in winter, when snow covers the ground. The elder does not read from a page. She speaks the story as it was told to her, and as it was told to her own grandmother.

Scholars call this . It means teachings, stories, songs and history passed from person to person by speaking and listening. In many Indigenous traditions, oral tradition does the work that a written scripture does in other religions. It holds the teachings a people treat as sacred. It explains how the world began and how people should live.

Oral tradition is not careless or loose. Many communities train certain people to remember long stories, songs and prayers exactly. Some use objects to help memory, such as Ojibwe birchbark scrolls with drawn figures, or beaded belts among some Eastern nations. The , a respected older person who carries knowledge, often decides when and how a teaching is shared.

23.2

Ancestors, Land and Seasons

Main ideaMany Indigenous traditions tie the sacred to ancestors, to a specific homeland and to the turning seasons.

In autumn, Ojibwe families in Minnesota and Wisconsin paddle canoes into shallow lakes to gather wild rice. They call it . One person pushes the canoe with a long pole. Another bends the stalks and taps the grain into the boat. Many Ojibwe people describe wild rice as a sacred gift. Their oral tradition says their ancestors traveled west to the place where food grows on the water.

This harvest shows three ideas found in many Indigenous traditions. The first is the land. Many traditions teach that a people belongs to a particular place, with its rivers, hills and animals. The second is the , family members who lived long ago. Many communities honor them and believe they stay connected to the living. The third is the seasons. Ceremonies often follow planting, harvest, hunting or the return of the sun.

These ideas differ from nation to nation. Still, many adherents describe the relationship with land as a duty, not ownership. The land gives food and medicine. People give thanks, take only what they need and care for it for those not yet born.

23.3

Olodumare and the Orisha

Main ideaYoruba tradition teaches of a supreme being, Olodumare, and many orisha who each guide part of life and nature.

Each August in the city of Osogbo, in southwestern Nigeria, crowds walk to a sacred grove beside the Osun River. A young woman carries a covered calabash on her head, and drummers follow her. The festival honors Osun, whom adherents associate with the river, with fresh water, healing and children. The grove is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Osun is one of the . In Yoruba tradition, the orisha are powerful spirit beings who each watch over part of nature and human life. Ogun is linked with iron and work. Sango is linked with thunder and justice. Yemoja is linked with the waters and with mothers. Above them, Yoruba tradition teaches of , the supreme being, who is far above daily life.

The Yoruba are one of the largest peoples of West Africa, living mostly in Nigeria and Benin. Today many Yoruba people are Muslim or Christian. Others follow the orisha tradition, and some people combine practices. Worship of the orisha includes songs, drumming, offerings and festivals. Each town and family may have its own special orisha.

23.4

Ifa: Reading for Guidance

Main ideaIn Ifa divination, a trained priest recites memorized verses to help a person seek guidance.

A woman sits on a mat across from a , a trained Ifa priest. She has a hard choice to make. The priest casts a chain of seed pods onto a mat, or marks signs in powder on a round wooden tray. The pattern points to one of many sacred signs. Then he begins to recite verses tied to that sign.

This practice is called , a way of seeking guidance about a choice or a problem. In Yoruba tradition, Ifa divination is linked to Orunmila, the orisha of wisdom. The system has 256 signs, called odu. Each odu has many verses full of stories, advice and examples. A babalawo trains for many years to learn them by heart.

The verses are a large body of oral literature. The priest does not simply tell the future. He shares stories that fit the person’s question, and the person thinks about what they mean. In 2005, UNESCO recognized the Ifa divination system as an important part of the world’s cultural heritage.

23.5

Traditions Formed Under Slavery

Main ideaEnslaved Africans carried their traditions to the Americas, where they grew into Santeria, Candomble and Vodou.

In a home in Havana, Cuba, a small altar holds a statue of a Catholic saint. Beside it sit stones, shells and bowls. To a visitor, it may look like a Catholic shrine. To a follower of Santeria, the saint also calls to mind an orisha. This pairing grew during slavery, when many enslaved people could not practice their African traditions openly.

From the 1500s to the 1800s, the transatlantic slave trade forced millions of Africans across the ocean. Many were Yoruba, Fon or from Kongo peoples. They carried their songs, prayers and spirits in memory. In Cuba, Yoruba traditions grew into , also called Regla de Ocha or Lucumi. In Brazil, they grew into . In Haiti, Fon, Kongo and Catholic elements joined in .

These are sometimes called diaspora traditions. A is a people scattered far from their homeland. Many adherents see the saint and orisha pairing as a way their ancestors protected their faith. Today, these traditions have temples and followers in the Americas, including in U.S. cities such as Miami, New York and Chicago.

Native Nations of North America

23.6

Many Nations, Many Traditions

Main ideaThere is no single Native American religion; each nation has its own language, stories and ceremonies.

A Hopi village in Arizona, a Lakota community in South Dakota and a Tlingit town in Alaska are all Native American. Yet their languages, stories and ceremonies are very different. The Hopi calendar follows farming in the desert. Tlingit ceremonies honor clan ancestors along the coast. Lakota ceremonies grew up on the open Plains.

The United States recognizes more than 570 tribal nations. Each is a , meaning it has its own government. Each also has its own traditions. So it is not accurate to speak of one Native American religion. Scholars and Native people often say traditions or ways of life instead. Many Native languages have no separate word for religion, because the sacred runs through all of life.

Many Native people today also practice Christianity, and some combine Christian and traditional ways. The Native American Church uses peyote, a small cactus, as a sacrament in prayer meetings. Its members come from many nations. This variety is one reason students should name the specific nation when they describe a practice.

23.7

Nations of the Great Lakes

Main ideaThe Potawatomi, Ojibwe and Miami peoples lived in the Great Lakes region, including the land that is now Chicago.

Walk along the Chicago River today and you are on land where the Potawatomi once lived and traded. Many historians say the name Chicago comes from a Miami-Illinois word, shikaakwa, for a wild onion that grew along the river. The Potawatomi, Ojibwe and Odawa call themselves the Anishinaabe. They have long described themselves as the Council of Three Fires.

Many Anishinaabe families teach the . They are wisdom, love, respect, bravery, honesty, humility and truth. Each teaching is often linked to an animal. The Ojibwe also have the Midewiwin, a society that keeps healing knowledge and sacred teachings. The Miami people, whose homeland included parts of Indiana and Illinois, have their own language and ceremonies.

In the Treaty of Chicago of 1833, the Potawatomi gave up their last lands in the area. By the late 1830s, most were forced west. The Miami were forced out of Indiana in 1846. Today, Potawatomi nations live in Kansas, Wisconsin, Michigan and elsewhere. The Miami Tribe of Oklahoma works to bring back the Myaamia language. Chicago has one of the largest urban Native communities in the country.

23.8

Banned, Then Protected

Main ideaU.S. policy once banned many Native ceremonies and sent children to boarding schools; the 1978 law declared a duty to protect Native religious freedom.

In 1883, the U.S. government issued rules called the Code of Indian Offenses. They made many Native ceremonies, such as the Sun Dance, punishable on reservations. Agents could withhold food rations or jail people who took part. Some communities kept ceremonies going in secret. Many sacred objects were taken and sent to museums.

At the same time, the government and many churches ran for Native children. The Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania opened in 1879. Children were taken far from home, given new names and punished for speaking their languages. Many never came home. The schools aimed at , making people give up their own culture for the majority’s.

Native people pushed back for decades. In 1978, Congress passed the . It said the United States would protect Native people’s right to practice their traditional religions, visit sacred sites and use sacred objects. In 1994, Congress added protection for the use of peyote by members of the Native American Church.

The United States will protect and preserve the right of American Indians, Eskimos, Aleuts and Native Hawaiians to believe, express and practice their traditional religions. This includes access to sacred sites, use and possession of sacred objects, and freedom to worship through ceremonies and traditional rites.

23.10

Revival and Respect Today

Main ideaIndigenous communities are reviving languages and ceremonies, and outsiders show respect by following each community's protocol.

In the 1970s, a group of Hawaiians built a voyaging canoe called Hokule’a. In 1976 it sailed from Hawaii to Tahiti with no modern instruments, guided by stars and swells. The voyage helped spark a Hawaiian renaissance. Hula schools, the Hawaiian language and old chants grew strong again. Across North America and Africa, communities are doing similar work.

Revival also brings questions about respect. Many ceremonies are private. Some songs belong to certain families or clans. Some knowledge is shared only with people who have trained for it. This is called , the proper way of doing something according to a community’s rules. Visitors may be asked not to take photos, or to wait to be invited.

In 2007, the United Nations adopted the Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. It says Indigenous peoples have the right to practice their spiritual traditions and to protect sacred sites. For a student, respect means learning what a community has chosen to share, naming the nation, and not copying sacred items as costumes or crafts.

The story

One Question, Three Rooms

In a courtroom, a classroom and a kitchen, people ask how different beliefs can live side by side.

In a courtroom in Washington, D.C., in 1943, the justices of the Supreme Court read a case from West Virginia. Children who were Jehovah's Witnesses had been expelled from school. Their faith taught them not to salute a flag, and the school required it. The Court ruled that the government could not force the students to salute or recite the pledge.

Many years later, in a middle school classroom in Illinois, a teacher writes a question on the board: How do Muslims, Hindus and Jews each mark a new year? A student raises her hand. She asks, Are we allowed to learn about religion at a public school? The teacher smiles. She says yes, we can learn about religions, as long as school does not tell anyone what to believe.

That evening, at a kitchen table on the North Side of Chicago, a family eats dinner. The grandmother is Catholic. The father is Muslim. The oldest daughter says she does not follow any religion. A cousin visiting from India is Hindu. They start to talk about a holiday coming up. They disagree about some things. But they listen, and they keep eating together.

The same question sits in all three rooms. How do people who believe different things, or nothing at all, share a country? Laws give one answer. Schools give another. Families and neighbors give a third, one conversation at a time.

Freedom in American Law

24.1

Forty-Five Words

Main ideaThe First Amendment's two religion clauses keep government from setting up a religion and protect each person's right to practice.

The First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution was added in 1791 as part of the Bill of Rights. Its whole text is only forty-five words long. It begins with religion. Before it says anything about speech or the press, it says what Congress may not do about faith.

The first part is the . It says the government may not set up an official religion or favor one faith over others. Several of the colonies had official churches, and many Americans remembered religious wars in Europe. The second part is the . It protects each person’s right to practice their religion, or no religion.

The two clauses work as a pair. The government may not push religion on people. It also may not stop people from living by their beliefs. Thomas Jefferson later wrote of a wall of separation between church and state. Americans still debate exactly where that wall stands, and the courts decide many of those questions.

From the source
The Religion Clauses
Congress may not pass any law that sets up an official religion, and it may not pass any law that stops people from freely practicing their religion.
U.S. Constitution, First Amendment, 1791 (paraphrased in plain words) (paraphrased)

24.2

Cases That Tested the Clauses

Main ideaSupreme Court cases apply the First Amendment to real conflicts between faith and government.

In 1943, in West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, the Supreme Court ruled that public schools could not force students to salute the flag. The case came from Jehovah’s Witness families. Their faith teaches that saluting a flag is a form of worship owed only to God. The Court decided the government could not make them do it.

Other cases followed. In Engel v. Vitale in 1962, the Court ruled that public schools could not lead students in an official prayer. That case used the Establishment Clause. In Wisconsin v. Yoder in 1972, the Court ruled that Amish families could end school after eighth grade. The Amish believe their faith calls for a simple farm life away from the wider world.

In 1993, the Court heard a case from the Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, a Santeria church in Hialeah, Florida. The city had passed rules aimed at stopping the church’s animal offerings. The Court ruled the rules unfairly targeted one religion. Each case turned on a , an earlier ruling that later courts follow.

24.3

Learning About Religion at School

Main ideaPublic schools may teach about religion objectively, but they may not teach students to practice or believe it.

You are reading this lesson in a public school, and that is allowed. In 1963, in Abington School District v. Schempp, the Supreme Court ruled that public schools could not hold daily Bible readings as worship. But in the same case, Justice Tom Clark wrote that studying the Bible or religion as part of a course is allowed when it is taught objectively.

Educators often explain the difference in simple terms. Schools may teach about religion, but may not practice religion. A school may explain what Muslims believe about Ramadan. It may not ask students to fast. A school may read a psalm as literature. It may not lead the class in praying it. This is the : describing traditions fairly, from the outside.

Students also keep their own rights. They may pray on their own, wear religious clothing, or share their beliefs in a respectful way. They may also say they follow no religion. Teachers stay , not taking a side, so that every student feels welcome.

From the source
Study Is Allowed
Nothing we have said means that the study of the Bible or of religion, when presented objectively as part of a secular program of education, may not be carried out under the First Amendment. A person's education may not be complete without a study of comparative religion.
Justice Tom C. Clark, majority opinion, Abington School District v. Schempp, 1963 (paraphrased) (paraphrased)

Freedom Around the World

24.4

A Right for Everyone

Main ideaThe Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 names freedom of thought, conscience and religion as a right for all people.

In December 1948, just after World War II, the United Nations met in Paris. Delegates from many countries voted to adopt the . Eleanor Roosevelt, the former U.S. First Lady, led the committee that wrote it. Many delegates had seen people killed because of their religion or background during the war.

Article 18 of the Declaration is about religion. It says everyone has the right to freedom of thought, and religion. Conscience is a person’s inner sense of right and wrong. The article includes the right to change one’s religion. It also covers practicing faith alone or with others, in public or in private.

The Declaration is not a law that courts enforce by itself. It is a statement of shared goals. Many later treaties and national constitutions draw on it. People who work for religious freedom around the world still point to Article 18.

From the source
Article 18
Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion. This includes the freedom to change one's religion or belief, and to show it in teaching, practice, worship and observance, alone or with others, in public or in private.
Universal Declaration of Human Rights, United Nations, 1948, Article 18 (paraphrased) (paraphrased)

24.5

When Freedom Is Denied

Main ideaIn many places people still face harm for their religion, and watchdog groups track these limits.

In August 2017, hundreds of thousands of Rohingya people crossed from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Most carried only what they could hold. The Rohingya are a mostly Muslim people. United Nations investigators reported mass killings and burned villages. By the end of that year, estimates said about 700,000 had fled.

This is : harming or punishing people because of who they are or what they believe. It has happened to people of many faiths. In 2014, the group called ISIS attacked Yazidi communities in northern Iraq. Other groups report that Christians, Muslims, Baha’is, Jews and others face pressure in various countries today. People with no religion can face it too.

Groups such as the Pew Research Center measure how much governments and neighbors limit religion. Their studies find that limits are high in many countries. Religious freedom is still unfinished work. Many people of faith, and many without faith, work together to protect it.

Chicago and the Count

24.6

The 1893 Parliament

Main ideaThe World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago in 1893 was an early meeting of many world traditions in one place.

On September 11, 1893, a hall in downtown Chicago filled with thousands of people. The building later became the home of the Art Institute of Chicago. On the stage sat leaders from many faiths. It was the opening of the World&#x27;s Parliament of Religions, held alongside the World’s Columbian Exposition, a great fair.

A young Hindu monk from India, , rose to speak. He began, Sisters and brothers of America. The crowd stood and cheered. He spoke about acceptance of all faiths. Other speakers included Anagarika Dharmapala, a Buddhist from Sri Lanka, and Jewish, Christian and Muslim speakers. It was one of the first times many Americans heard Hindus and Buddhists speak for their own traditions.

Historians note the Parliament had limits. Most organizers were Protestant Christians, and many traditions were left out or had few voices. Still, it is often named as a starting point for dialogue in the West. In 1993, a hundred years later, a new Parliament of the World’s Religions met again in Chicago.

Chicago's Houses of Worship

Main ideaThe Chicago area is home to houses of worship from nearly every major world tradition.

Drive north from Chicago along Lake Michigan and you reach Wilmette. There a white, lacy dome rises above gardens. It is the Bahá&#x27;í House of Worship, dedicated in 1953. It has nine sides and nine doors. Baha’is believe the doors welcome people of every faith. Anyone may enter to pray or sit quietly.

The region holds many more. In Lemont, the Hindu Temple of Greater Chicago stands on a hill. In Bridgeview, the Mosque Foundation serves a large Muslim community. Chicago has historic Catholic churches, Black Protestant churches, Orthodox cathedrals, synagogues of every branch of Judaism, Buddhist temples and Sikh gurdwaras. The American Indian Center serves the city’s Native community.

Groups in the city also work across lines of faith. Neighbors from different congregations serve meals together, speak up after attacks on a house of worship and hold interfaith services. This kind of , many groups living side by side and taking part in public life, is one answer to how people share a city.

24.8

Counting the World's Faiths

Main ideaResearchers estimate how many people follow each religion, but the numbers differ because counting belief is hard.

How many Christians are there in the world? How many Hindus or Buddhists? Researchers try to answer with data and large surveys. A census is an official count of a country’s people. Some countries ask about religion on the census. Others, including the United States, do not.

Groups such as the Pew Research Center combine many sources to make an , a careful best guess. Their numbers are rounded. Different groups get different numbers for several reasons. Some people follow two traditions at once. In China and Japan, many people visit temples and honor ancestors but do not say they belong to a religion.

People also answer differently depending on how a question is asked. Someone may say I am Jewish to describe family and culture, or I am not religious to describe belief, and both can be true for the same person. So every chart of world religions is an estimate. Good readers check who made it, what year it covers, and how they defined each group.

24.9

People With No Religion

Main ideaA large and growing share of people do not belong to any religion, and they hold a wide range of views.

When a survey asks, What is your religion? some people check a box that says none. Researchers call this group the , or nones. In recent Pew surveys, about three in ten adults in the United States describe themselves this way. The share has grown since the 1990s.

Nones are not all alike. An believes there is no God or gods. An holds that people cannot know whether God exists, or is not sure. Many nones are neither. They may pray, believe in God or feel spiritual, but not belong to any group. Some describe themselves as spiritual but not religious.

Many nonreligious people draw on humanism, a way of life based on reason, science and caring for others. The American Humanist Association and similar groups hold gatherings and help people in need. A course on world religions includes the nonreligious, because they are part of every society’s conversation about meaning and values.

Living Together

24.10

Dividing and Uniting

Main ideaReligion has been part of both terrible conflicts and great movements for justice.

From 1618 to 1648, much of central Europe was torn by the Thirty Years’ War. Catholic and Protestant rulers fought, along with struggles over land and power. Millions died from war, hunger and disease. In 1947, the partition of British India into India and Pakistan brought violence between Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs. Estimates of the dead range from about 200,000 to 2 million, and millions fled their homes.

Religion has also brought people together. In the 1800s, many Quakers and Black church leaders worked to end slavery. In March 1965, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., a Baptist minister, led marchers from Selma, Alabama. Beside him walked Rabbi Abraham Joshua Heschel, Catholic nuns, Protestant clergy and people of no faith. Heschel later said he felt his legs were praying.

Historians remind us that conflicts called religious usually have many causes: land, money, power and fear. Faith can be used to divide people. It can also give them reasons to stand together. Studying both sides helps you read the news with care.

24.11

Disagreeing With Respect

Main ideaPeople can disagree deeply about religion and still treat one another with respect.

Two friends are eating lunch. One says her family fasts during Ramadan. The other says his family eats together every Sunday after church. Neither has to agree with the other’s beliefs. But each can ask questions, listen and learn. This is : holding your own view while treating the other person fairly.

Many traditions teach a version of what is often called the . In the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus tells his followers to treat others as they would want to be treated. In the Talmud, Rabbi Hillel is reported to have said that what is hateful to you, you should not do to your neighbor. Confucius taught something similar in the Analects. Many nonreligious people hold this rule too.

A few habits help. Ask, do not assume. Say Many Hindus believe, not All Hindus think. Let people describe their own faith. Disagree with ideas, not with a person’s worth. These habits are the same ones you have used all year to study religion.

From the source

Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets.
Matthew 7:12, King James Version, 1611

Hillel's version says what not to do. This version says what to do. How are they alike, and how are they different?
