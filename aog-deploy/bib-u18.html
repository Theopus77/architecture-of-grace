The Bible in Culture and Capstone

Capstone

This is the last unit of the course, and it turns outward and then back in. First it follows the Bible out of the synagogue and the church into the wider world. You will meet Milton's epic and Morrison's novels, the Sistine ceiling and Handel's Messiah. You will hear the spirituals, the sermons of 1860 and Lincoln's answer in 1865. You will read the speeches of Frederick Douglass and Martin Luther King Jr. And you will study the Supreme Court cases that explain why a public school can study this book at all. At every stop you will ask what a reader kept, what a reader changed, and what a reader's own moment supplied.

Then it asks you to do the work yourself. The capstone is a close reading of one passage, argued from the text. You will choose it with care and read it in two translations. You will map its genre and shape, trace its key words, and set it in its world. You will check it against Jewish, Christian and academic readers. Then you will write a thesis about what the passage does and means, support it with citations, and state the readings you do not adopt in their strongest form. You will deliver the argument twice: as an essay and as a short presentation with the text open.

By the end you will be able to trace a verse through a culture. You will be able to argue about a passage with evidence and attribute every belief to the people who hold it. You will be able to read a sacred text in a room full of people who read it differently. Those are skills for the rest of your life. This book turns up in courts, museums, elections and conversations, and you will know how to read it when it does.

The big question

How has the Bible shaped the culture around you, and how do you read one of its passages closely, fairly and with an argument of your own?

Reception
Big questionHow has one book shaped writers, artists, courts and public arguments in the English-speaking world, and why do readers who share it still disagree?
The story

Two Sermons, One Text, and a Third Reading

In 1860 two American preachers opened the same Bible and argued opposite things; five years later a president answered them both.

In the winter of 1860, as states began to leave the Union, ministers across the country preached on slavery. At the First Presbyterian Church in Brooklyn, a minister named Henry J. Van Dyke argued that the Bible allowed slavery. He pointed to laws in Leviticus that regulated the holding of enslaved people, to the letter to Philemon, in which Paul sends an enslaved man back to his master, and to Ephesians 6:5, which tells servants to obey their masters. The text, he said, had never called slavery a sin.

In the same months, at Plymouth Church a short walk away, Henry Ward Beecher preached the opposite from the same book. He pointed to Exodus, where God frees enslaved people from Egypt, to Exodus 21:16, which makes stealing and selling a person a capital crime, to the Golden Rule in Matthew 7:12, and to Galatians 3:28, which says that in Christ there is neither slave nor free. Slavery, he said, broke the spirit of the whole book. Both men were trained readers. Both quoted chapter and verse. They could not both be right, and each side had already split churches over the question.

On March 4, 1865, with the war nearly over, Abraham Lincoln gave his second inaugural address. It is only about 700 words long, and it quotes or echoes the Bible several times. Lincoln did not choose a side in the sermon war. He said that both North and South read the same Bible and prayed to the same God, and that each asked God's help against the other. Then he quoted Matthew 18:7 on offenses and Psalm 19:9 on the justice of God's judgments, and he asked the nation to finish its work with malice toward none.

Historians still argue about what Lincoln meant. Some read the speech as a sermon of its own, one that treats the war as a judgment on the whole nation for slavery. Others read it as a careful refusal to claim that God was on anyone's side. The lesson for a reader of the Bible is plain either way. A text that many people share does not settle a question by itself. How people read it, which passages they weigh, and what they bring to the page all shape what they find there.

This chapter follows the Bible out of the synagogue and the church. It goes into poems, paintings, oratorios, spirituals, courtrooms and speeches. You will meet Milton and Morrison, Michelangelo and Handel, the Puritans and Frederick Douglass. You will also meet a 1963 Supreme Court case from Pennsylvania. That case explains why a public school like yours can study this book at all.

Talk about itVan Dyke and Beecher read the same Bible and reached opposite conclusions. What, besides the words on the page, could explain the difference?

The Bible in English Literature

35.1

Milton Retells Genesis

Main ideaParadise Lost (1667) turns the few verses of Genesis 2–3 into a twelve-book epic, and the choices Milton made still shape how many English readers picture Eden, Satan and the fall.

Genesis 2–3 tells the story of the garden, the tree, the serpent and the leaving of Eden in about fifty verses. John Milton, an English poet who had gone blind, dictated a poem on those chapters that runs to more than ten thousand lines. was published in 1667 in ten books and revised into twelve in 1674. Milton announced his aim in the opening lines: to tell of humanity’s first disobedience and the fruit of the forbidden tree, and to justify the ways of God to men. Genesis has no such sentence. The purpose is Milton’s.

Milton filled the gaps that Genesis leaves open. Genesis never names the serpent as Satan; that reading came later in Jewish and Christian tradition. Milton makes Satan a fallen angel with speeches, a war in heaven and a long journey to Earth. Genesis says nothing about what Adam and Eve talked about; Milton gives them arguments, work and a marriage. Readers have noticed that his Satan is so vivid that the poet William Blake later joked that Milton was of the devil’s party without knowing it. That is a claim about the poem, not about Genesis.

This is an example of what scholars call : the history of how a text has been read, retold and reused. Reception study does not ask whether Milton got Genesis right. It asks what he did with it and why. Milton wrote after the English Civil War, as a Puritan who had served a government that had executed a king. His questions about obedience, freedom and authority were the questions of his day. When students say the Bible teaches that Satan was the snake in the garden, they are often remembering Milton, not the Hebrew text, which calls the creature simply the serpent.

35.2

Bunyan's Road and Melville's Whale

Main ideaThe Pilgrim's Progress (1678) and Moby-Dick (1851) show two ways literature uses the Bible: as a map for an allegory and as a store of names, sermons and questions.

John Bunyan was a tinker and preacher who spent about twelve years in Bedford jail for preaching outside the Church of England. There he began The Pilgrim&#x27;s Progress, published in 1678. It is an , a story in which characters and places stand for ideas. A man named Christian leaves the City of Destruction with a burden on his back and travels through the Slough of Despond, Vanity Fair and the Valley of the Shadow of Death toward the Celestial City. Bunyan built the road out of Bible verses: the burden echoes Psalm 38:4, the valley comes from Psalm 23:4, and the margins of early editions cite chapter and verse.

For two centuries The Pilgrim’s Progress was among the most read books in English after the Bible itself. It was carried to America by Puritans, printed cheaply, and given to children. When Louisa May Alcott wrote Little Women in 1868, the March sisters play at being pilgrims with burdens, and readers were expected to catch the reference. Bunyan’s book shows one kind of reception: the Bible used as a map. Readers who knew the verses could follow the road, and readers who did not could still follow the story.

Herman Melville used the Bible differently in (1851). His narrator opens with ’Call me Ishmael,’ the name of Abraham’s exiled son in Genesis 16 and 21. His captain is named Ahab, after the king in 1 Kings whom the text calls the wickedest of Israel’s rulers. Early in the novel Father Mapple preaches a long sermon on Jonah, the prophet who ran from God and was swallowed by a great fish. Melville does not turn the Bible into a tidy lesson. He borrows its names and its questions, about fate, pride and an unanswerable God, and leaves them open. Critics have noted that Job, with its whirlwind and its Leviathan (Job 41), sits behind the whole book.

35.3

Toni Morrison and the Bible Reread

Main ideaToni Morrison's novels take biblical names and verses and reread them through African American history, showing that reception can question a text as well as honor it.

Toni Morrison, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1993, grew up in Ohio in a family that read the Bible aloud. Her novels are full of it. (1977) takes its title from the biblical book of love poetry, and its characters carry names picked from the Bible at random, a family practice that some Black families actually followed. One character is named Pilate, after the Roman governor, because her father put his finger on the page. The novel asks what a name means when its story is not your own.

(1987) opens with an from Romans 9:25, where Paul quotes the prophet Hosea about calling a people who were not a people ’beloved.’ Morrison sets that verse over the story of a woman who escaped slavery in Kentucky, based on the real case of Margaret Garner in 1856. The verse does not explain the novel. It hangs over it as a question: who is called beloved, and by whom? Critics have read the epigraph as a claim, as a plea and as an accusation. Morrison did not settle it.

Her 1997 novel Paradise is set in an all-Black town in Oklahoma whose founders read Exodus as their own story, a people led out of oppression into a promised land. Then the town turns on a house of women outside it. The book uses the biblical pattern to ask what happens when a chosen people begins to exclude. Reception of this kind does not simply repeat the Bible. It talks back to it, using its own language. Readers of many faiths and of none have found that Morrison makes them reread the verses she borrows.

The Bible in Art and Music

35.4

Michelangelo and Rembrandt

Main ideaPainters had to decide what a verse looks like; Michelangelo's Sistine ceiling (1508–1512) and Rembrandt's biblical scenes are readings of the text made in paint.

Genesis 1 never describes God’s appearance. Between 1508 and 1512 Michelangelo painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in Rome for Pope Julius II, and in the center he painted nine scenes from Genesis: creation, Adam and Eve, Noah. In the , an old bearded God reaches toward a reclining Adam, their fingers almost touching. Genesis 2:7 says God formed the man from dust and breathed into his nostrils. Michelangelo painted a gesture, not a breath. For five centuries that image has been how many people picture the verse.

Every painting is a reading. A painter must choose a moment, a face, a setting and what to leave out. Michelangelo gave God a body, which Jewish tradition and many Christians would not draw at all, since Exodus 20:4 forbids images of anything in heaven above. Islamic art likewise avoids depicting God or the prophets. So the Sistine ceiling belongs to one tradition’s way of reading, the Western Catholic one of Renaissance Italy, and not to all readers of Genesis. His Last Judgment (1536–1541), on the altar wall, reads Matthew 25 and Revelation 20 the same way, with bodies rising and falling.

A century later Rembrandt van Rijn, working in the Protestant Dutch Republic, painted and etched hundreds of biblical scenes. He set them in dim rooms with ordinary faces, often modeled on his Amsterdam neighbors; he lived near the city’s Jewish quarter. His (c. 1668) shows the father from Luke 15 with his hands on the kneeling son’s back, while the older brother stands stiffly to one side. Luke’s parable does not describe the father’s hands. Rembrandt’s choice to make them the center of the picture is his interpretation, and it has shaped how readers picture the parable since.

35.5

Handel's Messiah and the Spirituals

Main ideaHandel's Messiah (1741) set King James verses to music for concert halls, while enslaved African Americans made the Exodus and the prophets into songs of their own; both are readings of the text in sound.

In 1741 George Frideric Handel, a German-born composer living in London, wrote an in about three weeks. An oratorio is a large work for singers and orchestra on a sacred subject, performed without costumes or acting. His friend Charles Jennens had assembled the words entirely from the King James Bible and the psalms of the Book of Common Prayer. opens with Isaiah 40:1, ’Comfort ye, comfort ye my people,’ moves through prophecies that Jennens read as pointing to Jesus, then the Passion and resurrection, and ends with Revelation. It was first performed in Dublin in 1742.

Jennens’ choice of verses is itself an interpretation. He placed Isaiah 7:14 and Isaiah 53 next to Gospel passages so that listeners would hear the Hebrew prophets as foretelling Christ. That is the traditional Christian reading. Jewish readers of the same chapters hear Isaiah speaking to his own time about a child soon to be born and about the suffering of Israel. Messiah does not argue the point; it simply sings one reading. Knowing that helps a listener hear the ’Hallelujah’ chorus, drawn from Revelation 19 and 11, as a piece of eighteenth-century Christian reception rather than as the Bible itself.

At the same time, in the American South, enslaved people who were often forbidden to read were hearing the same book and making it their own in . ’Go Down, Moses’ turns Exodus into a demand: let my people go. ’Swing Low, Sweet Chariot’ takes Elijah’s chariot from 2 Kings 2. ’Ezekiel Saw the Wheel’ comes from Ezekiel 1. Frederick Douglass wrote that these songs carried double meanings, and that ’Canaan’ could mean heaven or the free North. After the Civil War the Fisk Jubilee Singers, from a school for freed people in Nashville, toured from 1871 and carried the spirituals into concert halls. Handel and the spirituals stand at opposite ends of power, yet both read the Bible aloud in music.

Section 3

The Bible in American History

35.6

Puritans and a City on a Hill

Main ideaThe Puritans of New England read the Bible as their own story, and the phrase 'city upon a hill' from Matthew 5:14 has traveled from a 1630 sermon into American political speech.

In 1630 John Winthrop sailed toward Massachusetts with a fleet of Puritan settlers. In a sermon usually called ’A Model of Christian Charity,’ he told them they would be as a city upon a hill, with the eyes of all people on them. The phrase comes from Matthew 5:14, where Jesus tells his followers that a city set on a hill cannot be hid. Winthrop meant it as a warning as much as a promise: if they failed, the whole world would see. The were English Protestants who wanted to purify the Church of England, and they read the Bible as a book about themselves.

That habit of reading shaped the colony. The Puritans called their journey an exodus, the Atlantic a Red Sea and New England a wilderness, following the pattern of the Torah. The first book printed in the English colonies was the Bay Psalm Book of 1640, a new translation of the Psalms for singing. In 1663 John Eliot printed a complete Bible in the Massachusett language, the first Bible printed in the Americas, for use in his missions to Native people. Historians note that the same biblical pattern that gave the settlers hope also let some of them read Native nations as the Canaanites of Joshua, with grave results.

Winthrop’s phrase has had a long afterlife. It was little known until the twentieth century, when historians such as Perry Miller revived it. Presidents John F. Kennedy in 1961 and Ronald Reagan, who often said ’shining city on a hill,’ made it a standard piece of American political speech, and later presidents of both parties have used it. Each use is a reception, and often a change: Winthrop’s warning to a small religious community became a description of a nation. Tracing a phrase from Matthew to Winthrop to a modern speech is one way to see how the Bible moves through a culture.

35.7

Scripture on Both Sides of Slavery

Main ideaIn the decades before the Civil War, defenders and opponents of slavery both argued from the Bible, and the debate split American churches before it split the nation.

Defenders of slavery in the American South built a biblical case. They cited Genesis 9, where Noah curses Canaan, the son of Ham, and read it, without support in the text, as a curse on African peoples. They cited laws in Leviticus 25 that allowed Israelites to hold foreign slaves, Paul’s instruction in Ephesians 6:5 that servants obey their masters, and Paul’s letter to Philemon, which returns an enslaved man named Onesimus to his master. They argued that neither Jesus nor Paul had condemned slavery outright. Many of these preachers were learned, and they were confident the plain text was on their side.

answered from the same book. They pointed to Exodus, in which God hears the cry of enslaved people and brings them out of Egypt. They cited Exodus 21:16, which makes stealing and selling a person a capital crime, and argued that American slavery began in exactly that theft. They cited the Golden Rule in Matthew 7:12, Paul’s claim in Galatians 3:28 that in Christ there is neither slave nor free, and Acts 17:26, that God made all nations of one blood. Some argued that the spirit of the whole Bible outweighed particular verses; others said the verses about slavery described a different institution with different rules.

The argument tore churches apart. The Methodists divided over slavery in 1844 and the Baptists in 1845, when the Southern Baptist Convention was formed; the Presbyterians followed. Historians such as Mark Noll have called this a theological crisis: Americans who agreed that the Bible was the highest authority could not agree on what it said, and in the end the question was settled by war rather than by exegesis. For students today the lesson is about method. A verse can be quoted for almost anything; a reading must account for context, for the whole book and for the interests of the reader.

35.8

Douglass, Lincoln and King

Main ideaFrederick Douglass, Abraham Lincoln and Martin Luther King Jr. each used the Bible in public argument, and each read it against the way it was being used around them.

Frederick Douglass escaped slavery in 1838 and published his Narrative in 1845. He had learned to read partly by studying the Bible. He had also seen slaveholders who were church members. In an appendix to the Narrative he drew a sharp line between what he called the Christianity of this land and the Christianity of Christ. He loved the one, he wrote, and hated the other. In his 1852 speech ’What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?’ he quoted Psalm 137, the exiles weeping by the rivers of Babylon. He asked how he could celebrate a freedom he did not share. Douglass used the Bible as a witness against those who quoted it.

Lincoln’s Second Inaugural of March 4, 1865, is the shortest of his great speeches and the most biblical. He noted that both sides read the same Bible and prayed to the same God. He called it strange that any man should ask God’s help in wringing bread from the sweat of other men’s faces, an echo of Genesis 3:19. He quoted Matthew 18:7 on the offenses that must come. He suggested that the war might be a judgment on the whole nation for slavery. Then he quoted Psalm 19:9, that the judgments of the Lord are true and righteous altogether. He closed with the call to act with malice toward none and charity for all. Lincoln belonged to no church, and historians debate his beliefs. His use of the text is what the speech shows.

On August 28, 1963, at the March on Washington, Martin Luther King Jr. gave the speech known as ’I Have a Dream.’ King was a Baptist minister. Near the end he drew on Amos 5:24, the call for justice to roll down like waters. He also drew on Isaiah 40:4–5, where every valley shall be exalted and the glory of the Lord revealed. In his ’Letter from Birmingham Jail’ that same year, he compared himself to Paul answering a call for help, and he cited the prophets. King’s speeches are still under copyright, so they are described here rather than quoted. The verses themselves are not. King read the prophets as a demand on the present, in the tradition of the spirituals and of Douglass.

Abington v. Schempp and the Public School

Main ideaIn 1963 the Supreme Court ruled that public schools may not hold devotional Bible readings, and in the same opinion said that studying the Bible for its literary and historical value is permitted.

Curlett, was decided with it.

The Court’s reasoning had two parts. First, the government must be neutral toward religion, neither favoring nor opposing it. A required devotional reading, even with an opt-out, was a religious exercise led by the state. Second, and just as important for this course, Clark wrote that the Bible is worthy of study for its literary and historic qualities, and that nothing in the decision forbids studying the Bible or religion when it is presented objectively as part of a secular program of education. That sentence is the legal ground on which a public school course like this one stands.

Illinois had already been in this story. In 1948, in McCollum v. Board of Education, a case from Champaign, Illinois, the Court struck down a program that brought religious teachers into public school buildings during the school day. The line the cases draw is between teaching religion, which public schools may not do, and teaching about religion, which they may. Later cases kept the line: Engel v. Vitale (1962) ended state-written school prayer, and Stone v. Graham (1980) removed required Ten Commandments posters from Kentucky classrooms. Courts continue to hear new cases on these questions, so the details keep changing while the basic distinction has held.

Translation and the Spread of Reading

Main ideaBible translation has driven literacy and printing for five centuries, from Luther and Tyndale to the Cherokee syllabary, and the Bible remains the most translated book in the world.

Translation and literacy have moved together. Johannes Gutenberg’s first major printed book, around 1455, was a Latin Bible. Martin Luther’s German New Testament of 1522 helped fix the German language and gave ordinary people a reason to learn to read. William Tyndale’s English New Testament of 1526 was smuggled into England, and Tyndale was executed in 1536; much of his wording survived into the King James Version of 1611. Wherever a Bible appeared, a language of everyday speech gained a printed standard, and schools followed. Many readers learned their letters from a Bible or a catechism.

The same pattern crossed the Atlantic and moved into new languages. John Eliot’s Massachusett Bible of 1663 was the first Bible printed in the Americas. In the 1820s Sequoyah created a syllabary for the Cherokee language, and within a few years Cherokee speakers were reading a Cherokee newspaper and portions of the New Testament in their own script. Missionary translators often had to write down a language for the first time, which preserved some languages and, critics note, sometimes altered the cultures they entered. The American Bible Society, founded in 1816, and the British and Foreign Bible Society, founded in 1804, printed Bibles by the million.

The work continues. Groups such as Wycliffe Bible Translators, founded in 1942, aim to put at least part of the Bible into every language. By common estimates, the complete Bible now exists in more than 700 languages, the New Testament in about 1,600 more, and some portion in over 3,600 languages, out of roughly 7,000 spoken in the world. Figures vary by source and year. In Chicago, the Moody Bible Institute, founded in the 1880s, has trained translators and missionaries for generations, and the city’s libraries hold Bibles in the languages of every immigrant community that has settled here. A translated Bible is both a religious act and, for many peoples, the first book in their language.

Tracing a Verse Through a Culture

Main ideaA reception study follows one verse or story from its text through later art, speech and argument, asking at each step what was kept, what changed and why.

This chapter has shown the Bible in poems, paintings, songs, sermons, speeches and court opinions. A reception study puts that method to work on one passage. Begin with the text in a named translation and its own setting. Isaiah 40, for instance, opens with comfort to a people in exile in Babylon, in the sixth century BCE by most scholars’ dating. Then follow it forward. Handel’s Messiah sings its first verse as a prophecy of Jesus. King echoed its fourth and fifth verses in 1963 as a promise for the United States. Each user kept the words and moved the setting.

Ask three questions at every stop. First, what did the reader keep? King kept the sweep of Isaiah’s valleys and mountains. Second, what did the reader change or drop? Handel’s librettist dropped the exile and Babylon. Third, what did the reader’s own moment supply? Winthrop’s 1630 fleet, Lincoln’s 1865 war, Morrison’s 1987 memory of slavery. Reception study is not a hunt for misreadings. It is a way of seeing that every reading, including your own, happens somewhere and for some reason.

The final rule is fairness. Describe each reading as its readers would recognize it. Say that Christians hear Isaiah 7:14 as pointing to Jesus and that Jewish tradition hears a child of Isaiah’s own time, and do not decide between them. Say that Van Dyke and Beecher both quoted the text, and show what each left out. Say that Michelangelo’s God has a body and that Jewish and Muslim artists would not draw one. This is the discipline the next chapter asks you to practice on a passage of your own: read closely, attribute every claim, and let the disagreements stand where they are.

That was already an argument.

The commentaries pulled in three directions. A Christian commentary pointed to Matthew 1:5, where Ruth appears in the genealogy of Jesus, and read her as a Gentile brought into God's people. A university commentary set the book in the Persian period and read it as a quiet answer to Ezra 9–10, where foreign wives are sent away. Each reading used the same seventeen words.

Maya's first draft tried to say all three were right. Her teacher handed it back with one note: what do you think the text is doing? She went back to the Hebrew word hesed, which appears in Ruth 1:8, 2:20 and 3:10 and which the KJV renders as kindness. Her thesis became this: Ruth's speech is the book's first act of hesed, a loyalty that goes past what any law requires, and the book keeps measuring every other character against it. She could support that from the text. She could say how Rashi, the Christian commentary and the Persian-period reading each fit or strained against it. And she did not need to decide whether God had anything to do with it.

Her presentation was seven minutes long. A classmate who was Jewish said Rashi's reading had felt right to him and asked why she had not gone further with it. Another, who was not religious, asked whether hesed was really different from ordinary kindness. Maya answered both from the text, page open. That, her teacher said afterward, is the whole course in one afternoon: a passage, its words, its readers and a student who can argue about it fairly.

A Passage and a Question

Main ideaA capstone begins with a passage short enough to read closely and a question that the text can answer and that its readers have answered differently.

The first decision is size. A capstone passage should be something you can read in full several times and hold in your head: a , the scholars’ word for a self-contained unit, such as a psalm, a parable, a speech, a law or a scene. Ruth 1:16–17 is two verses. The parable of the prodigal son is twenty-two. Genesis 22, the binding of Isaac, is nineteen. Psalm 137 is nine. A whole chapter of Romans is too much for one argument; a single verse is often too little to show how it works. Choose the unit the text itself marks off by a change of speaker, place or subject.

The second decision is the question. A weak question can be answered by looking something up: who was Ruth’s husband? A weak question of another kind cannot be answered from the text at all: did this really happen, or is this the word of God? Those questions belong to history and to faith, and this course leaves them there. A strong question asks what the text is doing and how: what is Ruth promising, and to whom? Why does Psalm 137 end the way it does? What does the older brother’s silence do in Luke 15? Strong questions have a range of defensible answers, and readers over the centuries have in fact given different ones.

Test your question against three checks before you commit. Can the text answer it, at least in part, from its own words? Have Jewish, Christian or academic readers actually disagreed about it, so that you will have something to weigh? Can you state, in one sentence, what would count as evidence? If a question fails a check, narrow it or move it. Write the passage, the translation you started with, and the question on one card and keep it in front of you. Every later step in the capstone returns to that card.

Two Translations Side by Side

Main ideaReading a passage in two translations exposes the choices every translator makes and points you toward the words that carry the argument.

No translation is neutral. Every one chooses between words, and the choices add up to a reading. Set two translations of your passage in parallel columns and mark every difference, however small. Some will be style: thee and thou against you. Some will be vocabulary: kindness against loyalty, virgin against young woman, charity against love. Some will be punctuation and line breaks, which the Hebrew and Greek manuscripts largely lack. Each difference is a place where the translator had to decide, and each is a possible doorway into your argument.

Translations sit on a spectrum. At one end is , which follows the words and order of the original as closely as English allows; the King James Version (1611), the American Standard Version (1901) and the JPS Tanakh of 1917 lean this way. At the other end is , which aims at the meaning of a sentence in natural English; many twentieth-century versions lean this way. Neither is more faithful in every case. A formal translation keeps a strange phrase strange; a dynamic one may explain it and hide the strangeness. For a capstone, one of each is the most useful pair.

Public-domain translations can be quoted freely. Modern ones such as the NIV, NRSV, ESV or NJPS (1985) are under copyright, so describe or paraphrase them with a citation. When you find a difference that matters, do not stop at the English. A study Bible’s notes, an interlinear text or a good lexicon will tell you the underlying Hebrew or Greek word and its range of meaning. Then you can say, with evidence, that the King James has one reading and the JPS another. You can also say whether the word behind both can bear either.

Main ideaNaming a passage's genre and mapping its structure tells you what kind of claims it can make and where its weight falls.

Every passage belongs to a , a kind of writing with its own rules. A law states a case and a consequence. A psalm addresses God in parallel lines. A parable tells a short story that turns on a surprise. A prophetic oracle speaks in God’s name to a specific audience. A letter answers a situation the reader can only partly see. Then map the . Where does the passage begin and end, and what marks the borders? Who speaks, and to whom, and when does the speaker change? What is repeated? Main ideaHistorical context, stated carefully and with 'c.' where dates are uncertain, explains what a passage's first hearers would have taken for granted.

A passage carries assumptions its first audience shared and you do not. Reconstructing that world is the work of , and it is where a capstone most often goes wrong by overstating.

Use dates with care. Scholars date the book of Ruth anywhere from the early monarchy to the Persian period, roughly the tenth to the fourth century BCE. The honest sentence is this: most scholars date Ruth to the Persian period, though some argue for an earlier date. Write ’c.’ before any date that is not fixed. Key Words in Hebrew and Greek

Main ideaTracing one key word through your passage and the rest of the Bible often supplies the strongest evidence in a capstone.

Behind every English Bible stand Hebrew and Aramaic for the Hebrew Bible and Greek for the New Testament. You do not need to read those languages to use them. Start with the two or three words in your passage that carry the most weight, and find the original word behind each. Then see where else it appears and how it is translated there.

The Hebrew word is the classic example. The King James renders it kindness in Ruth 1:8, 2:20 and 3:10, mercy in Psalm 23:6 and lovingkindness in Psalm 36:7. Jewish, Christian and Academic Readers

Main ideaEvery capstone consults at least three kinds of readers, Jewish, Christian and academic, and reports each reading in its own terms with attribution.

The Bible has been read closely for more than two thousand years, and a capstone joins that conversation rather than starting it. Jewish reading reaches back to the rabbis of the and Talmud, through medieval commentators such as Rashi (1040–1105) and Ibn Ezra, to modern scholars. Rashi’s commentary, printed beside the text in traditional Jewish Bibles, explains word by word and draws on earlier midrash. On Ruth 1:16 he reads Ruth’s replies as answers to Naomi’s instruction in the laws a convert must accept. That is a Jewish reading, and it should be reported as one.

Christian reading runs from the New Testament’s own use of the Hebrew Bible, through the church fathers such as Augustine, the medieval fourfold sense, the Reformers Luther and Calvin, to modern commentaries of every denomination. Christian readers of Ruth often note Matthew 1:5, where Ruth stands in the genealogy of Jesus, and read her as a Gentile brought into the people of God. That is a Christian reading, and a Jewish reader would not share the frame. Neither is the text’s own statement. Your job is to describe each as its readers would recognize it.

Academic reading, from the nineteenth century on, asks when and why a text was written. It asks how the text fits its world and how it works as literature. It does not assume the text’s religious claims are true or false. Scholars of every faith and none work this way, and they disagree among themselves. A Persian-period reading of Ruth as a reply to Ezra’s ban on foreign wives is one proposal; others date the book earlier and read it differently. Cite at least one reader of each kind by name, title and year. Then say where they agree, where they part, and which of their observations your own argument can use.

36.7

A Thesis About What the Text Does

Main ideaA capstone thesis makes a claim about what the passage does and means that is specific, arguable and answerable from the text, not a claim about whether it is true.

A is one sentence that answers your question and that someone could reasonably dispute. Compare three sentences about Ruth 1:16–17. ’Ruth’s speech is beautiful’ is a reaction, not a claim. ’Ruth’s speech shows that God rewards loyalty’ is a claim about God, which the capstone cannot argue from a public-school desk. ’Ruth’s speech is the book’s first act of hesed, and the book measures every other character against it’ is a claim about the text: specific, arguable and testable against the words on the page. That is the kind of sentence you want.

Good theses tend to use verbs about what a text does: shows, withholds, repeats, reverses, invites, refuses. They name a feature you found in the reading stage. That might be a repeated word, a break in pattern, a silence or a translation choice. Then they say what it accomplishes. A thesis can be about meaning as readers have found it, so long as the reading is attributed. For example: read alongside Ezra 9–10, as many scholars do, Ruth’s speech becomes a quiet argument that a foreign woman can be a model Israelite. The attribution is part of the thesis.

Draft the thesis early and expect to revise it. Maya’s first thesis was that all three commentaries were right, which is not a claim. Her second was that Ruth converts to Judaism, which is Rashi’s reading, not hers, and which uses a later category. Her third, about hesed, came from the text and let her use all three readers as witnesses. Write your thesis on the index card under your question. If the evidence you gather does not fit it, change the thesis, not the evidence.

36.8

Evidence and Citation

Main ideaEvidence in a capstone means quoted or paraphrased text, features of its shape and language, and attributed readings, each cited by book, chapter and verse or by author, title and year.

Evidence from the text comes first. Quote the words you are arguing from, in a named translation, and cite them by book, chapter and verse, with the translation in parentheses on first use: Ruth 1:16 (KJV). A colon separates chapter and verse, a dash joins a range, and a semicolon separates references: Ruth 1:8; 2:20; 3:10. Use standard book abbreviations if your teacher asks; the Society of Biblical Literature’s handbook is the usual guide. When you paraphrase a copyrighted translation, say so: ’the NJPS (1985) renders the word as loyalty.’ Quote only as much as you need and then explain what it shows.

Evidence from the text’s world and its readers is cited like any other source. A commentary is cited by author, title and year, and by page or verse: Robert Alter, The Hebrew Bible (2019), on Ruth 1:16. Rashi is cited as Rashi on Ruth 1:16, with the edition you used. A study Bible’s note is cited by the Bible’s name and year and the note’s verse. Your teacher will name the citation style, usually MLA or Chicago; whichever it is, the reader must be able to find every source you used. A claim without a source is an opinion, and a source without a page is a rumor.

Weigh the evidence as you present it. Strongest is the passage’s own wording, structure and repeated words, which any reader can check. Next is the wider text, such as other uses of hesed or the law in Deuteronomy 25. Then comes context from the ancient world, always with ’c.’ and attribution. Last are the readers, who are witnesses to how the text has been understood rather than proof of what it means. A paragraph that quotes a verse, names a feature, cites a reader and then states what all of that shows is the basic unit of a capstone essay.

36.9

Other Readings, Fairly Stated

Main ideaA strong capstone states the best case for readings it does not adopt, in their readers' own terms, and explains why its thesis still stands.

An argument that ignores other readings is a sermon. The capstone asks you to present at least two readings that differ from your own, and to present each so well that a person who holds it would say, yes, that is what I think. This is sometimes called , the opposite of attacking a weak version of a view. For Ruth, Maya had to state Rashi’s conversion reading with its evidence, the Christian genealogy reading with Matthew 1:5, and the Persian-period reading with Ezra 9–10, before she said how her hesed thesis related to each.

There are three honest ways to respond to a reading you do not adopt. You can show that it depends on a frame the text does not supply, such as a later legal category or a later book, while granting that it is coherent within that frame. You can show that it accounts for part of the passage but not for the feature your thesis explains, such as the break in Ruth’s pairs. Or you can absorb it: Maya found that all three readers had noticed Ruth’s loyalty and could be cited as support. What you may not do is call a reading stupid, or say a tradition is wrong about God.

The language of fairness is attribution and comparison. ’Jewish tradition reads’, ’Christian interpreters have often held’, ’many scholars argue’, ’on this reading’. Compare readings to each other and to the text, never to the truth. If a reader relies on a translation choice, say so; if a reader has textual evidence you lack, say that too. A capstone earns trust by the way it treats the views it sets aside. The reader who disagrees with your thesis should still finish your essay feeling accurately described.

36.10

Essay, Presentation and Rubric

Main ideaThe capstone is delivered twice, as a written essay and as a short presentation with the text open, and both are scored on the same rubric.

The essay runs about four to six pages. It opens with the passage quoted in full in a named translation and the question. It states the thesis by the end of the first page. Its body paragraphs follow the order of the method: genre and shape, key words, context, readers, then the argument that ties them to the thesis, then the counter-readings and your response. It closes by saying what the reading shows and what it leaves open. Every paragraph has at least one citation. The bibliography lists the translations used, at least one Jewish, one Christian and one academic reader, and any context source.

The presentation is five to seven minutes, with the passage on the screen or on paper in front of everyone. Read the passage aloud in one translation. State the question and thesis. Show one piece of evidence from the text closely, such as the break in Ruth’s pairs, one word study and one reader. Then take questions with the text open, and answer from it. The presentation is not the essay read aloud; it is the argument made in a room, where a classmate can ask why you chose one reading over another and you can point to the verse.

The rubric scores five things equally: the question and thesis; evidence from the text; use of context and readers; fairness to other readings; and clarity, including citations and plain sentences. A capstone that argues something small and supports it fully outscores one that claims much and supports little. Fairness is scored as strictly as evidence. An essay that misrepresents a tradition, states a belief as fact or ranks one tradition’s reading above another loses points there even if its reading of the text is strong.

36.11

Reading a Sacred Text in a Public School

Main ideaStudying the Bible in a public school means reading a text that is sacred to many classmates as literature and history, respecting their beliefs without adopting or dismissing them.

This course has read a book that is scripture to Jews and Christians. Muslims honor it as earlier revelation. People of every tradition and of none read it as literature and history. The 1963 Schempp decision made that study legal in a public school, when it is presented objectively as part of a secular program. Objectivity does not mean pretending the text has no religious weight. It means describing that weight accurately. This is what the text says; this is how Jewish tradition reads it; this is how Christians read it; this is what scholars think; and here is my argument about the words.

Your classmates have read the same passages from different places. One may have heard Ruth chanted in Hebrew on Shavuot. Another may have heard Luke 15 preached. Another may have met the Bible for the first time this year. In Chicago, the 1893 World’s Parliament of Religions put readers of many scriptures in one hall for the first time on that scale, and the city’s neighborhoods still hold synagogues, churches, mosques and temples within blocks of each other. A classroom like yours is that hall in small. The rules you have practiced, attribute every belief, compare rather than judge, let disagreement stand, are what let it work.

For your reflection, write one page answering three questions. What did you learn to do this year that you could not do before: read in two translations, trace a word, tell a genre, cite a reader? Where did the course’s neutrality feel hardest, and what did you do? And what will you carry out of the room: into an argument about a speech that quotes scripture, into a museum, into a conversation with someone whose tradition is not yours? The Bible will keep appearing in your life, in courts, songs, novels and elections. You now know how to read it, and how to hear how others read it.
