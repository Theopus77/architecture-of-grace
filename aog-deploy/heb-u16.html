Interpretation Across the Centuries

Interpretation

Open a printed Hebrew Bible with commentaries and the verse sits in the middle of the page, surrounded by readers. Rashi, from eleventh-century France, explains a word. Ramban, from thirteenth-century Spain, answers him. A midrash compiled around 400 CE sits behind both. That page is the subject of this unit: how the Tanakh has been read, by whom, and with what questions. Jewish tradition treats the reading as a conversation that never closes, and this unit lets you listen in.

The first chapter follows Jewish interpretation from the rabbis of the midrash to Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ramban, Radak, Maimonides and the Zohar, and ends with the printed page that holds them together. The second chapter puts Jewish readers beside Christian and academic ones. It looks at Isaiah 7, Isaiah 53, Psalm 22 and Genesis 1, at the disputations of Paris and Barcelona, at modern dialogue, and at Spinoza, Wellhausen, the archaeologists and the literary and feminist critics. Nothing here decides which reading is true. Everything here describes what each reader saw and why.

By the end you will be able to sort a reading into peshat, remez, derash or sod; explain how Rashi and Ramban differ on the same verse; describe how a rabbi, a church father and a historian read Isaiah 53 without ranking them; and name the question each kind of reader is asking. That skill, telling questions apart, is what makes it possible to study a sacred text in a public school with respect for everyone in the room.

The big question

How has one text been read so differently across a thousand years, and how can we describe those readings fairly without deciding which is true?

The story

Two Rabbis on One Page

A student opens a printed Bible and finds the verse surrounded by voices that never met in life.

Dina is sixteen and it is her first week in a synagogue library on Chicago's North Side. Her teacher hands her a heavy book and says, "Find Genesis, chapter one, verse one." She finds it. The verse sits in large square letters in the middle of the page. Around it, in smaller and stranger letters, are columns of other text. There is a column of Aramaic. There is a block at the bottom, another down the side. The verse itself is nine Hebrew words. The page is full.

"Who wrote all this?" Dina asks. Her teacher points to the inner column. "That is Rashi. He lived in Troyes, in France, and died in 1105." She points to the outer column. "That is Ramban, Rabbi Moses ben Nahman. He lived in Girona, in Spain, and died around 1270." Dina reads slowly. Rashi asks why the Torah begins with creation instead of its first commandment. He answers with a story from the rabbis. Then he reads the grammar of the first word and says it means "in the beginning of God's creating."

Ramban's column starts by praising Rashi. Then it says, in effect, that Rashi's grammar is not the whole story. Ramban reads the verse as teaching that God made the world from nothing. He also hints that there is a deeper meaning he will not spell out. Dina looks up. "So the one on the outside is arguing with the one on the inside?" "Yes," says her teacher. "And the one on the inside is quoting rabbis who lived seven hundred years before him. And the one on the outside was answering him a hundred and fifty years after he died."

This kind of page is called a Mikraot Gedolot, a "great scriptures" edition. It was first printed in Venice in 1517. The layout puts the verse in the center and lets readers from different centuries talk around it. No one voice gets the last word. Dina runs her finger from the verse to Rashi to Ramban and back. The page is a room, and the argument in it has been going on for a very long time.

This chapter walks into that room. It starts with the rabbis of the midrash, who read the Torah as a text that answers questions if you ask them well. It moves to Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ramban and Radak, the great medieval commentators. It stops at Maimonides, who asked what the Torah means when it says God has a hand. And it ends with the mystics of the Zohar and with the printed page itself.

Section 1

How the Rabbis Read

31.1

Two Kinds of Midrash

Main ideaThe rabbis of the early centuries CE read the Torah in two modes, midrash halakhah for law and midrash aggadah for story, and both begin by asking a question of the text.

comes from a Hebrew root that means to seek or to search. In Jewish tradition it names a way of reading and the books that record it. The rabbis who shaped this reading lived in the Land of Israel and Babylonia between roughly 70 and 600 CE. They did not treat a verse as a closed statement. They treated it as a door. If a word was repeated, why? If a verse could be read two ways, which way, and what follows from each? Midrash is the record of those questions and the answers offered for them.

Rabbinic tradition sorts midrash into two families. Midrash reads the legal parts of the Torah to derive rules for life. The word halakhah means "the way one walks," that is, Jewish law. The Mekhilta on Exodus, the Sifra on Leviticus and the Sifre on Numbers and Deuteronomy are the classic collections. Historians date them, in their present form, to about the third century CE, with older material inside. When Exodus 21 speaks of injury, these works ask exactly what each phrase requires.

Midrash reads the stories, poems and gaps. Aggadah means "telling." This kind of midrash fills silences in the text. What was Abraham like before God called him? The Torah does not say, so Genesis Rabbah tells of a boy who smashed his father’s idols. Aggadah does not claim to report history. Jewish tradition treats it as teaching through story. A famous rule in the Talmud says a verse never loses its plain sense, even when a midrash builds on it (Shabbat 63a).

The two families share one habit. Each starts from a real feature of the text: an odd word, a missing detail, a verse that seems to clash with another. Then it proposes a reading. Later readers can accept it, reject it or put another beside it. That habit of reading closely and answering back is the seed of everything else in this chapter.

An Eye for an Eye, Read by the Rabbis

Exodus 21:23–25 lists "life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth." The Mekhilta and the Talmud (Bava Kamma 83b–84a) read this as a rule about payment: the one who injures pays the value of the eye, not the eye itself. One of their arguments is that a literal reading cannot be fair, since a blind man who blinds someone could not be punished in kind. Some later readers say the rabbis changed the law; others say they explained what the verse always meant.

31.2

Genesis Rabbah Opens the Torah

Main ideaGenesis Rabbah reads the first word of the Torah through a verse in Proverbs and pictures the Torah as the blueprint God consulted to build the world.

is the great collection of midrash aggadah on the book of Genesis. Scholars date its compilation to the Land of Israel around the fifth century CE. It moves verse by verse and sometimes word by word. Its first section takes on the first word of the Torah, , "in the beginning." The midrash does not ask what happened first. It asks what the word "beginning" is pointing to.

The answer comes by way of another verse. Proverbs 8:22 has Wisdom say that the LORD made her as the beginning of His way. The rabbis read Wisdom as the Torah, so "in the beginning" becomes "by means of the Torah." Genesis Rabbah 1:1 then offers a , a parable. A king who wants to build a palace does not build by guesswork. He uses the plans of an architect. So, the midrash says, God looked into the Torah and created the world.

This reading does several things at once. It links two books of the Tanakh into one conversation. It makes the Torah older than creation, an idea that shaped later Jewish thought and prayer. And it turns a hard, tiny word into a picture anyone can hold. Jewish tradition took the blueprint image seriously. Medieval readers, Rashi among them, quote or answer it.

Notice what the midrash is not doing. It is not denying that the verse describes creation. It reads the plain sense and then adds a layer on top. Later readers gave names to these layers, as you will see. Genesis Rabbah simply does it, and does it first.

31.3

The Talmud Cites a Verse

Main ideaThe Talmud uses set rules of reasoning, such as gezerah shavah and kal va-chomer, to draw law from verses, and it insists that even a heavenly voice cannot overrule a verse's interpretation by the majority.

The is the record of rabbinic debate built around the Mishnah, the law code of about 200 CE. Its Babylonian version was completed around 500 to 600 CE. On almost every page a rabbi quotes a verse. The quote is rarely decoration. It is evidence. The rabbis developed rules for how a verse can prove a point. A list of thirteen such rules, credited to Rabbi Ishmael, opens the Sifra and is recited in the daily morning prayers.

Two rules come up constantly. means "light and heavy." If a rule holds in a lesser case, it should hold in the greater one. means "equal cut." If the same unusual word appears in two verses, the law of one can teach the law of the other. These are not free association. Tradition limits a gezerah shavah to pairs received from a teacher. The rules make the argument checkable by another reader.

Alongside the rules sits a principle about who decides. In a famous story (Bava Metzia 59b), Rabbi Eliezer argues a point of law and calls on miracles, and then a voice from heaven, to back him. Rabbi Joshua stands and quotes Deuteronomy 30:12: the Torah "is not in heaven." The majority of sages, not a voice from above, decides how to read the text. The Talmud adds that God laughed and said, "My children have defeated Me." Jewish tradition reads the story as a charter for human interpretation.

The Talmud’s habit of citing verses shaped every later commentator. Rashi grew up on it. Maimonides organized it. Even readers who wanted only the plain sense had to say why they departed from a talmudic reading. When you see a verse quoted in a rabbinic text, ask three questions. What point is it proving? Which rule connects verse and point? And who else, in the same passage, reads the verse differently?

The Medieval Commentators

31.4

Rashi of Troyes

Main ideaRashi (1040–1105) wrote a running commentary that explains the plain sense and selects midrash that fits the words, and his work became the first companion to the text for Jewish readers.

Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known by the acronym , was born in Troyes, in the Champagne region of France, in 1040. He studied in the Rhineland academies of Mainz and Worms and returned home to teach. Tradition says he made his living from wine, though historians are not certain. He died in 1105. His commentaries cover almost the whole Tanakh and most of the Babylonian Talmud. For nine centuries Jewish students have met the Torah with Rashi at their side.

Rashi’s method is compact. He quotes a word or phrase, then explains it in a few lines. He gives the peshat, the plain sense in context, and he brings in , midrashic reading, where he thinks it settles a real difficulty in the words. On Genesis 3:8 he states his aim: he has come only to give the plain sense of Scripture and the aggadah that fits the words in their order. That sentence became a banner for later readers who wanted to know when a comment is grammar and when it is story.

His first comment on the Torah shows both sides. He cites a question from Rabbi Isaac: the Torah is a book of commandments, so why does it not begin with the first commandment, in Exodus 12:2, instead of with creation? The answer is a midrash about the nations and the land. Then, without pause, Rashi turns to grammar. The form of bereshit, he argues, is a construct, so the verse means "in the beginning of God’s creating." It is a heading, not a claim about what came first.

Rashi’s commentary was the first dated Hebrew book to be printed, at Reggio di Calabria in 1475. The semi-cursive typeface later used for commentaries is called Rashi script, though he never wrote in it. His grandsons, among them Rashbam and Rabbenu Tam, carried on his school. Rashbam pushed the plain sense even harder than his grandfather did, and sometimes said so.

31.5

Ibn Ezra the Grammarian

Main ideaAbraham ibn Ezra (c. 1089–c. 1164) read the Torah through Hebrew grammar and plain sense, and left careful hints about verses that puzzled him.

Abraham was born in Tudela, in Muslim Spain, around 1089. He was a poet, a grammarian, an astronomer and a wanderer. After about 1140 he left Spain and traveled through Italy, France and England, writing as he went. He died around 1164. His commentary on the Torah was written for readers far from the great academies, and he says so. He wanted them to have grammar and sense, not only story.

Ibn Ezra’s introduction sorts the ways people read the Torah into several paths and rejects most of them. He will not, he says, pile up midrash where the words are plain. He will not read allegory into laws. He will follow the grammar of the Hebrew and the plain sense of the sentence, and he will bow to rabbinic tradition where law is at stake. This puts him near Rashbam and often at a distance from Rashi. He does not name Rashi much, but readers of both can feel the difference.

He is famous for hints. On Genesis 12:6, the text says the Canaanite was then in the land. Ibn Ezra notes that this could mean the verse was written when the Canaanite was no longer there, and adds that the one who understands will keep silent. On Deuteronomy 1:2 he speaks of a "secret of the twelve," which many readers connect with the last twelve verses of Deuteronomy, the account of Moses’ death. He never spells the secret out.

Later readers took those hints in different directions. Some Jewish commentators explained them within tradition, for example by saying Joshua wrote the closing verses, as one view in the Talmud holds (Bava Batra 15a). In 1670 the philosopher Spinoza quoted Ibn Ezra’s hints as early evidence for a critical study of who wrote the Torah. The next chapter picks up that story. Here, notice that the hint began as an act of close reading.

31.6

Ramban Argues with Both

Main ideaRamban (1194–1270) wrote a commentary that openly debates Rashi and Ibn Ezra, reads the ancestors' deeds as signs for their descendants, and adds mystical hints "by way of truth."

Rabbi Moses ben Nahman, called or Nahmanides, was born in Girona, in Catalonia, in 1194. He was a physician, a community leader and a scholar of Talmud. Late in life he wrote a commentary on the Torah that is long, argued and personal. In 1263 the king of Aragon required him to debate a Christian preacher in Barcelona. A few years later, in 1267, he left for the Land of Israel and died there around 1270.

Ramban’s introduction sets out his plan in verse. He will study the words of Rashi with love, and he will treat Ibn Ezra with what he calls open rebuke and hidden love. In practice he quotes both, agrees sometimes and disagrees often. On Genesis 1:1 he rejects Rashi’s construct reading and holds that the verse teaches creation from nothing. Then he adds that the true meaning of the passage is deeper than the plain sense.

One of his signature ideas is that the deeds of the ancestors are a sign for their descendants. On Genesis 12:6 he writes that what happened to Abraham foreshadows what will happen to his children. The stories are read as a pattern, not only a record. Ramban also insists that the Torah’s stories carry real theology. Where Ibn Ezra sees a grammar question, Ramban often sees a teaching about God’s care.

Ramban was one of the first major commentators to bring , Jewish mystical teaching, into a mainstream Torah commentary. He signals these readings with the phrase "by way of truth" and keeps them brief. He says plainly that they cannot be understood without a teacher. His page is thus a place where plain sense, midrash and mystery sit together, each labeled, each answering the others.

Think it through

In the poem that opens his commentary, Ramban says he will keep Rashi's words before him and study them with devotion, and that toward Ibn Ezra he will show open rebuke and hidden love. Ramban answers that the verse teaches creation from nothing and that its full meaning is deeper still.

31.7

Radak and the Book of Roots

Main ideaDavid Kimhi, Radak (c. 1160–1235), made Hebrew grammar and a dictionary of roots the tools of commentary, and read the Prophets and Psalms with an eye on Christian readings.

Rabbi David Kimhi, known as , was born around 1160 in Narbonne, in Provence, and died there around 1235. His father Joseph and brother Moses were grammarians too. The family’s name became a pun on a line in Pirkei Avot, "if there is no flour there is no Torah," since kemach means flour. Later students said that without Kimhi there is no Torah. The joke shows how central his tools became.

Radak’s most lasting works are grammar books. The Mikhlol sets out the rules of Hebrew, and the , the Book of Roots, lists Hebrew words by their three-letter roots with their meanings and examples. For centuries it was the dictionary Jewish and later Christian scholars used. When Radak comments on a verse, he often begins with the root. That gives his reading a firm base and makes it easy to check.

He wrote commentaries on Genesis, on the Prophets, on Psalms and on Chronicles. He favored plain sense, like Ibn Ezra, but he quoted midrash more freely than Ibn Ezra did. In Psalms and Isaiah he sometimes stops to answer Christian readings of a verse. Radak lived in a Christian land where such readings were preached to Jews. His notes on Psalm 22 and Isaiah 53 explain why he reads them as about David or Israel, not about Jesus.

Radak’s place on the printed page came later. He does not appear in the first Rabbinic Bibles, but many editions add him beside Rashi and Ibn Ezra, especially for the Prophets. Reading Radak after Rashi, students notice a shift. Rashi answers a difficulty with a midrash. Radak answers it with a root. Both are trying to make the verse make sense.

Philosophers and Mystics

31.8

Maimonides and the Torah's Words for God

Main ideaIn the Guide of the Perplexed, Maimonides argues that the Torah's physical language about God, its hands, eyes and image, must be read as figures of speech, because the Torah speaks in human language.

Rabbi Moses ben Maimon, known as or Rambam, was born in Córdoba, Spain, in 1138. His family fled persecution, and he settled in Fustat, near Cairo, where he worked as a physician and led the Jewish community. He died in 1204. He wrote a code of Jewish law, the Mishneh Torah, in Hebrew. He also wrote, in Judeo-Arabic, a philosophical work, the , finished around 1190 and translated into Hebrew by Samuel ibn Tibbon in 1204.

The Guide is written for a reader who knows the Torah and knows philosophy and feels torn between them. Its first part is largely a dictionary of biblical words. Chapter by chapter, Maimonides takes a term the Torah uses for God: image, likeness, sitting, standing, seeing, hand, face. He shows that each word has more than one meaning in Hebrew. Then he argues that, when applied to God, only the non-bodily meaning can be intended.

His anchor is a rabbinic principle, "the Torah speaks in the language of human beings." The rabbis used it about repeated words in legal verses. Maimonides gives it wider reach. The Torah, he says, uses pictures so that everyone can grasp something true, but a careful reader must strip the picture away. On Genesis 1:26, the of God in which humans are made is not a shape but the capacity for thought. That is what makes humans like God, and nothing else.

Not everyone accepted this. In the decades after his death, a dispute broke out over the Guide, and reports say copies were burned in Montpellier in the 1230s. Some rabbis feared it made the Torah a philosophy book. Others, including later Kabbalists, thought Maimonides had emptied God of presence. Yet his reading of divine language became standard in much of Jewish thought. Readers of Rashi and Ramban still meet him whenever a verse gives God a body.

Paris 1240 and Barcelona 1263

Main ideaMedieval disputations were staged contests in which Jewish scholars had to defend their texts before Christian judges; Ramban's account of Barcelona shows a reader arguing about what counts as binding.

A in the Middle Ages was a public debate ordered by a Christian ruler in which Jews were made to answer charges about their texts. The Jewish side could not refuse and could not win in any ordinary sense. In Paris in 1240, Nicholas Donin, a convert from Judaism, brought charges against the Talmud before the court of King Louis IX. Rabbi Yechiel of Paris and other rabbis were summoned to respond. The Talmud was condemned, and in 1242 cartloads of manuscripts were burned in Paris.

In Barcelona in 1263, the contest was over the reading of the Tanakh and the Talmud. Pablo Christiani, also a convert, argued before King James I of Aragon that rabbinic texts themselves proved that the Messiah had come. Ramban was required to answer. He later wrote his own account of the debate, and a Latin account survives from the other side. The two accounts do not agree on who prevailed. Ramban’s version circulated widely among Jews; he left Aragon a few years later.

Ramban’s most famous move was about categories. Christiani cited , rabbinic stories, as if they were binding teaching. Ramban replied that Jews are bound by the Talmud’s law and by the Tanakh, but that aggadic stories are teachings a Jew may accept or set aside. Historians debate whether this was Ramban’s settled view or a position for the debate. Either way, it shows a reader arguing not about a verse but about what kind of text a verse belongs to.

These events are described here as history. Jewish memory keeps them as times of pressure, and the burning of 1242 is mourned in a medieval lament still recited by some on the fast of Tisha B’Av. Christian churches today have, for the most part, renounced forced disputations. Knowing this history explains why modern dialogue between the two traditions insists on equal footing and on each side describing its own reading.

Spinoza's Method

Main ideaSpinoza's Theological-Political Treatise (1670) argued that the Bible should be studied like nature, from its text and history alone, and used Ibn Ezra's hints to ask whether Moses wrote all of the Torah.

Baruch was born in Amsterdam in 1632 to a family of Portuguese Jewish origin. In 1656 the leaders of the community placed him under , a ban, for opinions the surviving document does not spell out. He worked as a lens grinder and wrote philosophy. In 1670 he published, without his name, the . It was banned in the Netherlands a few years later. He died in 1677.

The treatise’s central claim about reading is a method. Spinoza argued that the way to understand scripture is the way one understands nature: gather the data, in this case the language, the history of each book and the circumstances of its writing, and draw conclusions only from that. Meaning must come from the text itself, not from philosophy or from a church’s or synagogue’s authority. He called this interpreting scripture from scripture.

Applying the method, Spinoza reviewed passages that seemed to be written after Moses. He quoted Ibn Ezra’s hints on Genesis 12:6 and Deuteronomy 1:2 and said openly what Ibn Ezra had only hinted. He proposed that Ezra the scribe, centuries after Moses, had compiled the Torah and the historical books from older writings. He read prophecy as the work of vivid imagination and miracles as events not understood, positions that both Jewish and Christian readers of his day rejected.

Historians of biblical study treat the treatise as a beginning of : the reading of the Bible with the tools of history and language and without a prior commitment to its authority. Later scholars did not all share Spinoza’s philosophy. Many religious scholars, Jewish and Christian, have used historical tools while keeping their faith. But the question he made public, who wrote these books and when, has been on the table ever since.

Wellhausen and the Four Sources

Main ideaThe documentary hypothesis, given its classic form by Wellhausen in 1878, proposed that the Torah was woven from four sources, J, E, D and P; Jewish scholars responded in different ways, and the theory has been revised.

Julius (1844–1918) was a German Protestant scholar. In 1878 he published the book known in English as the Prolegomena to the History of Israel. It gave the classic form of the . Building on earlier scholars, he argued that the Torah combines four written sources. J uses the divine name and tells vivid stories; E prefers the word Elohim; D is Deuteronomy; and P, the , cares for genealogies, ritual and the tabernacle.

Wellhausen’s most contested claim was about order. He placed P last, after the Babylonian exile, which made the Torah’s priestly law a late product rather than the foundation. He connected D with the reform of King Josiah in 622 BCE, an idea proposed by W. M. L. de Wette in 1805. The whole was an argument about history: the religion of Israel, on his account, developed from prophets to priests, and the Torah’s final shape reflected the end of that process.

Jewish responses varied. Some Orthodox scholars, such as David Zvi Hoffmann in Berlin, wrote detailed rebuttals. Umberto Cassuto, at the Hebrew University, argued in 1941 that the changes in divine names follow meaning, not sources. Others in the Reform and Conservative movements accepted much of the method. Some also noted that Wellhausen’s picture of a late, legalistic priesthood echoed Christian views of Judaism, and that critique has been taken seriously by later scholars of all backgrounds.

Today the four-source model is taught as a starting point, not a settled result. Many scholars now speak of layers and supplements rather than four complete documents, and dates for P are argued in both directions. What survives is the practice: reading the Torah for seams, repetitions and differences of style, and asking what they show about how the book came to be. That is the historian’s question, and it sits beside, not above, the rabbis’ and the church’s.
