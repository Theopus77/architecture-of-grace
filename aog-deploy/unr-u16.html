Destiny: God's Family and the Nations Restored

Endgame

Every long story is heading somewhere. In this last unit you read how Michael Heiser understood the ending of the Bible's story. He read it as a family story. God has a heavenly family and a human family. Both had rebels. In Heiser's reading, the story ends with the two families restored together, the nations won back, and God dwelling with his people in a renewed Eden.

You will read the texts he built on: John 1 and 10, Romans 8, Galatians 4, 1 Corinthians 6, Psalm 82, Isaiah 14, and Revelation 16 and 20–22. You will weigh his boldest proposals, such as his reading of Har Magedon as the mount of assembly. Beside each one you will meet other readings: human judges in Psalm 82, Israel at Sinai in rabbinic writings, Eastern Orthodox theosis, Jewish readings of God's children, and four ways to read Revelation.

By the end you will be able to explain what Heiser argued about believers' destiny and why he said they never become equal to God. You will be able to trace a word from Hebrew into Greek, compare a symbol with its Hebrew Bible source, and describe a debate fairly without taking a side.

The big question

How did Heiser read the end of the Bible's story, from God's family restored to the nations healed, and how do other readers understand the same texts?

The story

Is It Not Written, Ye Are Gods?

A student reading John 10 for a class finds Jesus quoting a psalm that calls someone gods, and she has to find out why.

Rosa had a simple assignment for her senior seminar: read John chapter 10 and write one question about it. She expected the shepherd verses. She had heard those before. Then, near the end of the chapter, she stopped. In the King James Version, Jesus answers a crowd with a question of his own: 'Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?' She read it three times.

The footnote pointed her to Psalm 82:6. She opened the psalm. It was only eight verses long. God stands in a gathering and judges beings the psalm calls gods. Then comes the line Jesus quoted: 'I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.' The next verse says they will die like men. Rosa wrote in her margin: Who are these gods, and why would Jesus bring them up?

Her teacher, Mr. Okafor, did not give her one answer. He gave her three. Many older Christian commentators read the gods as human judges. Some rabbinic writings connect the verse with Israel receiving the Torah at Sinai. And Michael Heiser, a scholar of Hebrew and the ancient world, read Psalm 82 as a scene in God's heavenly council, the divine council, with real heavenly beings on trial.

Heiser went further. He argued that the whole Bible tells one long story about God's family. God has a heavenly family and a human family. Both were meant to live with him. Both had rebels. In Heiser's reading, the story ends with the two families restored together, and the people the New Testament calls children of God taking a place in that family.

Rosa changed her question. It was no longer only about John 10. It was now: What does the Bible say believers will become, and how do different readers keep that from sounding like people turning into God? This chapter follows her question through the texts.

The Goal of the Story

31.1

Two Families, One Household

Main ideaHeiser read the Bible as the story of God's two families, heavenly and human, and argued that its goal is to bring them back together in one household.

Job 38:7 describes the morning the earth was founded. The shouted for joy, the text says, while the morning stars sang together. The scene has an audience before any human appears. Heiser pointed to verses like this one to show that the Hebrew Bible pictures God already surrounded by heavenly beings when creation begins. He called this group God’s heavenly family, and he connected it to the , the gathering of heavenly beings around God’s throne.

Then Genesis 1:26–27 has God make humans in his image. Heiser argued in The Unseen Realm (2015) that ’image’ names a role more than a shape: humans were made to represent God on earth, the way the heavenly beings served him in the unseen realm. Eden, in his reading, was the place where the two families met. God, his heavenly household and his human household shared one home.

Both families had rebels in Heiser’s reading: the serpent in Eden, the sons of God in Genesis 6, and the gods over the nations after Babel. So the story’s goal, as he described it, is a . God does not scrap the plan. He brings a human family back into his household and removes the rebels who ruled in his place. Heiser’s summary is a family story, not a courtroom or a battle story first. Other scholars describe the Bible’s big story with different themes, such as covenant, kingdom or salvation history, and many see these as overlapping.

31.2

The Nations Given Away

Main ideaHeiser read Deuteronomy 32:8–9 as God handing the nations over to lesser heavenly beings after Babel, which sets up the Bible's promise to win them back.

Deuteronomy 32:8 says that when the Most High divided the nations, he set their borders by a number. In the , the standard medieval Hebrew text, the number matches the ’sons of Israel.’ In a Dead Sea Scroll of Deuteronomy found at Qumran, and in the , the old Greek translation, the reading points instead to the ’sons of God.’ Most modern text critics favor the older ’sons of God’ reading, and several modern English translations follow it.

Heiser built a large part of his work on this verse. He connected it to Babel in Genesis 11. In his reading, when the nations rebelled at Babel, God gave them over to heavenly sons of God and kept Israel as his own portion (Deuteronomy 32:9). Those heavenly rulers later went bad, and Psalm 82 shows God judging them. Heiser called the nations ’disinherited,’ meaning they had been handed away from God’s direct care.

This matters for the story’s ending. If the nations were given away, then the goal of the story includes winning them back. Heiser read Genesis 12:3, where all families of the earth will be blessed through Abraham, as the first hint of that plan. Readers who follow the Masoretic reading usually see Deuteronomy 32:8 as a statement about Israel’s place among the peoples, without heavenly rulers over them.

31.3

The Rebels Replaced

Main ideaHeiser argued that in the story's ending, faithful humans take up the places of rule once held by rebellious heavenly beings.

Psalm 82:7 ends the trial of the gods with a verdict: they will die like men and fall like one of the princes. Heiser read this as a sentence on the heavenly rulers of the nations. They had judged unjustly (Psalm 82:2–4), so they would lose their posts. Psalm 82:8 then asks God himself to rise and judge the earth, ’for thou shalt inherit all nations’ in the King James Version. Heiser saw the last verse as a promise that God would take the nations back.

Who would rule with God after that? Heiser pointed to Daniel 7. There, ’one like the Son of man’ receives a kingdom from the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:13–14). Later in the chapter the kingdom is given to ’the saints of the most High’ (Daniel 7:18, 27). Heiser read this as God’s people sharing the rule that the rebels lost. Scholars debate whether the ’saints’ in Daniel 7 are humans, angels, or both, and Heiser knew the debate.

Heiser’s word for this change was : faithful humans displacing the fallen gods of the nations. He did not picture humans becoming the same kind of being as God. He pictured them being raised to a place of honor in God’s council, as a new family around the same throne. Other readers describe the same passages as God’s kingdom coming through Israel’s Messiah, without stressing heavenly rulers being replaced.

Children and Heirs

31.4

Power to Become Sons of God

Main ideaJohn 1:12 says that those who received Jesus were given the right to become children of God, born of God rather than of human descent.

The Gospel of John opens with a poem about the Word. Near its end comes John 1:12. The King James Version says that to those who received him, ’gave he power to become the sons of God.’ The Greek word behind ’power’ is , which also means right or authority. Many modern translations say ’the right to become children of God.’ Verse 13 adds that they are born ’not of blood’ or human will, ’but of God.’

Heiser noticed that the phrase ’sons of God’ in the Hebrew Bible usually named heavenly beings, as in Job 1:6 and 38:7. In the New Testament the same family language is used for people who trust in Jesus. For Heiser this was no accident. He read it as the human family being brought into the same household as God’s heavenly family, which fits his picture of the story’s goal.

Other readers stress different things in the verse. Many Christian interpreters focus on the new birth: becoming God’s child is a gift received by faith, not something earned or inherited. Some scholars point out that John’s Gospel often contrasts birth ’from above’ with ordinary birth, as in John 3:3–7. Both readings agree on the plain words: the verse names a new family status that comes from God.

31.5

Adoption in Romans 8

Main ideaRomans 8:14–23 calls believers adopted children of God and pictures all creation waiting for them to be revealed.

Paul wrote Romans around 57 CE to believers in the city of Rome. In Romans 8:14 he says that those led by the Spirit of God are sons of God. In verse 15 he says they have received ’the Spirit of ,’ by which they cry ’Abba, Father.’ is an Aramaic word for father that Jesus himself uses in prayer in Mark 14:36. Paul keeps an Aramaic word inside a Greek letter, which shows how important the family word was to early believers.

Then Paul widens the picture. In Romans 8:19 the whole creation waits eagerly for ’the manifestation of the sons of God,’ meaning the moment they are revealed. Verse 22 says creation groans like a woman in labor. Verse 23 says believers groan too, ’waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.’ So adoption is both now and not yet: already given, and still to be completed.

Heiser read Romans 8 as part of his family story. The sons of God being revealed, in his reading, is the human family stepping into its full place in God’s household. Many Christian readers focus instead on the of the body and the renewal of creation. The two readings overlap: both see Romans 8 pointing to a future in which creation and God’s children are made whole together.

31.6

Heirs in Galatians 4

Main ideaGalatians 4:4–7 uses Roman-style adoption to say that believers are no longer slaves but sons and heirs of God.

In Galatians 4:4–5 Paul writes that when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman and under the law, so that ’we might receive the adoption of sons.’ Verse 7 draws the result: ’thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an of God through Christ.’ In the King James Version ’servant’ translates the Greek word for slave. Paul’s point is a change of status in a household.

Paul’s readers knew well. A Roman adopted son took his new father’s name, gained his rights and could inherit his estate. Old debts to his former family were set aside. The most famous case was Octavian, adopted in Julius Caesar’s will, who became the emperor Augustus. So when Paul said believers were adopted, his audience heard a legal and permanent change: full sons, with a full share.

Heiser read Galatians 4 alongside Deuteronomy 32. The nations had been handed away to other powers. Now, through Christ, people from those nations could be adopted into God’s own family and share his inheritance. Paul’s Greek word for adoption, , appears only in his letters. Other readers focus on Paul’s argument about the law in Galatians: adoption shows that belonging to God comes through Christ, not through keeping the law of Moses.

Judging and Ruling

31.7

We Shall Judge Angels

Main ideaIn 1 Corinthians 6:3 Paul tells believers they will judge angels, which Heiser read as a sign of their future place in God's council.

In 1 Corinthians 6 Paul is upset that believers in Corinth are taking each other to court. He asks in verse 2 whether they know that the saints will judge the world. Then in verse 3 he asks, ’Know ye not that we shall judge angels?’ His argument runs from larger to smaller: people who will judge angels should be able to settle ordinary disputes among themselves. Paul wrote this letter around 55 CE from Ephesus.

Paul does not explain the claim. He treats it as something his readers should already know. Heiser read the verse as a strong hint of his displacement idea. In the divine council, heavenly beings held roles of judgment and rule. If believers will judge angels, then they will stand in a place higher than those beings once held. Heiser linked this with Psalm 82, where God judges the gods who judged badly.

Readers take ’judge’ in more than one way. Some say believers will share in Christ’s final verdict on fallen angels, such as those mentioned in 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6. Others read ’judge’ as ’rule’ or ’govern,’ as the word sometimes means in the Hebrew Bible, where the judges were leaders. Some interpreters limit the angels to fallen ones, and others leave the question open. All agree the verse gives believers a surprising future role.

31.8

Rule Over the Nations

Main ideaRevelation 2:26–27 and 3:21 promise the one who overcomes a share in Jesus's rule over the nations and a seat on his throne.

Revelation 2–3 holds seven short letters to churches in Asia Minor, in what is now western Turkey. Each ends with a promise ’to him that overcometh.’ The letter to Thyatira (Revelation 2:26–27) promises ’power over the nations’ and borrows the words of Psalm 2:9: he will rule them with a rod of iron, and they will be broken like a potter’s vessels. The letter to Laodicea (Revelation 3:21) promises a seat with Jesus on his throne.

Psalm 2 was a royal psalm. In it God tells his anointed king, ’Ask of me, and I shall give thee the heathen for thine inheritance’ (Psalm 2:8, KJV). The word ’heathen’ there translates the Hebrew goyim, meaning nations. Revelation takes the promise made to the king and shares it with those who overcome. Heiser saw here the nations of Deuteronomy 32 finally coming back under God’s rule, with Jesus and his people ruling where the fallen gods had ruled.

The picture of breaking pottery can sound harsh. Many commentators note that in the ancient world a shepherd’s rod both guided and protected, and that Revelation’s Greek verb in 2:27 can mean ’shepherd.’ Others read the image as final justice against oppression. Interpreters also differ on when this rule happens: in a future reign on earth, in a heavenly reign now, or in both. The letters themselves stress faithfulness under pressure.

Gods, Theosis and Limits

31.9

Jesus Quotes Psalm 82

Main ideaIn John 10:34–36 Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6, 'Ye are gods,' and readers disagree about who those gods are and what his argument means.

In John 10 a crowd in the temple courts accuses Jesus of blasphemy for making himself God. Jesus answers by quoting Psalm 82:6: ’Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?’ (John 10:34). He goes on: if scripture called them gods ’unto whom the word of God came,’ and scripture cannot be broken, why accuse him for saying ’I am the Son of God’? (John 10:35–36). The argument moves from the lesser case to the greater.

Readers identify the ’gods’ in three main ways. Many Christian commentators, especially from the Reformation onward, read them as human judges who acted for God. Some rabbinic writings connect the verse with Israel at Sinai, when the people received the Torah. Heiser read Psalm 82 as a scene in the heavenly , so the gods are real heavenly beings, the who ruled the nations and failed.

Heiser argued that his reading still makes sense of John 10. In his view, Jesus points out that the scriptures themselves use elohim for beings other than the God of Israel, so the word alone cannot settle the charge. Critics of Heiser’s reading ask why the phrase ’unto whom the word of God came’ would fit heavenly beings. Heiser and others have offered answers, and the debate continues. Whatever the reading, Jesus’s point stays the same: the crowd’s charge does not fit their own scripture.

31.10

Theosis in Eastern Orthodoxy

Main ideaEastern Orthodox Christians teach theosis, being united with God by grace, and they read it as sharing God's life, never becoming God by nature.

In the early 300s CE, Athanasius, who became bishop of Alexandria in Egypt, wrote a short book called On the Incarnation. In one famous line he says, in a paraphrase, that God became human so that humans might become divine. Eastern Orthodox Christians use the word , or deification, for this idea. They often pair it with 2 Peter 1:4, which speaks of becoming ’partakers of the divine nature’ (KJV).

Orthodox teachers are careful about what theosis means. It is union with God by , a gift, not a change into God’s own being. In the 1300s the monk and bishop Gregory Palamas described this with a distinction between God’s essence, which no creature can share, and God’s energies, his active presence, which humans can share. An iron in a fire is a common Orthodox picture: the iron glows with the fire’s heat but remains iron.

Some readers notice that Heiser’s family picture sounds close to theosis. Both speak of humans raised into a share of God’s life and rule. Heiser’s own emphasis was on the divine council and the story of the nations, not on the Orthodox language of energies. Western Christian traditions tend to use other words, such as glorification or sanctification, and some are cautious about the term theosis. Comparing them shows different traditions working on the same biblical passages.

31.11

Children of God in Jewish Readings

Main ideaThe Hebrew Bible calls Israel God's son and children, and Jewish tradition reads this as a covenant bond of love and duty, not as becoming divine.

In Exodus 4:22 God sends a message to Pharaoh through Moses: ’Israel is my son, even my firstborn’ (KJV). Deuteronomy 14:1 tells the people, ’Ye are the children of the LORD your God.’ Hosea 11:1 remembers God calling his son out of Egypt. In these verses the whole people is God’s child. The family language belongs to Israel long before the New Testament uses it.

Jewish tradition has usually read this family bond through the , the binding agreement at Sinai. Being God’s children brings closeness and also duty: to keep the commandments and to live as a holy people. That line holds for believers too. In Heiser’s reading, glorified humans will share God’s council and rule, but they remain creatures. Readers who disagree with Heiser about the divine council still agree with him here. Heiser’s family picture, then, is about closeness and honor, not about humans turning into God.

Main ideaHeiser argued that the Greek Harmagedon can come from Hebrew har mo'ed, because Greek often wrote the Hebrew letter ayin with a g sound.

Heiser argued that John, who says the name is ’in the Hebrew tongue,’ was pointing readers to the mount of assembly. If so, the last battle is not about a hill in the Jezreel Valley. Healing for the Nations

Main ideaRevelation 21–22 shows the nations walking in the city's light and healed by the tree's leaves, which Heiser read as the nations reclaimed after Babel.

Revelation 22:2 says the leaves of the tree of life are ’for the healing of the nations.’ Revelation 21:24 says the nations will walk in the city’s light, and the kings of the earth will bring their glory and honor into it. Verse 25 says its gates will never be shut. Earlier, Revelation 7:9 describes a crowd from every nation, tribe, people and language standing before the throne.

Heiser read these verses as the ending of the Babel story. At Babel the nations were scattered and handed to other powers (Genesis 11; Deuteronomy 32:8). Throughout the Hebrew Bible, prophets hoped they would come back: Isaiah 2:2–3 pictures many peoples streaming to the mountain of the LORD’s house. In Revelation, the rulers who led the nations astray are gone, and the nations come to God’s mountain city in peace. The are welcomed home.

Other readers agree that Revelation borrows Isaiah’s hope for the nations, and they stress different parts. Some focus on the church drawn from every people. Some focus on the renewal of whole cultures, their ’glory and honor’ brought in. Some read the scene as a picture of a healed humanity. Across readings, the city’s twelve gates, open on every side, picture a welcome rather than a wall.

Ways to Read Revelation

32.11

Four Approaches

Main ideaReaders approach Revelation in four main ways, preterist, historicist, futurist and idealist, and many combine them.

Revelation has been read in many ways, and scholars usually group them into four approaches. A reading, from Latin for past, holds that most of the book describes events of John’s own time, such as Rome’s persecution of Christians or the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE. A reading holds that the book maps out church history from John’s day to the end. Many Protestant Reformers read it this way.

A reading holds that most of the book, from chapter 4 onward, describes events still to come before the end. This view is widespread among evangelical Christians in the United States today. An reading holds that the book pictures timeless truths, the struggle of good and evil and God’s final victory, rather than particular events. Each approach can point to features of the text that support it.

Many modern scholars mix approaches. They read Revelation first as a letter to seven real churches in the first century (Revelation 1:4), then ask what it hoped for beyond that. Heiser’s contribution is less about which approach to choose and more about the Hebrew Bible background that any approach can use: the divine council, the nations, the cosmic mountain and Eden. His readings of Har Magedon and the new Jerusalem can fit more than one of the four.

32.12

Reading Revelation Calmly

Main ideaRevelation is written in symbols drawn from the Hebrew Bible, and careful readers check claims against the text rather than headlines.

Revelation uses numbers as patterns. There are seven letters, seven seals, seven trumpets and seven bowls. Twelve returns in the gates, the foundations and the tree’s fruit. Many scholars read seven as completeness and twelve as God’s people, from Israel’s twelve tribes. This is not a code to be cracked with dates. It is a way of writing called , full of symbols, that many Jewish books of the time also used, such as Daniel and 1 Enoch.

Over the centuries, many people have announced that Revelation’s battles were about to happen in their own day, naming leaders and dates. Those predictions have not come true. Heiser was openly critical of sensational claims about the Bible. He asked readers to start with what the words meant to their first readers, in the Hebrew Bible and the ancient world. That is how he came to Har Magedon and the mount of assembly.

A careful reader can ask three questions of any claim about Revelation. What does the text actually say, in which verse? What Hebrew Bible passage does it echo? Who holds this reading, and who reads it differently? The book itself ends not with fear but with an invitation, ’Come’ (Revelation 22:17), and with God wiping away tears (Revelation 21:4). Readers of every approach notice that ending.
