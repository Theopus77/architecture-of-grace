Endgame

Every long story is heading somewhere. In this last unit you read the ancient texts about where the Bible's story ends. Psalm 82 puts the gods of the nations on trial. Daniel 7 gives a kingdom to the holy ones. Paul calls believers adopted children and heirs. Revelation ends with a battle, a bound dragon, and a garden city whose gates never shut.

You will read the documents first: the Hebrew Bible and the Septuagint, a Dead Sea Scroll, Jubilees and 1 Enoch in R. H. Charles's 1917 translation, the New Testament, and church fathers such as Justin, Irenaeus, Athanasius and Augustine. Then you will meet the researchers who read them: Mullen and Smith on the divine council, Collins on Daniel, Clifford on the holy mountain, Beale and Bauckham on Revelation, and Michael Heiser on God's family and Har Magedon.

By the end you will be able to explain what these texts promise the people they call children of God, and why readers of every tradition say those people never become equal to God. You will be able to trace a word from Hebrew into Greek, compare a symbol with its older source, and describe a debate fairly without taking a side.

What do the ancient texts say about the end of the story, from God's children raised to honor to the nations healed, and how do different readers understand them?

The story

Is It Not Written, Ye Are Gods?

A student reading John 10 for a class finds Jesus quoting a psalm that calls someone gods, and she has to find out why.

Rosa had a simple assignment for her senior seminar: read John chapter 10 and write one question about it. She expected the shepherd verses. She had heard those before. Then, near the end of the chapter, she stopped. In the King James Version, Jesus answers a crowd with a question of his own: 'Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?' She read it three times.

The footnote pointed her to Psalm 82:6. She opened the psalm. It was only eight verses long. God stands in a gathering and judges beings the psalm calls gods. Then comes the line Jesus quoted: 'I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.' The next verse says they will die like men. Rosa wrote in her margin: Who are these gods, and why would Jesus bring them up?

Her teacher, Mr. Okafor, did not give her one answer. He gave her three. Many older Christian commentators read the gods as human judges. Some rabbinic writings connect the verse with Israel receiving the Torah at Sinai. And many modern scholars, from E. Theodore Mullen in 1980 to Michael Heiser in 2015, read Psalm 82 as a scene in God's heavenly council, with heavenly beings on trial.

Then he handed her a small stack of photocopies. There was Daniel 7, where a kingdom is given to 'the saints of the most High.' There was a page of 1 Enoch in R. H. Charles's 1917 translation, promising the righteous a place with the hosts of heaven. There was a description of a Dead Sea Scroll that reads Psalm 82 about a heavenly figure named Melchizedek. 'Start with these,' he said. 'Then see what the scholars do with them.'

Rosa changed her question. It was no longer only about John 10. It was now: What do the old texts say believers will become, and how do different readers keep that from sounding like people turning into God? This chapter follows her question through the documents.

The Goal of the Story

31.1

Two Families, One Household

Main ideaJob 38:7 and Genesis 1:26–27 picture heavenly sons of God and humans made in God's image, and some scholars read them as two families meant to share one home.

Job 38:7 describes the morning the earth was founded. The shouted for joy, the text says, while the morning stars sang together. The scene has an audience before any human appears. The Hebrew Bible pictures God already surrounded by heavenly beings when creation begins. Scholars call this gathering the , the assembly of heavenly beings around God’s throne, seen also in 1 Kings 22:19 and Psalm 82:1.

Genesis 1:26–27 then has God say, ’Let us make man in our image.’ Readers have long asked who ’us’ is. Early Christian writers often read it as the Trinity. The medieval Jewish commentator Rashi read it as God speaking with his heavenly court. Many Old Testament scholars today read the as a royal role: humans are placed on earth to represent God, the way a king’s statue stood for the king in his lands.

Michael Heiser, in The Unseen Realm (2015), joined these passages into one picture. He read God as having two families, one heavenly and one human, meant to meet in Eden. In his reading, both families had rebels, and the Bible’s story aims to restore one household. Other scholars describe the Bible’s big story with different themes, such as covenant, kingdom or salvation history. Many see these themes as overlapping rather than competing.

31.2

The Nations Given Away

Main ideaDeuteronomy 32:8 reads 'sons of Israel' in the Masoretic Text but 'sons of God' at Qumran and 'angels of God' in the Septuagint, and scholars read the older wording as nations placed under heavenly rulers.

Deuteronomy 32:8 says that when the Most High divided the nations, he set their borders by a number. The copies disagree about the number. The , the standard medieval Hebrew text, says ’the sons of Israel.’ A Dead Sea Scroll of Deuteronomy from Qumran Cave 4 reads ’the sons of God.’ The , the old Greek translation, reads ’the angels of God’ in Brenton’s English of 1844. Most text critics today think ’sons of God’ is the older wording.

Other ancient Jewish writers knew this picture of nations under heavenly rulers. Daniel 10:13 and 10:20 speak of a ’prince of Persia’ and a ’prince of Grecia’ who oppose a heavenly messenger. The Book of , in R. H. Charles’s 1917 translation, says in chapter 15 that God placed spirits in authority over all the nations. Over Israel, it says, he set no angel or spirit, because he alone rules them.

Scholars read the older wording in more than one way. Mark S. Smith, in The Origins of Biblical Monotheism (2001), argued that Deuteronomy 32:8–9 keeps an old picture in which the Most High gives each divine son a nation and the LORD receives Israel. Other scholars read the Most High and the LORD as one God in these verses. Readers who follow the Masoretic reading usually see the verse as a statement about Israel’s place among the peoples.

31.3

The Rebels Replaced

Main ideaPsalm 82 sentences the gods of the nations, and Daniel 7 gives the kingdom to the holy ones of the Most High; readers debate who those holy ones are.

Psalm 82:7 ends the trial of the gods with a verdict: they will die like men and fall like one of the princes. They had judged unjustly and ignored the poor (Psalm 82:2–4). Psalm 82:8 then asks God himself to rise and judge the earth, ’for thou shalt inherit all nations’ in the King James Version. The psalm moves from rulers who failed to God taking the nations back.

Daniel 7 carries the picture further. ’One like the Son of man’ comes to the and receives a kingdom (Daniel 7:13–14). Later in the chapter the kingdom is given to ’the saints of the most High’ (Daniel 7:18, 27). A Dead Sea Scroll called the War Scroll hopes for something similar: the rule of the angel Michael among the heavenly beings, and the rule of Israel among all people.

Scholars debate who the are. John J. Collins, in his commentary Daniel (1993), read the ’holy ones’ as angels, with ’the people of the holy ones’ as faithful Israel on earth. Other scholars read the saints as faithful Israel itself. Michael Heiser read these texts together as a : faithful humans take the places of the fallen gods of the nations, raised to honor in God’s council. Many readers describe the same passages as God’s kingdom coming through Israel’s Messiah.

Children and Heirs

31.4

Power to Become Sons of God

Main ideaJohn 1:12 says that those who received Jesus were given the right to become children of God, using family language that older Jewish texts already used.

The Gospel of John opens with a poem about the Word. Near its end comes John 1:12. The King James Version says that to those who received him, ’gave he power to become the sons of God.’ The Greek word behind ’power’ is , which also means right or authority. Many modern translations say ’the right to become children of God.’ Verse 13 adds that they are born ’not of blood’ or human will, ’but of God.’

The family language is older than John. In the Hebrew Bible the phrase ’sons of God’ usually names heavenly beings, as in Job 1:6 and 38:7. But Hosea 1:10 promises that Israel will be called ’the sons of the living God’ (KJV), and Paul quotes that verse in Romans 9:26. Jubilees 1:24–25, in Charles’s 1917 translation, has God promise that his people will be called children of the living God, and that every angel will know it.

Readers stress different things in John 1:12. Many Christian interpreters focus on the : becoming God’s child is a gift received by faith, not earned or inherited. Some scholars note that John’s Gospel often contrasts birth ’from above’ with ordinary birth, as in John 3:3–7. Divine-council readers notice that a title once used for heavenly beings is here given to people. All agree the verse names a new family status that comes from God.

31.5

Adoption in Romans 8

Main ideaRomans 8:14–23 calls believers adopted children of God and pictures all creation waiting for them to be revealed.

Paul wrote Romans around 57 CE to believers in the city of Rome. In Romans 8:14 he says that those led by the Spirit of God are sons of God. In verse 15 he says they have received ’the Spirit of ,’ by which they cry ’Abba, Father.’ is an Aramaic word for father that Jesus himself uses in prayer in Mark 14:36. Paul keeps an Aramaic word inside a Greek letter, which shows how important the family word was to early believers.

Then Paul widens the picture. In Romans 8:19 the whole creation waits eagerly for ’the manifestation of the sons of God,’ meaning the moment they are revealed. Verse 22 says creation groans like a woman in labor. Verse 23 says believers groan too, ’waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.’ So adoption is both now and not yet: already given, and still to be completed.

Paul’s hope has roots in the prophets. Isaiah 65:17 promises ’new heavens and a new earth,’ and many scholars see Romans 8 drawing on that hope for a renewed creation. Many Christian readers focus on the of the body. Readers interested in the divine council notice that ’sons of God,’ once a name for heavenly beings, now names people about to be revealed. The readings overlap: creation and God’s children are made whole together.

31.6

Heirs in Galatians 4

Main ideaGalatians 4:4–7 uses Roman-style adoption to say that believers are no longer slaves but sons and heirs of God.

In Galatians 4:4–5 Paul writes that when the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman and under the law, so that ’we might receive the adoption of sons.’ Verse 7 draws the result: ’thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an of God through Christ.’ In the King James Version ’servant’ translates the Greek word for slave. Paul’s point is a change of status in a household.

Paul’s readers knew well. A Roman adopted son took his new father’s name, gained his rights and could inherit his estate. Old debts to his former family were set aside. The most famous case was Octavian, adopted in Julius Caesar’s will, who became the emperor Augustus. So when Paul said believers were adopted, his audience heard a legal and permanent change: full sons, with a full share.

Paul also says what the Galatians had been before. In Galatians 4:8 (KJV) they ’did service unto them which by nature are no gods.’ Verses 3 and 9 call their old masters the ’elements’ of the world, Greek stoicheia. Some scholars read these as spiritual powers; others read them as basic principles or rules. Paul’s word for adoption, , appears only in his letters. Many readers stress his argument that belonging to God comes through Christ, not the law of Moses.

Judging and Ruling

31.7

We Shall Judge Angels

Main ideaIn 1 Corinthians 6:3 Paul tells believers they will judge angels, a claim that echoes older Jewish hopes that the faithful would share in God's judgment.

In 1 Corinthians 6 Paul is upset that believers in Corinth are taking each other to court. He asks in verse 2 whether they know that the saints will judge the world. Then in verse 3 he asks, ’Know ye not that we shall judge angels?’ His argument runs from larger to smaller: people who will judge angels should be able to settle ordinary disputes among themselves. Paul wrote this letter around 55 CE from Ephesus.

Paul does not explain the claim. He treats it as something his readers should already know. Older texts show where it may come from. Daniel 7:22 says ’judgment was given to the saints of the most High’ (KJV). The Wisdom of Solomon, a Jewish book from before Paul’s time kept in the Septuagint, says in 3:8 that the righteous ’shall judge the nations, and have dominion over the people’ (KJV Apocrypha). Many scholars hear these hopes behind Paul’s question.

Readers take ’judge’ in more than one way. Some say believers will share in the final verdict on , such as those in 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6, which draw on 1 Enoch’s Watchers. Others read ’judge’ as ’rule’ or ’govern,’ as the word sometimes means in the Hebrew Bible, where the judges were leaders. Divine-council readers link the verse with Psalm 82, where God judges gods who judged badly. All agree the verse gives believers a surprising future role.

31.8

Rule Over the Nations

Main ideaRevelation 2:26–27 and 3:21 promise the one who overcomes a share in Jesus's rule over the nations and a seat on his throne.

Revelation 2–3 holds seven short letters to churches in Asia Minor, in what is now western Turkey. Each ends with a promise ’to him that overcometh.’ The letter to Thyatira (Revelation 2:26–27) promises ’power over the nations’ and borrows the words of Psalm 2:9: he will rule them with a rod of iron, and they will be broken like a potter’s vessels. The letter to Laodicea (Revelation 3:21) promises a seat with Jesus on his throne.

Psalm 2 was a . In it God tells his anointed king, ’Ask of me, and I shall give thee the heathen for thine inheritance’ (Psalm 2:8, KJV). The word ’heathen’ there translates the Hebrew , meaning nations. Revelation takes the promise made to the king and shares it with those who overcome. Readers who study the divine council see here the nations of Deuteronomy 32 coming back under God’s rule, shared with his people.

The picture of breaking pottery can sound harsh. Many commentators note that in the ancient world a shepherd’s rod both guided and protected, and that Revelation’s Greek verb in 2:27 can mean ’shepherd.’ Others read the image as final justice against oppression. Interpreters also differ on when this rule happens: in a future reign on earth, in a heavenly reign now, or in both. The letters themselves stress faithfulness under pressure.

31.9

Jesus Quotes Psalm 82

Main ideaIn John 10:34–36 Jesus quotes Psalm 82:6, 'Ye are gods,' and ancient and modern readers disagree about who those gods are.

In John 10 a crowd in the temple courts accuses Jesus of blasphemy for making himself God. Jesus answers by quoting Psalm 82:6: ’Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?’ (John 10:34). He goes on: if scripture called them gods ’unto whom the word of God came,’ and scripture cannot be broken, why accuse him for saying ’I am the Son of God’? (John 10:35–36). The argument moves from the lesser case to the greater.

Psalm 82 was already being read in different ways in Jesus’s time. A Dead Sea Scroll known as 11QMelchizedek applies Psalm 82:1, God standing in the divine assembly, to Melchizedek, a heavenly figure who brings judgment. Later rabbinic writings connect verse 6 with Israel at Sinai, when the people received the Torah. The early Christian writer Justin Martyr read the verse as said of humans made worthy by God.

Modern readers identify the ’gods’ in three main ways. Many Christian commentators, especially from the Reformation onward, read them as human judges who acted for God. Mullen’s study (1980) and many later scholars read Psalm 82 as a scene with heavenly beings, the who ruled the nations and failed. Michael Heiser held this view too. He argued that Jesus’s point is that scripture itself uses elohim for beings other than Israel’s God.

Critics of the heavenly reading ask why ’unto whom the word of God came’ would fit heavenly beings. Scholars on each side have offered answers, and the debate continues. Whatever the reading, Jesus’s point in John 10 stays the same: the crowd’s charge does not fit their own scripture.

31.10

Theosis in Eastern Orthodoxy

Main ideaEastern Orthodox Christians teach theosis, being united with God by grace, and they read it as sharing God's life, never becoming God by nature.

In the early 300s CE, Athanasius, who became bishop of Alexandria in Egypt, wrote a short book called On the Incarnation. In one famous line he says, in a paraphrase, that God became human so that humans might become divine. Eastern Orthodox Christians use the word , or deification, for this idea. They often pair it with 2 Peter 1:4, which speaks of becoming ’partakers of the divine nature’ (KJV).

Athanasius was not the first. More than a century earlier, Irenaeus of Lyons wrote in Against Heresies that Christ became what humans are so that he might bring them to be what he is. English readers can find it in the Ante-Nicene Fathers series (1885–87). Irenaeus also quoted Psalm 82:6, ’I said, Ye are gods,’ in the same work. So the church fathers were already reading the psalm as a promise about people.

Orthodox teachers are careful about what theosis means. It is union with God by , a gift, not a change into God’s own being. In the 1300s the monk and bishop Gregory Palamas described this with a distinction between God’s , which no creature can share, and God’s energies, his active presence, which humans can share. An iron in a fire is a common Orthodox picture: the iron glows with the fire’s heat but remains iron.

Western Christian traditions tend to use other words, such as glorification or sanctification, and some are cautious about the term theosis. Some modern readers compare theosis with divine-council readings of Psalm 82, since both speak of humans raised into a share of God’s life and rule. Comparing them shows different traditions working on the same biblical passages.

Main ideaSome scholars, including Michael Heiser, argue that Greek Harmagedon can come from Hebrew har mo'ed, because Greek often wrote the Hebrew letter ayin with a g sound.

So a Hebrew phrase like har mo’ed, spelled in Greek letters, could come out close to har-mog-ed, and with a common ending, Harmagedon. Ezekiel 28:13–14 calls ’the garden of God’ and speaks of ’the holy mountain of God.’ In 1 Enoch 24–25 (Charles, 1917), Enoch sees a high mountain like a throne, where God will sit when he comes down. Beside it grows a fragrant tree no one may touch until the judgment. Then its fruit will be given to the righteous and planted near the house of God.

Ezekiel 47 gives another parallel: a river flows from the temple and trees grow along its banks, with fruit every month and leaves for healing (Ezekiel 47:12). Many scholars see John drawing on Genesis, Ezekiel and wider Jewish hopes like those in 1 Enoch. Readers differ on whether Revelation 22 describes a literal renewed earth, a heavenly reality, or a picture of life with God. They widely agree the story that began in a garden ends in a garden city.

32.10

Healing for the Nations

Main ideaRevelation 21–22 shows the nations walking in the city's light and healed by the tree's leaves, drawing on Isaiah's hope that the nations would come to God's mountain.

Revelation 22:2 says the leaves of the tree of life are ’for the healing of the nations.’ Revelation 21:24 says the nations will walk in the city’s light, and the kings of the earth will bring their glory and honor into it. Verse 25 says its gates will never be shut. Earlier, Revelation 7:9 describes a crowd from every nation, tribe, people and language standing before the throne.

The prophets had hoped for this. Isaiah 2:2–3 pictures many peoples streaming to the mountain of the LORD’s house. Isaiah 60:3 says the Gentiles shall come to Zion’s light and kings to its brightness, and Isaiah 60:11 says its gates shall be open continually (KJV). Revelation 21:24–26 takes up these lines closely. The nations once scattered at Babel (Genesis 11) now come to God’s city in peace.

Readers stress different parts of the scene. Richard Bauckham, in The Climax of Prophecy (1993), argued that Revelation hopes for the conversion of the , not only their defeat. Readers who study the divine council see the ending of the Babel story, with the powers that led the nations astray removed. Some focus on the church drawn from every people; others on healed cultures bringing their ’glory and honor’ in. Across readings, the twelve open picture a welcome rather than a wall.

Section 4

Ways to Read Revelation

32.11

Four Approaches

Main ideaReaders approach Revelation in four main ways, preterist, historicist, futurist and idealist, and many combine them.

Revelation has been read in many ways, and scholars usually group them into four approaches. A reading, from Latin for past, holds that most of the book describes events of John’s own time, such as Rome’s persecution of Christians or the fall of Jerusalem in 70 CE. A reading holds that the book maps out church history from John’s day to the end. Many Protestant Reformers read it this way.

A reading holds that most of the book, from chapter 4 onward, describes events still to come before the end. This view is widespread among evangelical Christians in the United States today. An reading holds that the book pictures timeless truths, the struggle of good and evil and God’s final victory, rather than particular events. Each approach can point to features of the text that support it.

Many modern scholars mix approaches. They read Revelation first as a letter to seven real churches in the first century (Revelation 1:4), then ask what it hoped for beyond that. Researchers who study the Hebrew Bible background, from Clifford on the cosmic mountain to Heiser on the divine council, offer material that any approach can use: the nations, the holy mountain and Eden. Their readings of Har Magedon and the new Jerusalem can fit more than one of the four.

32.12

Reading Revelation Calmly

Main ideaRevelation is written in symbols drawn from the Hebrew Bible, and careful readers check claims against the text rather than headlines.

Revelation uses numbers as patterns. There are seven letters, seven seals, seven trumpets and seven bowls. Twelve returns in the gates, the foundations and the tree’s fruit. Many scholars read seven as completeness and twelve as God’s people, from Israel’s twelve tribes. This is not a code to be cracked with dates. It is a way of writing called , full of symbols, that many Jewish books of the time also used, such as Daniel and 1 Enoch.

Over the centuries, many people have announced that Revelation’s battles were about to happen in their own day, naming leaders and dates. Those predictions have not come true. Ancient readers already urged care. Irenaeus, in Against Heresies (Book 5), discussed the number 666 but advised waiting rather than guessing a name. Augustine, in The City of God (Book 18), warned against trying to calculate the time of the end.

A careful reader can ask three questions of any claim about Revelation. What does the text actually say, in which verse? What Hebrew Bible passage does it echo? Who holds this reading, and who reads it differently? The book itself ends not with fear but with an invitation, ’Come’ (Revelation 22:17), and with God wiping away tears (Revelation 21:4). Readers of every approach notice that ending.
