Bill Cooper and the Conspiracy Age, and Capstone

Capstone

This last unit brings the whole course together. First you will meet Bill Cooper, a Navy and Air Force veteran whose shortwave broadcasts and 1991 book reached listeners and readers across the country. Many people still respect him. He warned his listeners about real government secrets, and he also passed on claims that have no evidence and one famous forgery. His life is a hard, honest test of everything you have learned about sorting claims.

You will read his story from service records, court papers and the work of historians. Then you will build a ledger with three columns: documented, disputed or unproven, and shown false. You will not be told what to think about him. You will be shown the evidence on each side, and you will do the sorting.

Then you will do your own capstone. You will choose one claim about a secret society from this course, trace it to its earliest source, gather evidence from more than one side and sort it into three piles. You will write and present an argument from evidence. Finally, you will practice disagreeing with respect. By the end you will be able to ask, about any claim you meet, how do we know?

The big question

How can we tell a documented secret from an unproven or false claim, and how should we talk with people who see it differently?

Cooper
Big questionWhen one writer mixes real government secrets with unproven and false claims, how can a reader sort one from the other?
The story

A Voice on the Night Road

Somewhere on an interstate in the 1990s, a trucker turns a dial and finds a voice that tells him not to trust what he is told.

Picture a long-haul trucker crossing the plains after midnight in the mid-1990s. The FM stations have faded to hiss. He reaches for a shortwave radio wedged beside the seat. Shortwave signals bounce off the upper air at night, so a station hundreds of miles away can come in clear.

He turns the dial slowly past sermons, music and static. Then a calm, gravelly voice fills the cab. The speaker is a Navy veteran named Bill Cooper. He is talking about secret government programs, powerful private clubs and a hidden plan to rule the world. He tells his listeners to check everything for themselves.

Some of what the trucker hears that night is true. The CIA really did test drugs on people who did not know it. The FBI really did spy on American citizens. Other things he hears have no record behind them at all. And one book the voice recommends contains a famous forgery aimed at Jewish people.

The trucker cannot stop the rig to look anything up. He has only the voice and his own judgment. In this chapter you will have more tools than he did: service records, court papers, Senate reports and the work of historians. You will follow Cooper's life, then sort his claims into three piles, one claim at a time.

Talk about itWhy might a voice on late-night radio feel more trustworthy than a newspaper to some listeners? What would you want to check first?
Section 1

A Veteran's Record

33.1

A Military Childhood and the Air Force

Main ideaMilton William Cooper was born in 1943, grew up in a military family and served in the Air Force until an honorable discharge in 1965.

Milton William Cooper was born on 6 May 1943 in Long Beach, California. Friends and listeners later knew him as Bill. He grew up in a military family and moved often, as many service families do. That background shaped how he spoke. He talked like a veteran briefing other veterans, and many of his listeners had served too.

As a young man Cooper joined the U.S. Air Force. He served in the , the branch that ran long-range bombers and nuclear missiles during the Cold War. He later said he worked around B-52 bombers, tanker planes and Minuteman missiles and held a secret security clearance. He received an from the Air Force in 1965. An honorable discharge means a person left the service in good standing.

Historians separate two kinds of evidence about a life. A is the official file the military keeps on each member: dates, rank, units and awards. A personal account is what the person later says about that service. Both matter. The record shows what the military wrote down at the time. The account shows how the person understood and explained it. In Cooper’s case the two line up well for the Air Force years. As the next lesson shows, they line up less well for his Navy years.

33.2

What the Navy Records Show

Main ideaPublic Navy records show Cooper served honorably, including a tour in Vietnam, but they do not show the intelligence job he later described.

After the Air Force, Cooper joined the U.S. Navy. He served until 1975 and again left with an honorable discharge. Public records show he reached pay grade E-5, which is a petty officer second class. They show a tour in Vietnam and two service medals. That is a real record of service, and many veterans in his audience respected it.

Cooper later told a bigger story. He said that in the early 1970s he served on an intelligence briefing team for the commander of the U.S. Pacific Fleet. In that job, he said, he read top-secret papers about UFOs and a secret group called MJ-12. This is a , a statement someone says is true. It is different from a , which is evidence written down at the time by someone else.

Here the two do not match. The public records confirm Navy service but not a place on an intelligence team. A petty officer at pay grade E-5 was a skilled enlisted sailor, not a senior officer. Historians say that makes top-secret intelligence access unlikely, though not impossible. No document yet released places him on such a team. So the service is . The intelligence job is disputed.

33.3

Weighing a Veteran's Word

Main ideaA fair judge of Cooper's intelligence story lists the best evidence for it and against it before deciding how far it can be trusted.

Many of Cooper’s listeners believed his intelligence story because he told it with confidence and detail. That respect is worth taking seriously. Veterans often cannot prove what they did, because some duties were never written down in public files. A fair reader starts by asking what evidence would help, not by assuming the speaker is lying.

Here is the case for his story. His service in the Pacific was real. Briefing teams of the kind he described did exist. He told the story many times over many years. Here is the case against it. No document places him on such a team. His rank makes top-secret access unlikely. His story grew after the MJ-12 papers appeared in public in 1987. Some former colleagues in UFO research later broke with him and questioned his account.

When evidence runs out, historians use careful words. They do not say false, because nothing proves he never saw such papers. They do not say true, because nothing confirms it. They say and they explain what is missing. They also watch for , meaning a second, independent source that backs up the first. Cooper’s intelligence story has none so far. Released military files, if any exist, could still change the picture.

Section 2

From Bulletin Boards to Shortwave

33.4

The MJ-12 Lectures

Main ideaIn 1988 and 1989 Cooper began telling the public about MJ-12, a secret committee that the FBI and Air Force investigators said was never formed.

In 1988 Cooper began posting his story on ParaNet, a . A bulletin board was an early online system where people dialed in by computer to read and post messages. Soon he was giving public lectures. A paper he wrote, dated 23 May 1989, was titled The Secret Government: The Origin, Identity, and Purpose of MJ-12. It told of alien contact and a hidden committee that managed it.

Cooper did not invent the name MJ-12. In December 1984 a TV producer named Jaime Shandera received undeveloped film in the mail. When printed, it showed a supposed top-secret briefing for President Eisenhower about a committee called Majestic 12. The pages were published in 1987. In 1988 the FBI began looking into them.

In September 1988 an agent of the Air Force Office of Special Investigations contacted the FBI about the papers. In November 1988 the Air Force investigators said no such committee had ever been authorized or formed. An FBI official called the document bogus. Cooper took an unusual position. He said the Shandera papers were , false material spread on purpose. Yet he still held that MJ-12 itself was real. That leaves his claim resting on his own memory of papers no one else has produced.

33.5

Behold a Pale Horse

Main ideaCooper's 1991 book gathered lectures, reprinted documents and essays to argue that a secret society steers world events.

In 1991 Light Technology Publishing in Arizona printed Cooper’s book Behold a Pale Horse. The title comes from the Book of Revelation in the Bible, where a rider on a pale horse brings death. The title told readers that Cooper saw his subject as a warning about the end of an age.

The book is an , a collection of separate pieces bound together. It holds Cooper’s lectures, essays and many reprinted documents. Its main , or central argument, is that a secret society he called the Illuminati steers world events. In his telling it works through governments, banks, private clubs and fraternal orders such as the Freemasons.

Because it is a collection, the book mixes very different materials. Some pieces point to real events that official records confirm. Others are documents with no known author or archive. One is a manual called Silent Weapons for Quiet Wars. The book says it was found in a used copier bought at a surplus sale. No author, agency or earlier copy has been traced. Historians treat the book as a for what many Americans believed in the 1990s. They do not treat it as proof of the events it describes.

33.6

The Hour of the Time

Main ideaCooper's shortwave program The Hour of the Time reached listeners across the country and made him a leading voice of the 1990s militia movement.

In the 1990s Cooper hosted a radio program called The Hour of the Time. It was carried on WWCR, a station in Nashville, Tennessee. Shortwave radio uses signals that bounce off the upper atmosphere. At night they can travel across a whole continent. A cheap receiver was enough to listen, and no one needed a computer or a subscription.

The early 1990s were a time of deep distrust for many rural Americans. In 1992 a standoff at Ruby Ridge, Idaho, left a U.S. marshal and two members of the Weaver family dead. In 1993 the siege at Waco, Texas, ended in a fire, and more than 70 people died. Many listeners saw these as proof that the government would turn on its own citizens.

Cooper became a leading voice of the , armed private groups that saw the federal government as a threat. His was wide. It included truckers, farmers, veterans and people in prison. Many said he was the first person who took their fears seriously. That trust is part of the story historians try to understand, not something to mock.

Charges and a Final Night

33.7

The 1998 Federal Charges

Main ideaIn 1998 Cooper was charged with tax evasion and bank fraud; he refused to surrender and was never tried.

In 1998 federal authorities in Arizona charged Cooper with and bank fraud. Tax evasion means illegally avoiding paying taxes that are owed. The charges came from a federal grand jury. The exact counts in the charging papers have not been checked for this course, so this lesson names only the two kinds of charge.

An is a formal charge by a grand jury. It says there is enough evidence for a trial. It is not a finding of guilt. Cooper was never tried on these charges, so no court ever ruled on them. A fair account says he was charged, not that he was convicted.

Cooper did not surrender. He stayed at his home in Eagar, a small town in the White Mountains of eastern Arizona, and said he would not be taken. He became a , a person running from the law. According to a 2002 report by the Southern Poverty Law Center, federal agents put off serving his arrest warrant for more than three years. They wanted to avoid a violent confrontation like Ruby Ridge or Waco.

33.8

Eagar, November 2001

Main ideaOn the night of 5 November 2001, a deputy was shot and badly wounded while trying to arrest Cooper, and another deputy's return fire killed Cooper.

In July 2001, after a dispute with a local man, Arizona brought state charges against Cooper: aggravated assault with a deadly weapon, and endangerment. The details of that dispute have not been confirmed for this course. These charges were separate from the federal tax case. They were local, and they were handled by the Apache County Sheriff’s Office.

On the night of 5 November 2001, a team of about 17 officers went to Cooper’s home in Eagar to arrest him on the state charges. By the official account, Cooper shot Deputy Robert Marinez in the head. Marinez survived but was badly hurt. Another deputy returned fire and killed Cooper. He was 58 years old. Some sources give the date as 6 November, because the shooting came close to midnight.

Two accounts of that night still circulate. The official account comes from the sheriff’s office and an investigation by the Arizona Department of Public Safety. Some of Cooper’s supporters tell a different story. They say he was killed to silence him. A careful reader asks which rests on records. The official file is a public record that can be requested. The silencing claim has no document behind it. It also has to explain why agents had avoided arresting him on the federal charges for years.

33.9

An Audience That Kept Growing

Main ideaCooper's influence grew after his death, and his biographer links it to a wider loss of public trust in American institutions.

Cooper’s death did not end his influence. Recordings of The Hour of the Time were copied and shared, first on tapes and then online. Behold a Pale Horse stayed in print for decades. Reporters have found it passed hand to hand in prisons and quoted in hip-hop culture and online movements. Its readers include people of many backgrounds and politics.

The journalist Mark Jacobson wrote the first full of Cooper, Pale Horse Rider, published in 2018. A biography is the story of a person’s life written by someone else. Jacobson interviewed Cooper’s family, fellow broadcasters and longtime listeners. He tried to explain why so many people trusted Cooper more than they trusted their government.

Scholars add another angle. The legal scholar Mark Fenster argues that belief in conspiracy is often a response to real and real distrust, not simple foolishness. Secrecy means keeping information hidden from people who might want it. Cooper’s audience had real reasons for distrust, as the next section shows. The hard question is what to do with that distrust. It can lead a person to demand records, or it can lead a person to believe anything that sounds defiant.

The Claims Ledger

33.10

What the Records Confirm

Main ideaSeveral things Cooper talked about are documented: MKUltra, COINTELPRO, Operation Northwoods, and real elite groups such as the Bohemian Grove, the CFR and the Trilateral Commission.

A is a book of accounts with columns. Historians borrow the idea to sort claims. The first column holds what official records confirm. Cooper deserves credit here. He told a mass audience to take government secrecy seriously, and some of the secrets were real.

The CIA’s MKUltra program, approved in 1953, secretly tested drugs such as LSD on people who did not know. The FBI’s COINTELPRO, from 1956 to 1971, spied on and tried to disrupt American political groups. Both came fully to light in the 1970s through the press and Congress, including the Church Committee report of 1976. In 1962 the Joint Chiefs of Staff proposed Operation Northwoods, a plan to stage attacks and blame Cuba. President Kennedy’s administration rejected it.

Some groups Cooper named are also real. The Bohemian Club, founded in San Francisco in 1872, gathers powerful men each July at the Bohemian Grove in California. The Council on Foreign Relations was founded in New York in 1921. The Trilateral Commission was founded in 1973 by David Rockefeller. All three have members in high office. Their existence is documented. That they secretly run the world is a separate claim, and the records do not show it.

33.11

Disputed and Unproven Claims

Main ideaCooper's claims about MJ-12, a treaty with aliens, Kennedy's assassination and a single shadow government lack the records needed to prove them.

The second column of the ledger holds claims that are disputed or unproven. Cooper said MJ-12 was a real committee formed to handle crashed alien craft. The only papers ever produced were judged fake by Air Force investigators, and Cooper himself rejected them. He also said President Eisenhower met aliens in 1954 and signed a treaty with them. No document, witness record or budget line supports that. In 2024 a Defense Department office reviewed the record and found no evidence of agreements with aliens.

Cooper said the Secret Service driver of Kennedy’s car, William Greer, fired the fatal shot in Dallas in 1963. He pointed to a flash in a copy of the Zapruder film. Researchers who studied clearer copies found no gun. A congressional medical panel in the late 1970s found the wounds came from behind and above. Some historians still debate whether Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone, but the driver theory is not part of that serious debate.

Cooper’s biggest claim was a , one hidden group that runs the country behind the elected one. Real cases of hidden programs exist, such as MKUltra and the Iran-Contra affair of the 1980s. But each was exposed by Congress, the press, courts or . Historians see many separate secrets, not one master plan. That is why the big claim stays unproven.

33.12

Shown False: A Forgery and a Spoof

Main ideaTwo items in Cooper's book are shown false: the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, a proven antisemitic forgery, and Alternative 3, a TV spoof.

The third column holds claims shown false. The first is the Protocols of the Elders of Zion. The 1991 edition of Behold a Pale Horse reprinted it as a full chapter. The Protocols claim to be secret plans of Jewish leaders to rule the world. In 1921 Philip Graves showed in The Times of London that whole passages were copied from an 1864 French satire by Maurice Joly. That satire attacked Napoleon III and never mentioned Jews. It was a , and it has fed hatred and violence against Jewish people for more than a century.

Scholars such as Michael Barkun report that Cooper added a note before the chapter. It told readers to read the word Illuminati wherever the text said Jews. That swap does not repair the text. It is a forgery whatever word is put in, and it was written to accuse Jews. The swap keeps the forgery in circulation. Later revised printings of the book reportedly dropped the chapter, but this has not been confirmed from the publisher.

The second false item is Alternative 3. On 20 June 1977 Anglia Television in Britain aired a program that looked like a documentary about a secret colony on Mars. It was a , a made-up program in the style of a real one. It was written by David Ambrose, meant for April Fools’ Day and credited its actors. Cooper’s book repeated its plot as fact. A fair reader can say both things at once: Cooper raised real questions about power, and his book also passed on a known forgery and a known spoof.

Main ideaAsking who first made a claim, for what audience and with what purpose helps a researcher judge how much weight it can bear.

Take the so-called Knights of Columbus oath. In the early 1900s anti-Catholic activists spread a text they said was the secret oath of that Catholic fraternal order. It described violent promises. Most Catholics and Jews, of course, had nothing to do with any plot at all.

Main ideaAn argument from evidence states a claim, backs it with specific sources, explains the reasoning and answers the strongest counterclaim.

9. Which claim is testable as written?

Why: It names one object, one group and one action, so records can answer it.

10. What does a source chain help you find?

Why: A source chain lists who repeated a claim, when and where they got it.

11. Where was the Eye of Providence first proposed for the Great Seal?

Why: The first seal committee's report of 20 August 1776 proposes the eye.

12. How should a researcher report sources that disagree on a number?

Why: Honest handling of numbers builds credibility.

13. In the three-pile method, what does disputed mean?

Why: Disputed sits between documented and unsupported.

14. What did George Washington's 1798 letters to Snyder show?

Why: Washington separated a general worry from a specific charge he had no evidence for.

15. Which reply shows respectful disagreement?

Why: It agrees where the evidence agrees, criticizes the claim rather than the person, and shares a source.
