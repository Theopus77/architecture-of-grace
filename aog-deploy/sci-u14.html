Ecosystems, Heredity and Evolution

Life Science

A silver carp leaps out of the Illinois River and lands in a boat. A pea plant grows wrinkled seeds even though both its parents grew round ones. A bacterium shrugs off a drug that would have killed its great-grandparents. These three scenes look unrelated. They are not. Each one is about the same thing: living things, the instructions inside them, and the pressure of the world around them.

This unit moves from the outside in and then back out again. First, ecosystems: what limits a population, how energy and matter move through a food web, and what happens when a drought, a fire or an invader shakes things up. Next, heredity: how DNA, genes and chromosomes carry instructions from parents to offspring, and why offspring are never exact copies. Finally, evolution: how the variation that heredity creates, meeting the limits that ecosystems impose, changes populations over generations and has produced every species on Earth.

By the end you will be able to read a food web and predict what happens when one species vanishes. You will be able to draw a Punnett square and explain why a trait can hide for a generation. And you will be able to lay out the evidence, from rock layers to finch beaks to DNA, that all living things share one family tree.

The story

The Fish That Jumped Into the Boat

On the Illinois River, a motor's hum can fill the air with flying fish.

Maya's uncle eases the boat away from the ramp near Havana, Illinois. The Illinois River is wide and brown here. He pushes the throttle. The motor hums. Then the water in front of them explodes. Silver fish, some as long as her arm, leap out of the river. They fly higher than her head. One slams into the side of the boat. Another lands, flopping, at her feet.

Her uncle cuts the motor and laughs, but he is not happy. "Silver carp," he says. "They jump when a motor scares them. Twenty years ago you never saw one here." He holds the fish up. It has a low, forward-set eye and a big silver body. It did not come from Illinois. It did not come from anywhere in North America.

Silver carp and their cousins, bighead carp, were brought to the southern United States in the 1970s. Fish farms wanted them to eat algae and clean their ponds. Floods let some escape into the Mississippi River. From there they swam north, up the Illinois River, toward Chicago and Lake Michigan. They eat tiny floating plankton, the same food that young native fish need.

Today biologists net, count and track these carp all along the river. Electric barriers near Romeoville, in the Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal, are meant to keep them out of the Great Lakes. Restaurants are learning to cook them. Illinois even gave the fish a new market name, copi, to get more people to eat them.

Maya looks at the fish at her feet. It is just a fish, doing what fish do: eating and making more fish. So why is it a problem? Her uncle points at the water. "Everything down there is connected," he says. "Change one thing, and you change the whole river."

What Limits a Population

30.1

Counting a Population

Main ideaA population is all the members of one species in one place, and it can only grow as far as its resources allow.

Picture a pond in a Chicago park in early summer. Frogs call from the edges. If you counted every bullfrog in that pond, you would have measured a : all the individuals of one species living in one place at one time. The pond also has a population of bluegill, a population of cattails and a population of mosquitoes. Together, all of them and the water, mud and sunlight make an ecosystem.

No population can grow forever. Each frog needs food, water, space and a place to lay eggs. These are . When resources are plentiful, a population grows fast. A pair of frogs can produce thousands of eggs. When the pond gets crowded, food runs short and growth slows. Some frogs starve, and fewer eggs hatch.

Scientists call the largest population a place can support over time its . The resource that runs out first sets that limit. Ecologists call it a . For pond frogs it might be insects to eat. For prairie plants it might be water in a dry August. For nesting birds it might be tree holes. Find the limiting factor, and you can often predict how a population will change.

30.2

Competition and Predation

Main ideaLiving things compete for the same resources, and predators and prey keep each other's numbers in check.

Two oak seedlings sprout side by side in a forest preserve. Both need light, water and soil minerals. There is not enough for both to grow tall. This is : two or more living things trying to use the same limited resource. The seedling that grows a little faster shades the other. The slower one dies. Competition happens within a species and between species.

Now watch a red-tailed hawk drop onto a field mouse. The hawk is a , an animal that hunts and eats other animals. The mouse is its . Predation is not cruelty; it is how the hawk gets energy. It also shapes the mouse population. When mice are plentiful, hawks raise more chicks. When hawks are plentiful, mice become scarce, and then some hawks go hungry.

Ecologists have tracked predators and prey for decades. The numbers often rise and fall in linked waves. Prey numbers climb first. Predator numbers climb a little later. Then prey numbers crash, and predators follow. The pattern is evidence that the two populations control each other. Remove the predator, and prey can boom until they eat their food supply bare.

30.3

Partners and Parasites

Main ideaSpecies can help each other, use each other, or harm each other, and each kind of relationship shapes an ecosystem.

A bumblebee lands on a purple coneflower in an Illinois prairie. The bee drinks sugary nectar. Pollen sticks to its fuzzy body and rides to the next flower, letting the plant make seeds. Both partners gain. Ecologists call this . Clownfish and sea anemones, and the bacteria in your own gut that help you digest food, are also mutualists.

Not every close relationship is fair. A tick clings to a deer and drinks its blood. The tick gains; the deer loses blood and may get sick. This is . A parasite lives on or in another organism, called the host, and harms it, usually without killing it right away. Tapeworms, fleas and the fungus that causes athlete’s foot are parasites.

Sometimes one partner gains and the other is not affected much. A barnacle riding on a whale gets a free trip to food-rich water; the whale barely notices. Scientists call that commensalism. Together, mutualism, parasitism, competition and predation are the threads that tie an ecosystem together. Pull one thread, and others move.

Energy and Matter on the Move

30.4

Sunlight Into Sugar

Main ideaPlants, algae and some bacteria use light energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide and water, and that sugar feeds almost every living thing.

Put a leaf under the site’s microscope and you will see tiny green dots packed inside the cells. These are chloroplasts. Inside them, happens. The plant takes in carbon dioxide from the air through tiny pores and water from the soil through its roots. Using the energy of sunlight, it links those simple molecules into sugar. Oxygen is released as a leftover.

That sugar is stored chemical energy. The plant uses some of it to grow. The rest is stored in leaves, stems, roots and seeds. When a rabbit eats clover, it takes in that stored energy. When a fox eats the rabbit, the energy moves again. Nearly all the energy in an ecosystem entered it as sunlight caught by a , an organism that makes its own food.

You can test this. Put a water plant like elodea in a jar of water in bright light. Bubbles rise from the leaves. In the dark, the bubbling stops. The bubbles are oxygen, released only when light drives photosynthesis. In the 1770s, Jan Ingenhousz did a version of this experiment and showed that green plants give off oxygen only in light.

Photosynthesis also explains the air you breathe. About 21 percent of the air is oxygen, almost all of it made by producers over billions of years. Carbon dioxide is far rarer, about 0.04 percent, yet every gram of sugar in every plant was built from it.

30.5

Food Webs and the Ten Percent Rule

Main ideaEnergy flows one way through a food web, and only a small share passes from each level to the next.

A food chain is a simple line: grass to grasshopper to sparrow to hawk. Real ecosystems are messier. The sparrow also eats seeds. The hawk also eats snakes. A is a diagram of many linked chains that shows who eats whom in a whole community. Arrows point in the direction the energy moves, from the eaten to the eater.

Every organism in a web sits at a level. Producers are the first level. Animals that eat producers are herbivores, the second level. Animals that eat herbivores are the third, and so on. An organism that eats other organisms is a . Some consumers, like raccoons and people, eat at several levels.

Energy is lost at every step. A grasshopper uses most of the energy from the grass it eats just to move, breathe and stay alive. That energy leaves as heat. Only about 10 percent gets stored in the grasshopper’s body where a sparrow can get it. The same thing happens at each level. This is why a prairie can feed millions of insects, thousands of birds and only a few dozen hawks.

The pattern is called an energy pyramid. It explains something you can see everywhere: big predators are rare. It also explains why a food web rarely has more than four or five levels. By the fifth level there is almost no energy left to pass along.

30.6

Decomposers Close the Loop

Main ideaEnergy flows through an ecosystem and is lost as heat, but matter cycles, and decomposers are what keep it cycling.

Turn over a rotting log in a forest preserve. The wood is soft and crumbling. Threads of white fungus run through it. Beetles, worms and millions of bacteria are at work. These are : organisms that break dead material into simple substances. Without them, every fallen leaf and dead animal would pile up forever, and the minerals locked inside would never get back to the soil.

This points to a big difference between energy and matter. Energy moves one way. It enters as sunlight, passes up the food web, and leaves as heat. It is not recycled. Matter is different. The carbon atom in a leaf becomes part of a caterpillar, then a bird, then a decomposer, then carbon dioxide in the air, then a new leaf. Atoms are used over and over. This is of matter.

The carbon cycle is the easiest to see. Plants pull carbon dioxide from the air. Animals eat plants and breathe out carbon dioxide. Decomposers release it as they rot dead things. Nitrogen and water cycle too. Rain falls, plants drink it, animals drink it, and it evaporates back to the sky. The same water has been through countless living things.

Illinois farmers rely on these cycles. Rich prairie soil formed over thousands of years as decomposers broke down dead grasses and roots. That dark soil holds nutrients that corn and soybeans now use. Take away the decomposers, and the soil would stop being renewed.

Biodiversity and Disruption

30.7

Why Variety Matters

Main ideaBiodiversity, the variety of living things in a place, makes an ecosystem stronger and gives people services they cannot easily replace.

Walk a restored prairie at the Midewin National Tallgrass Prairie southwest of Chicago. In one square meter you may find a dozen kinds of grass and flower. Beneath them are insects, spiders, fungi and bacteria by the thousands. This variety is : the number of different species in a place and the variety within each species.

Why care about variety? Think of a bank with many kinds of savings. If one loses value, others still hold. A prairie with thirty plant species can survive a dry summer better than a field of one crop. Some species will do poorly, but others will thrive and hold the soil. A monoculture, a field of just one kind, has no backup plan.

Ecosystems also do work for people for free. Wetlands soak up floodwater. Forests filter drinking water and hold hillsides in place. Bees and other insects pollinate the apple and pumpkin crops of Illinois. Decomposers build soil. Scientists call these . When biodiversity falls, these services get weaker, and replacing them with machines is costly or impossible.

30.8

When an Invader Arrives

Main ideaA species carried to a new place can spread fast if nothing there eats it or competes with it, and it can push out native species.

Silver carp did not evolve on the Illinois River. Back home in Asia, their eggs and young are eaten by many predators, and diseases keep their numbers down. In the Mississippi basin, few of those checks exist. A single female can release hundreds of thousands of eggs. With little to stop them, the carp population exploded. A species that spreads in a new place and causes harm is an .

The carp cause harm in a quiet way. They filter plankton, the tiny floating organisms at the base of the river’s food web. Nearly every native fish eats plankton when it is young. When carp take that food, fewer young bass, crappie and bluegill survive. This is competition on a huge scale. In some stretches of the Illinois River, biologists have found that carp make up a large share of all the fish by weight.

Illinois is not alone. Zebra mussels arrived in the Great Lakes in ship ballast water in the 1980s and now coat pipes and rocks. Emerald ash borer beetles have killed millions of ash trees across the state. Garlic mustard shades out spring wildflowers in forest preserves. In each case the invader lacked the predators, parasites and competitors that limited it at home.

Fighting invaders takes many tools. For the carp, crews use nets, electric barriers in the canal, and a push to turn the fish into food. None of these will remove every carp. The goal is to keep the population low enough that native fish can recover and to keep the carp out of Lake Michigan.

30.9

Drought, Fire and Recovery

Main ideaDisturbances like drought and fire can knock an ecosystem down, and its resilience decides how well it bounces back.

In the summer of 2012, much of Illinois went weeks without real rain. Corn leaves curled. Ponds shrank to mud. Fish died in shallow water that got too warm and held too little oxygen. A is any event that changes an ecosystem quickly. Drought, flood, fire, storms and the arrival of an invader are all disturbances.

Fire sounds like pure destruction, but on a prairie it is an old friend. Before farms, lightning and Native American land managers set fires that swept the tallgrass every few years. Prairie plants grow from deep roots and buds below the soil, so they survive. Fire clears dead stems and kills young trees that would shade out the grass. Today, land managers at forest preserves set careful controlled burns for the same reason.

is an ecosystem’s ability to recover after a disturbance. A prairie with many species and deep roots is resilient to fire and drought. A lawn of one grass type is not. Resilience has limits. If fires come too often, or a drought lasts years, or an invader never leaves, an ecosystem can tip into a different state and stay there. A shallow lake choked with algae may not turn clear again on its own.

Scientists measure resilience by tracking a place over time. They count species before and after a disturbance and record how long recovery takes. That evidence helps decide where to protect land, where to burn, and when to step in.

Protecting an Ecosystem

30.10

Define the Problem

Main ideaBefore designing a fix, engineers and ecologists state exactly what must be protected, what limits the solution, and how success will be measured.

Suppose your class is asked to help a small creek in your town. Fish have disappeared. Where do you start? Engineers begin by defining the problem. What exactly went wrong? Perhaps a new parking lot sends muddy water into the creek after every storm. Mud buries the gravel where fish lay eggs. Now the problem is clear: too much sediment reaching the creek.

Next come the : what a good solution must do. It must cut the mud reaching the creek. It must not flood the parking lot. Then come the : the limits. There is a budget. The lot must stay open. The fix must be built by spring. A solution that fails a constraint is not a real option, no matter how clever.

Finally, decide how you will know it worked. Ecologists set a measurable goal, like the amount of mud in a water sample or the number of fish counted next summer. Without a measurement, you can only guess. Defining the problem well is often half the job.

30.11

Compare, Test and Choose

Main ideaGood ecosystem solutions come from comparing several ideas against the criteria, testing them on a small scale, and weighing trade-offs.

With the muddy creek problem defined, the class brainstorms. One group wants a strip of native plants between the lot and the creek. Roots slow the water and trap mud. Another group wants a small basin that catches storm water. A third wants to replace part of the lot with pavement that lets rain soak through. None of these is obviously best. Each must be scored against the criteria and constraints.

Engineers then on a small scale. The class builds three tray models with a sloped surface, sprinkles water from a can, and measures how much soil washes into a cup at the bottom. The plant strip cuts the mud by about half. The basin catches nearly all of it but overflows in a heavy pour. The porous pavement works but costs the most.

Every choice has a : something you give up to get something else. The basin takes space. The plant strip needs years to grow. Porous pavement needs cleaning. Sometimes the best answer combines ideas. The class might pick a plant strip plus a small basin and plan to measure creek clarity every month to see if fish come back.

This is how real projects go. When Illinois planned electric barriers against the carp, engineers compared a barrier with dams, locks and nets, weighed cost and shipping traffic, and then monitored the results for years. Protecting an ecosystem is not one decision. It is a cycle of trying, measuring and improving.

The story

The Monk Who Counted Peas

In a monastery garden, one patient man found the rules of heredity in thousands of pea plants.

In 1856, in the town of Brno in what is now the Czech Republic, a monk named Gregor Mendel walked out to a small garden beside the monastery wall. He had been a physics student. He liked numbers. He had a question that farmers had asked for centuries: why does a child look like its parents, and why does it sometimes not?

Mendel chose the garden pea. Peas are easy to grow, and each flower usually fertilizes itself, so a plant's offspring stay true year after year. He picked traits that came in two clear forms with nothing in between: round or wrinkled seeds, yellow or green seeds, tall or short stems, purple or white flowers. Then he did something almost no one had done before. He counted.

He crossed a true-breeding round-seeded plant with a true-breeding wrinkled one, brushing pollen from one flower onto another by hand. Every seed in the next generation was round. The wrinkled form had vanished. Then he let those plants fertilize themselves. Wrinkled seeds came back: 1,850 of them among 5,474 round ones. Almost exactly one in four.

He tried yellow and green, tall and short, one trait after another. The same pattern appeared every time: the hidden form disappeared for one generation, then returned in about one quarter of the next. Over eight years he grew and counted about 28,000 plants. In 1866 he published his results, and hardly anyone read them.

Mendel died in 1884, still mostly unknown as a scientist. In 1900, three biologists working separately found the same three-to-one ratio and then found Mendel's paper. The rules he uncovered in that garden are now the foundation of genetics. He had never seen a chromosome or a gene. He had only counted, carefully, and trusted the numbers.

Main ideaA gene is a stretch of DNA that tells a cell how to build one protein, and proteins do the work that gives an organism its traits.

Main ideaA mutation is a change in the DNA sequence, and it can come from copying mistakes or from damage by chemicals or radiation.

Selective Breeding

Main ideaFor thousands of years people have chosen which plants and animals reproduce, and that choice slowly reshapes a species.

The story

The Beak That Changed With the Rain

On a tiny island in the Galápagos, two scientists measured finches for decades and watched evolution happen.

In 1835 a young naturalist named Charles Darwin stepped off the ship HMS Beagle onto the Galápagos Islands, 1,000 kilometers off the coast of South America. He collected small brown birds. Only later, back in England, did an expert tell him they were all finches, a dozen or so species, each with a different beak. Some had thick beaks like nutcrackers. Some had thin ones like tweezers. Darwin wondered whether one kind of finch had reached the islands and, over ages, become many.

Nearly 140 years later, in 1973, Peter and Rosemary Grant landed on Daphne Major, a volcanic islet in the same archipelago with no people, no fresh water and one main species of ground finch. They decided to catch, band and measure every finch on the island, every year. Beak length, beak depth, wing, weight. Then they would follow each bird for life and record which ones had chicks.

In 1977 the rains did not come. Plants withered. The small soft seeds the finches liked were eaten first. What was left were big, tough seeds with hard cases. Finches died by the hundreds. The Grants found that the survivors were not a random sample. Birds with slightly deeper, stronger beaks could crack the tough seeds. Those birds lived. Thinner-beaked birds mostly starved.

The next spring, the survivors bred. Their chicks, on average, had deeper beaks than the chicks born before the drought. Beak depth is inherited, so the drought had shifted the whole population in one generation. A few years later, a very wet season flooded the island with small seeds, and the advantage flipped toward smaller beaks. The population shifted back.

The Grants kept measuring for more than forty years. Their notebooks hold the strongest direct record ever made of natural selection in the wild. Darwin had guessed the process was too slow to see. On a bare rock in the Pacific, it turned out to be fast enough to catch with a ruler, a scale and patience.

Layers of Time

Main ideaRock layers stack up over time, so deeper fossils are usually older, and the sequence shows how life on Earth has changed.

Southwest of Chicago, near the town of Braidwood, old coal mine spoil piles hold round lumps of ironstone. Crack one open and you may find a fern leaf, a jellyfish, a shrimp, or the strange Tully monster, the state fossil of Illinois. These Mazon Creek fossils formed about 300 million years ago. Back then this land was a steamy swamp near the sea. A is any preserved trace of a living thing from the past. It can be a bone, a shell, a leaf print or a footprint.

Fossils form in layers of sediment. Mud settles on a river delta or sea floor, burying a dead animal. More mud settles on top. Over millions of years the layers harden into rock. In 1669 the Danish scientist Nicolas Steno stated the simple rule that follows: in undisturbed layers, the deepest layer formed first and the top formed last. This is the law of . It lets geologists put fossils in order without any dates at all.

Read the layers from the bottom up and a story appears. The oldest rocks hold only fossils of microbes. Higher up appear soft sea animals, then shells, then fish, then the first land plants and insects, then amphibians, reptiles, dinosaurs, and only near the very top, mammals like us. The same order shows up on every continent. Fish never appear below the first microbes. Mammals never appear below the first fish.

Since the early 1900s, scientists have also measured the steady decay of radioactive elements in rock. That gives real ages for layers. Those numbers agree with the order from superposition. They also give the timeline its scale. Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, and life has been here for most of that time.

Main ideaAnimals as different as bats, whales and people share the same bone pattern in their limbs, evidence of a shared ancestor.

Two lower bones. A cluster of wrist bones. Then five sets of finger bones, stretched long and thin in the bat’s wing, shortened and buried inside the whale’s flipper. Main ideaEarly embryos of different animals look alike, and many animals carry useless leftover parts, both signs of shared descent.

How Natural Selection Works

32.4

Variation Is Everywhere

Main ideaIndividuals in every population differ, and many of those differences are inherited, which gives natural selection something to act on.

Line up twenty acorns from one oak. No two are the same size. Measure twenty ground finches, as the Grants did, and beak depth ranges over several millimeters. Look around your classroom. Heights, hair, the shape of an earlobe, how well someone tastes bitter foods. is the rule in every population, not the exception. Much of it comes from mutation and from the shuffling of alleles in sexual reproduction.

Not all variation counts for evolution. A finch that lost a toe in an accident will not pass that on. Only variation, differences carried in genes, can move from parents to offspring. The Grants tested this on Daphne Major. Parents with deep beaks had chicks with deep beaks, on average, even in years with plenty of food. Beak depth was heritable.

Darwin’s first insight was simply to take variation seriously. Before him, many naturalists treated the differences among individuals as noise around an ideal type. Darwin saw that the differences were the raw material. If some variants survive and reproduce more than others, and the variants are inherited, the population must change. Everything else follows from that.

32.5

More Are Born Than Survive

Main ideaEvery species produces more offspring than the environment can support, so most die before reproducing, and which ones survive is not random.

A single oak can drop thousands of acorns in a year. A cod lays millions of eggs. A pair of finches can raise several clutches. If every offspring lived, the world would be buried in oaks, cod and finches within a few centuries. It is not. Populations stay roughly steady over long stretches, which means most offspring die before they reproduce. Darwin called this the struggle for existence.

Darwin got the idea partly from an economist. In 1798 Thomas Malthus wrote that human populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so hunger, disease and conflict hold them back. Darwin read Malthus in 1838 and saw the same logic in every species. The pressure never lets up. Food, water, space, predators and disease keep numbers in check, just as the limiting factors in the ecosystems chapter do.

Here is the key step. If survival were pure luck, the survivors would be a random sample and nothing would change. But survival is not pure luck. In the 1977 drought, finches with deeper beaks survived far more often than thin-beaked ones. Individuals whose heritable traits fit the environment better survive and reproduce more. Darwin named this : nature, not a breeder, does the choosing.

32.6

Selection Step by Step

Main ideaNatural selection changes a population over generations by making helpful heritable traits more common, and the result is adaptation.

Put the pieces together and follow one generation on Daphne Major. Step one: variation. Finch beaks range from shallow to deep, and the difference is inherited. Step two: overproduction. More chicks hatch than the island can feed. Step three: selection. A drought leaves only tough seeds, and deep-beaked birds crack them and survive while others starve. Step four: inheritance. The survivors breed, and their chicks inherit deeper beaks.

The result is a shift in the whole population. The average beak got deeper. No finch changed its own beak, and no finch tried to grow a deeper one. The population changed because some finches left more descendants than others. Repeat those four steps for thousands of generations and small shifts add up to big ones. A trait that spreads because it helps survival and reproduction in a particular environment is an .

Two warnings. First, selection has no goal. When wet years brought small seeds, smaller beaks did better and the population shifted back. The environment decides what is helpful, and the environment changes. Second, selection can only work with variation that already exists. If no finch had carried genes for a deeper beak, the drought would have simply killed finches. Mutation supplies new options; selection sorts them.

This is also how new species arise. Split a population, say on two islands with different seeds. Let selection push each in a different direction for long enough, and the two groups may become so different that they no longer interbreed. Darwin’s dozen finch species, with their dozen beaks, are what that process looks like after a few million years.

Evolution You Can Watch

32.7

Germs That Fight Back

Main ideaBacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics by natural selection, fast enough to see in hospitals within years.

In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed that a mold growing on one of his dishes had killed the bacteria around it. The mold made penicillin, the first , a drug that kills bacteria or stops them from growing. By the mid-1940s penicillin was saving soldiers’ lives. Within a few years, doctors were already finding bacteria it no longer killed.

The reason is natural selection running at high speed. Bacteria divide every twenty minutes or so, and a single infection holds billions of cells. Among those billions, a few carry a mutation that lets them survive the drug: an enzyme that chops it up, or a pump that pushes it out. Give the antibiotic, and the drug kills the sensitive cells. The rare cells survive, divide, and soon make up the whole population.

Two habits make the problem worse. Stopping a prescription early leaves the toughest survivors behind. Using antibiotics when they are not needed, such as for a viral cold, gives bacteria more chances to be selected. Today the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention counts millions of resistant infections and tens of thousands of deaths in the United States each year, and some strains resist nearly every drug we have.

The fight is a race. Scientists search soil and sea for new antibiotics, and doctors work to use existing ones carefully. Every step follows the same rules the finches did: variation, overproduction, selection, inheritance. Here, though, a generation lasts minutes, not years.

Main ideaDarwin used the breeding of pigeons, dogs and crops as a model for natural selection: the same process, with a breeder instead of the environment choosing.

Darwin kept pigeons. He joined pigeon clubs in London and studied fancy breeds with fan tails, feathered feet, puffed chests and tumbling flight. If human choice could turn one bird into dozens of forms in a few centuries, Darwin reasoned, what could nature’s choice do in millions of years? He opened the Origin of Species with pigeons for exactly that reason.

Breeding by people is called . The steps mirror natural selection: variation exists, a breeder chooses which individuals reproduce, and the offspring inherit the chosen traits. The difference is who chooses and why. A breeder picks the sweetest corn or the fastest horse. The environment picks whatever survives and breeds, with no plan at all.

You can run the model yourself. On the site’s turntables, spin a disk of colored beans and pick out only the darkest ten each round, then imagine those breed and the next round has more dark beans. Or track a real case: the University of Illinois corn experiment has chosen high-oil kernels every year since 1896, and oil content has climbed far beyond anything in the starting field. Selection, repeated, moves a population a long way.

32.9

How the Idea Grew

Main ideaDarwin and Wallace proposed natural selection in 1858, and later discoveries in genetics and DNA filled in what they could not know.

Darwin returned from the Beagle in 1836 and worked on his idea for twenty years, filling notebooks and breeding pigeons. In 1858 a letter arrived from Alfred Russel Wallace, a naturalist collecting in the islands of Southeast Asia. Wallace had reached the same idea on his own. Friends arranged for both men’s papers to be read together at a scientific meeting in London that year. Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859.

Darwin had a serious gap. He did not know how traits were inherited. Mendel’s pea paper appeared in 1866, but Darwin never saw it, and no one connected the two until after 1900. In the twentieth century, genes, chromosomes and mutations gave natural selection its missing piece: a way for variation to arise and be passed on. Then in 1953 the structure of DNA was worked out, and the code itself came into view.

DNA turned out to be the strongest evidence of all. Every living thing uses the same code of four bases and the same basic set of amino acids. Species that fossils and anatomy suggested were close relatives also turned out to have the most similar DNA. Scientists can now draw the family tree of life from DNA alone, and it matches the tree drawn from bones and rocks.

Evolution is not a claim that living things are getting better, or that any change is planned. It is the observation that populations change over generations, plus the explanation, tested for more than 160 years, of how that happens. Like every good scientific idea, it is still being refined, but its core has survived every test so far.

32.10

Cousins, Not Parents

Main ideaHumans did not descend from chimpanzees; humans and the great apes share common ancestors, with chimpanzees as our closest living relatives.

Stand a human skeleton beside a chimpanzee skeleton. The bones match one for one: same skull plan, same 206 or so bones, same five fingers with a thumb. Humans and chimpanzees share about 98 to 99 percent of their DNA sequence. Gorillas are a little more distant, orangutans a little more still. Together with humans, these animals are the great apes.

A common mistake is to say humans came from chimps. They did not. Chimpanzees are cousins, not ancestors. Both lines descend from a common ancestor that lived in Africa roughly six or seven million years ago. That ancestor was neither a modern chimp nor a modern human. One line led to today’s chimpanzees and bonobos. Another line, with many branches, led to us. Every other branch on our side has died out.

Chromosomes give a striking clue. Chimpanzees, gorillas and orangutans have 48 chromosomes; humans have 46. Human chromosome 2 carries, end to end, the same genes found on two separate ape chromosomes, with leftover pieces at the join. Two ancestral chromosomes fused into one somewhere along the human line. That is exactly what a shared ancestry predicts and very hard to explain any other way.

Main ideaFossils from Africa show a branching human family over millions of years, with upright walking coming long before large brains.

Lucy belongs to a species called Australopithecus afarensis, one of many extinct relatives on the human branch.

Lucy overturned an old guess. Many scientists had expected big brains to come first and upright walking later. The fossils say the opposite. Upright walking appears by around four million years ago. Brains stayed small for a long time afterward and grew mainly in the last two million years, along with stone tools, control of fire, and eventually language and art.

The family tree is bushy, not a ladder. At several times, more than one kind of human relative lived at once. Neanderthals lived in Europe and Asia until about 40,000 years ago and even interbred with our ancestors; many people today carry a small amount of Neanderthal DNA. Our own species, Homo sapiens, appears in Africa about 300,000 years ago and then spread across the world. Every person alive today belongs to that one species.

11. Why is the sickle cell allele common where malaria is widespread?

Why: Having one copy protects against severe malaria, so selection keeps the allele common despite its cost.

12. Which order of first appearance matches the fossil record from bottom to top?

Why: The lowest layers hold only microbes; fish appear far above, and mammals near the top.

13. After the 1977 drought, the average beak of Daphne Major finches was deeper. This happened because:

Why: Individuals did not change. The population shifted because survival depended on a heritable trait.

14. Bacteria that survive a course of antibiotics are the ones that:

Why: The drug selects for resistant cells that existed before treatment; their descendants take over.

15. Humans and chimpanzees are best described as:

Why: Neither came from the other. Both descend from an ape ancestor that lived roughly six or seven million years ago.
