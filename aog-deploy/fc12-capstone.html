A plan is built backwards

The dot at the left is now. The heavy line at the right is the moment it has to be ready. Three jobs, not placed yet.

You build a timeline backwards from the moment it has to be ready, not forwards from now. A task that cannot start until another one finishes is a dependency, and dependencies set the order. The time left over at the now end is slack — your margin on the day something goes wrong.

Four things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

I will start today and see how far I get by Friday.

Counting forward always looks like there is enough time, because you stop counting when you run out of tasks. Start at the due date and walk back one task at a time. When you reach today, you know whether the plan fits — or that it already does not.

Start at the end. Forward counting always looks fine.

⚠️ People often think…

My materials come to $18.00, so I need $18.00.

Prices move and packages come up short. A ten percent contingency on $18.00 is $1.80, so you plan to spend $19.80. Against a $24.00 budget that still leaves $4.20 untouched. A budget with no contingency is a hope, not a plan.

Ten percent set aside — and written down when you spend it.

⚠️ People often think…

I have six hours of work and eight hours left, so I am fine.

Some tasks wait on others. You cannot sew a seam until the pieces are cut, and you cannot cook the rice until you own the rice. Find the longest chain of waiting tasks — that chain, not the pile of hours, is how long the project really takes.

Measure the chain, not the pile.

⚠️ People often think…

I will write the quality standards once I see how it came out.

Standards written afterwards are not standards; they are excuses. Say what finished means before you start — the seam straight and pressed, the chicken at 165 °F, the rice tender — then test the work against what you wrote.

Written first it is a standard. Written last it is an excuse.

Start at the end and walk back

1 Watch one

Dinner is on the table at 6:00. Chicken takes 50 minutes plus a 5-minute rest, rice takes 20 plus 5, green beans take 8. When does each one start?

  1. Write the serving time first: 6:00. Every start time is measured back from it.
  2. Chicken: 50 minutes to cook and 5 to rest. 50 + 5 = 55. 6:00 minus 55 minutes is 5:05.
  3. Rice: 20 minutes covered and 5 off the heat. 20 + 5 = 25. 6:00 minus 25 minutes is 5:35.
  4. Green beans: 8 minutes, nothing to rest. 6:00 minus 8 minutes is 5:52.
  5. Chicken in at 5:05, rice at 5:35, beans at 5:52. The longest job starts first and the quickest starts last — and all three are ready at 6:00. ✓

Six ingredients cost $18.00 and the dish serves six. What is the cost per serving?

I want a hint first

Six servings share one total. The total cost is the number that gets divided, and the servings are what you divide it by: 18.00 ÷ 6.

Helpful Hints

🧭 The unit in one line

Define the problem → research → write the spec → plan the time backwards → build the budget → make it → test it → evaluate it → present it. One cycle, every project, for the rest of your life.

Spec, money, time, make, check, show.

🔑 The one idea

Decide on paper first. A choice made in writing costs a pencil; the same choice made at the cash register or at the sewing machine costs money, fabric and a week. The plan is the cheap part, and it is the part you are graded on.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Shopping before the spec is written. You come home with what caught your eye, and then you bend the project to fit the bag.
  • A budget with no contingency. Every real project has at least one surprise; a plan with no room for one is a hope, not a plan.
  • Counting forward from today instead of backwards from the due date. Counting forward hides whether you will actually finish.
  • Writing “good” in an evaluation. Crisp, chewy, bland, snug, puckered, straight — a descriptor tells the reader what you noticed.
  • Setting the quality standards after you see the result. Standards written afterwards are not standards; they are excuses.
  • Forgetting that some tasks wait on others. You cannot sew a seam before the pieces are cut, and you cannot cook the rice before you own the rice.
  • Leaving the record for the last night. Photographs, the cost sheet and the changes you made are written down as you go, or they are lost.

  • “Put the nine steps of the project cycle in order.”
  • “Write the spec for your project — what it is, who it is for, what finished means.”
  • A cost sheet and a number of servings — “what is the cost per serving?”

✅ Before the test, can you…

  • Name the nine steps of the project cycle in order?
  • Write a spec someone else could check you against?
  • Add a ten percent contingency to a total and say what is left of the budget?
  • Build a timeline backwards from a serving time or a due date?
  • List what the meal track demands and what the sewn-item track demands?
  • Evaluate your own work in descriptors, and name one thing you would change?
