Angels Up Close

Angels in the Texts

Angels are everywhere in modern life: on greeting cards, in movies, on holiday trees and in everyday sayings. Most of those pictures come from centuries of art and tradition. This unit goes back to the texts themselves. You will read what the Hebrew Bible, the New Testament and other ancient Jewish writings actually say about the beings they call angels, cherubim, seraphim, watchers and holy ones.

Each lesson starts from an ancient text: Isaiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, the Gospels, Revelation, the Greek Septuagint, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, Josephus and the Dead Sea Scrolls. Then you will hear how researchers read it. Michael S. Heiser, in Angels (2018), argued that some terms describe a being's role, such as messenger, and others its status, such as elohim. E. Theodore Mullen, J. T. Milik, George Nickelsburg, Carol Newsom, Clinton Arnold and Walter Wink add other angles.

By the end of the unit you will be able to explain the main words for heavenly beings and where they appear. You will be able to tell what a text says from what later writers and artists added. You will also be able to explain why the texts insist that angels serve God and are never to be worshiped, and how different traditions read those texts.

The big question

What do the ancient texts really say about angels, and how can a careful reader tell that apart from what later traditions added?

Names, Ranks and Roles

The Heavenly Host
Big questionWhen the Bible names heavenly beings, is it describing what they are, what they do, or where they stand?
The story

Holy, Holy, Holy

In the year a king died, the prophet Isaiah says he saw the LORD enthroned, with burning beings calling out above him.

Isaiah 6 opens with a date. "In the year that king Uzziah died," Isaiah says, he saw the LORD sitting on a throne, high and lifted up. Uzziah had ruled Judah for decades, and his death is usually dated around 740 BCE. A long reign had ended. The prophet's vision begins at a moment of change for his people.

In the vision, the hem of the LORD's robe fills the temple. Above him stand seraphim. Each has six wings. With two they cover their faces, with two they cover their feet, and with two they fly. They call to one another: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory." The doorposts shake, and the house fills with smoke.

Isaiah does not feel ready. He is shaken. He says he is a man of unclean lips, living among a people of unclean lips. Then one of the seraphim flies to him with a live coal from the altar. It touches his mouth, and the seraph tells him his guilt is taken away. The scene moves from fear to cleansing.

Then a voice asks, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" Isaiah answers, "Here am I; send me." Many scholars read the word "us" as God speaking in front of his heavenly council, like the scene the prophet Micaiah describes in 1 Kings 22:19 to 22. Other readers hear a royal way of speaking, or, in many Christian readings, a hint of the Trinity.

The seraphim's song did not stay in Isaiah's book. Jewish prayer services still repeat it in a prayer called the Kedushah. Many Christian services sing it in the Sanctus. In this chapter you will look closely at the names the Bible gives to heavenly beings like these, and at what those names tell you.

Talk about itThe seraphim cover their faces even while standing near God. What might that detail be meant to show a reader about them?
Section 1

Words for Heavenly Beings

27.1

Angel Is a Job Title

Main ideaThe Hebrew and Greek words for angel both mean messenger, so the word names a job, not a kind of being.

Open Genesis 32. In verse 1, Jacob is traveling, and "the angels of God" meet him. Two verses later, Jacob sends "messengers" ahead to his brother Esau. English uses two different words. The Hebrew uses one word both times: mal&#x27;ak, plural mal’akim. Jacob’s messengers were ordinary men. The beings who met him were from God. The word is the same because both groups do the same task. They carry a message.

Greek works the same way. The New Testament word is , and it too means messenger. Mark 1:2 uses it for John the Baptist, a human being. The last book of the Old Testament is called Malachi, which means "my messenger" in Hebrew. So when readers see "angel" in an English Bible, the older languages are usually saying "one who is sent." Translators decide from the context whether the messenger is human or heavenly.

Ancient readers noticed this too. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish writer of the first century CE, wrote that the beings other philosophers called daemons, Moses called angels. He pictured them as souls living in the air (On the Giants, C. D. Yonge’s translation). In his book Angels (2018), Michael Heiser built on the language point. He argued that some biblical terms describe a being’s role, while others describe its status or its home. Angel, in his reading, is a role word. It tells you what a being is doing, not what kind of being it is. Other scholars make the same language point, even when they read particular passages differently.

Think it through

27.2

Elohim, Sons of God, Holy Ones

Main ideaElohim, sons of God and holy ones are words that place beings in the unseen realm around God.

Psalm 82:1 reads, in the King James Version, "God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods." In Hebrew, both "God" and "gods" are the same word: . The God of Israel is called elohim. So are the members of his council. So, once, is the spirit of the dead Samuel in 1 Samuel 28:13. Heiser argued that elohim is not a name for one particular being. It is a word for any being whose natural home is the spirit world.

Another phrase is . In Job 1:6 and 2:1 the sons of God come to present themselves before the LORD. In Job 38:7 they shout for joy when the earth’s foundations are laid. Clay tablets from ancient Ugarit, in Syria, speak of a council of the gods around the high god El. E. Theodore Mullen compared that council with Israel’s in The Divine Council in Canaanite and Early Hebrew Literature (1980). Many readers agree the sons of God in Job are heavenly beings. Genesis 6, where the phrase also appears, is more debated.

A third term is , in Hebrew qedoshim. Psalm 89:5 to 7 speaks of an assembly of the holy ones in the heavens. It asks who among the heavenly beings can compare to the LORD. Holy means set apart. All three terms describe status: where a being belongs and how it stands near God. They say little about what job it is doing at a given moment. That is what role words like messenger do.

Psalm 89:6–7, King James Version (1611)

The word translated "saints" here is qedoshim, holy ones. Who does the psalm say is in this assembly, and where is it?

27.3

Host, Ministers and Watchers

Main ideaHost, ministers and watchers name the heavenly beings as an army, as servants and as those who keep watch.

In 1 Kings 22:19 the prophet Micaiah says he saw the LORD on his throne with "all the host of heaven" standing on his right and his left. The word translates the Hebrew tsaba, a word for an army. The title "LORD of hosts" appears hundreds of times in the Hebrew Bible. Isaiah’s seraphim use it. In Luke 2:13 a "multitude of the heavenly host" praises God over the shepherds. The picture is of a great, ordered company around a king.

Psalm 103:20 to 21 calls on the angels and hosts to bless the LORD. It calls them "ministers of his, that do his pleasure." The word here means servants who attend a king. Psalm 104:4 says God makes his ministers a flaming fire, and Hebrews 1:7 quotes that verse. These words describe what the heavenly beings do. They serve and carry out orders. In that sense they are role words, like messenger.

Daniel 4 adds an unusual term. King Nebuchadnezzar dreams of "a watcher and an holy one" coming down from heaven (4:13). A decision is said to come "by the decree of the watchers" (4:17). This part of Daniel is in Aramaic, and the word is ’ir, plural ’irin, meaning one who is awake. The watchers in Daniel are God’s faithful servants. In 1976 J. T. Milik published the Aramaic fragments of 1 Enoch found in Qumran Cave 4. They use the same word for the Watchers who sinned. So the word by itself does not mean good or bad. The story around it does.

From the source
The Decree of the Watchers
This matter is by the decree of the watchers, and the demand by the word of the holy ones: to the intent that the living may know that the most High ruleth in the kingdom of men, and giveth it to whomsoever he will.
Daniel 4:17, King James Version (1611)

The watchers and holy ones announce a decision here. According to the verse, who really rules, and what are the watchers doing?

Why: Tsaba is a word for an army. "LORD of hosts" pictures God as commander of a heavenly company.

2. In which book does a watcher and a holy one come down from heaven in a king's dream?

Section 2

Beings Around the Throne

27.4

Cherubim

Main ideaCherubim in the Bible are guardians of God's presence, not the chubby babies of later art.

The first in the Bible stand at the east of Eden in Genesis 3:24. They guard the way to the tree of life with a flaming sword. In Exodus 25:18 to 22, two golden cherubim are made for the cover of the ark of the covenant. Their wings stretch over it. God says he will meet Moses and speak with him from between them. In both places, cherubim guard the space where God is present.

Ezekiel 1 describes four living creatures beneath a throne-chariot. Each has four faces and four wings. In Ezekiel 10:20 the prophet says he knew these were cherubim. Many scholars compare them to the winged guardian figures of the ancient Near East. The Assyrian kings, for example, set huge winged creatures with human heads at their palace gates. Read in that setting, the biblical cherubim are throne guardians around a divine king. Philo of Alexandria read them another way. He took the two cherubim of Genesis 3 as symbols of God’s two chief powers, goodness and authority (On the Cherubim).

Later art changed the picture. Renaissance painters filled their works with winged baby figures, called , borrowed from the cupids of Greek and Roman art. Over time people began calling these babies cherubs. Many scholars point out that nothing in Genesis, Exodus or Ezekiel describes cherubim as babies. The texts describe powerful, many-faced beings who guard what is holy.

27.5

Seraphim

Main ideaSeraphim, the burning ones of Isaiah 6, stand above God's throne and call out his holiness.

Isaiah 6 is the only place in the Bible where appear clearly as heavenly beings. They stand above the LORD’s throne. Each has six wings. They cover their faces and feet and call out, "Holy, holy, holy." One of them brings a coal from the altar to cleanse Isaiah’s lips. They are worshipers and also agents who act.

The Hebrew root saraph is linked to burning. So seraphim can be translated "burning ones." Many readers take this as a picture of fiery brightness around God. But the same word appears elsewhere for serpents. In Numbers 21:6 the LORD sends "fiery serpents" among the people, and the Hebrew there is seraphim. Isaiah 14:29 and 30:6 mention a flying saraph.

Heiser noted this link. He argued that the seraphim may have been pictured as shining, serpent-like throne guardians. Egyptian royal art often shows cobras, called serpents, guarding a king. Other scholars prefer to keep the image simpler, as glowing beings with human-like faces, feet and hands. The text of Isaiah 6 gives faces, feet and wings, and leaves the rest to the reader.

27.6

Living Creatures in Revelation

Main ideaRevelation 4 draws on both Ezekiel and Isaiah for its four living creatures around God's throne.

In Revelation 4:6 to 8, John sees four around God’s throne. One is like a lion, one like a calf, one has a face like a man, and one is like a flying eagle. Each has six wings and is full of eyes. Day and night they say, "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty, which was, and is, and is to come."

Readers who know the Hebrew Bible notice the echoes. The four faces of lion, ox, man and eagle come from Ezekiel 1:10, where each creature has all four. The six wings and the triple "holy" come from Isaiah 6. Revelation blends the two visions into one scene. This kind of borrowing is called . A writer brings in older texts so that readers will recall them.

Many scholars read Revelation 4 as another picture of God’s throne room, with the council setting found in 1 Kings 22 and Daniel 7. Christian tradition from the late 100s onward also linked the four faces to the four Gospels. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, made one such pairing in Against Heresies, Book 3 (Ante-Nicene Fathers, volume 1). Later writers, such as Jerome, paired them differently. These links are readings of the symbols, not something Revelation itself states.

Section 3

Ranks in the Text and Beyond

27.7

Archangels and Named Angels

Main ideaThe Bible uses the word archangel only twice, and names very few angels, but later Jewish writings name more.

The word means chief angel. It appears only twice in the New Testament. 1 Thessalonians 4:16 says the Lord will come down "with the voice of the archangel." Jude 9 calls Michael "the archangel" and tells of his dispute with the devil over the body of Moses. In Daniel 10:13 Michael is "one of the chief princes," and in Daniel 12:1 he is "the great prince" who stands for Daniel’s people.

Only two angels are named in the Protestant Bible. Michael appears in Daniel, Jude and Revelation 12:7. explains visions to Daniel in Daniel 8 and 9. In Luke 1 he announces the births of John and Jesus. The book of Tobit, which is in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, names Raphael. There he calls himself one of seven holy angels who stand before God (12:15).

Jewish writings from the Second Temple period name more. 1 Enoch 20, in R. H. Charles’s 1917 translation, lists holy angels who watch. They include Uriel, Raphael, Michael and Gabriel. 1 Enoch 40 names four angels who stand before God: Michael, Raphael, Gabriel and Phanuel. George Nickelsburg’s commentary, 1 Enoch 1 (2001), traces how such lists grew. Many readers see them as developing hints in the Bible, such as Daniel’s chief princes. A careful reader notices the difference between what the canonical texts say and what later writers added.

27.8

Nine Choirs: A Later System

Main ideaPaul's words for powers hint at ranks, but the nine choirs of angels come from a writer about 500 CE.

Colossians 1:16 says that all things were created through Christ, "whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers." Ephesians 1:21 lists principality, power, might and dominion. These are words for rulers and their territories. Paul does not arrange them into a ladder. Clinton Arnold, in Powers of Darkness (1992), read them as spiritual powers, as Jewish writings of the time did. Walter Wink, in Naming the Powers (1984), argued that the words cover both heavenly powers and earthly rulers and institutions.

About 500 CE a Christian writer produced a book called The Celestial Hierarchy. He wrote under the name of Dionysius, a man Paul met in Athens (Acts 17:34). Scholars call him , since the real author lived centuries later. He arranged angels into nine orders, often called the nine , in three groups of three. Seraphim, cherubim and thrones came first. Dominions, powers and authorities came next. Principalities, archangels and angels came last. Translations name the middle group in slightly different ways.

This system shaped later thought. Thomas Aquinas discussed it, and Dante’s Paradiso pictures the nine orders circling God. Catholic and Orthodox traditions still use it. Many Protestant readers see it as a thoughtful guess that goes beyond the text. Readers who start with the terms in the Bible mark the full ranking as a later built on them.

Angels and People

27.9

Guardian Angels?

Main ideaA few verses link angels to believers, and traditions differ on how far to take the idea of a personal guardian.

In Matthew 18:10 Jesus says not to despise "these little ones," because "in heaven their angels do always behold the face of my Father." In Acts 12, Peter is freed from prison and knocks at the door of Mary’s house. A servant named Rhoda runs to tell the others. They answer, "It is his angel" (12:15). Psalm 91:11 says God will give his angels charge over the one who trusts him.

Many Christians read these verses as teaching that each believer has a . An early Christian book, the Shepherd of Hermas, says each person has two angels, one of righteousness and one of wickedness (Mandate 6.2, Ante-Nicene Fathers, volume 2). The Catholic Church teaches that each believer has an angel as protector and keeps a feast of the Guardian Angels on October 2. The Talmud (Shabbat 119b) tells of two ministering angels who walk a person home on the eve of the Sabbath.

Careful readers separate what these verses say from what later teaching builds on them. Matthew 18:10 says the little ones have angels who see God’s face. It does not say how many angels or what they do. Acts 12:15 tells what Peter’s friends believed. It is a report of their words, not a separate teaching. The Sabbath song Shalom Aleichem greets the angels of the Talmud’s story. Careful readers weigh each verse on its own before building a doctrine.

27.10

People Do Not Become Angels

Main ideaIn the Bible, angels and humans are different kinds of beings, and the texts do not say the dead become angels.

In the 1946 film It’s a Wonderful Life, a man who died long ago returns as Clarence, "Angel, Second Class," trying to earn his wings. Many people today say that someone who has died has become an angel. Heiser pointed out that this is a popular idea, not a biblical one. In the Bible, angels and humans are different kinds of beings.

Several texts support his point. Job 38:7 has the sons of God shouting for joy when the earth was founded, before any humans existed. In Matthew 22:30 Jesus says the raised dead will be "as the angels" in not marrying. The word as makes a comparison. It does not say they become angels. Hebrews 12:22 to 23 lists "an innumerable company of angels" and, separately, "the spirits of just men made perfect." The two groups stand side by side.

Heiser also drew on 1 Corinthians 6:3, where Paul says believers will judge angels. In his reading, the New Testament pictures redeemed humans raised to share in God’s rule, as humans, even above the angels. Other Christian readers stress different parts of these texts, but most agree on the basic between human and angel. The popular idea often grows from grief and love for someone who has died.

Angels in Worship, Law and Story

Angels in Scripture
Big questionWhat jobs do angels do in the Bible's stories, and why do the texts insist that they are never to be worshiped?
The story

Get Up, I Am a Servant Too

Near the end of Revelation, John falls at an angel's feet, and the angel tells him to stop.

The book of Revelation says it was written by a man named John on the island of Patmos, off the coast of what is now Turkey (Revelation 1:9). It is full of visions. John sees thrones, lampstands, sealed scrolls and a city of gold. Angels guide him through much of it. They show him scenes and explain what they mean.

In Revelation 19, an angel tells John to write down a blessing on those invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb. John is overwhelmed. He falls at the angel's feet to worship him. The angel stops him at once: "See thou do it not: I am thy fellowservant, and of thy brethren that have the testimony of Jesus: worship God."

Then, near the very end of the book, it happens again. In Revelation 22:8 to 9, John says that when he had heard and seen these things, he fell down to worship before the feet of the angel who showed them to him. The angel gives almost the same answer. He is a fellow servant with John, with the prophets and with all who keep the words of the book. Worship God.

Readers have long noticed that the scene appears twice. Some think the repetition is there on purpose, to make the lesson impossible to miss. The angel does not scold John or frighten him. He simply puts himself on the same level as John, as a servant, and points away from himself.

Many readers treat scenes like this as a key to how the Bible talks about angels. They are powerful and they serve, but worship is for God alone. In this chapter you will follow angels through the giving of the law, the worship of heaven, the Gospel stories and later Jewish writings, and see how that key keeps turning up.

Main ideaDeuteronomy 33:2 links Sinai with God's holy ones, and the Greek Septuagint names angels there directly.

Exodus 19 and 20 tell of God giving the law at Mount with thunder, fire and a thick cloud. Those chapters do not mention angels. But a later poem does. In Deuteronomy 33:2, Moses blesses the tribes and says that the LORD came from Sinai. In the King James Version, he came "with ten thousands of saints," and from his right hand went "a fiery law." The Hebrew of this verse is hard. The words translated "fiery law" are unusual, and scholars still debate them. The , the ancient Greek translation made in the centuries before Jesus, reads the end of the verse differently. It says that at God’s right hand there were angels with him. So in the Greek, the angels at Sinai are named outright. A difference like this between two forms of a text is called a .

Psalm 68:17 adds another picture. It says the chariots of God are twenty thousand, and the Lord is among them, as in Sinai. Heiser read these verses together. In his view, the Hebrew Bible pictures God at Sinai surrounded by his heavenly host, and the Septuagint made that picture plainer. Other scholars read the Hebrew holy ones as possibly Israel’s own people, or stress how uncertain the verse is.

28.2

The Law Given Through Angels

Main ideaThree New Testament passages say the law came through angels, a tradition shared with other Jewish writings of the time.

In Acts 7, Stephen speaks before the council in Jerusalem. He ends by saying his hearers "received the law by the disposition of angels, and have not kept it" (7:53). Paul writes in Galatians 3:19 that the law "was ordained by angels in the hand of a ." Hebrews 2:2 calls the law "the word spoken by angels." Three different writers assume the same thing. Angels had a part in giving the law at Sinai.

This idea was not new with the New Testament. The book of , written in Hebrew around the 100s BCE, retells Genesis and part of Exodus. In it, an angel of the presence writes out history for Moses on the mountain (Jubilees 1:27 to 2:1, in R. H. Charles’s 1917 translation). Jubilees is part of the Bible only in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Josephus, writing in the 90s CE, has King Herod tell his troops that the Jews learned the holiest part of their laws from God through angels (Antiquities 15.5.3, Whiston’s 1737 translation). Scholars debate whether Herod means heavenly angels or human messengers such as prophets.

Many scholars think the New Testament writers drew on this shared , which grew from verses like Deuteronomy 33:2. Readers differ on why each writer mentions it. In Galatians, many scholars think Paul uses it to say the law came less directly than God’s promise to Abraham. In Hebrews, the point is that if the word spoken by angels was binding, the message spoken by the Son matters even more.

28.4

Colossians 2:18: Two Readings

Main ideaColossians 2:18 warns about the worship of angels, and scholars read the phrase in two main ways.

Paul’s letter to the Colossians warns its readers about teachers who were judging them. In the King James Version, Colossians 2:18 says, "Let no man beguile you of your reward in a voluntary humility and worshipping of angels, intruding into those things which he hath not seen." The key phrase in Greek is thrēskeia tōn angelōn, "worship of the angels."

The phrase is , meaning it can be read more than one way. In the first reading, it means worship given to angels. Some people in Colossae, on this view, were honoring angels in a way the letter rejects. In the second reading, it means the worship that angels offer. On this view, some teachers claimed to join the angels’ worship in heaven through visions, and looked down on those who did not.

Both readings have strong defenders. The second reading gained support after the Dead Sea Scrolls showed Jewish groups who hoped to join the worship of angels. Either way, the letter’s answer is the same. Colossians 2:19 says the readers should hold fast to the head, which in this letter is Christ. On both readings, the letter keeps angels in a serving role, never sharing God’s worship.

Main ideaA Dead Sea Scrolls text pictures angels as priests leading worship in a heavenly temple.

Main ideaAll four Gospels place heavenly messengers at Jesus' empty tomb, though they describe them differently.

Angels in Art and Music

Main ideaArtists and composers have retold the Bible's angel scenes for centuries, adding details of their own.

Around 1320, the poet Dante finished his Paradiso, the last part of his Divine Comedy. In it he pictures the nine orders of angels circling God in rings of light, following Pseudo-Dionysius. In 1512 the painter Raphael finished the Sistine Madonna. Two small winged children lean on a ledge at the bottom of the painting. These became some of the most copied angels in the world.

Music carried the angel scenes too. Charles Wesley’s Christmas hymn, now known as Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, was first published in 1739. George Frideric Handel composed Messiah in 1741. One of its choruses sets the angels’ words from Luke 2:14, "Glory to God in the highest." Choirs in Chicago and around the world still perform Messiah each winter.

Art and music are forms of . Each artist chooses what to show and adds details the text does not give, such as halos, harps, feathered wings or baby faces. A reader can enjoy such works and still know where their details come from. A good habit is to ask of any image: which verse is this showing, and what has the artist added?

Why: The Greek phrase can mean worship directed to angels or the worship angels offer, which some teachers claimed to join.

5. In which book does an angel guide a young man named Tobias on a journey?

Why: In Tobit, Raphael, calling himself Azarias, guides Tobias to Media and home again.

6. What do all four Gospel accounts of the empty tomb share?

Why: The accounts differ in details, but each gives the core message that Jesus is not in the tomb and is risen.

7. What do the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice picture?

Why: The Songs, found at Qumran and Masada, describe heavenly beings praising God as priests in a heavenly temple.

8. Which popular idea about angels comes mostly from Renaissance art?

Why: Renaissance putti, like those in Raphael's Sistine Madonna, turned cherubs into winged babies. The texts describe guardians.

Unit test

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. The Hebrew mal'ak and the Greek angelos both mean what?

Why: Both words mean messenger. They name a job, which is why Genesis 32 uses mal'ak for heavenly and human messengers alike.

2. Which Hebrew word can name the God of Israel and the members of his council?

Why: Psalm 82:1 uses elohim for both. Heiser read it as a word for any being of the spirit world.

3. In which book does Nebuchadnezzar dream of a watcher and a holy one?

Why: Daniel 4:13 and 4:17 describe the watchers and holy ones in the king's dream.

4. What do the cherubim do in Genesis 3:24 and Exodus 25?

Why: Cherubim guard the way to the tree of life and cover the ark, where God meets Moses.

5. Which beings have six wings in Isaiah 6?

Why: Isaiah 6:2 says each seraph has six wings.

6. Where does the idea of nine ranked choirs of angels come from?

Why: Pseudo-Dionysius arranged nine orders about 500 CE. Paul names some powers without ranking them.

7. Which two angels are named in the Protestant Bible?

Why: Only Michael and Gabriel are named in the Protestant Bible. Raphael appears in Tobit.

8. Which verse lists angels and the spirits of just men as separate groups?

Why: Hebrews 12:22-23 names the company of angels and the spirits of just men side by side.

9. Hebrews 1:6 quotes a line about angels worshiping the Son. Which form of Deuteronomy 32:43 does it match?

Why: The line matches the Greek Septuagint; a Dead Sea Scrolls copy has a similar Hebrew line.

10. In Revelation 22:8-9, why does the angel refuse John's worship?

Why: The angel calls himself John's fellow servant and says, "worship God."

11. Stephen, Paul and the writer of Hebrews all say the law was given with the help of whom?

Why: Acts 7:53, Galatians 3:19 and Hebrews 2:2 all speak of angels at the giving of the law.

12. Which statement about Colossians 2:18 is accurate?

Why: The phrase may mean worship given to angels or joining in the angels' own worship.

13. In the birth stories, how does the angel speak to Joseph?

Why: Matthew 1:20, 2:13 and 2:19 describe the angel speaking to Joseph in dreams.

14. Which reading of the empty-tomb accounts fits them together into one fuller story?

Why: Harmonizing combines the Gospel accounts into one story and explains differences as different focus.

15. Which popular idea is not found in the Bible's texts?

Why: The texts keep angels and humans distinct; Hebrews 12:22-23 lists them as separate groups. The dead do not become angels.

1. (Unit 13) Which churches keep 1 Enoch in their Bibles?

Why: Only the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo and Eritrean Orthodox churches include 1 Enoch in their canon.

Why: The manuscripts of Luke 10:1 differ between seventy and seventy-two, matching the Table of Nations counts.

Why: Augustine held that Christ's Spirit spoke through Noah to the people of his day.

Why: Jubilees says God placed spirits over the many nations but appointed no angel or spirit over Israel; he alone is its ruler.

Why: Jerome wrote in 393 CE that Jude's quotation of Enoch made many reject it.
