Great Debates and Capstone

Capstone

This last unit brings the course together. You will read two famous passages in full. In Kiddushin 30b-32a, the rabbis ask what it means to honor and revere parents, and they learn from a jeweler in Ashkelon. In Eruvin 13b, two schools argue for three years, and a heavenly voice calls both sides the words of the living God.

Then you will do what the Talmud does. With a partner, in chavruta, you will choose a sugya, map its moves, bring in Rashi, Tosafot and a modern reader, and present both sides. Your thesis will name what the argument decides and whom the ruling protects.

By the end, you will be able to read a whole sugya, cite it by tractate and page, attribute every view to its reader and argue fairly with someone who disagrees. Those are the course's two threads, argument and mercy, in your own hands.

The big question

How can a text that keeps its arguments open still guide people toward what is right and kind?

Honoring Parents, and "These and Those": A Whole Sugya

Kiddushin and Eruvin
Big questionWhen two duties or two opinions pull against each other, how does the Talmud decide without silencing the losing side?
The story

The Key Under the Pillow

A jeweler in Ashkelon turns down a fortune so his father can sleep, and the rabbis make him a lesson.

The Gemara in tractate Kiddushin, page 31a, tells of a man named Dama ben Netina. He lived in Ashkelon, a port city on the coast. He was not Jewish. He owned precious stones, and the sages came to buy one for the high priest's breastplate. The price the Gemara gives is enormous: 600,000 gold coins, and one teacher, Rav Kahana, reports 800,000.

The key to the chest was under his father's pillow. His father was asleep. Dama would not wake him, and the buyers left. The Gemara says that the next year a red heifer, a rare and needed animal, was born in his herd. When the sages came to buy it, he asked only for the sum he had lost the year before.

The story is not told for its own sake. It answers a question. Rabbi Eliezer was asked how far the duty to honor parents reaches. He answered, in the Gemara's telling, that his students should go and see what one non-Jew did in Ashkelon. A Jewish legal discussion takes its model from outside the community.

This chapter reads that page slowly, then crosses to tractate Eruvin 13b. There, two schools argue for three years, and a voice from heaven gives an answer that keeps both sides. You will map each passage move by move and ask what each argument protects.

Talk about itWhy might the rabbis choose a non-Jew as their model for honoring parents, rather than a famous sage?
Section 1

Honor and Reverence

33.1

Two Verses, Two Duties

Main ideaThe Gemara reads two Torah verses as two different duties: honor (kibbud) and reverence (mora).

The Ten Commandments in Exodus 20:12 say to your father and mother. Leviticus 19:3 says to revere, or fear, your mother and father. The rabbis of the Talmud held that no word of the Torah is extra. So two verses with two different verbs must teach two different duties.

A , a teaching from the time of the Mishnah that was left out of it, spells out the difference. Kiddushin 31b records it. Reverence means not standing in a parent’s set place, not sitting in a parent’s seat, not contradicting a parent’s words and not deciding against a parent in a dispute. Honor means giving food and drink, clothing and covering, and helping a parent come in and go out.

Notice the shape. Reverence is mostly things you hold back from doing. Honor is mostly things you actively do. Later readers, like Maimonides in his code (Mishneh Torah, Laws of Rebels, chapter 6), kept this two-part structure. It gave Jewish law a practical checklist rather than a vague feeling.

33.2

Three Partners in a Person

Main ideaKiddushin 30b compares honoring parents to honoring God and calls a person's parents God's partners.

Kiddushin 30b sets verses side by side. Exodus 20:12 says to honor parents. Proverbs 3:9 says to honor the LORD with your wealth. A baraita notes the same verb in both and says Scripture compares the honor of parents to the honor of God. Rabbinic tradition takes this as a sign of how weighty the duty is.

The same page teaches that there are three partners in making a person: the Holy One, the father and the mother. When people honor their parents, the baraita says, God counts it as if God dwelled among them. This is , the Talmud’s teaching through stories and sayings, placed right next to the legal rules.

The Gemara also notices order. Exodus names the father first. Leviticus names the mother first. The rabbis read this as a signal that both parents are equal in honor and reverence. A detail of word order becomes an argument about fairness inside the family.

Think it through
Word Order as Evidence

33.3

Whose Money Pays?

Main ideaThe Gemara asks who pays for a parent's food and clothing and records two views before it rules.

The baraita says a child must feed and clothe a parent. The Gemara in Kiddushin 32a asks the practical question: from whose money? Rav Yehuda says from the child’s money. Rav Natan bar Oshaya says from the parent’s money. This is the classic shape of a , a unit of Talmudic discussion built from question, proposal, proof and objection.

Each side reaches for proof. One side points to the verse comparing honor of parents to honor of God with one’s wealth, which might suggest the child’s own money. The other side answers from a teaching about a person’s duties. After back-and-forth, the Gemara reports that the halakhah follows the view that it comes from the parent’s money.

What does that ruling protect? Later codes, such as Maimonides and the Shulchan Arukh (c. 1565), add that if the parent has no money and the child does, the child must provide. The ruling guards both sides. It keeps a poor child from ruin and still keeps a needy parent from being left alone.

Section 2

Stories at the Edge of Duty

33.4

Dama ben Netina

Main ideaThe Gemara makes a non-Jewish jeweler from Ashkelon its model of how far honoring a parent can reach.

Kiddushin 31a opens the story with a question put to Rabbi Eliezer: how far does honoring parents reach? His answer is a story. Dama ben Netina of Ashkelon would not wake his sleeping father to get a key, though a huge sale was waiting. The Gemara adds that the next year a red heifer was born in his herd, and he asked only for what he had lost.

The Gemara tells a second story about Dama. His mother, in its telling, tore his silk garment and hit him in front of the city’s leaders. He did not shame her in return. Rabbinic readers set the two stories together. One is about losing money. The other is about swallowing pride. Both are about restraint.

Readers have noticed the choice of hero. Rabbi Eliezer does not point to a sage or to himself. He points to someone outside the covenant community. Many modern teachers read this as the Talmud saying that goodness is visible everywhere. The Gemara itself draws a lesson for Jews: if one not commanded acts this way, how much more one who is commanded.

33.5

Rabbi Tarfon's Hands

Main ideaThe rabbis praise extreme devotion to parents and still say it is only a part of what the duty asks.

Kiddushin 31b tells of Rabbi Tarfon, a , or teacher of the Mishnah’s era, in the late first and early second century CE. Whenever his mother wanted to climb into bed, he bent down so she could step on him. When he praised this act in the study house, the sages told him he had not reached even half of the honor due.

Why would the rabbis answer such devotion so sharply? The Gemara’s point seems to be that honor has no ceiling. It also records Rav Yosef, blind in old age, saying that when he heard his mother’s footsteps he would stand up for the Divine Presence that comes near.

These stories sit beside the legal list. They do not replace it. The baraita tells what a court could expect; the stories show an ideal. Students of the page learn to ask of every story in the Gemara: is this a rule, a model or a warning? Keeping them apart helps a reader see what the page asks.

33.6

Where Honor Stops

Main ideaThe Talmud limits the duty: a parent cannot command a child to break the Torah.

Leviticus 19:3 joins two commands: revere your parents, and keep my Sabbaths. The Gemara in Bava Metzia 32a and Yevamot 5b reads the pairing as a limit. If a parent tells a child to break the Sabbath, the child does not obey, because, the Gemara reasons, both child and parent are bound to honor God.

The Gemara also shows the duty meeting real hardship. Kiddushin 31b tells of Rav Assi, whose aged mother made demands he could not meet. He left Babylonia for the Land of Israel. Maimonides later ruled that if a parent’s mind is failing and a child cannot bear it, the child may arrange for others to give care.

Modern readers across Judaism use these passages in different ways. Some Orthodox teachers stress the high bar of honor. Some Conservative and Reform teachers stress the limits, especially where a parent is harmful. The Talmud gives material to both, which is part of why the page is studied so closely.

Section 3

These and Those

33.7

Three Years of Argument

Main ideaEruvin 13b says a heavenly voice declared both schools' words living, yet the law follows Beit Hillel.

Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel were two schools of the first century CE, named for the sages Shammai and Hillel. Eruvin 13b says they argued for three years. Each said the law followed its own view. Then, the Gemara reports, a , a heavenly voice, came out and said: these and those are the words of the living God, and the law follows Beit Hillel.

The sentence is famous because it holds two things at once. Both positions are called words of God. Only one becomes practice. Readers have asked how both can be true. The medieval commentator Ritva, in Spain around 1300, reports a teaching that Moses was shown many ways to rule on each question, and the sages of each generation would decide.

33.8

Why Hillel Wins

Main ideaThe Gemara says Beit Hillel's law prevailed because they were kind, humble and taught the other side first.

The Gemara does not leave the ruling unexplained. It asks: if both are words of the living God, why did Beit Hillel merit that the law follow them? It answers that they were gentle and patient. They studied their own words and the words of Beit Shammai. They even quoted Beit Shammai’s view before their own.

Eruvin 13b gives a case. Mishnah Sukkah 2:7 records that Beit Shammai held a person whose head and most of the body were in the sukkah, but the table in the house, had not fulfilled the duty. Beit Hillel held that the person had. In the Mishnah, Beit Hillel’s teaching opens by stating Beit Shammai’s view first.

The Gemara ends with a moral. Whoever humbles themselves, the Holy One raises up. Whoever chases greatness, greatness runs from them. Here ARGUMENT and MERCY meet. The winner is not the stronger debater but the side that took the other side seriously.

33.9

Keeping the Losing View

Main ideaThe Mishnah records minority opinions on purpose, so a later court may rely on them.

If the law follows Beit Hillel, why record Beit Shammai at all? Mishnah Eduyot 1:5 asks this about minority views in general. It answers that they are kept so that a later court might rely on them if it sees reason. The losing view stays on the page as a possible future ruling.

Pirkei Avot 5:17 (numbered differently in some editions) adds a test. An argument for the sake of heaven will last. Its model is Hillel and Shammai. An argument not for the sake of heaven will not last. Its model is Korah and his company, who rebelled against Moses in Numbers 16.

Readers compare Eruvin 13b with Bava Metzia 59b, the oven of Akhnai. There, a heavenly voice sides with Rabbi Eliezer, and Rabbi Yehoshua answers, ’It is not in heaven’ (Deuteronomy 30:12). The majority rules. Some read the two passages as in tension; others say both place the decision in human hands.

Section 4

Mapping a Whole Sugya

33.10

The Moves of the Page

Main ideaEvery sugya can be mapped as a chain of moves: source, question, proof, objection, answer and ruling.

Take the sugya on whose money pays for a parent’s food. It starts with a source, the baraita. It asks a question: from whose funds? It gives two positions, Rav Yehuda and Rav Natan bar Oshaya. It tests each with a proof, raises an objection and answers it. It closes with a ruling. This chain is the Talmud’s method.

The uses short Aramaic signals for each move. ’Ta shema’, come and hear, brings a proof. ’Metivei’, they objected, launches a challenge. ’Teyuvta’, a refutation, means a view has fallen. ’Hilkheta’, the law is, gives a ruling. Learning these words is like learning road signs for the page.

Mapping helps students keep track of who is speaking. The Gemara’s anonymous voice, the , asks and answers much of the time. Historians such as David Weiss Halivni (1927-2022) argued that this anonymous layer was added late, after the named amoraim. Traditional readers often treat the page as one conversation.

33.11

Argument and Mercy as One Method

Main ideaIn both Kiddushin and Eruvin the argument's form serves the people the ruling protects.

Look at what each passage protects. The ’whose money’ ruling protects a poor child from ruin and a needy parent from neglect. The limit in Bava Metzia 32a protects a child from being ordered to do wrong. Eruvin 13b protects the losing side’s dignity and its view’s place on the page.

The method and the mercy are not separate. The Gemara tests every claim, so a harsh reading does not pass unchallenged. It keeps minority opinions, so a later court can show more mercy. Its heroes, Dama and Beit Hillel, win by restraint, not by force.

Readers disagree about how far to push this. Some academic readers stress that the rabbis were a small elite whose rulings reflected their own time. Many religious readers stress the text’s lasting moral voice. A careful student can hold both views in the room and let the page speak.

33.12

Two Sugyot Side by Side

Main ideaSetting Kiddushin 31 beside Eruvin 13b shows one Talmudic habit: rules with stories, rulings with dissent.

Kiddushin 30b-32a moves from verses to a baraita list, to stories of Dama, Tarfon and Assi, to a legal question about money, to limits. Eruvin 13b moves from a long dispute to a heavenly voice, to a question about why, to a Mishnah example, to a moral about humility. Both mix halakhah and aggadah.

Both also leave something open. Kiddushin’s stories set an ideal no one fully meets. Eruvin keeps Beit Shammai’s ’words of the living God’ on record after the ruling. The Babylonian Talmud, completed c. 500-600 CE, rarely closes every door. Later commentators kept arguing over both pages.

When you present a sugya in the capstone, you will do what these pages do. You will state the source, trace the moves, name who is speaking and ask what is protected. You will also name what is left open and who might read it differently. That habit is the heart of this unit.

Capstone: Chavruta — Present a Sugya, Its Argument and Its Mercy

Capstone
Big questionWhat does it take to read a sugya with a partner, argue its sides fairly and say clearly what it protects?
The story

Two Sides of One Page

Two students take opposite sides of a Talmud passage and end up agreeing on what it is about.

Maya and Luis stand at the front of the room with one printed page between them. It is Bava Metzia 21a-b, on lost objects, in paraphrase. Maya argues that a finder may keep scattered coins. Luis argues that the owner still has a claim. The class has a rubric and a timer.

They do not shout. Maya reads the Mishnah's line and explains the idea of an owner who has given up hope, called *yiush* in the Gemara. Luis answers with an objection the Gemara itself raises: what if the owner does not yet know the coins are gone? They trade the page back and forth, the way the rabbis traded proofs.

Near the end, Maya says something the class did not expect. She says both of them are really arguing about the same thing: how to protect a person who loses something without punishing the person who finds it. Luis nods. Their thesis is that the sugya weighs the owner's claim against the finder's honesty.

This is the capstone. You will choose a sugya, read it with a partner in *chavruta*, map its moves, bring in commentators and a modern reader, and present both sides. The goal is not to win. It is to show the argument and the mercy on the page.

Section 1

Choosing and Reading

34.1

Choosing Your Sugya

Main ideaA good capstone sugya is short, has a clear dispute and asks what a ruling protects.

This course has read many sugyot: lost objects in Bava Metzia, workers’ wages, saving a life, honoring parents in Kiddushin, and ’these and those’ in Eruvin 13b. Any of them can be a capstone. You may also choose another passage, as long as you can cite tractate and page and find a translation.

Choose for three things. First, length: one sugya, often half a page to a full daf. Second, a clear dispute, with named sides such as Abaye and Rava or Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel. Third, stakes: a person the ruling protects, such as the worker, the finder, the owner or a person in danger.

Translations differ in status. Michael Rodkinson’s Babylonian Talmud (1896-1903) is public domain but abridged and uneven, so check it against a modern edition. The Soncino (1935-52), Steinsaltz and Sefaria’s William Davidson edition are not public domain; use them to read, and paraphrase them in your own words when you present.

34.2

Reading in Chavruta

Main ideaChavruta is paired study in which partners read aloud, question each other and argue toward understanding.

comes from the Aramaic word for friend or companion. It means study in pairs. In many yeshivas and batei midrash, study halls, the room is loud with pairs reading aloud and arguing. The Talmud itself praises this. In Taanit 23a, Rava is quoted: either chavruta or death.

Taanit 7a applies Proverbs 27:17, iron sharpens iron, to students. Just as one iron blade sharpens another, the Gemara says, two scholars sharpen each other in law. Berakhot 63b warns that those who study alone grow foolish. The ideal is not a lecture but a partner.

In practice, partners take turns. One reads a line aloud and explains it. The other asks: who is speaking, what is the question, what is the proof? Then they switch. When they disagree, they return to the text. The rule of the room is to argue with ideas, never to attack the person.

34.3

Map the Moves

Main ideaBefore arguing, partners map the sugya: Mishnah, question, proof, objection, answer and ruling.

Start with the Mishnah or baraita the sugya discusses. Write it in one sentence. Then list each move the Gemara makes. Label it: question, proof, objection, answer, story or ruling. Mark each speaker by name or as the anonymous voice. Keep the map on one sheet so both partners can see it.

Use the signal words you have learned. ’Ta shema’ brings a proof. ’Metivei’ raises an objection. ’Teyku’, let it stand, marks a question left unresolved. ’Hilkheta’ gives the ruling. When no signal appears, ask what job the line is doing. Circle each signal word you find on the page.

A map for Bava Metzia 21a might have eight to twelve moves. Do not try to capture every word. Capture the path of the argument. Your map is the backbone of your presentation, and the rubric gives it real weight. A clear map also helps your listeners follow along.

Think it through
Label Each Line

Line 1: The Mishnah says scattered coins belong to the finder. Line 2: Why? Because the owner has given up hope. Line 3: But he may not know he lost them yet! Line 4: A person often touches his purse, so he knows. Line 5: The law follows this.

Label each line as source, question, answer, objection or ruling. Which line is the key move?

Section 2

Commentators and Modern Readers

34.4

Rashi at Your Elbow

Main ideaRashi's commentary explains the plain sense of each line and sits beside the Gemara on the printed page.

Rashi, Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, lived in Troyes, France, from 1040 to 1105. His commentary on the Talmud explains words, fills in missing steps and tells the reader who is speaking. In the standard printed page, it runs in a column on the inner side, next to the binding.

Students often say they read the Gemara ’with Rashi.’ When a line is unclear, Rashi supplies a short gloss. He might say what a coin was, what a verse proves or why an objection works. Many learners treat him as a patient tutor at their elbow. Each gloss is short and to the point.

For your capstone, find one place where Rashi explains a key move. Paraphrase what he says and name the line. Rashi’s commentary is not a public-domain English text you can quote freely, so present it in your own words with the tractate, page and ’Rashi, s.v.’ plus the word he comments on.

34.5

Tosafot Push Back

Main ideaTosafot compare passages across the Talmud and often challenge Rashi, modeling respectful disagreement.

, meaning ’additions’, are comments by medieval scholars in France and Germany, many of them Rashi’s own descendants and students. They were active from the twelfth into the fourteenth century. Their notes run on the outer side of the printed page.

Where Rashi explains one line, Tosafot often ask how this sugya fits with another page. They notice contradictions and try to resolve them. Many times they begin by quoting Rashi, then write, in effect, ’this is hard,’ and give another reading. Rashi’s grandson Rabbeinu Tam was one of the best known.

In your capstone, one Tosafot comment, paraphrased, can show a second reading of your sugya. It shows that disagreement continued long after the Gemara was complete. Like Beit Hillel, Tosafot usually state the view they question before offering their own.

34.6

One Modern Reader

Main ideaA modern reader, religious or academic, shows how the sugya is understood and used today.

Your capstone also brings in one modern reader. Rabbi Adin Steinsaltz (1937-2020) translated and explained the whole Talmud in Hebrew and later English, aiming to open the page to beginners. His notes often explain history, objects and plants. Other editions and translations exist as well.

Academic readers ask different questions. David Weiss Halivni studied how the anonymous layer was built. Daniel Boyarin and others read aggadah as literature and culture. Women scholars and teachers, including those at women’s batei midrash, have raised questions earlier readers left aside.

Pick one reader and say what their approach does. Does it explain the plain sense, trace history, or draw an ethical lesson? Attribute every claim: ’Steinsaltz explains…’, ’Halivni argues…’. Do not present any reader’s view as the only one. Let each reader speak in their own terms.

Section 3

Thesis and Presentation

34.7

A Thesis About Argument and Mercy

Main ideaA strong thesis names what the argument decides and what the ruling protects, with a citation.

A thesis is one or two sentences your whole presentation defends. For this capstone it has two parts. First, what the argument decides. Second, what the ruling protects. For example: ’Bava Metzia 21b decides that a finder may keep coins once the owner has given up hope, and so protects both the owner’s claim and the finder’s honesty.’

Cite exactly: tractate, folio and side, like Bava Metzia 21b or Mishnah Bava Metzia 2:1. Name the translation you read, such as Rodkinson (1896-1903) or the Soncino (1935-52). If you paraphrase, say so. Paraphrase any translation that is not public domain, and mark it clearly.

Keep the minority view in your thesis or right after it. The Talmud does. A thesis that ignores the losing side misses half the page. Your partner will test this when they take the other side. A fair thesis makes room for the other side from the start.

34.8

Presenting With Your Partner

Main ideaIn the presentation, partners take opposite sides, then state together what the sugya is about.

Each pair gets about ten minutes. One partner presents the ruling side, the other the minority or objecting side. Each reads the key line, explains its moves and cites the page. Then you switch roles for one exchange, so each partner argues both ways.

End together. State your shared thesis: what the argument decides and what it protects. Name one commentator, Rashi or Tosafot in paraphrase, and one modern reader. Close with an open question the page leaves for later readers. Keep this closing short and clear.

The rubric below shows how points are shared. Notice that the map of moves and fair treatment of both sides carry the most weight. Winning is not on the rubric. Clear, respectful argument is. Fairness to both sides is what earns the most points here.

34.9

Studying a Sacred Text in a Public School

Main ideaStudying the Talmud in a public school means describing what readers believe, not telling anyone what to believe.

In Abington School District v. Schempp (1963), the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against required Bible reading as a devotional act in public schools. The same decision said that studying religion objectively, as part of a secular education, is allowed. This course follows that line.

That is why every belief in this course is attributed. ’Rabbinic tradition holds…’, ’Rashi explains…’, ’historians date…’. The course does not say whether the Oral Torah came from Sinai or whether any ruling binds the reader. Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and academic readers each read the page their own way.

Close your capstone with a short reflection. What did you learn about argument, about mercy and about listening to a partner? Students of many backgrounds, and of none, have found that a page about lost coins or sleeping fathers can teach how to disagree well.

From the source
The Court on Studying Religion
The Court said that study of the Bible or of religion, when presented objectively as part of a secular program of education, is consistent with the First Amendment.
Abington School District v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), opinion of Justice Tom C. Clark, paraphrased (paraphrased)

34.10

Looking Back, Looking Ahead

Main ideaThe capstone gathers the course's threads: the page's moves, its mercy and a partner who disagrees well.

Across this course you met the Mishnah (c. 200 CE), the Gemara of Babylonia (completed c. 500-600 CE), Rashi and Tosafot, codes like Maimonides and the Shulchan Arukh, and modern readers. You learned to cite a daf and to name a translation. You also practiced reading in pairs.

The Daf Yomi program, begun in 1923, has readers study one folio a day. A full cycle of about 2,711 folios takes about seven and a half years. In January 2020, tens of thousands filled MetLife Stadium in New Jersey to mark a completion, a Siyum HaShas. Readers in Chicago and around the world took part.

You do not need to finish the Talmud to use its method. Read the source. Map the moves. Hear the other side. Ask who is protected. That is the capstone, and it is also a skill you can carry into any argument. The page is waiting for the next pair of readers.
