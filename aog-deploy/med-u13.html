Traditional Medicine and the Evidence

Integrative Medicine

Millions of people around the world use healing traditions that are hundreds or thousands of years old. A grandmother in Chicago brews an herbal tea. A doctor in Beijing offers acupuncture after surgery. A family in Tokyo picks up a packet of Kampo herbs from the pharmacy. These traditions carry culture, family memory and real knowledge.

This unit looks at Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ayurveda, Unani, Kampo and healing traditions of the Americas. First you will learn each tradition's ideas in its own terms. Then you will ask the questions scientists ask: What was tested? Compared with what? What did the results show? Some practices, such as tai chi for balance and yoga for back pain, have held up in careful tests. One old recipe led to a Nobel Prize.

By the end, you will be able to explain a tradition respectfully, read a study's result carefully, and name the risks of herbs and supplements. You will also know the single most useful habit for safe care: telling your doctor, nurse or pharmacist about everything you take.

The big question

How can we honor traditional medicine and still let evidence decide what helps?

Big questionHow can we respect an old healing tradition and still ask what the evidence shows?
The story

A Recipe from the Fourth Century

A scientist in Beijing reads an old fever remedy and changes one step in her lab.

In 1969, a scientist named Tu Youyou joined a secret research program in China called Project 523. Its job was to find a new medicine for malaria. Malaria is a disease spread by mosquitoes. The parasite that causes it had learned to survive the drug chloroquine, and soldiers and villagers were getting sick.

Tu and her team searched old medical books and asked practitioners of traditional medicine for remedies. They gathered about 2,000 old recipes and tested extracts from many plants. One plant, qinghao, or sweet wormwood, showed promise in early tests on animals. But the results kept changing. Sometimes the extract worked. Often it did not.

Tu went back to a book by Ge Hong, a Chinese writer of the fourth century. His handbook of emergency remedies said to soak a handful of qinghao in water, wring out the juice and drink it. The recipe did not say to boil the plant. Tu wondered if heat was destroying the active substance.

She changed the method. Her team used a solvent that works at a low temperature. In 1971 the new extract cleared the malaria parasite in mice, and soon after in monkeys. The active chemical was named artemisinin. In 2015, Tu Youyou shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this discovery.

The Framework of TCM

25.1

Qi and the Meridians

Main ideaTraditional Chinese Medicine explains health as the smooth flow of qi through channels called meridians.

Picture a clinic in Shanghai. A practitioner takes a patient’s pulse at both wrists for a long time. She looks at his tongue. She asks how he sleeps, what he eats and whether he feels hot or cold. These steps come from , or TCM, a system of healing developed in China over more than 2,000 years.

TCM teaches that the body is filled with (said "chee"), often translated as vital energy. In this tradition, qi moves along pathways called . Health means qi flows smoothly and stays in balance. Illness means qi is blocked, too weak or out of balance. Treatments such as acupuncture, herbs and exercise aim to restore that flow.

Qi and meridians are ideas inside the TCM framework. Scientists have not found meridians as physical structures, and qi has not been measured as a form of energy. That does not mean a treatment is useless. It means we test each treatment by its results in people, not by whether its explanation matches modern biology.

25.2

Yin, Yang and Five Phases

Main ideaTCM uses yin-yang and the five phases to describe balance and change in the body.

Think of a day. Morning light grows, noon is bright and hot, and evening cools into a dark night. Chinese thinkers used this kind of pattern to describe . Yin stands for cool, dark, still and inward qualities. Yang stands for warm, bright, active and outward qualities. Each needs the other, and each slowly turns into the other.

TCM applies this idea to the body. A person with a high fever, a red face and a fast pulse may be described as having too much yang. A person who feels cold, tired and pale may be described as having too much yin. The practitioner then chooses herbs or foods that are said to cool or warm, bringing the two back into balance.

TCM also uses the : wood, fire, earth, metal and water. Each phase is linked to organs, seasons, colors and emotions. In the tradition, the phases support and control one another in cycles. These are ways of organizing observations, like a map. Modern medicine uses a different map, based on cells, chemistry and experiments.

TCM in China's Hospitals

Main ideaIn China, TCM and Western medicine are often practiced side by side, sometimes in the same hospital.

Walk into a large hospital in Beijing and you may find two pharmacies. One hands out pills made in factories. The other has rows of drawers filled with dried roots, bark and flowers. In China, many hospitals offer both Western medicine and TCM, and many doctors are trained in both.

After 1949, the government of the People’s Republic of China supported TCM and built colleges to train its practitioners. It also encouraged , meaning TCM and Western medicine working together. A patient might have surgery for a broken hip and also receive acupuncture or herbal tea during recovery.

TCM is also used far beyond China. Acupuncture clinics exist in cities across the United States, including Chicago. In the U.S., each state decides who may practice acupuncture and what training is required. Researchers in China and around the world now run clinical trials to test which TCM treatments help, and for what.

25.4

Acupuncture and Chronic Pain

Main ideaStudies suggest acupuncture may help some people with some kinds of chronic pain and nausea.

Maria, a nurse, has had low back pain for more than a year. Her doctor suggests physical therapy, and she also tries . In acupuncture, a trained practitioner places very thin needles into the skin at chosen points. Many patients say it feels like a small tap or a dull ache, not like a shot.

Researchers have studied acupuncture in thousands of patients with , which is pain that lasts three months or longer. Large reviews of these trials suggest that acupuncture may help some people with back pain, neck pain, knee arthritis and headaches. The benefit is usually modest, not dramatic. Some people improve a lot, and some not at all.

Acupuncture has also been tested for nausea. Studies of pressing or needling a point on the inner wrist suggest it may reduce nausea after surgery for some patients. Acupuncture is usually safe when done by a licensed practitioner with clean, single-use needles. Anyone thinking about it should talk with a doctor first.

25.5

What Sham Trials Show

Main ideaIn many trials, fake acupuncture works almost as well as real acupuncture, which raises questions about how it helps.

How do you test a needle treatment fairly? In a trial, one group gets real acupuncture. Another group gets a fake version. The fake might use needles placed at the wrong points, or blunt needles that touch the skin but do not go in. Patients do not know which group they are in.

Many sham-controlled trials find that real and sham acupuncture give similar results. Both groups often feel better than people who get no acupuncture at all. When real acupuncture does beat sham, the difference is usually small. This tells researchers that much of the benefit may come from the whole experience, not the exact needle points.

Part of that experience is the : real improvement that comes from expecting to get better and from caring attention. Placebo effects are real for symptoms such as pain. Scientists still debate what these results mean. Some say acupuncture works mainly through placebo. Others say needling any spot may have real effects on nerves.

Tai Chi for Balance

Main ideaTai chi, a slow Chinese exercise, has good evidence for improving balance and preventing falls in older adults.

Early in the morning in a park in Chicago’s Chinatown, a group of older adults move together in slow, flowing steps. They shift weight from one foot to the other and turn their arms in wide circles. This is , a mind-body exercise that began in China as a martial art.

In TCM, tai chi is said to help qi flow. Scientists have studied it in a different way, by counting falls. Falls are a leading cause of injury in older adults. Many trials show that regular tai chi improves and leg strength. Reviews of these trials find that exercise programs that include tai chi lower the number of falls in older people.

Because of this evidence, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists tai chi among programs that can help prevent falls. Tai chi is gentle, and people can adjust it for their own bodies. It shows how a traditional practice can earn a place in modern care by passing careful tests.

From Old Text to New Drug

25.7

Tu Youyou and Project 523

Main ideaTu Youyou used an ancient text as a clue and modern chemistry as a test to discover artemisinin.

In the 1960s, malaria was killing soldiers and civilians in Southeast Asia. The malaria parasite had become resistant to chloroquine, the main drug of the time. means the germ is no longer killed by a medicine that used to work. China started a secret program, Project 523, to find new drugs.

Tu Youyou’s team gathered more than 2,000 old recipes and tested extracts from about 200 herbs. Sweet wormwood looked promising but gave uneven results. Tu reread a fourth-century handbook by Ge Hong, which described soaking the herb in water, with no boiling. She guessed that heat was breaking down the active chemical, so she used a low-temperature with a solvent called ether.

The new extract worked in animals in 1971. The team then tested it in people, including themselves, to check safety. Chemists found the structure of the active molecule, . The old book gave the clue. Careful lab work, animal tests and clinical trials turned the clue into a proven medicine.

25.8

Artemisinin in the World

Main ideaArtemisinin-based combination therapies are now a main treatment for malaria and have saved many lives.

Today a child with malaria in Nigeria or Uganda is often treated with an , or ACT. An ACT pairs a drug made from artemisinin with a second malaria drug. The World Health Organization recommends ACTs as a first-line treatment for the most common dangerous type of malaria.

Why combine two drugs? Artemisinin clears parasites very fast, but it leaves the body quickly. The partner drug lasts longer and kills parasites that remain. Using two drugs together also makes it harder for the parasite to develop resistance, because it would need to resist both at once.

In 2015, Tu Youyou shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with William Campbell and Satoshi Omura, who discovered a drug against parasitic worms. Tu was the first Chinese woman to win a Nobel Prize in science. Her story shows that a can hold a real clue, but the clue must be tested.

Using TCM Safely and Fairly

25.9

Herbs, Drugs and Contamination

Main ideaHerbal products can interact with medicines or be contaminated, so tell your doctor about any herbs you take.

Mr. Chen takes a blood thinner after a heart problem. His cousin mails him a box of herbal tea said to help circulation. Some herbs can change how a medicine works in the body. This is called a . A few herbs may add to a blood thinner’s effect and raise the risk of bleeding.

Another risk is . In some tests of herbal products, including some sold as TCM, researchers have found lead, mercury or arsenic. Others have found hidden prescription drugs or the wrong plant. In the 1990s, patients in Belgium had serious kidney damage after taking a slimming product that contained a plant with aristolochic acid.

"Natural" does not always mean safe. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration does not approve herbal for safety and effectiveness before they are sold, as it does for medicines. That is why the best step is simple: tell your doctor or pharmacist about every herb, tea and supplement you use.

25.10

Endangered Animals and Respect

Main ideaSome traditional products use endangered animals, and respectful talk about TCM includes this problem and the evidence.

A ranger in Africa guards a rhinoceros through the night. Poachers kill rhinos for their horns, which are sold in some markets as traditional medicine. Tiger bone and pangolin scales have also been sold this way. These animals are now , which means they are at risk of dying out.

An international treaty called controls trade in endangered wildlife, and most countries have signed it. Rhino horn is made of keratin, the same protein as fingernails. There is no good scientific evidence that it treats disease. Many TCM practitioners and organizations support using plant or farmed substitutes instead of wild animals.

Talking about TCM fairly means holding two ideas at once. The tradition has deep roots and has given the world real gifts, such as artemisinin and tai chi. Some of its products also have risks or lack evidence. discussion respects the people who use TCM by asking the same careful questions of every treatment, Eastern or Western.

26.1

The Three Doshas

Main ideaAyurveda teaches that health depends on the balance of three doshas: vata, pitta and kapha.

In a clinic in Kerala, in southern India, a practitioner asks a new patient about her sleep, her digestion and her moods. She notes that the patient is thin, moves quickly and often feels cold. These questions come from , a healing tradition from India whose classic texts are about 2,000 years old. Its name means "knowledge of life."

Ayurveda holds that each body is shaped by three , or forces. Vata is linked to air and movement. Pitta is linked to fire and digestion. Kapha is linked to earth, water and structure. In the tradition, each person is born with their own mix, called their , or constitution.

Ayurveda teaches that illness comes when the doshas fall out of balance. The practitioner in Kerala might say her patient has a lot of vata and suggest warm food and a regular routine. Like qi in Chinese medicine, the doshas are ideas within the tradition. They have not been measured as physical substances.

26.2

Diet and Daily Routine

Main ideaAyurveda gives great weight to food, sleep and a steady daily routine.

Before sunrise, an Ayurvedic daily plan might begin with waking early, rinsing the mouth, drinking warm water and stretching. This routine is called , or daily practice. Ayurveda teaches that doing the same healthy things at the same times each day helps keep the doshas in balance.

Food is central. Ayurveda sorts foods by six tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, pungent and astringent. A practitioner may suggest more warm, cooked food for a person with strong vata, or cooling foods for strong pitta. Meals are meant to be eaten calmly, at regular times, with the largest meal near midday.

Some of this advice overlaps with modern , which studies how sleep, diet, activity and stress affect health. Regular sleep, plenty of vegetables and calm meals are supported by many studies. The dosha reasons behind the advice are the tradition’s own. The habits themselves can be tested and often hold up well.

India's Ministry of AYUSH

Main ideaIndia's government recognizes Ayurveda and several other traditions through its Ministry of AYUSH.

26.4

Yoga and Low Back Pain

Main ideaYoga can help some adults with chronic low back pain a little, about as much as other exercise.

James drives a delivery truck and has had low back pain for months. His doctor suggests a weekly class designed for back pain. Yoga began in India as a spiritual practice. It joins body postures, breathing and focused attention. Many people today practice it mainly for health.

Researchers have tested yoga in many trials of adults with chronic low back pain. Reviews of these trials find that yoga can lower pain and improve daily function a small to moderate amount, about as much as other exercise programs. In 2017, the American College of Physicians advised doctors to try non-drug options such as exercise, yoga and tai chi first for chronic low back pain.

Yoga is not a cure-all. Some poses can strain the body, so beginners do best with a trained teacher who can adjust poses. People with health conditions should ask their doctor first. Yoga shows how a practice from one tradition can pass modern tests for a specific , which means a specific use.

26.5

Meditation and Stress

Main ideaMeditation programs have evidence for easing stress and anxiety for some people.

Before a big exam, Aisha sits quietly for ten minutes. She breathes slowly and notices her thoughts without chasing them. This is a form of , a practice of training attention. Meditation has roots in several Asian traditions, including those of India, and is part of Ayurvedic and yogic teaching.

Scientists have tested meditation programs, especially , which means paying calm attention to the present moment. A large review published in 2014 in JAMA Internal Medicine found moderate evidence that mindfulness programs can ease anxiety, depression and pain for some adults. The effects were similar in size to some other helpful treatments.

Meditation is low-risk for most people and costs little. It is a tool, not a replacement for care. Someone who feels very anxious or sad for a long time should talk with a trusted adult, a school counselor or a doctor. Meditation can work alongside that support, and many people find it helps them feel steady.

26.6

Turmeric in the Lab and in People

Main ideaCurcumin from turmeric looks promising in lab studies, but proof of benefit in people is limited.

Turmeric is the golden spice that colors many curries. Ayurveda has used it for centuries for digestion, skin and aches. Its best-known chemical is . In lab dishes and animal studies, curcumin has shown effects on inflammation, which is the body’s response to injury or infection.

Results in a lab dish do not always carry over to people. Curcumin is poorly absorbed by the gut and breaks down quickly in the body. That means very little may reach the tissues. Many human trials of curcumin have been small or short, and their results are mixed. A few suggest it may ease knee arthritis pain a little, but those studies are not strong. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says there is little reliable evidence for most uses.

Some chemists have warned that curcumin can look active in many lab tests for reasons that do not mean it works as a drug. This is a lesson about research: early lab and animal studies are a starting point, not an answer. Turmeric in food is generally safe, but supplements can interact with some medicines, so tell your doctor.

26.7

Heavy Metals in Some Products

Main ideaSome imported Ayurvedic products have been found to contain lead, mercury or arsenic.

In some Ayurvedic practice, metals and minerals are processed into preparations called medicines. The tradition teaches that careful processing makes them safe. Testing tells a more worrying story. Some of these products, and some herbal ones too, have been found to contain , such as lead, mercury and arsenic.

In 2008, researchers led by Robert Saper tested Ayurvedic products bought online. Published in JAMA, the study found that about one in five contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic. Products made in India and products made in the U.S. both had problems. Doctors have reported cases of lead poisoning in people who took such products.

Lead can harm the brain, kidneys and blood, and children are most sensitive to it. This does not mean every Ayurvedic product is unsafe. It means matters. Buy from trusted sources, show products to a pharmacist, and tell your doctor, who can order a simple blood test for lead if needed.

26.8

Unani Medicine

Main ideaUnani medicine grew from Greek ideas carried forward by Arabic and Persian physicians and is practiced today in South Asia.

In Delhi, a patient visits a , a practitioner of Unani medicine. The hakim asks about his temperament and diet and may suggest herbs, a change in food or rest. The word Unani comes from the Arabic word for Greek. The system began with Greek medicine, especially the ideas of Hippocrates and Galen.

Physicians in the Islamic world translated, tested and expanded these Greek ideas. The Persian physician Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, wrote the around 1025. It was used as a medical textbook in Europe and the Middle East for centuries. Unani medicine kept the Greek idea of four humors, the body fluids once thought to control health.

Western medicine gave up the four humors in the 1800s, after experiments showed how germs and cells cause disease. Unani medicine still uses humors as its framework. Today it is practiced mainly in India, Pakistan and nearby countries, and it is part of India’s AYUSH ministry. As with any tradition, each remedy should be judged by fair tests.

26.9

Kampo in Japan

Main ideaKampo is Japan's adaptation of Chinese herbal medicine, and many Kampo formulas are used within Japan's national health system.

In a Tokyo clinic, a doctor trained in Western medicine writes a prescription for a packet of powdered herbs. This is , Japanese herbal medicine. Kampo came to Japan from China more than 1,000 years ago. Over the centuries, Japanese doctors adapted it, simplified many formulas and developed their own ways of diagnosing.

Kampo is unusual because it is part of mainstream care. Since the 1970s, Japan’s national health insurance has covered many Kampo formulas, which are made in factories to set standards. In Japan, Kampo is usually prescribed by medical doctors, not by a separate group of practitioners. Many doctors use Kampo alongside modern drugs.

Researchers in Japan study Kampo formulas in clinical trials. Some show benefits for specific uses, while others have little evidence. Like any medicine, Kampo can cause . For example, some formulas contain licorice, which in large amounts can raise blood pressure and lower potassium. That is why doctors track Kampo like any other prescription.

Indigenous and Curandero Traditions

Main ideaIndigenous healers and curanderos in the Americas treat body, mind, family and spirit together.

In the Pilsen neighborhood of Chicago, a small shop called a sells dried herbs, candles and teas. Across North and South America, Indigenous nations have their own healing traditions. These differ from nation to nation, and many are private to each community. They often treat a person’s health as linked to family, community, land and spirit. Some U.S. health programs that serve Native American communities now include traditional healers in patient care when patients ask for them.

Some herbs used in these traditions have been studied and some have not. A good doctor asks about them openly, so patients feel safe telling the whole story.

26.11

Integrative Clinics and Telling Your Doctor

Main ideaIntegrative medicine combines proven complementary practices with standard care, and it starts with telling your doctor everything you take.

At a large cancer center, a patient finishing chemotherapy meets with a team. One person teaches gentle yoga. Another offers acupuncture for nausea. A dietitian plans meals. Her cancer doctor leads the plan. This is , which combines standard care with complementary practices that have evidence for a specific use.

Integrative medicine is different from , which means using an unproven practice instead of standard care. Using an alternative remedy in place of proven cancer treatment can be dangerous. In the United States, a federal agency, the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, funds research on which practices help and which do not.

The simplest safety step is also the most important. Tell your doctor, nurse or pharmacist about every herb, tea, powder and supplement you use, including ones from family or from other countries. Bring the labels. A good clinician will listen with respect, check for interactions and help you keep what is safe.

Tell all your health care providers about any complementary health approaches you use. Give them a full picture of what you do to manage your health. This will help ensure coordinated and safe care.
