Recitation as Art, and Capstone

Capstone

This last unit asks two things. First, it looks at the Qur'an as sound and sight: the doctrine that no one can imitate it, the reciters whose voices fill stadiums and radio stations, the calligraphers who shaped its letters, and the mosques whose walls carry its words. Muslims have read the book with their ears and eyes as much as with their minds.

Then it hands the work to you. In the capstone you choose one verse or a short surah and one question. You read it in two translations, place it in its surah and its history, and gather what classical and modern commentators have said. Then you write and present your own careful argument about what the verse says and how it has been read.

By the end you will be able to explain i'jaz, recitation styles and the main calligraphic scripts. You will also be able to cite surah and ayah, translator and commentator, and describe readings you do not hold fairly. Those are the skills of anyone who studies a text people care about deeply.

The big question

How have people experienced the Qur'an through sound, art and careful reading, and how can a student describe those readings fairly?

Sound and Beauty: Recitation, Calligraphy and the Inimitable

Art
Big questionWhy have Muslims treated the sound and the written shape of the Qur'an as arts, and what does hearing or seeing the book add to reading it?
The story

One Vowel, One Breath

In a packed hall, a reciter stretches a single sound until the room forgets to breathe.

The hall in Cairo holds several thousand people, and every seat is full. On the stage sits one man in a plain robe, with no microphone stand in front of him, only a small book open on a low table. He closes his eyes. He begins with the words Muslims say before reciting, asking protection from Satan, and then the opening formula, "In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful." His voice is low at first, almost a speaking voice.

Then he reaches a long vowel in the middle of an ayah, and he holds it. He does not hurry. The note rises a little, bends, and settles. Tajwid, the rules of recitation, tell him exactly how many counts a vowel like this may last. Within that rule he has freedom, and he uses all of it. The sound fills the room. Somewhere near the back a listener whispers "Allah," the traditional way of praising a beautiful phrase, and the whisper spreads.

When the breath ends, the silence is complete. Nobody claps. Clapping is not done for the Qur'an. The reciter takes a breath, repeats the same ayah in a different melodic mode, and the crowd leans forward, listening for what he will do with it this time. A listener who speaks no Arabic could still follow the shape of the sound: the rise, the pause, the return.

Muslims call this listening a form of worship, and they also call it an art. Reciters are known by name and by voice, the way singers are. Their recordings play in taxis, kitchens and mosques from Jakarta to Chicago. This chapter asks what it means for a book to be heard before it is read, and seen before it is understood. It begins with a claim the Qur'an makes about itself: that no one can produce anything like it.

Section 1

The Inimitable Book

33.1

The Challenge Verses

Main ideaIn several passages the Qur'an dares its doubters to produce something like it, and Muslims read these verses as the root of the doctrine of i'jaz, the book's inimitability.

The Qur’an says a striking thing about itself. In Surah 2 (Al-Baqarah, The Cow), ayah 23, it addresses people who doubt that the book comes from God and tells them to produce one surah like it, calling on any helpers they wish. Surah 17 (Al-Isra, The Night Journey), ayah 88, widens the dare: even if all humans and (unseen beings the Qur’an says God created from fire) joined together, they could not produce the like of this Qur’an. Similar passages appear in 10:38, 11:13 and 52:33–34. Scholars call these the verses, in Arabic tahaddi.

Muslims read these verses as more than a boast. They became the seed of a doctrine called i&#x27;jaz, from an Arabic root meaning to make someone unable. The idea is that the Qur’an cannot be imitated, and that its inimitability is itself a sign that it comes from God. In the traditional account, the Prophet’s Meccan opponents were master poets, and the fact that none of them met the challenge is treated as evidence. Muslims hold this as a matter of faith; historians describe it as a claim the text makes and a tradition built around it.

Notice what the challenge asks for and what it does not. It does not say the book is the longest or the oldest. It asks for something "like it," which leaves open the question of what the likeness would be. Is it the sound? The meaning? The knowledge in it? The arrangement of words? For a thousand years Muslim scholars have argued about exactly this, and their answers are the subject of the next lesson. For now the key point is that the Qur’an itself invites a comparison, and that invitation shaped how Muslims listen to it and look at it.

33.2

How the Scholars Argued

Main ideaClassical scholars gave different answers to what makes the Qur'an inimitable: its arrangement of words, its sound, its knowledge, or God's turning people away from trying.

By the 900s the doctrine of i’jaz had a shelf of books devoted to it. Al-Baqillani (d. 1013), a theologian in Baghdad, wrote a work called I’jaz al-Qur’an. He compared the Qur’an with the most admired Arabic poems and argued that the book’s language stood apart from every human style. Around the same time, al-Rummani and al-Khattabi wrote short treatises listing the features of Qur’anic speech. Their method was literary: they read the Qur’an the way a critic reads a poem, and then argued that no poet could have written it.

The most influential answer came from ’Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani (d. c. 1078), a grammarian from Gorgan in Persia. In his book Dala’il al-I’jaz (The Proofs of Inimitability) he argued that the miracle lies in , the arrangement of the words. Single words, he said, are shared by everyone; what no one could match is the way the Qur’an’s words fit together so that each carries exactly the meaning the sentence needs. Later rhetoric in Arabic grew out of his ideas, and modern literary readers of the Qur’an still cite him.

Not every scholar agreed. Some early theologians of the Mu&#x27;tazila school, such as al-Nazzam (d. c. 845), proposed a different theory called , "turning away." On this view, humans could in principle write something like the Qur’an, but God turned their minds away from doing so. Most later scholars rejected sarfa, because it seemed to locate the miracle outside the text. Others pointed to the Qur’an’s predictions, or to its knowledge of past prophets that an unlettered man in Mecca could not have had. These are Muslim arguments; a public-school class describes them and compares them rather than deciding among them.

33.3

A Book Meant to Be Heard

Main ideaThe name Qur'an means recitation, and Muslim tradition says its first audience judged it by ear, which is why sound has always been part of its meaning.

The word Qur&#x27;an comes from the Arabic root q-r-’, to recite or read aloud. The first word Muslims believe was revealed, in Surah 96 (Al-’Alaq, The Clot), is from that same root: "Read," or "Recite." For its first twenty years the Qur’an existed mainly as sound. Muslims memorized it and recited it in prayer. Written notes on bone, leather and palm stalks were aids to memory, not a book. Tradition says the complete written copy, the , was gathered after the Prophet’s death, and that the sound came first.

Arabic of the 600s was a culture of the ear. Tribes competed through poets, and audiences memorized long odes. The Qur’an entered that world, and its early listeners judged it, tradition says, by how it sounded. Its sentences often end in rhyme or near-rhyme, a pattern called saj&#x27;, and its short Meccan surahs move in quick, pulsing lines. Later Muslim scholars pointed to this sound as part of i’jaz. Modern scholars of Arabic literature, Muslim and not, describe the same features without deciding what they prove.

This is why, in Muslim practice, a person who has never studied Arabic can still be moved by the Qur’an. A Bosnian grandmother, a Nigerian schoolboy and an Indonesian shopkeeper all hear the same Arabic sounds in prayer. Each reads the meaning in a different language. Muslims say that a translation is not the Qur’an but an explanation of it, because the sound of the Arabic belongs to the revelation. For a student in a public school this is a fact about how the book is used. It explains why the rest of this chapter is about sound and sight as much as about words.

Section 2

The Art of Recitation

33.4

Tajwid, the Rules of Sound

Main ideaTajwid is the set of rules for pronouncing the Qur'an, governing the length of vowels, nasal sounds and pauses, which reciters learn from a teacher by ear.

Surah 73 (Al-Muzzammil, The Enshrouded One), ayah 4, tells the Prophet to recite the Qur’an slowly and in measure. From that command, Muslims developed , a word meaning to make good or beautiful. Tajwid is not a melody. It is a set of rules for pronouncing each letter from its correct point in the mouth and throat, for how long a vowel is stretched, and for where a reciter may pause. A reciter who sings beautifully but breaks these rules is judged to have recited badly.

The rules have names. governs the lengthening of vowels: some are held for two counts, some for four or five, some for six. is a nasal hum on certain letters, held for about two counts. Qalqalah is a slight bounce given to five letters when they close a syllable. Idgham merges one letter into the next. A student learns these not from a chart but from a teacher, repeating an ayah until the teacher approves and gives an , a certificate that links the student in a chain of teachers reaching back, Muslims say, to the Prophet.

Because tajwid was passed on by ear, the written rules came later than the practice. Scholars in Baghdad and elsewhere wrote them down in the 900s and after; Ibn al-Jazari (d. 1429) composed a short poem of rules that students still memorize. Tajwid explains something the visitor at the beginning of this chapter noticed: the reciter held a vowel for exactly as long as the rule allowed and no longer. In Muslim understanding, beauty in recitation comes from obeying the rule precisely, not from escaping it.

33.5

The Readings: Hafs and Warsh

Main ideaThe Qur'an is recited in several accepted readings, the qira'at, that differ in small points of pronunciation and wording; two of them are heard most widely today.

Listen to a recording from Cairo and one from Morocco and you will hear small differences: a vowel that is long in one and short in the other, a word pronounced with a slight variation. These are the qira&#x27;at, the accepted readings. Muslim tradition holds that the Prophet recited the Qur’an in more than one way and that his companions carried these ways to the cities they settled. In Baghdad in the early 900s the scholar Ibn Mujahid (d. 936) selected seven readings as authoritative, each named after a master reciter of the 700s. Later scholars accepted three more, making ten.

Each reading is transmitted through two named students of its master, so a full name looks like "Hafs from ’Asim" or "Warsh from Nafi’." The reading of from ’Asim of Kufa became the basis of the Cairo edition printed in 1924. It is the one heard across most of the Muslim world today. The reading of from Nafi’ of Medina is common in North and West Africa. The differences are real but limited: pronunciation, some vowels, a few word endings. Muslim scholars say the readings all go back to the Prophet. Historians study them as evidence of how the text was transmitted by voice.

Why does this matter for an art chapter? Because a reciter chooses a reading the way a musician chooses an instrument. Some master reciters are famous for performing the same passage in several readings in one sitting, and listeners who know the readings hear the shift. It also matters for study. When two printed Qur’ans seem to disagree on a word, the answer is often that they follow different readings. A careful student names the reading, just as a careful student names the translation.

33.6

Murattal and Mujawwad

Main ideaReciters perform in two main styles, the measured murattal and the slow, ornamented mujawwad, and the great Egyptian reciters of the twentieth century made both famous through radio and recordings.

Two words describe how the Qur’an is performed. is measured recitation: steady, clear, at a reading pace, the style used in daily prayer and in teaching. is slow, melodic and ornamented, the style of the concert hall and the radio broadcast, in which a reciter may repeat an ayah several times, exploring it in different melodic modes. Both follow tajwid. The difference is in pace and ornament, not in rules. A third term, hadr, names quick recitation used for review.

Twentieth-century Egypt produced the reciters whose voices define these styles for most Muslims. Mohamed Siddiq El-Minshawi (1920–1969) was known for a grave, sorrowful tone. Mahmoud Khalil al-Husary (1917–1980) was chosen to record the complete Qur’an in murattal, a recording used for teaching around the world. Abdul Basit Abdus-Samad (1927–1988) had a voice of unusual range and was invited to recite in mosques across the Muslim world; his mujawwad recordings remain among the most played. Mustafa Ismail (1905–1978) was admired for daring melodic choices. Egyptian state radio, which began broadcasting recitation in the 1930s, carried their voices far beyond Cairo.

Mujawwad reciters draw on the system, the melodic modes of Arabic music, with names such as Bayati, Rast, Hijaz and Saba. A reciter does not sing in the sense of following a written tune; he improvises within a mode, matching its mood to the meaning of the verse. Muslim scholars have long debated how much melody is proper. Most accept it within the bounds of tajwid; some warn against turning the Qur’an into entertainment. Music historians study these recitations as one of the great vocal traditions of the world, while noting that reciters themselves usually do not call it music.

33.7

Competitions and the Hafiz

Main ideaInternational recitation competitions, held since the 1960s, honor young hafiz who have memorized the whole Qur'an, and they show how memory, tajwid and voice are judged together.

A (feminine hafiza) is a person who has memorized the entire Qur’an, about 6,200 ayahs, and can recite any passage on request. Muslim tradition honors the hafiz because memory was the first way the book was preserved. Many children begin memorizing at five or six and finish by their early teens; others complete it as adults. Memorization is taught by repetition with a teacher, a page or a few lines a day, with constant review so that older passages do not fade.

In the twentieth century, states began to hold competitions. Malaysia’s international recitation competition in Kuala Lumpur dates from 1961 and is among the oldest. Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Iran, Turkey and Dubai (whose international award began in 1997) hold others, and competitors travel from dozens of countries. Judges score memory, tajwid, voice and the handling of pauses. A judge may stop a reciter mid-verse and ask for a passage from another part of the book. Prizes can be large, and winners become known at home. The 2011 documentary Koran by Heart followed three ten-year-olds at the Cairo competition, including a girl from the Maldives and a boy from Tajikistan who knew little Arabic beyond the text he had memorized.

The competitions raise a question that Muslims themselves discuss. Is recitation a performance to be scored, or an act of worship that should not be? Supporters say the events encourage children and preserve tajwid. Critics worry that prizes and cameras change the purpose. A student in a public school does not need to settle that debate; the point is to see how a religious community argues about its own art. Chicago’s Muslim communities hold local competitions in the same spirit, and many mosques in the area run memorization classes for children.

The Written Word as Art

33.8

From Hijazi to Kufic

Main ideaThe earliest Qur'an manuscripts use a slanting script called Hijazi and then the angular Kufic, written on parchment, and they show the book becoming a visual object.

The oldest surviving Qur’an leaves are written on , prepared animal skin, in a tall, slanting script that scholars call , after the region of Mecca and Medina. The letters lean to the right, the lines are uneven, and there are few marks for short vowels. Two such leaves in the University of Birmingham library were radiocarbon-dated in 2015 to between about 568 and 645 CE with high probability. The dating is of the skin, not the ink. Scholars debate how soon after preparation the writing was done. Still, the leaves are among the earliest witnesses to the text.

By the 700s a new script took over: , named after the city of Kufa in Iraq, though it was used far more widely. Kufic is angular and horizontal. Its letters stretch along the line, its verticals are short, and its pages are often wider than they are tall. Scribes ruled the parchment carefully and placed a fixed number of lines on each page, so that a Kufic Qur’an looks as ordered as a building. Red dots marked vowels; gold rosettes marked the ends of verses. The text now had a visual rhythm to match its spoken one.

The most famous Kufic manuscript is the Blue Qur&#x27;an, made around the 800s or 900s, probably in North Africa. Its pages are dyed deep indigo and its Kufic letters are written in gold, fifteen lines to a page. Only scattered leaves survive, in museums from Tunis to New York. No one is sure who ordered it; some scholars link it to the Fatimid court, others to earlier rulers. What is certain is that by then the written Qur’an had become a work of art, made to be seen as well as read, and that its beauty was meant to honor the words rather than decorate them.

33.9

The Six Pens

Main ideaFrom the 900s calligraphers developed rounded scripts governed by proportion, and Naskh, Thuluth and their relatives became the standard hands of the Qur'an for the next thousand years.

In Baghdad in the early 900s a vizier and calligrapher named Ibn Muqla (d. 940) worked out a system of for Arabic letters. Every letter was measured against the diamond-shaped dot made by the pen’s tip and the height of the letter alif. With this system, rounded scripts could be as disciplined as Kufic. A generation later Ibn al-Bawwab (d. 1022) refined the method, and a Qur’an he copied in Baghdad in the year 1000 or 1001, now in the Chester Beatty Library in Dublin, is the earliest surviving complete Qur’an in the rounded script.

Naskh, whose name means "copying," is clear, even and quick to write. It became the everyday script of Qur’ans and remains the model for printed Arabic. , meaning "one third," is larger and more sweeping, with tall verticals and deep curves, and it is used for headings and for inscriptions on buildings. Together with Muhaqqaq, Rayhan, Tawqi’ and Riqa’, these make up the , the classical rounded scripts, which the calligrapher Yaqut al-Musta’simi (d. 1298) is credited with perfecting in Baghdad. Other regions kept their own hands: Maghribi in North Africa and Spain, with its rounded loops below the line, and Bihari in India.

The Ottoman Turks made calligraphy a court art. Sheikh Hamdullah (d. 1520) set the Ottoman style of Naskh, and Hafiz Osman (d. 1698) copied Qur’ans that later scribes imitated for centuries. A calligrapher trained for years under a master and, like a reciter, received an ijazah before signing work. The finest mushafs were also : the opening pages framed in gold and lapis blue, verse markers picked out in color, surah titles in gold on ornamented panels. Figures of people and animals never appear in a Qur’an; the art is the letter itself. In 2021 UNESCO placed Arabic calligraphy on its list of intangible cultural heritage.

33.10

The Qur'an on Buildings

Main ideaSince the 600s Muslims have written Qur'anic verses on mosques, shrines and tombs, so that the walls themselves speak the text to anyone who can read them.

Walk into almost any mosque and you will see writing where a church might have pictures. Bands of Thuluth run around the base of the dome; Kufic frames the doorway; a roundel over the prayer niche carries a single verse. The choice of verse is deliberate. The Throne Verse (2:255) is common because it speaks of God’s power and care. Surah 112 (Al-Ikhlas, Sincerity), which declares God’s oneness, appears on countless walls. The Light Verse (24:35) often surrounds a lamp or a window, since it compares God’s light to a lamp in a niche.

The oldest surviving example is the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed in 691 or 692 under the Umayyad caliph ’Abd al-Malik. A mosaic inscription about 240 meters long runs around the inner arcade. It includes Surah 112 and passages about Jesus (’Isa) such as 4:171. Historians note that these were addressed to the largely Christian city around it. The Taj Mahal in Agra, built in the 1630s and 1640s, carries whole surahs in black stone inlaid into white marble. They include Surah 36 (Ya Sin), often recited for the dead. The calligrapher Amanat Khan signed his work on the building. Each year a new , the black cloth covering the Kaaba in Mecca, is embroidered with verses in gold and silver thread.

Historians read these inscriptions as evidence. A verse chosen for a building tells what its patron wanted said and to whom, and the script tells the date and region. Art historians also note that Qur’anic writing on buildings is rarely placed where it can be read easily; a band under a dome is far above the eye. The writing works as presence as much as message. Muslims say the text sanctifies the space. A visitor to one of Chicago’s mosques can see the same tradition in newer form: Thuluth bands over the prayer hall, often made by calligraphers trained in Turkey, Egypt or Pakistan.

33.11

The Qur'an in Poetry

Main ideaPersian poets such as Rumi and Hafez wove Qur'anic verses into their poems, and through them the Qur'an reached readers far outside the mosque, including Goethe in Germany.

Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207–1273), a scholar and Sufi teacher who lived in Konya in what is now Turkey, wrote a vast Persian poem called the Masnavi. It is made of stories, some funny, some strange. Nearly every one turns on a Qur’anic verse. Rumi quotes the Arabic, then unfolds it in Persian. A later Persian poet is often credited with calling the Masnavi "the Qur’an in the Persian tongue." Rumi himself, in the way, read the Qur’an as having layers. There is an outer meaning for everyone and inner meanings for those who search.

Hafez of Shiraz (d. c. 1390) took his pen name from the word hafiz; in his own verses he says he knew the Qur’an by heart. His ghazals, short lyric poems about wine, love and the beloved, are read by Muslims in at least two ways. Some hear them as songs about earthly love and pleasure. Others hear the wine as a symbol of God’s love, in the Sufi manner. Iranian families keep a volume of Hafez beside the Qur’an and open it for guidance, a practice called fal-e Hafez. The debate over how to read him mirrors the debate over how to read the Qur’an itself: literally, or for a hidden sense.

In 1819 the German poet Goethe published the West-östlicher Divan, a book of poems written in answer to Hafez, whom he had read in translation. Goethe also read the Qur’an in German and Latin versions. He copied out passages in his notebooks. Through Hafez and Goethe, Qur’anic ideas traveled into world literature. Later, translations of Rumi became bestsellers in the United States. A student reading Rumi in English today may not notice that a line is a verse of the Qur’an. Part of the work of this course is to learn to hear it.

33.12

Fiction, Film and the Screen

Main ideaNovelists and filmmakers have brought the Qur'an to audiences who may never open it, while working within Muslim reluctance to show the Prophet.

The best-known film about the Qur’an’s origins is The Message, released in 1976 and directed by Moustapha Akkad, a Syrian-born filmmaker who worked in Hollywood. It tells the story of Muhammad’s mission from the first revelation to the return to Mecca. Following long Muslim custom, the Prophet is never shown or heard; the camera takes his point of view, and other characters speak to him. Akkad shot the film twice, once with an English-speaking cast and once with an Arabic one. Some Muslim authorities objected before release; today the film is widely shown in Muslim countries.

Documentaries have taken a different route, filming the Qur’an’s readers rather than its story. Koran by Heart (2011) followed children at the Cairo recitation competition. Other films have recorded calligraphers at work and reciters in performance. Novelists, too, have written about people who live with the book. Leila Aboulela, born in Sudan, writes in English about Muslim characters whose faith is part of ordinary life; her novel The Translator (1999) is one example. Mohja Kahf’s The Girl in the Tangerine Scarf (2006) follows a Syrian American girl growing up in Indiana, with Qur’anic verses woven into her story.

These works matter for a student of the Qur’an because they show reception: how the text lives in the minds of readers. They also show limits. A film cannot recite for you; a novel cannot show calligraphy. And both filmmakers and novelists must decide how to handle a text that many of their audience hold sacred. Some avoid quoting it, some quote it reverently, and a few use it critically. Reading how each artist made that choice is itself a way of studying the Qur’an’s place in the world.

Listening and Looking as Reading

Main ideaFor Muslims, hearing the Qur'an recited and seeing it written are ways of receiving the text, and a student can learn to notice what each way adds to reading for meaning.

This chapter has moved from sound to script to poetry and film. One idea runs through all of it. In Muslim practice, the Qur’an is not only read for information; it is heard, seen, memorized and inscribed. A hadith recorded by Abu Dawud has the Prophet tell his followers to beautify the Qur’an with their voices, and another, in al-Bukhari’s collection, praises the person who learns the Qur’an and teaches it. Sufi writers speak of sama&#x27;, listening, as a discipline of the heart. Muslims describe these as ways of drawing near to the text; historians describe them as ways a community has kept a text alive for fourteen centuries.

A student can borrow the habit without borrowing the belief. Listening to a recording of Surah 1 in the Hafs reading, even without Arabic, teaches you where the pauses fall and which sounds repeat. This is also good practice for the capstone that follows. When you choose a verse to study, you will ask what it says, but also how it sounds in recitation, where it has been inscribed, and which poets or artists have taken it up. Those are all forms of about how a text has been read. The habit of asking them transfers to any text you will meet: a poem, a speech, a law. Sound and beauty are not a distraction from meaning. In this tradition, Muslims would say, they are part of it.

Main ideaMost Arabic words grow from a three-letter root, so looking up the root of a key word shows the range of meanings a translator had to choose from.

Arabic builds most words from a of three consonants. The root k-t-b carries the idea of writing: kitab (book), katib (writer), maktab (office or school). The root s-l-m carries the idea of wholeness and peace: salam (peace), islam (submission or surrender to God), muslim (one who submits). When a translator meets a word in an ayah, the root shows its family of meanings.

Take the key word of 2:256, din, rendered "religion" by Pickthall. The word translated "compulsion," ikrah, comes from a root about dislike and forcing someone against their will. Knowing that range helps a student see why commentators could read the ayah in more than one way.

You do not need to read Arabic to do this well. Edward William Lane’s Arabic-English Lexicon (1863–1893) is public domain and lists roots with their classical meanings and examples. Say plainly that you are working from a lexicon, not from your own reading of the Arabic.

34.5

The Occasion of Revelation

Main ideaReports called asbab al-nuzul tell when and why some verses were revealed, and commentators use them, carefully, to read a verse.

Muslim scholars gathered reports about the moments that prompted particular verses. These are the , the occasions of revelation. Al-Wahidi of Nishapur (died 1075) wrote a well-known book of them. Not every verse has one, and for some verses the reports disagree. Scholars also debated whether a verse applies only to its occasion or to everyone. A common principle holds that the general wording counts, not the particular cause.

For 2:256, al-Tabari (died 923) records several reports. The best known, also found in the hadith collection of Abu Dawud, tells of women in Medina whose children had not survived. They vowed that if a child lived, they would raise it among the Jews. When the Jewish tribe of Banu al-Nadir was expelled from Medina, some of those children were among them. Their Muslim relatives said, "We will not leave our sons," and the verse is reported to have come then. The children were not forced.

Historians handle these reports with care. They were written down generations later, and more than one occasion is often given for the same verse. So a strong essay says "al-Tabari reports" or "according to al-Wahidi," not "this is what happened." Treat the occasion as one context among several, alongside the words, the surah and the long history of reading.

34.6

The Qur'an Explaining Itself

Main ideaClassical scholars held that the best explanation of one verse is another verse, so a capstone traces the key words of its ayah across the whole book.

A common principle of (commentary) holds that the Qur’an explains itself. Ibn Taymiyya (died 1328) wrote that the best method is to explain the Qur’an by the Qur’an, then by the Prophet’s sayings, then by the words of his Companions. Scholars call this tafsir al-Qur’an bi’l-Qur’an. In practice it means finding other verses that use the same word or treat the same theme.

For 2:256 a student might gather 10:99, where God asks the Prophet whether he would compel people to believe, and 18:29, which says the truth is from your Lord, so let whoever will believe and whoever will disbelieve. A student might also read 9:5 and 9:29, verses about fighting. Some classical scholars held that those later verses limited or abrogated 2:256. Others, like Ibn Kathir (died 1373), read 2:256 as a lasting principle that no one is forced to enter Islam.

Laying verses side by side is how the commentators themselves worked. It is also where their disagreements come from. When you trace your key word through the Qur’an, record every verse you use with surah name and number. Then report which scholars connected which verses, and what each concluded.

What idea do the three verses share? Why might a commentator who reads them together find it hard to call 2:256 abrogated?

Section 3

Commentators Old and New

34.7

The Classical Commentaries

Main ideaA handful of classical commentaries, Sunni and Shia, are the standard places to see how a verse has been read over a thousand years.

Long before modern scholarship, Muslim scholars wrote verse-by-verse commentaries. Al-Tabari (died 923) gathered the reports of earlier generations and often weighed them. Al-Zamakhshari (died 1144) focused on grammar and style. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (died 1210) wrote a vast commentary full of philosophy and theology. Al-Qurtubi (died 1273) drew out legal rulings. Ibn Kathir (died 1373) explained verses by other verses and by hadith.

Shia scholars wrote major commentaries too. Al-Tusi (died 1067) and al-Tabrisi (died 1153) wrote classical works. In the 20th century, Muhammad Husayn Tabataba’i (died 1981) wrote al-Mizan, which reads the Qur’an by the Qur’an. Sufi commentators such as al-Qushayri (died 1072) looked for inner, spiritual meanings. A strong capstone uses at least two classical voices and, where it fits, notes Sunni, Shia or Sufi readings without ranking them.

Most of these works are in Arabic, but parts of several are in English, and The Study Quran (2015) summarizes many of them in its notes. When you use a summary, say so: "according to The Study Quran’s note on al-Razi." Your job is to report what each commentator said and why, in your own words, with the name and the verse.

34.8

A Modern Reader

Main ideaModern commentators read the Qur'an with new questions, and adding one modern voice shows how an old verse speaks to a new age.

Readers in the last two centuries brought new questions to the text. Muhammad Abduh (died 1905) of Egypt urged fresh reasoning and taught a commentary later completed by his student Rashid Rida. Sayyid Qutb (died 1966) wrote In the Shade of the Qur’an, a political and spiritual commentary. Muhammad Asad (died 1992) added full notes to his translation, The Message of the Qur’an (1980). Fazlur Rahman (died 1988), who taught at the University of Chicago, argued for reading verses by the moral aims behind them.

Modern readers often disagree with each other as much as with the classical scholars. On 2:256, many modern writers read the ayah as a charter of religious freedom for all people. Others stay closer to the classical debate on who it covers. Some women scholars, such as amina wadud in Qur’an and Woman (1992), reread verses on gender by looking at the Qur’an’s wider principles.

For the capstone, choose one modern reader who actually wrote about your verse or its theme. Describe the reading in your own words, since modern works are under copyright. Then place it beside your classical voices. Does the modern reader agree, build on them or break from them? Say which, and give the reason the modern reader offers.

34.9

Acknowledging Other Readings

Main ideaA fair essay names the readings it does not follow and explains them accurately, which makes its own argument stronger.

Every serious reader of a verse knows other readers disagree. Classical commentators often listed several views and then said which they preferred. Al-Tabari regularly wrote that people of interpretation differed and gave each view. A capstone should do the same. This part of an essay is sometimes called the , but here it is not about winning. It is about describing the whole conversation.

Describe the other reading at its best, in words its holders would accept. If you argue that 2:256 is read by most modern commentators as a general principle, say plainly that many classical scholars held it was limited or abrogated, and give their reason: they read it together with later verses on fighting. Then explain why others disagreed. Do not call a reading ignorant or extreme without showing where scholars answered it.

One more kind of difference matters. Muslims read the Qur’an as the word of God. Many historians and scholars of religion study it as a text in history. Your essay does not choose between those views. It can report both: how believers have read a verse, and what historians say about its setting. That is what describing the text fairly means.

Section 4

Making and Sharing the Argument

34.10

Writing a Thesis

Main ideaA thesis for the capstone makes a claim about what the verse says and how it has been read, and every part of it can be checked against sources.

A is the one-sentence claim your whole essay supports. In this course, a thesis is not about whether the Qur’an is true. It is about the text and its readers. Here is an example: "Classical commentators such as al-Tabari tied 2:256 to a specific moment in Medina and debated its reach, while many modern readers treat it as a general principle of religious freedom; both readings build on the verse’s context in Surah 2."

Look at what makes it work. It names the verse. It names readers. It makes a claim someone could dispute, since another student might argue the classical and modern readings are closer than this. And every part can be checked: a reader can open al-Tabari, a modern commentary and Surah 2 and see whether the thesis holds.

Build the essay from the thesis. A useful order is: the verse in two translations; its place in the surah; its occasion, if reports exist; two classical readings; one modern reading; other readings you do not follow; and your conclusion. Each paragraph should begin by saying which of these it covers.

34.11

Main ideaClear citations, a short presentation and a shared rubric let others check your work and see how it will be judged.

Cite the Qur’an by surah name and number and by ayah: Al-Baqarah 2:256. Name the translator and year every time you quote: Pickthall, 1930. Cite commentators by name, work and verse: al-Tabari, Jami’ al-Bayan, on 2:256. If you used a summary, name it. Hadith are cited by collection, such as Sahih al-Bukhari or Sunan Abu Dawud. If you play a recording of the ayah recited, say so beforehand, and keep it short. Listeners should leave knowing what the verse says and how people have read it.

34.12

Studying Scripture in a Public School

Main ideaU.S. public schools may teach about religion, including a sacred text, when the teaching is academic and does not promote or reject any faith.

In 1963 the U.S. Supreme Court decided Abington School District v. Schempp. It ruled that public schools may not lead students in devotional Bible reading or prayer. In the same decision, the Court said schools may teach about religion. Justice Tom Clark wrote that the Bible may be studied for its literary and historical qualities when presented objectively as part of a secular program of education.

That is the line this whole course has walked. You have learned what the Qur’an says, how it was written down and recited, and how Sunni, Shia, Sufi and modern readers have understood it. You have not been asked to believe or disbelieve. Many Muslim students recite the Qur’an at home or at a mosque; many classmates have never opened it. An academic course gives both a shared way to talk about it.

Take a moment to reflect. What did you learn from reading one verse so closely? What surprised you about how many ways careful people have read it? How did it feel to describe a view you do not hold in words its holders would accept? Those skills reach well beyond the Qur’an. They help with any text people care deeply about, and with any neighbor who reads it differently.
