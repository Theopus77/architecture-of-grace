Scarcity and the Choices People Make

Foundations

You want a new book, a trip to the zoo and a pizza party. But you have only so much money and so much time. Every day, you have to choose. So does your family, your school and your town. This unit is about those choices.

First you will learn why we cannot have everything, and what we give up when we choose. Then you will visit a pizza shop and a paper airplane race. You will see how people make things, and why splitting up work helps.

By the end, you will be able to name the next-best thing you give up in a choice. You will sort resources into three groups. You will use a decision grid, and you will explain why people depend on each other.

The big question

How do people make good choices when they cannot have everything they want?

The story

Four Ideas and $200

The student council has money for one big idea, and four good ones to pick from.

The student council met in Room 9 after lunch. Mr. Diaz wrote a number on the board: $200. "This is our money for the spring," he said. "We get to decide how to use it." Five council members sat up straight.

Hands went up fast. Leo wanted new balls for recess, about $150 (an example price). Priya wanted a class trip to the nature center, about $200. Sam wanted a movie night with popcorn, about $120. Grace wanted new books for the library corner, about $180.

Mr. Diaz added up the ideas. Together they cost $650. "That is much more than $200," Priya said. "We cannot do all of them." Leo sighed. "Maybe we can only do one." The room got quiet while everyone thought.

Grace had a plan. "Let's make a chart," she said. "We can list what matters most. How many kids does it help? How long does it last?" They drew a grid on the board. Soon each idea had a score, and a hard choice began to look clearer.

Talk about itIf the council picks the books, what do they give up? Which idea would you choose, and why?

Wants Bigger Than What There Is

7.1

What Scarcity Means

Main ideaScarcity means our wants are bigger than the things we have to meet them.

The council had $200. Their four ideas cost $650 in all. They wanted more than their money could buy. This problem has a name. It is called .

Scarcity is not just about money. A day has only 24 hours. A park has only so much grass. A class has only one teacher. Our keep growing, but time, land and money run out.

Everyone faces scarcity, rich or poor. A rich person still has only 24 hours a day. So people must choose. Scarcity is the reason economics exists. Economics is the study of how people choose.

7.2

Needs and Wants

Main ideaNeeds keep us alive and safe; wants make life nicer, and both must fit what we have.

Food, water, a home and warm clothes are . We need them to live and stay safe. A new video game is a want. It is nice to have, but we can live without it.

Families often pay for needs first. Say a family has $100 left for the week (an example). Groceries cost $70. That leaves $30 for wants, like a movie or a toy.

Some things can be both. You need shoes. But you may want shoes with lights. The plain pair meets the need. The extra money for lights pays for a want.

7.3

Scarcity Everywhere

Main ideaFamilies, schools and towns all face scarcity, so they all have to make choices.

Families face scarcity. There is only so much pay each month. A family may save for a car or take a trip. It may not be able to do both this year. It has to pick.

Schools face scarcity too. A school has only so many rooms and so much money. It might add a music room or a science lab. With one empty room, it must pick one.

Towns face scarcity. A town may want a new pool and a new fire truck. Taxes pay for both, and tax money runs out. Leaders must choose what comes first.

Human Resources

Main ideaHuman resources are the work and skills people bring to making goods and services.

A pizza does not make itself. A cook stretches the dough. A cashier takes the order. The work people do is a . Economists also call it labor.

Skills make human resources stronger. A new cook might make 10 pizzas an hour. After practice, the same cook might make 15 an hour (example numbers). That is 5 more pizzas each hour.

People learn skills in school and on the job. Reading, math and teamwork all help. Your learning today is a resource you are building for later. It grows every day.

7.6

Capital Resources

Main ideaCapital resources are tools, machines and buildings made by people to make other things.

The pizza shop has an oven, a mixer and a cutter. These are . People made them. Now they are used to make other things, like pizza.

Good tools help people make more. With a small oven, a shop might bake 4 pizzas at once. With a big oven, it might bake 8 at once (example numbers). That is twice as many.

Money in a jar is not a capital resource. It can buy an oven, but it cannot bake. Capital resources are the real tools. So every shop uses three kinds of resources: natural, human and capital.

Every Choice Has a Cost

7.7

The Next-Best Thing

Main ideaThe opportunity cost of a choice is the next-best thing you give up.

The council ranked its ideas. The trip came first. The books came second. So if they pick the trip, they give up the books. The books are the of the trip.

Opportunity cost is only the next-best thing. It is not every idea you did not pick. The council did not also give up the balls and movie night as the cost. Only the second choice counts.

Time has an opportunity cost too. Say you spend an hour on video games. If you would have played soccer next, soccer is your opportunity cost. Every has one.

7.9

Incentives Change Choices

Main ideaIncentives are rewards or costs that make people more or less likely to do something.

A store puts up a sign: juice is $1 today instead of $2 (example prices). More people buy juice. The lower price is an . It pulls people toward a choice.

Some incentives are rewards. A library gives a sticker for every 5 books read. Kids read more. Some incentives are costs. A late fee for a library book makes people return it on time.

People compare the reward and the cost. Say a late fee is 10 cents a day (an example). A book 6 days late costs 60 cents. That cost helps people remember to return books.

The story

The Paper Airplane Race

Two teams fold paper airplanes for ten minutes, and one team tries a new plan.

8.6

When Splitting Work Is Hard

Main ideaThe division of labor helps, but jobs can get boring and one slow step can hold up the line.

The assembly line has problems too. Lucy drew stripes for ten minutes straight. By the end, she felt bored. Doing the same small task all day can feel dull.

Teams fix this in smart ways. They add a second person to the slow step. They switch jobs so no one gets bored. Good teams look for the slow spot and help it.

Why: Doing one job means you need others for the rest.

5. A line has steps that take 4, 5 and 2 units a minute. What limits the line?

Why: A line goes only as fast as its slowest step.

6. Which is a human resource in a bakery?

Why: Work and skill come from people.

7. A worker makes 5 toys an hour, then 7 with a new tool (example). What went up?

Why: Making more in the same time is higher productivity.

8. What risk does an entrepreneur take?

Why: An entrepreneur spends money first and may not earn it back.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. What is scarcity?

Why: Scarcity means wants are bigger than what there is.

2. Zoe picks swimming. Her next choice was the library. Her third was a nap. What is her opportunity cost?

Why: Only the next-best choice, the library, is the opportunity cost.

3. Which is a natural resource?

Why: Soil comes from nature.

4. Which is a capital resource?

Why: A sewing machine is a tool people made to make other goods.

5. A class has $80. A trip costs $65 (example). How much is left?

Why: $80 minus $65 is $15.

6. A store gives a free drink with every 5 visits. What is the free drink?

Why: It is a reward that makes people want to come back.

7. Who is a producer?

Why: The barber gives a service, so the barber is a producer.

8. Team A makes 12 cards in 10 minutes. Team B splits the work and makes 18. How many more did Team B make?

Why: 18 minus 12 is 6.

9. Why does the division of labor often make more goods?

Why: Repeating one step builds skill and saves switching time.

10. A lemonade stand spends $8 and sells 20 cups at $1 each (example). What is the profit?

Why: 20 times $1 is $20. $20 minus $8 is $12.

11. What does a decision grid list down the side?

Why: Choices go across the top. Criteria go down the side.

12. A storm stops trucks and stores run low on milk. What does this show?

Why: Stores depend on trucks, and people depend on stores.

13. A line has steps that do 6, 3 and 5 parts a minute. About how many products a minute?

Why: A line goes only as fast as its slowest step, 3 a minute.

14. Why do entrepreneurs take a risk?

Why: They pay costs before they know how much they will sell.

15. A worker makes 4 bowls an hour, then 6 with a new wheel (example). How many more in 3 hours?

Why: 3 times 4 is 12. 3 times 6 is 18. 18 minus 12 is 6.

  • Name your opportunity cost: just the next-best idea.
