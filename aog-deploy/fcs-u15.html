Cooking Methods and Heat

Food Preparation

Every recipe is really an instruction about heat: how much, from where, for how long, with or without water. A pan that is not hot enough turns a sauté into a stew. A crowded sheet pan turns a roast into a steam bath. A cheap cut of beef grilled like a steak is a jaw workout, and the same cut left for three hours under a lid falls apart into the best dinner of the week. Once you can see heat moving, most of the mystery of why a dish went wrong disappears, and so does most of the fear of cooking without a recipe.

This unit starts with the three ways heat travels in a kitchen: by touch, through moving air and water, and across open space from a glowing element. From there you will learn the dry-heat methods that brown, the moist-heat methods that soften, and the combination methods that do both, along with the two chemical changes behind browning and the reason a wet or crowded pan never gets there. Then you will learn to match method to food: which cuts want ten minutes of high heat and which want an afternoon of low, how to read a label at the meat counter, what each oven temperature does, and how to braise, stir-fry and build a pan sauce step by step.

Through all of it runs one tool and one set of numbers. The food thermometer, pushed into the thickest part of the food, is the only honest judge of doneness, and the USDA safe minimum temperatures, 165 °F for poultry, 160 °F for ground meat, 145 °F with a rest for whole cuts and fish, are the standard. By the end you will be able to look at any ingredient, choose a method, set a temperature, plan the time, and know when it is done.

How does understanding the way heat moves let a cook choose the right method for any food and get it right the first time?

The story

Two Trays, One Oven

Marcus and Jaylen roasted the same vegetables at the same temperature for the same time. Only one tray came out right.

The cooking lab had one assignment on the board: roasted vegetables, 425 °F, 25 minutes. Marcus and Jaylen worked at the same station. They cut the same bag of carrots, broccoli and red onion into pieces about an inch across, tossed them with 2 Tbsp of oil and a half teaspoon of salt, and split the bowl between two sheet pans. Both pans went into the same preheated oven on the same rack, side by side.

The difference was in how they loaded the pans. Marcus spread his vegetables in one layer with a little space between the pieces. Jaylen had cut a few extra carrots because he liked them, and he piled everything on, two and three pieces deep in the middle. "Same oven, same time," he said. "It will all cook."

At 12 minutes Ms. Okafor had them pull the oven door open and look. Marcus's pan was already sizzling. The edges of the onion had gone dark and sweet-smelling, and the cut faces of the carrots were turning brown. Jaylen's pan was wet. There was a shine of liquid on the bottom, and a puff of steam rolled out when the door opened. The vegetables were softening, but they were the same color they had been going in.

At 25 minutes the two pans came out. Marcus's broccoli had crisp brown tips and the carrots were tender with dark edges. Jaylen's vegetables were cooked through, but they were pale, limp and a little watery, and the ones in the middle of the pile were still firm. Same recipe, same oven, same timer.

"You did not roast those," Ms. Okafor told Jaylen, not unkindly. "You steamed them. Every vegetable is mostly water. Pile them up and the water they give off cannot escape, so the pan never gets hot enough on the surface of the food to brown it." She handed him a second sheet pan. "Next time, two pans. Crowding is the mistake almost every new cook makes, and it is the easiest one to fix."

Section 1

Three Ways Heat Travels

29.1

Conduction: Pan to Food

Main ideaConduction is heat moving by direct touch, and it is why the side of the food that touches the pan is the side that browns.

Set a dry skillet on a burner over medium heat and wait two minutes. The metal of the pan warms first where the flame or coil touches it, and the heat spreads outward through the metal. Lay a slice of bread in the pan. Within a minute the underside toasts while the top stays soft. Heat passed from the burner into the pan and from the pan into the bread by : heat moving from one thing to another that it is touching.

Conduction only works where surfaces meet. That is why a steak browns only on the side pressed against the pan and why a pancake needs to be flipped. It is also why the shape of the food matters. A whole potato takes an hour to bake, but the same potato cut into half-inch cubes cooks in fifteen minutes. Heat has to travel from the outside of each piece to its center by conduction, and a thick piece has a long way to go.

Pans conduct heat at different speeds. Copper and aluminum move heat fast and evenly. Cast iron is slower to heat but holds heat well, so it does not cool off when cold food hits it. Stainless steel is sturdy but heats unevenly on its own, so good stainless pans have an aluminum or copper layer inside the base. A thin, cheap pan has hot spots right over the flame and cool spots at the edges, which is why food burns in the middle and stays pale at the rim.

Conduction also explains a common mistake. If you lay meat in a pan that is still cold, the meat warms slowly along with the pan, its juices leak out, and it ends up gray and steamed instead of brown. Heat the pan first, add the oil, and wait until the oil shimmers. Then add the food. The hot metal delivers heat to the surface fast enough to brown it before the inside overcooks.

29.2

Convection: Moving Air and Water

Main ideaConvection carries heat through moving air, water or oil, which is why a convection oven cooks faster and why boiling water cooks evenly on every side.

Drop a handful of pasta into a pot of boiling water. Every piece is surrounded by water at 212 °F, top, bottom and sides, and every piece cooks at the same rate. That is : heat carried by a moving liquid or gas. In the pot, hot water rises from the bottom, cooler water sinks to take its place, and the whole pot keeps circulating. Nothing has to touch the pan directly to get hot.

An oven works by convection too, just with air instead of water. The heating element warms the air, the warm air rises and moves, and the food is surrounded by hot air on all sides. Air carries heat much less efficiently than water does, which is why you can reach into a 350 °F oven for a moment but never into 212 °F water. A adds a fan that blows the hot air around. Moving air strips away the layer of cooler, damp air that clings to food, so it browns faster and more evenly. Many recipes say to lower the temperature about 25 °F when using the fan.

Deep frying is convection in oil. Oil can be heated far past 212 °F, usually to 350 or 375 °F, and it flows around the food just like water. The surface of the food dries and browns quickly while the moisture inside turns to steam and cooks it from within. That is why fried food is crisp outside and soft inside.

Convection needs room to move. In a boiling pot, too much pasta in too little water drops the temperature and the pieces stick together. In an oven, a pan pushed against the wall or two racks stacked close together block the air and leave cool pockets. Leave space around pans and rotate them halfway through baking so every side sees moving air.

29.3

Radiation: Heat Across Space

Main ideaRadiant heat travels in straight lines from a glowing element or coals to whatever faces it, which is why the broiler cooks only the top and the grill cooks only the bottom.

Hold your hand a few inches below the glowing coil of a broiler, not touching anything, and you feel the heat right away. Nothing hot is touching your hand and the air is barely moving. The heat is arriving as : energy that travels in straight lines from a hot surface to whatever faces it, the same way sunlight warms your face. A toaster, a broiler, a grill and a campfire all cook mostly by radiant heat.

Radiant heat only reaches the side of the food that faces the source, and it weakens quickly with distance. Under a broiler, the top of a piece of fish browns and the bottom does not. On a grill, the underside chars while the top stays pale until you flip it. Moving food two inches closer to a broiler makes a large difference; moving it four inches away can turn broiling into gentle warming. That is why broiler recipes tell you which rack position to use.

In a regular oven, radiation and convection work together. The walls and the bottom element radiate heat while the air carries it. A dark metal pan absorbs more radiant heat than a shiny one, so cookies on a dark pan brown faster on the bottom and can burn. A glass baking dish lets radiant heat pass through to the food, so many recipes say to lower the oven by 25 °F when baking in glass.

Microwaves are a special kind of radiation. The oven sends out waves that make water molecules in the food vibrate, and that vibration is heat. Microwaves heat the water inside the food directly, an inch or so deep, rather than heating the surface first. That is why microwaved food does not brown and why it can be scalding in one spot and cold in another. Stir, cover and let food stand a minute so the heat evens out.

29.4

Preheating Pays Off

Main ideaPreheating the oven, the pan and the grill puts full heat on the surface of the food from the first second, which is what browning and good texture depend on.

Turn the oven to 425 °F and the display reads 425 °F within seconds. The oven is not there yet. The display shows the setting, not the temperature. Most home ovens take 15 to 20 minutes to come up to a roasting temperature, and the walls and racks take longer still. Many ovens beep when the air near the sensor reaches the setting, which is before the walls have caught up. Give it a few extra minutes after the beep, and use an oven thermometer hung from the middle rack if you want the truth.

Why does it matter? Roasted vegetables, bread, cookies and pizza all depend on a burst of high heat in the first minutes. Vegetables put into a cold oven sit in their own moisture as it slowly warms and end up soft and pale. A loaf of bread needs high heat right away to spring up before its crust sets. Cookies spread and go flat in an oven that is still warming. Preheating also makes timing honest: a recipe’s 25 minutes assumes the oven was already at temperature.

Pans need preheating too. For sautéing, heat the empty pan over medium-high for a minute or two, add the oil, and wait until it shimmers and flows like water. A drop of water flicked in should sizzle and dance. Cast iron takes longer, five minutes or more, because it heats slowly. A grill needs 10 to 15 minutes with the lid closed so the grates are hot enough to sear grill marks and release the food instead of gluing it.

There are exceptions. Braises, casseroles and a whole roast that will cook for hours do not need a screaming-hot start, and a few recipes call for a cold-oven start on purpose. Pastry and dough that must stay cold, such as pie crust, go into a fully preheated oven so the butter turns to steam before it melts out. Read the recipe, and when it says preheat, do it first, before you start chopping.

Dry Heat Methods

29.5

Roasting and Baking

Main ideaRoasting and baking surround food with hot, dry air in an oven, and high heat with space around the food is what makes the surface brown and crisp.

Roasting and baking are the same method with two names. Both cook food uncovered in the dry, hot air of an oven. Cooks usually say for meat and vegetables and for bread, pastry and casseroles, but the oven does not know the difference. Because the air is dry, the surface of the food dries out and then browns. That browning is what gives roasted chicken its skin and a roasted carrot its sweet, dark edges.

Here is a basic vegetable roast. Preheat the oven to 425 °F. Cut vegetables into even pieces about an inch across so they finish together. Toss them in a bowl with about 1 Tbsp of oil per pound and a half teaspoon of salt. Spread them in a single layer on a rimmed sheet pan with space between the pieces, cut sides down where you can, since the cut face against the hot metal browns best. Roast 20 to 30 minutes, turning once halfway, until the edges are dark and a fork slides in easily.

Meat roasts by the same rules but on a rack or a bed of vegetables so air reaches every side. A high temperature, 400 to 450 °F, browns the outside; a moderate one, 325 to 375 °F, cooks the inside gently. Many cooks use both, starting hot and turning the oven down, or finishing hot. Whatever the plan, the thermometer decides when it is done: 165 °F for poultry, 145 °F with a 3-minute rest for whole cuts of beef, pork and lamb.

The mistakes are the usual ones. Crowding traps steam and stops browning. Skipping the oil leaves the surface dry and leathery instead of crisp, because oil conducts heat into the surface and carries flavor. Cutting pieces in different sizes means the small ones burn before the large ones are done. Opening the oven door every few minutes drops the temperature and throws off the timing.

29.6

Sautéing and Pan-Frying

Main ideaSautéing cooks small pieces fast in a little fat in a hot pan, and it only works if the pan is hot, the food is dry, and there is room for steam to escape.

Sauté comes from a French word for jump, and that is the picture: small pieces of food tossed in a hot pan with a little fat until they are browned and cooked through. Here is how to sauté sliced mushrooms. Heat a wide skillet over medium-high for about two minutes. Add 1 Tbsp of oil and let it shimmer. Add the mushrooms in a single layer and do not touch them for a full minute so the undersides brown. Then stir or toss, add a pinch of salt, and cook until they are golden, 5 to 7 minutes.

Three things make a sauté succeed. First, a hot pan, so the surface of the food browns before the inside overcooks. Second, dry food. Pat vegetables and meat dry with a paper towel; water on the surface must boil away before browning can start, and it cools the pan while it does. Third, space. If the pan is crowded, the food gives off steam faster than it can escape, the temperature drops, and the food boils in its own juices. Cook in batches if you must.

Lay food into the pan away from you so hot fat does not splash toward your hand. Turn once, when the first side is deeply brown and the food releases from the pan on its own.

Fat choice matters. Butter tastes wonderful but burns above medium heat because of its milk solids; use it for gentle sautéing or add it at the end. Neutral oils such as canola or vegetable oil handle high heat. Olive oil works for most sautéing. If any fat starts to smoke heavily, pull the pan off the heat, let it cool a moment, and lower the burner. Never add water to hot fat, and keep a lid nearby in case of a flare-up.

29.7

Grilling and Broiling

Main ideaGrilling and broiling cook with intense radiant heat from one side, which suits thin, tender foods and demands close attention and a flip.

Grilling and broiling are the same idea turned upside down. On a grill, radiant heat from coals or burners comes from below. Under a broiler, it comes from the element above. Both are hot and fast, both brown only the side facing the heat, and both are best for foods that cook through quickly: burgers, chicken pieces, fish fillets, vegetables cut into slabs, thin steaks and chops. A thick roast or a tough cut needs a gentler method.

To broil, set a rack so the food will be about 4 inches from the element, and preheat the broiler for 5 minutes with the door closed. Put the food on a broiler pan or a rimmed sheet, pat it dry and oil it lightly. Slide it in and stay in the kitchen. Broiling is measured in minutes, and a piece of fish can go from pale to burnt in the time it takes to answer a text. Turn once, and check doneness with a thermometer: 165 °F for chicken, 160 °F for a burger, 145 °F for fish.

To grill, preheat with the lid closed for 10 to 15 minutes, then scrape the grates with a brush and wipe them with an oiled paper towel held in tongs. Lay food on the hot grates and leave it alone until it releases and shows dark marks, then turn. Set up two zones if you can, one hot and one cooler, so food that browns too fast can be moved to finish gently. Use long tongs, keep a spray bottle of water for small flare-ups on charcoal, and never squirt water at a grease fire on a gas grill; close the lid and turn off the burners.

Safety around grills is not optional. Grill outdoors only, never in a garage or under an overhang; the carbon monoxide from charcoal and gas is deadly indoors. Keep children and pets three feet away. Use one plate and one set of tongs for raw meat and a clean plate for cooked, so juices from raw chicken never touch the finished food. Let coals cool completely before disposing of them.

Moist and Combination Heat

29.8

Boiling, Simmering, Poaching, Steaming

Main ideaMoist-heat methods cook food in or over water at or below 212 °F, and the difference between boiling, simmering and poaching is how much the water is moving.

Water sets a ceiling. At sea level it boils at 212 °F and cannot get hotter as a liquid, no matter how high the burner is. That is why nothing browns in a pot of water, and it is why moist-heat cooking is gentle and forgiving. The choice among the moist methods comes down to how much the water is moving, which you can see. A has big, rolling bubbles breaking all over the surface. A has small bubbles rising lazily, a few at a time. A has almost no bubbles at all, just a shimmer, with the water well below boiling.

Use a rolling boil for pasta and for blanching green vegetables, where you want speed and the water’s movement keeps pieces from sticking. Use a simmer for soups, stews, dried beans, rice and anything that cooks a long time; a hard boil would break the food apart and boil away the liquid. Use a poach for delicate food that would fall apart or toughen: eggs, fish, chicken breast. Bring the liquid to a bare simmer, add the food, and hold the heat low.

cooks food over boiling water, not in it. Put an inch of water in a pot, set a steamer basket above the waterline, bring the water to a boil, add the food and cover. Steam surrounds the food at 212 °F and cooks it fast without washing away flavor or nutrients into the water. Broccoli steams in about 5 minutes, and green beans in about 5 to 7. Open the lid away from your face; a cloud of steam burns worse than boiling water because it carries more heat.

Common mistakes are easy to spot. Boiling a stew hard turns the meat stringy and the vegetables to mush. Salting pasta water lightly or not at all leaves it bland; use about 1 Tbsp of salt per 4 quarts. Letting a steamer boil dry scorches the pot and can crack it. And a pot that is too small for the pasta drops back to a simmer when the pasta goes in, so the pieces glue together at the bottom.

29.9

Braising and Stewing

Main ideaBraising and stewing brown the food first in dry heat, then finish it slowly in a little liquid with a lid, which makes tough cuts tender.

Some of the best-tasting dishes use two kinds of heat, one after the other. Braising starts by browning a large piece of meat or a batch of vegetables in a hot pan with a little fat. Then liquid is added, only partway up the food, the pot is covered, and it cooks slowly at a low simmer, on the stovetop or in a 300 to 325 °F oven, until it is fork-tender. is the same idea with food cut into bite-sized pieces and enough liquid to cover them. Both are called combination methods because dry heat does the browning and moist heat does the tenderizing.

Here is the shape of a braise. Pat the meat dry and season it with salt. Heat 1 to 2 Tbsp of oil in a heavy pot over medium-high until it shimmers. Brown the meat on all sides, taking your time, about 3 to 4 minutes per side, and do not crowd the pot. Set the meat aside. Cook chopped onion, carrot and celery in the same pot until soft. Add liquid, such as broth, and scrape up the brown bits stuck to the bottom. Return the meat, bring the liquid to a simmer, cover, and cook low and slow for two to three hours.

Time is the secret. Tough cuts such as chuck, shoulder and shank are full of connective tissue, the tough web that holds muscle together. Fast cooking leaves it chewy. Long, gentle, moist heat slowly turns it into gelatin, which is silky and rich, and the meat falls apart. Rush it with a hard boil and the muscle fibers seize up and dry out before the connective tissue has time to soften. Check every hour, keep the liquid at a lazy simmer, and add a splash more if it runs low.

Braises and stews reward planning. They taste better the next day, so they suit cooking ahead. Cool them within two hours of cooking, refrigerate at 40 °F or below, and reheat to 165 °F. Skim the fat that rises and hardens on top before reheating if you like a lighter sauce. Leftover braising liquid is the base for a great soup.

Why Food Browns

29.10

The Crowded Pan

Main ideaFood is mostly water, and if the water it gives off cannot escape, the pan never gets hotter than 212 °F on the food's surface and nothing browns.

Go back to Jaylen’s pan. A raw carrot is close to 90 percent water, and mushrooms, zucchini and onions are about the same. As they heat, that water turns to steam and leaves the surface. On a pan with space, the steam floats away, the surface dries, and the temperature of the food’s skin climbs past 212 °F toward the oven’s 425 °F. Then it browns. On a crowded pan, steam from one piece condenses on its neighbor. The food sits in a wet fog at 212 °F, which is exactly the temperature of a steamer basket.

The same thing happens on the stovetop, faster. A skillet holds only so much heat. Every cold, wet piece of food you drop in pulls heat out of the metal. Add too many at once and the pan’s temperature crashes below the point where water boils off quickly. Juice pools, the food gray-steams, and by the time the liquid finally cooks away the inside is overdone. That is why recipes say in batches, why a good sauté pan is heavy, and why the first batch should always be smaller than you think.

The fix is space, and it is cheap. On a sheet pan, leave a finger’s width between pieces and use two pans instead of one; put them on two racks and swap and rotate halfway through. In a skillet, cook the food in two or three rounds and set the finished ones on a plate; they reheat in seconds when everything goes back together at the end. For meat, one piece per hand-sized area of pan is a fair rule. For a big crowd, roast at a higher temperature rather than piling more on a pan.

Wet food is crowded food’s twin. Vegetables straight from the colander carry a film of water that must boil off before browning starts. Spin salad greens dry, pat vegetables and meat with a towel, and let frozen vegetables thaw and drain before they hit the oil. A dry surface, a hot pan, and room to breathe: those three things are the difference between roasted and steamed.

Maillard and Caramelization

Main ideaBrowning is two chemical changes, the Maillard reaction between proteins and sugars and the caramelization of sugar alone, and both need dry heat well above the boiling point of water.

Why does a seared steak taste different from a boiled one made of the same meat? The answer is the , named for the French chemist who described it. When a food that contains both proteins and sugars, which is almost every food, gets hot and dry enough, those molecules react with each other and form hundreds of new compounds. Those compounds are the brown color and the deep, roasted, savory flavors of bread crust, seared meat, toasted nuts and roasted coffee. The reaction gets going quickly only above roughly 300 °F, which is why a wet surface at 212 °F stays pale.

is a different change with a similar look. It happens when sugar itself is heated until it breaks apart and rebuilds into new molecules, giving the deep amber color and the nutty, slightly bitter taste of caramel. It needs even higher heat than Maillard, starting well above 300 °F. Onions cooked slowly until they are soft and sweet and brown are caramelized; the sugar in the onion has changed. The dark edge on a roasted sweet potato is both reactions at once.

Both reactions explain the rules you already know. Preheat, because the surface must reach browning temperature fast. Dry the food, because water holds the surface at 212 °F. Do not crowd, because steam does the same. Use a little fat, because it conducts heat into the surface and carries the flavor compounds. And do not move the food too soon; the browned crust forms in the first minute or two of stillness, and then it releases from the pan on its own.

Browning has a limit. Go past it and the same reactions run to black char, which tastes bitter and acrid. Toast is delicious; burnt toast is not. Watch the color, not just the timer, and pull food when it is deep golden brown. If a pan’s brown bits, called fond, have gone black, they will make any sauce bitter, so wipe them out and start again rather than trying to save them.

Matching Method to Food

30.1

Tender Cuts and Tough Cuts

Main ideaMuscles that worked hard are tough and need slow moist heat; muscles that did little are tender and need fast dry heat.

Meat is muscle, and muscles that did a lot of work are different from muscles that did not. The shoulder, the leg and the neck of a cow or pig carried the animal’s weight all day. Those cuts, such as chuck, brisket, shank and pork shoulder, are full of , the tough web that holds muscle bundles together, and they are chewy if cooked fast. The muscles along the back did very little, so cuts from there, such as tenderloin, loin chops, ribeye and strip steak, are tender from the start and cost more.

The rule follows from that. suit dry-heat methods that are fast and hot: grilling, broiling, sautéing, pan-frying, roasting. Cook them just until done and stop; more time only dries them out. suit moist-heat and combination methods that are slow and gentle: braising, stewing, simmering. Time and moisture turn connective tissue into soft gelatin, and the meat becomes tender and the sauce becomes rich.

The same idea works for poultry, fish and vegetables. Chicken breast is lean and tender, so it dries out with long cooking; sear or roast it just to 165 °F. Chicken thighs and legs have more connective tissue and stay juicy through a braise. Most fish is delicate and cooks in minutes by any method. Hard root vegetables, such as beets and potatoes, need time to soften; tender ones, such as spinach and asparagus, need seconds. Dried beans and whole grains need long simmering to take in water.

Getting this wrong wastes money in both directions. A ribeye braised for three hours turns gray and dry, because it had no connective tissue to melt, only muscle fibers to tighten. A chuck roast grilled for eight minutes is a workout to chew. Learn a few cuts from each group, and you can walk up to any meat counter, buy what is on sale, and know what to do with it.

Oven Temperatures and What They Do

Main ideaA low oven cooks gently and evenly, a moderate oven cooks most things, and a hot oven browns and crisps, so the temperature you choose is a choice about what the surface and center should look like.

Ovens have a dial from about 200 to 500 °F, and cooks group the range into bands. , about 250 to 325 °F, is for long, gentle cooking: braises, slow-roasted tough cuts, custards, meringues, and holding food warm. Heat creeps in slowly, so the outside of the food is not much more cooked than the center by the time it is done. A oven, 350 to 375 °F, is the everyday setting for cakes, cookies, casseroles, meatloaf and chicken pieces. It is hot enough to brown the surface over the course of the cooking but slow enough for the center to catch up.

A oven, 400 to 450 °F, is for roasting vegetables, roasting a chicken with crisp skin, baking bread, and anything that should have dark edges and a crisp outside. Heat hits the surface fast and drives the Maillard reaction. A oven, 475 to 500 °F, is for pizza, flatbread and searing a roast before turning the oven down. At these settings, minutes matter, and food that is thin or sugary can burn while you turn your back.

Thickness decides how the bands play out. A thin sheet of cookies at 375 °F browns and sets in about 10 minutes. A thick loaf of meatloaf at the same temperature needs an hour, because heat must travel to the center by conduction. A large roast at 450 °F would be black outside before the center was safe, which is why big things go in cooler ovens for longer, or start hot and finish low. When you change a recipe’s pan size or piece size, expect to change the time and maybe the temperature too.

Two habits make ovens honest. First, an oven thermometer, because many home ovens run 25 °F or more off the dial. Second, the middle rack unless the recipe says otherwise; the top rack is closer to the broiler element and browns faster, and the bottom rack sits near the heating element and burns bottoms. Keep the door closed; every peek costs heat and time.

30.4

Braising a Tough Cut

Main ideaA braise is brown, soften, deglaze, submerge halfway, cover, and simmer low for hours, and each step is there for a reason.

Here is a full braise for a 3-pound beef chuck roast, feeding six to eight. Take the roast out of the package, pat it dry all over with paper towels, and season it with about 1 tsp of salt and some black pepper. Set a heavy lidded pot, such as a Dutch oven, over medium-high heat and add 2 Tbsp of oil. When the oil shimmers, lower the roast in away from you. Leave it alone 3 to 4 minutes, then turn it with tongs and brown every side. Do not rush; deep brown is the goal.

Move the roast to a plate. Add 1 chopped onion, 2 chopped carrots and 2 chopped celery stalks to the pot and cook, stirring, until soft and lightly browned, about 6 to 8 minutes. Add 2 cloves of chopped garlic for the last minute. Pour in 2 cups of broth and scrape the bottom with a wooden spoon until every brown bit lifts off; this is deglazing, and those bits carry most of the flavor. Return the roast and any juices from the plate. The liquid should come about halfway up the meat, so add a little more if needed.

Bring the liquid to a simmer, put on the lid, and move the pot to a 325 °F oven. The oven surrounds the pot with steady heat from all sides, which is easier than a stovetop burner that can scorch the bottom. Check once an hour: the liquid should show a few lazy bubbles, and if it is boiling hard, lower the oven 25 °F. At about 2 1/2 hours, test with a fork. When it slides in and twists the meat apart with no fight, it is done, usually 3 to 3 1/2 hours.

Move the meat to a board, tent it with foil, and let it rest 15 minutes. Skim the fat off the liquid with a spoon, or pour it into a measuring cup and let the fat rise, then spoon it off. Simmer the liquid a few minutes to thicken it, taste, and add salt if needed. Slice or shred the meat and serve it with the sauce. Cool leftovers within two hours and refrigerate; reheat to 165 °F. A braise is one of the most forgiving dishes there is, as long as it never boils and never runs dry.

Cut everything first and line it up in bowls in the order it will go in. For a chicken and vegetable stir-fry for four: 1 pound boneless chicken thighs sliced thin across the grain; 1 sliced onion; 2 cups broccoli florets; 1 sliced bell pepper; 2 cloves minced garlic and 1 Tbsp minced ginger; and a sauce of 3 Tbsp soy sauce, 1 Tbsp water and 1 tsp cornstarch stirred together.

You have just seared two pork chops and moved them to a plate to rest. Look at the pan: the bottom is dotted with dark brown, sticky bits, the , and it is the best flavor in the kitchen. Do not wash it. A pan sauce takes about three minutes and uses what is already there. It works for chops, chicken breasts, steaks and fish, and it is the fastest way to make a plain weeknight dinner taste like more.

Pour off all but about 1 Tbsp of the fat, keeping the fond. Set the pan over medium heat and add 1 Tbsp of finely chopped shallot or onion; cook it 30 seconds until soft. Pour in 1/2 cup of liquid: chicken broth, or half broth and half apple juice, or broth with a splash of vinegar. Pour it in away from you, because it will sputter. Scrape the bottom with a wooden spoon as it bubbles until every bit of fond has dissolved into the liquid. That is deglazing, the same move as in a braise, just faster.

Now : let the liquid boil until about half of it has cooked off, 1 to 2 minutes, and it looks slightly thicker and coats the spoon. Reducing concentrates flavor because the water leaves and the flavor stays. Add any juices that have collected on the plate under the resting meat. Take the pan off the heat and swirl in 1 Tbsp of cold butter, cut in pieces, until it melts into a glossy sauce. This is with butter; do it off the heat, because boiling the butter breaks the sauce and makes it greasy.

Taste, add a pinch of salt or a squeeze of lemon, and spoon it over the meat. The mistakes are few and clear. Black fond means the sear went too far, and the sauce will be bitter; wipe the pan and skip the sauce. Too much fat left in the pan makes a slick, greasy sauce. Skipping the reduction gives a thin, watery one. And adding the butter over a hot burner makes it separate. Three minutes, four steps, and a plain chop becomes dinner.

Section 3

Knowing When It Is Done

30.7

The Thermometer Decides

Main ideaColor, time and touch are guesses; a food thermometer in the thickest part, away from bone, is the only way to know meat is safe and not overcooked.

A chicken thigh can look done on the outside and be pink at the bone. A burger can be brown all the way through and still be under 160 °F, or gray and dry at 180 °F. Cooking times in recipes are averages for a certain thickness in a certain oven. The only measurement that tells you both safety and doneness is the internal temperature, and the tool is an instant-read , which costs about as much as a pound of steak and lasts for years.

To use it, push the probe into the thickest part of the food, away from bone, fat and the pan, since bone and metal read hot. Go in from the side for thin pieces such as chops and burgers so the tip sits in the center. Wait for the reading to settle, a few seconds on a digital model. Check more than one spot on a large roast. Wash the probe with hot soapy water between checks on raw and cooked food, and after every use.

The USDA safe minimums are the numbers to memorize. Poultry, whole or pieces or ground: 165 °F. Ground meats, beef, pork and lamb: 160 °F. Whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb and veal, such as steaks, chops and roasts: 145 °F, then rest 3 minutes before cutting. Fish: 145 °F, or until it flakes. Egg dishes: cooked until firm. Leftovers and casseroles reheated: 165 °F. Those temperatures kill the bacteria that cause food poisoning; below them, you are guessing.

A thermometer also saves food from overcooking. Meat keeps cooking after it leaves the heat, a few degrees for a chop and 5 to 10 for a large roast, so many cooks pull a roast a few degrees under the target and let the rest finish it. Test the thermometer’s accuracy now and then: in a glass of ice water with a little liquid, it should read 32 °F. If it does not, adjust it or replace it.

30.8

Resting Meat

Main ideaMeat rests for a few minutes after cooking so the temperature finishes rising and the juices settle, and cutting too soon leaves those juices on the board.

Slice a steak the second it comes off the grill and watch the board: a puddle of juice runs out, and the slices go dry and gray at the edges. Slice one that has rested 5 minutes and the juice stays in the meat. means moving cooked meat to a plate or board, tenting it loosely with foil, and waiting before cutting. During cooking, the outside of the meat is hotter and tighter than the center, and the juices get pushed toward the middle. Resting lets the heat even out and the fibers relax so the juices redistribute.

Resting also finishes the cooking. The hot outer layers keep passing heat inward after the meat leaves the pan, so the center temperature keeps climbing. This is about 3 to 5 °F for a chop or steak and 5 to 10 °F for a large roast. That is why the USDA includes a 3-minute rest in the 145 °F rule for whole cuts: the meat reaches and holds its safe temperature during the rest. Cooks aiming for a certain doneness pull the meat a few degrees early and let carryover land it.

How long to rest depends on size. A chop, a chicken thigh or a small steak: 3 to 5 minutes. A whole chicken: 10 to 15 minutes. A large roast or a turkey: 15 to 30 minutes. Tent, do not wrap; a tight wrap traps steam and turns a crisp crust soft. The meat will stay hot longer than you expect. Use the time to make the pan sauce or finish the vegetables.

Two cautions. Resting is for the finishing of cooked meat; it does not replace reaching a safe temperature, and it is not a rest on the counter for an hour. Cooked food should be served or refrigerated within two hours. And resting applies to meat, not to vegetables, pasta or fish, which are best served at once. Fish in particular goes from flaky to dry in a few minutes of waiting.

Section 4

Grains and Legumes

30.9

Cooking Rice and Other Grains

Main ideaGrains cook by soaking up a measured amount of simmering water under a lid, so the ratio, the low heat and the closed lid matter more than anything else.

Rice is the side dish half the world eats every day, and the method is simple if the ratio is right. For long-grain white rice: measure 1 cup of rice into a fine strainer and rinse it under cold water until the water runs mostly clear, which washes off surface starch that would make it gummy. Put it in a pot with 2 cups of water and 1/2 tsp of salt. Bring to a boil over high heat, stir once, then turn the heat to the lowest setting, cover tightly, and simmer 18 minutes without lifting the lid. Turn off the heat and let it stand, still covered, 5 to 10 minutes. Fluff with a fork.

The lid is the whole trick. The rice is not boiling in a big pot of water; it is absorbing a measured amount of water and steam. Lift the lid and steam escapes, the water runs short, and the bottom scorches while the top is crunchy. Stir it during cooking and the grains release starch and clump. If the pot is too thin, the bottom burns at even the lowest heat; a heavy pot or a flame tamer fixes that. Rice on the bottom that is stuck but not burnt is called the crust and some cooks fight over it.

Other grains follow the same pattern with different ratios and times, so a chart is worth keeping. Brown rice: 1 cup rice, 2 1/2 cups water, about 45 minutes, because the bran layer slows the water down. Quinoa: 1 cup, 2 cups water, 15 minutes, rinsed first to remove its bitter coating. Oatmeal from rolled oats: 1 cup oats, 2 cups water, 5 minutes stirred. Pasta and couscous are different: pasta boils in a large pot of salted water and is drained, and couscous is simply soaked in an equal amount of boiling water for 5 minutes.

Cooked rice has one safety rule that surprises people. Rice can carry a bacterium whose spores survive cooking, and if cooked rice sits at room temperature it can produce toxins that reheating will not destroy. So treat rice like any leftover: cool it quickly, refrigerate within two hours at 40 °F or below, use it within a few days, and reheat it to 165 °F. Rice left in the cooker overnight is not a leftover; it is trash.

30.10

Cooking Dried Beans

Main ideaDried beans need a soak, a long gentle simmer with salt, and, for kidney beans, a hard ten-minute boil first; done right they cost a fraction of canned.

A one-pound bag of dried beans makes about 6 cups cooked, the same as roughly four cans, for a fraction of the price (example: a $1.50 bag against $1.00 or more per can). The trade is time. Pour the beans onto a sheet pan and pick out any stones or shriveled ones, then rinse. them in a large bowl covered by three inches of cold water for 8 hours or overnight; they will double in size. Or use the quick soak: cover with water, boil 2 minutes, turn off the heat, and let stand 1 hour. Drain either way.

Put the soaked beans in a pot with fresh water to cover by two inches, add 1 tsp of salt and, if you like, a bay leaf, half an onion or a clove of garlic. Bring to a boil, then lower to a gentle simmer and partly cover. Skim any foam in the first minutes. Check every 20 minutes and add hot water if the beans show above the surface. Most beans take 1 to 2 hours; older beans take longer. They are done when several beans in a row are creamy all the way through with no chalky center.

One kind of bean has a special rule. Raw or undercooked red kidney beans contain a natural toxin that causes severe stomach illness, and a slow cooker does not get hot enough to destroy it. After soaking, boil kidney beans hard, at a full rolling boil, for at least 10 minutes before you turn them down to simmer. That step, from the FDA’s guidance, applies to red kidney beans in particular, and it is a good habit for all dried beans.

Lentils and split peas are the shortcut: no soak, 20 to 30 minutes at a simmer. Beans do not get tender in a hard boil, which bursts the skins, and they cook slowly if the water is very hard or if a lot of acid, such as tomatoes, goes in early; add tomatoes near the end. Cool cooked beans in their liquid within two hours, refrigerate up to about four days, or freeze in 2-cup portions, which is one can’s worth, ready for a soup or a pot of chili.
