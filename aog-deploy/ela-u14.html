Writing Across Forms

Writing

A teacher writes one sentence on the board, erases it, and writes it again, five times, until it finally argues something. Across the hall a student writes his grandmother's kitchen as a paragraph, then as a poem, and finds that the poem knows something the paragraph did not. Both of them are doing the same thing: taking what is in their heads and shaping it so a stranger can follow it and feel it.

This unit is about the shapes writing can take. First the essay: a thesis that takes a real position, paragraphs that each prove one point, evidence that is introduced and explained instead of dropped, a voice that sounds fair and sure, and the honest work of rewriting. You will also learn the informative shapes, compare and contrast, cause and effect, and definition, and how to write well when a clock is running.

Then the story and the poem: openings that pull, dialogue that sounds like people, pacing that speeds and slows, details from all five senses, a point of view chosen on purpose, and an ending that is earned. You will write a true story and a made-up one, then poems, with attention to line breaks, images and sound, and you will meet the haiku, free verse and the sonnet. At the end, you will read your work aloud and gather it into a class book.

The big question

How does the form you choose, essay, story or poem, change what your writing can say?

Big questionHow do you turn an opinion into an essay that a careful reader has to take seriously?
The story

Five Tries at One Sentence

A teacher writes a thesis on the board, erases it, and writes it again until it finally says something worth arguing.

Ms. Alvarez wrote one sentence on the board and stepped back. "Cell phones are a big topic in schools today." She asked the class what the sentence argued. Nobody answered, because it did not argue anything. It was true, and it was empty. You could not disagree with it, so you could not write an essay about it. She erased it.

Try two: "Cell phones are bad." Now at least someone could disagree. But a boy in the back row asked, bad for what? Bad for whom? Bad how? The sentence was a shrug with a period. She erased it. Try three: "Schools should ban cell phones because they are distracting." Better. It took a side and gave a reason. But the class had read three articles that week, and two of them said phones could help students look things up. The thesis ignored what the class actually knew.

Try four: "Schools should limit cell phone use during class because phones pull attention away from learning, even when students believe they are multitasking." Now the room got quiet in a different way. That sentence made a promise. It said what the essay would prove, and it hinted at the evidence, the studies about multitasking. Someone could argue against it. Someone would have to work to argue against it.

Try five was almost the same, with one change. Ms. Alvarez replaced "limit" with "require students to store," because "limit" was vague and the essay needed a clear policy to defend. Then she put the chalk down. Five tries, one sentence, twenty minutes. "This is the job," she said. "Not the five paragraphs. This. If the sentence argues, the essay can argue. If it does not, nothing you stack under it will help."

That is what this chapter is about: a thesis that argues, paragraphs built to support it, evidence that is explained instead of dropped, a voice that sounds like it means business, and the messy, honest process of rewriting until it works. You will also learn the shapes of informative essays, which explain rather than argue, and how to do all of this when the clock is running.

Talk about itThink of an opinion you hold strongly. Could you write it as one sentence that someone else could argue against? What would they say?
Section 1

The Thesis

32.1

A Claim You Can Argue

Main ideaA thesis is one sentence that takes a position a reasonable person could disagree with, and the whole essay exists to support it.

Read these two sentences. One: Chicago has many parks. Two: Chicago should turn more of its vacant lots into small neighborhood parks. The first is a fact. Nobody will argue with it, so there is nothing for an essay to do. The second is a . Someone could say no, the city should sell those lots to builders instead. That disagreement is what gives an argument essay a job.

A is the main claim of an essay, usually stated in one sentence near the end of the introduction. A good thesis does three things. It takes a side. It is specific enough to be proved or disproved. And it is , which means a thoughtful reader could push back. If nobody could push back, you have a topic or a fact, not a thesis.

Here is a quick test. Put the words "I believe that" in front of your sentence and then imagine someone answering, "I disagree, because..." If you can hear a real answer, you have a thesis. "I believe that Chicago has many parks." "I disagree, because..." Nothing comes. "I believe that Chicago should turn vacant lots into parks." "I disagree, because the city needs the money from selling them." That is a thesis.

One more thing. A thesis is not a question and not an announcement. "Should the school day start later?" asks; it does not claim. "This essay will discuss school start times" announces; it does not claim. "The school day should start an hour later because teenagers’ bodies are wired to sleep late" claims. Write that kind of sentence.

Think it through
Three Sentences on the Board

A. Homework is something most students have. B. Should schools give less homework? C. Middle schools should cap homework at thirty minutes a night because more than that cuts into sleep without raising grades.

Which sentence is a thesis? What is wrong with each of the other two? Rewrite B so that it claims instead of asks.

claim
a statement that takes a position and can be supported or challenged with reasons
thesis
the main claim of an essay, stated in one sentence, that the rest of the essay supports
debatable

Check yourself

1. Which sentence is a debatable thesis?

Why: Only the first sentence takes a side that a reader could argue against. The others state a fact, announce a topic, or ask a question.

2. Why is 'Chicago has many parks' a weak thesis?

Why: A thesis must be debatable. A plain fact gives the essay no position to defend.

3. What does the 'I believe that... I disagree, because...' test check?

Why: If you can imagine a real counterargument, the sentence is debatable, which is what a thesis needs to be.
32.2

Making a Thesis Specific

Main ideaA strong thesis names a precise position and hints at the reasons, so the reader knows exactly what the essay promises to prove.

"Social media is bad for teenagers." It takes a side. It is debatable. And it is still a weak thesis, because it is . Bad how? Which teenagers? Which parts of social media? The writer of that sentence has not decided what the essay will actually prove, so the essay will wander.

Compare: "Late-night scrolling harms teenagers mainly by cutting into sleep, and schools should teach students to set phone curfews." Now the reader knows the problem (lost sleep), the cause (late-night scrolling), and the position (schools should teach phone curfews). The sentence is longer, but every extra word narrows the promise. That is what means: the reader can picture what the body paragraphs will contain.

A useful shape for a first draft is claim plus because. "X should happen because A and B." The "because" part previews your reasons, which will become your body paragraphs. You do not have to keep that exact shape in the final draft, but it forces you to decide on your reasons before you start writing. Many weak essays are weak because the writer never made that decision.

Ms. Alvarez rewrote her thesis five times. Each version got narrower. That is normal. Expect your first thesis to be a rough guess at what you think. Write the essay, find out what you can actually prove, and then go back and rewrite the thesis to match. The final thesis is often the last sentence you fix.

Section 2

Building the Essay

32.3

The Introduction

Main ideaAn introduction earns the reader's attention, gives the background they need, and ends with the thesis.

Read this opening. "On a Tuesday in March, a seventh grader in Rockford fell asleep in second period for the third time that week. She was not lazy. She had been awake until one in the morning, and school started at 7:30." Then, two sentences later: "Middle schools should start no earlier than 8:30, because teenagers’ sleep cycles shift later and early starts cost them learning." That is an doing its three jobs in order.

Job one is the , the first sentence or two that makes a reader want the next one. A hook can be a brief scene, a surprising fact, a sharp question, or a short quotation. It should connect to your argument, not just grab attention. A hook about sharks in an essay about sleep is a trick, and readers do not like tricks.

Job two is : the few facts a reader needs before the thesis will make sense. For the sleep essay, that might be what time local schools start and what doctors say about teenage sleep. Keep it short. Two or three sentences usually do it. The body paragraphs will carry the detail.

Job three is the thesis, and in most school essays it comes last in the introduction. That placement is not a rule of nature, but it works: the reader arrives at your claim with the background already in hand, and the body paragraphs can begin immediately. Hook, background, thesis. Learn the order, and later you can bend it.

Think it through
Four Hooks for One Essay

An essay argues that the school should add a second lunch period. Hook A: "Lunch is an important part of the day." Hook B: "At 11:52 the line is forty students long, and the bell rings at 12:15." Hook C: "Have you ever wondered about lunch?" Hook D: "Sharks can smell blood from far away."

Rank the four hooks from best to worst and explain each ranking. What does the best one do that the others do not?

Words to know
introduction
the opening paragraph of an essay, which hooks the reader, gives background, and states the thesis
hook
the first sentence or two, written to make the reader want to keep reading
background
the basic facts a reader needs before the thesis will make sense
Check yourself

1. In what order do the three parts of a standard introduction usually appear?

Why: The hook draws the reader in, background prepares them, and the thesis lands last so the body can begin.

2. Why is a hook about sharks a bad choice for an essay about school start times?

Why: A hook must lead toward the thesis. Attention grabbed for no reason is wasted and annoys the reader.

3. How much background should an introduction give?

Why: Background is brief. Detail belongs in the body paragraphs.
32.4

Body Paragraphs and Topic Sentences

Main ideaEach body paragraph makes one point that supports the thesis, states it in a topic sentence, and then proves it.

Look at the first sentence of a body paragraph from the sleep essay: "Early start times hurt learning because tired students remember less of what they hear." That sentence is a . It states the single point of the paragraph, and it clearly connects to the thesis. A reader who saw only the topic sentences of an essay should be able to follow the whole argument.

A is the essay’s working unit. Each one has a job: prove one reason from your thesis. Inside it, a simple pattern holds up well. Topic sentence. Evidence, such as a fact, a study, an example, or a quotation. Explanation of how that evidence proves the point. Sometimes a second piece of evidence and a second explanation. Then a sentence that closes the point or leads to the next paragraph.

The most common problem is a paragraph that tries to do two jobs. "Early starts hurt learning, and also buses would run better later." Those are two reasons, so they need two paragraphs. When you find an "and also" in a topic sentence, split it. A second common problem is a topic sentence that is just a fact: "Many schools start at 7:30." A fact does not argue. Make the sentence claim something.

Order matters too. Put your strongest reason either first or last, where readers pay the most attention. A middle paragraph can carry a weaker reason. And use between paragraphs, words or phrases like "in addition," "more important," or "even so," so the reader knows how each new point relates to the last one.

Early start times hurt learning because tired students remember less. (topic) In one study, students who slept less than seven hours scored lower on a memory test the next morning. (evidence) That gap shows up in class as forgotten instructions and repeated lessons. (explanation) A later bell would give those students the sleep their memory depends on. (close)

1. What should a topic sentence do?

Why: A topic sentence names the paragraph's one point and ties it to the thesis.

2. A topic sentence reads: 'Uniforms save families money, and also they reduce bullying.' What is the problem?

32.5

The Conclusion

Main ideaA conclusion restates the argument in fresh words and shows the reader why it matters beyond the essay.

Here is a weak conclusion: "In conclusion, schools should start later because of sleep and learning. Thank you for reading." It repeats the thesis with fewer words and then apologizes for existing. Readers can feel the writer running for the exit. A should feel like the writer stopped on purpose.

A conclusion has two jobs. First, remind the reader of the argument, but in new words and with the weight of the evidence behind it. You are not introducing the claim now; you are confirming it. "By 8:30 a seventh grader is awake, fed and able to hold a lesson in her head. By 7:30 she is not. The bell should follow the body." Same claim, different sentences, more confidence.

Second, answer the question so what. Why should the reader care now that the essay is over? You might name what happens if nothing changes, suggest one concrete next step, or connect the argument to something larger. "A later bell will not fix every problem in a middle school. But it fixes one, for every student, every morning." That is a , and it is where the reader decides whether the essay was worth it.

Avoid three habits. Do not add a brand-new reason; if it was important, it needed a body paragraph. Do not begin with "In conclusion"; the reader knows where they are. And do not end with a question you never answered. A conclusion is a landing, not a takeoff.

Think it through
Two Endings

Ending A: "In conclusion, the city should add more bus routes for the reasons I said. That is my opinion." Ending B: "A bus that comes every ten minutes changes what a neighborhood can be. It decides whether a nurse makes her shift and whether a student makes first period. The city can keep printing maps of routes that do not exist, or it can send the buses."

What does Ending B do that Ending A does not? Point to the sentence that answers 'so what.' Then rewrite Ending A in your own fresh words.

Words to know
conclusion
the final paragraph of an essay, which confirms the argument and shows why it matters
closing thought
the last idea in a conclusion, which answers 'so what' and gives the reader a reason to care
Check yourself

1. What are the two jobs of a conclusion?

Why: A conclusion reminds the reader of the claim with new wording and then answers 'so what.'

2. Why should a conclusion not introduce a new reason?

Why: A reason dropped in at the end has no evidence or explanation behind it and weakens the essay.

3. Which sentence is a closing thought that answers 'so what'?

Why: That sentence tells the reader why the argument matters now. The others repeat, ask, or state a bare fact.
Section 3

Evidence and Voice

32.6

Bringing in Evidence and Quotations

Main ideaEvidence must be introduced, quoted or summarized accurately, and credited, so the reader knows where it came from and why it is there.

Read this: "Students need sleep. ’Teenagers need eight to ten hours a night.’ So schools should start later." The quotation in the middle appears from nowhere. Who said it? Why should we trust them? It is a , and readers stumble over it. Now read: "According to the pediatrician who wrote the school’s health guide, ’Teenagers need eight to ten hours a night.’" Same words, but now the reader knows the source and can weigh it.

There are three ways to bring into an essay. You can quote, using the exact words inside quotation marks. You can , restating the idea in your own words at about the same length. Or you can summarize, boiling a long passage down to its main point. Quote when the exact wording matters. Paraphrase or summarize when only the idea matters. Most essays should use far more paraphrase than quotation.

Whichever you choose, do three things. Introduce the source with a such as "According to," "The report found," or "As Douglass writes." Report it accurately; changing a quotation’s words or a study’s numbers is a kind of lying. And credit it, so the reader can find it. In a school essay, that means naming the author or source in the sentence or in parentheses after it.

Length matters. A quotation should be as short as it can be while still making the point. If you find yourself quoting three sentences, ask which phrase actually matters and quote only that. Frederick Douglass, in his 1845 autobiography, wrote the line "You have seen how a man was made a slave; you shall see how a slave was made a man." You would quote that exact sentence, because its shape is the point. You would paraphrase the paragraph around it.

A Sentence Worth Quoting
You have seen how a man was made a slave; you shall see how a slave was made a man.

Why: A dropped quotation appears without a signal phrase or credit, so the reader cannot tell where it came from or why it is there.

2. When should you quote instead of paraphrase?

32.7

Explaining Evidence

Main ideaEvidence does not speak for itself; after every piece of evidence, the writer must explain how it proves the point.

A student writes: "Schools should start later. A study found that students at a school that moved its start time to 8:30 had fewer car crashes." Then the paragraph ends. The evidence is good. But the student has left the reader to do the thinking. What do car crashes have to do with learning? Why did the crashes drop? The paragraph is a sandwich with no filling.

is the sentence or two that follows evidence and connects it to the claim. It answers the question, "So what does this prove?" For the crash study: "Fewer crashes means the students were more alert, and an alert student behind the wheel at 7:45 is also an alert student in a classroom at 8:00. The same sleep that prevents a crash lets a lesson stick." Now the evidence is doing work for the argument.

A useful habit is to follow every piece of evidence with the phrase "This shows that" in your draft, and then finish the sentence. Later you can cut the phrase itself. If you cannot finish the sentence, the evidence does not fit your point, and you should find better evidence or change the point. Claim, evidence, reasoning: that three-part rhythm is the skeleton of every good body paragraph.

Reasoning is also where you deal with the other side. Naming a , the strongest objection to your claim, and answering it fairly, makes a reader trust you. "Some argue that a later start would push after-school jobs and sports later too. That is true, and it is a real cost. But an hour of lost practice time does not compare to a year of lessons half-heard." That is not weakness. That is confidence.

Think it through
Finish the Sentence

Claim: The school should plant trees along the front parking lot. Evidence: In a survey, 62 percent of students said the lot is the hottest place on campus in May and September. This shows that...

Finish the reasoning sentence so the evidence proves the claim. Then write one counterargument and a fair answer to it.

Words to know
reasoning
the explanation that connects evidence to a claim and shows why the evidence proves it
counterargument
the strongest objection to your claim, which a careful writer names and answers
Check yourself

1. What does reasoning do in a body paragraph?

Why: Reasoning is the link between evidence and claim. Without it, the reader has to guess why the evidence is there.

2. If you cannot finish 'This shows that...' after a piece of evidence, what should you do?

Why: Evidence that cannot be connected to the claim is not supporting it. Either the evidence or the point must change.

3. Why does answering a counterargument make an essay stronger?

Why: Readers trust a writer who names the best objection and answers it fairly.
32.8

Formal Style and Objective Tone

Main ideaArgument and informative essays use a formal style and an objective tone so the reader focuses on the evidence, not the writer's feelings.

Two sentences, same idea. One: "Honestly the cafeteria food is so gross, everybody hates it, I mean come on." Two: "In a school survey, 71 percent of students rated the cafeteria food below average." The first sounds like a text message. The second sounds like someone worth listening to. The difference is not intelligence. It is and .

A means the language of writing rather than the language of hallway talk. Use complete sentences. Avoid slang and contractions like "gonna" or "can’t" in a final draft. Choose precise words: not "a lot of kids," but "most seventh graders" or "seventy percent of students." Do not write "I think" or "in my opinion" before every claim; the essay is your opinion, so just state the claim.

An means you sound fair, even when you are arguing hard. Objective does not mean without a position. It means your position rests on evidence rather than on how loudly you feel. Words like "gross," "stupid," "obviously" and "everybody knows" tell the reader you are upset; they do not tell the reader you are right. Cut them and let the numbers, examples and reasoning carry the weight.

Formal does not mean stiff or fancy. "The aforementioned nutritional deficiencies necessitate reform" is formal and terrible. "The lunch menu needs to change, and the survey shows why" is formal and clear. Aim for the voice of a calm expert explaining something to a smart stranger. That voice is not something you are born with. It is something you practice.

Section 4

Informative Forms and the Process

32.9

Compare, Contrast, Cause and Effect

Main ideaInformative essays explain rather than argue, and two of their most useful shapes are compare-and-contrast and cause-and-effect.

Not every essay argues. An explains something the reader wants to understand: how a process works, how two things differ, why something happened. It still needs a controlling idea in one sentence, but that sentence promises understanding rather than a fight. "Chicago’s two baseball parks reflect two different ideas about what a ballpark is for" is informative. It sets up an explanation, not a debate.

The compare-and-contrast essay explains two subjects by their likenesses and differences. It has two common shapes. In the block shape, you cover everything about subject A, then everything about subject B. In the point-by-point shape, you take one feature at a time: age of the park, then neighborhood, then seating, and cover both subjects under each. Point-by-point is usually clearer for readers, because they never have to hold subject A in memory while reading about B.

The cause-and-effect essay explains why something happens or what follows from it. The 1871 Chicago fire is a classic subject. Causes: a long dry summer, a city built mostly of wood, a strong wind from the southwest. Effects: about a third of the city destroyed, new building codes, and a rebuilt downtown that became a laboratory for tall buildings. The essay can move from causes to the event, or from the event to its effects, or both.

Two warnings. First, do not confuse before with because. The fire came after a dry summer, and the dry summer helped cause it; but not everything that comes before something causes it. Second, in compare-and-contrast, do not just list. "A is old; B is new. A is north; B is south." A list is not an explanation. Tell the reader what the differences add up to. That is the controlling idea doing its job.

Think it through
Two Outlines for the Same Essay

Block: Paragraph 2, all about walking to school. Paragraph 3, all about taking the bus. Point-by-point: Paragraph 2, time (walking vs. bus). Paragraph 3, cost (walking vs. bus). Paragraph 4, what you notice along the way (walking vs. bus).

Which outline makes it easier for a reader to compare the two? When might the block shape be the better choice?

Words to know
informative essay
an essay that explains a subject clearly rather than arguing a position
compare and contrast
an essay shape that explains two subjects through their similarities and differences
cause and effect
an essay shape that explains why something happens or what results from it
Check yourself

1. How does an informative essay differ from an argument essay?

Why: Informative writing aims at understanding; argument aims at persuasion. Both use evidence and a controlling idea.

2. In a point-by-point compare-and-contrast essay, how are paragraphs organized?

Why: Point-by-point takes each feature in turn and compares both subjects under it, so readers never lose track.

3. 'The fire happened after the summer, so the summer caused the fire.' What mistake is this?

Why: Coming before something is not the same as causing it. A cause-and-effect essay must show the actual link.
32.10

Defining a Term

Main ideaA definition essay explains what a word or idea really means by placing it in a class, separating it from its neighbors, and showing examples.

What is courage? A dictionary gives you a line. A gives you the idea itself: what it includes, what it does not, and what it looks like in a real life. Writers choose this form for words people use loosely, such as courage, friendship, fairness, or home. The essay’s controlling idea is the writer’s own careful definition.

A strong definition has three moves. First, put the term in a : courage is a kind of choice. Second, tell what separates it from other members of that class: it is a choice made while afraid, not one made without fear. Third, give and non-examples. A student who reads her poem aloud with her hands shaking shows courage. A student who is not scared of anything and jumps off a diving board does not, at least not by this definition; that is boldness, or maybe just fun.

The examples are where the essay comes alive. Abstract words stay abstract until a reader sees a person doing something. Choose two or three examples that show different sides of the term: courage in a hospital, courage in a friendship, courage in front of a class. Each example becomes a body paragraph, with its own topic sentence explaining which part of the definition it shows.

One trap to avoid: do not open with "The dictionary defines courage as..." Readers have seen that opening a thousand times, and it hands your job to someone else. Start with a scene, a question, or a case that does not quite fit the usual meaning. Then earn your own definition across the essay.

Real Revision

Main ideaRevision means re-seeing the whole essay and changing what it says and how it is built, not just fixing commas.

It asks bigger questions. Does the thesis still match what the essay proves? Is any body paragraph doing two jobs? Is there evidence with no reasoning after it? Would the essay be stronger if paragraph four came second?

The is usually described in stages: plan, draft, revise, edit, publish. Real writers loop through those stages more than once. Ms. Alvarez rewrote her thesis five times before the class had drafted anything; that was revision happening during planning. You might draft a body paragraph, realize your thesis is wrong, and go back. That is not failure. That is the process working.

Here is a revision routine that catches the big problems. Read the essay aloud; your ear will catch what your eye skips. Then write each topic sentence on a separate line and read only those. If the list does not make an argument, the essay does not either. Then find every quotation or statistic and check that a sentence of reasoning follows it. Then, and only then, fix the commas.

Real revision often means cutting. A paragraph you liked may not fit the thesis you ended up with. Cut it and save it somewhere; it may fit another essay. Writers who cannot cut end up with essays that are long and weak. Writers who can cut end up with essays that are short and strong. Nobody ever complained that an argument was too clear.

Writing Under a Time Limit

Main ideaWhen the clock is running, spend the first minutes planning a thesis and three reasons, and save the last minutes to check what you wrote.

The prompt appears, the timer starts, and a student begins writing the first sentence that comes to mind. Twenty minutes later she has a page and a half that argues two different things. Writing , as on a test or a timed class assignment, rewards planning more than any other kind of writing, because there is no second draft. The plan is the draft.

Use a rough budget. In a forty-minute essay, spend the first five minutes reading the twice, underlining what it actually asks, and writing a thesis and three reasons on scratch paper. Spend thirty minutes drafting, one paragraph per reason, with one piece of evidence and one sentence of reasoning in each. Spend the last five minutes reading what you wrote and fixing anything unclear. Students who skip the first five minutes almost always lose more than five minutes wandering.

Read the prompt like a contract. If it says "argue," take a side. If it says "explain," do not. If it says "use evidence from the passage," quote or paraphrase the passage, and name where the evidence came from. Many low scores come from good writing that answered a different question. Underline the verb in the prompt and check your thesis against it before you write anything else.

When time runs short, protect the shape. A finished essay with three short body paragraphs beats an unfinished one with two long ones. If you have two minutes left and no conclusion, write two sentences: restate the claim in new words, and say why it matters. Then stop. Readers forgive a plain ending. They do not forgive a missing one.

The Kitchen, Twice

One student writes the same memory as a paragraph and then as a poem, and discovers that the shape changes what the memory means.

Jalen's assignment was a memory, any memory, told in prose. He picked his grandmother's kitchen on the South Side, Sunday mornings, before she moved to the nursing home. He wrote: "My grandma's kitchen was small and yellow. She made greens and cornbread every Sunday. The radio was always on, playing old songs, and she sang along even when she didn't know the words. I sat at the table and did my homework and she would check it without looking like she was checking it. I miss that kitchen."

It was fine. It was true. His teacher, Mr. Okafor, read it and said, "I believe you. But I am not there yet. What did the greens smell like? What song? What did her hands look like when she pretended not to check your math?" Jalen went back and added: "The greens smelled like vinegar and smoke. The radio played the same Aretha Franklin record every week, and she got the words wrong on purpose to make me laugh. Her hands were dry and quick, and she would tap the wrong answer twice with one finger and walk away."

Now the reader was in the room. That is what narrative does: it puts a stranger somewhere they have never been and lets them stay a while. Then Mr. Okafor gave a second assignment. Same memory, as a poem. No rules about rhyme. Just lines. Jalen fought it for a day. Then he wrote: "Yellow kitchen, Sunday. / Vinegar and smoke. / Her hands, dry and quick, / tapping the wrong answer twice, / then walking away, / then Aretha, wrong on purpose, / then me, laughing, / then the answer, right."

Something happened in the poem that had not happened in the paragraph. The line breaks slowed everything down. "Then walking away" sat alone on its line, and suddenly it was about more than a kitchen. It was about a grandmother who was, in fact, walking away, slowly, into a place he could not follow. Jalen had not planned that. The form found it for him.

This chapter teaches both shapes. First, narrative: how to open, how to make people talk, how to speed up and slow down, how to see with all five senses, how to choose who tells the story, and how to end it so the ending feels earned. Then poetry: line breaks, images, sound, and three forms you can try. At the end, you will read your work aloud and gather it with your classmates' into a book. The kitchen is waiting. So is yours.

Telling a Story

33.1

Openings That Pull

Main ideaA strong narrative opening drops the reader into a specific moment and raises a question they want answered.

Two openings for the same story. One: "It was a normal day and I woke up and got ready for school." Two: "The bus was already pulling away when I saw my little brother’s face in the back window, and I was still on the porch in socks." The first could begin ten thousand stories. The second can only begin one, and you want to know what happens next.

A narrative has one job: make the reader need the second sentence. The most reliable way is to start inside a specific moment, with a person doing something, and to raise a small question. Where is the bus going? Why is the narrator on the porch? You do not have to explain anything yet. Explanation can wait. The reader will follow you a long way if you give them a reason to be curious.

Three openings to avoid. Waking up in the morning, which delays the story. Weather, unless the weather is the story. And a definition or a big general statement, such as "Family is the most important thing." Those are ways of clearing your throat. Cut them and begin where the story actually begins, which is usually later than your first draft thinks.

Mark Twain opened a novel in 1884 with a boy speaking straight to the reader: "You don’t know about me without you have read a book by the name of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer; but that ain’t no matter." In one sentence you hear his voice, his grammar, and his attitude. That is , Latin for "in the middle of things," in a small way: the boy is already talking, and you have to catch up.

Dialogue That Sounds Real

Main ideaGood dialogue sounds like real speech, is punctuated correctly, and does work: it shows character, moves the story, or raises tension.

"Hello, my brother. I see that you have missed the bus this morning because you were not ready on time." Nobody talks like that. Real speech is short, interrupted and full of things left unsaid. Compare: "You missed it." "I know." "Mom’s gonna—" "I know." That exchange tells you both boys’ moods and the whole shape of the morning in nine words.

has to earn its place. Each line should do at least one of three things: reveal who a character is, move the story forward, or raise tension. A line that does none of those, such as "Hi." "Hi." "How are you?" "Fine," should go. Real conversations have those parts; stories skip them.

The mechanics matter, because bad punctuation makes readers stop and reread. Put quotation marks around only the spoken words. Start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes. Put a comma, question mark or exclamation point inside the closing quotation mark before a like "she said": "You missed it," she said. Not: "You missed it." She said. And use "said" most of the time. "Exclaimed," "uttered" and "opined" call attention to themselves; "said" disappears, which is what you want.

You can cut tags entirely once the reader knows who is talking. In a two-person scene, alternating paragraphs are enough. Add a small action instead of a tag when you want the reader to see something: "You missed it." She did not look up from her phone. That action tag tells you more than "she said coldly" ever could.

33.3

Speeding Up and Slowing Down

Main ideaPacing is how fast a story moves; short sentences and skipped time speed it up, while long sentences and close detail slow it down.

Read this: "The door banged open. Nobody moved. Then everybody did." Three sentences, nine words. Your eye races. Now read this: "The kitchen held the last of the afternoon light, and the radio on the windowsill played a song my grandmother had known for sixty years." One sentence, twenty-five words. Your eye slows down and looks around. That difference is .

Writers control pacing with two dials. The first is sentence length. Short sentences feel fast and tense; long sentences feel slow and thoughtful. The second is how much time a passage covers. "The summer passed" moves three months in three words. Two pages about one minute in a hallway stretch time until the reader can hardly breathe. A story needs both settings, and the switch between them is where the reader feels the shape.

The rule of thumb: speed through what does not matter, and slow down for what does. The drive to the hospital is fast. The moment in the doorway of the room is slow. Many first drafts do the opposite, spending a page on breakfast and one sentence on the fight. When you revise, find the moment the story is really about and give it the most words. That is where belongs, with dialogue and detail; the rest can be .

You can also use a paragraph break as a brake. A one-line paragraph after a long one lands like a dropped plate. Try it: end a long paragraph of description with the short line, "Then the phone rang." Set it alone. The reader stops. Now the story can turn.

Senses and Precise Verbs

Main ideaSensory details and exact verbs let the reader experience a scene instead of being told about it.

"The kitchen was nice." "The greens smelled like vinegar and smoke." The first sentence asks you to trust the writer. The second sentence puts a smell in your nose. That is a : something the reader can see, hear, smell, taste or touch. Most first drafts use only sight. The other four senses are where a scene becomes a place.

Try this on any paragraph you have written. Underline every detail and label its sense. If every underline says "sight," add one sound and one smell. Sound might be the radio, the refrigerator hum, the tap of a finger on a wrong answer. Smell might be vinegar, bleach, rain on a hot sidewalk. You do not need all five senses in every paragraph. You need more than one.

Verbs do the same work in less space. "She walked across the kitchen" is a report. "She shuffled" tells you she is tired or old. "She marched" tells you she is angry. "She drifted" tells you she is thinking of something else. A replaces a weak verb plus an adverb: not "walked slowly" but "shuffled," not "said loudly" but "shouted." Hunt through your draft for "walked," "went," "looked" and "was," and ask each one whether a sharper verb could take its place.

One caution. Precise is not the same as fancy. "Perambulated" is not better than "walked"; it is worse, because it makes the reader think about the writer instead of the scene. The best verb is the one that shows exactly what happened and then gets out of the way. "Tapped the wrong answer twice" is plain, and it is perfect.

33.5

Who Tells It

Main ideaChoosing first or third person, and how much the narrator knows, changes what the reader can see and whom they trust.

Same event, two tellings. First: "I stood on the porch in my socks and watched the bus go, and I told myself it was not my fault." Third: "Jalen stood on the porch in his socks and watched the bus go. He told himself it was not his fault. His brother, in the back window, did not wave." The first version gives you the inside of one head. The second lets you see something the boy cannot: his brother’s face.

is the position from which a story is told. In , a character tells it using "I," and the reader knows only what that character knows, thinks and chooses to admit. That closeness is powerful, and it is also limited. If the narrator does not see the brother’s face, neither do you. A first-person narrator can even be wrong about things, and part of the reader’s job is to notice.

In , the narrator stands outside and uses "he," "she" or "they." A third-person narrator can stay close to one character, seeing only what that person sees, or can move between characters and know what each one thinks. The closer version feels almost like first person with a little more room. The wider version lets you show two people misunderstanding each other, which first person cannot do.

Which should you choose? Ask what the story needs the reader to know, and when. A story about a secret works well in first person, because the narrator can hold the secret back. A story about a misunderstanding between two people works better in third, so the reader can see both sides. Personal narratives are almost always first person, because they happened to you. Fiction is a real choice. Try a page both ways before you decide.

Reflection and the Earned Ending

Main ideaA narrative ends well when something has changed and the narrator briefly shows what it meant, without a tacked-on moral.

Jalen’s first draft ended: "I miss that kitchen." It is honest, and it is a stop, not an ending. Compare his last line in the poem: "then the answer, right." The kitchen is still gone. But the last line shows what the kitchen gave him, and the reader feels the loss without being told to. That is the difference between an ending that is tacked on and one that is .

An earned ending grows out of what came before. Something has changed: the narrator knows something, lost something, or sees something differently. The ending does not have to be happy. It has to be true to the story. If the story was about a missed bus, the ending is not "and then we had a great day anyway." It is what the narrator understood, standing on the porch in socks, about his brother, or himself.

is the narrator’s brief look back at the meaning of the events. In a personal narrative, it often comes in the last paragraph, in a sentence or two. The trap is the , a lesson pinned on the end like a tag: "And that is why you should always be ready on time." Readers pull away from morals. They lean toward reflection that stays inside the moment: "I still see his face in the window. He was not angry. That was the worst part."

Two tests for your ending. First, cover the last paragraph and read the story. If it feels complete without it, your ending was probably explaining what the story already showed; cut it or shorten it. Second, read only the first and last paragraphs. Something should have moved between them. If the narrator is in exactly the same place, the story has not happened yet.

Writing a Personal Narrative

Main ideaA personal narrative tells one true moment from your life in scene, with detail and reflection, and its truth is in the details, not the drama.

Students often think a needs a big event: a championship, an accident, a move across the country. It does not. Jalen’s kitchen had no plot. A grandmother checked homework. What made it work was that he chose one small moment and told it completely, with the smell, the record, the finger tapping twice. A true small moment beats a vague big one every time.

Start by choosing a moment, not a topic. "My grandmother" is a topic. "The Sunday she pretended not to check my math" is a moment. Then list what you can actually remember: three sensory details, one line someone said, one thing you did with your hands. If you cannot remember, it is honest to say so, or to choose a moment you remember better. Do not invent details and call the story true.

Build the narrative from the pieces this chapter has given you. Open inside the moment. Let people talk, briefly. Slow down where the meaning is. Use precise verbs. Tell it in first person, past tense, because it happened to you and it is over. End with reflection, one or two sentences that show what the moment meant, and stop before you start explaining.

Finally, read it as a stranger would. The stranger does not know your grandmother. Does the story give them enough to see her? The stranger does not know why the kitchen matters. Does the story show that, or just say it? The gap between what you know and what the page says is the whole job of revision in narrative. Close it.

33.8

Writing a Fictional Narrative

Main ideaFiction needs a character who wants something, an obstacle, and a change, and the writer invents details as carefully as a memoirist remembers them.

In , you are free to make it all up. That freedom is the hard part. With a memory, the details are waiting for you. With fiction, you must invent a kitchen, a grandmother, a record, and make them feel as real as the ones Jalen remembered. The trick is to invent as specifically as you would remember. "An old song" is not a detail. "The same Aretha Franklin record every week" is.

Every short story needs three things. A character who something, even something small: to be picked first, to not be noticed, to get one more Sunday. An between the character and the want: another person, a rule, a fear, time. And a change, so that by the end the character has the thing, or has lost it, or no longer wants it. Want, obstacle, change. If your story feels flat, one of the three is missing, and it is usually the want.

Keep the cast small and the time short. A story that covers one afternoon with two characters is easier to make real than one that covers a year with eight. Choose a point of view on purpose. Open in the middle. Let the characters talk. Slow down at the turning point. End when the change has happened, not three paragraphs later.

Fiction can borrow from life. Many writers start with a real moment and then ask "what if?" What if the grandmother had said something instead of walking away? What if the boy had been the one to check her work? The real kitchen gives you the details; the question gives you the story. You are not lying. You are building, and the reader knows it is built.

33.9

Line Breaks and Images

Main ideaA poem is made of lines, and where a line ends controls speed, emphasis and meaning; images give the reader something to see.

Read Jalen’s line again: "then walking away." In the paragraph, those words were part of a sentence about checking homework. In the poem, they sat alone, and they meant more. That is the power of the . Prose goes to the edge of the page and wraps. A poem ends its lines on purpose, and every ending is a small pause where the reader stops and feels the weight of the last word.

Try it. Take a plain sentence: "She tapped the wrong answer twice and walked away without a word." Break it after "twice." Now the reader holds "twice" for a beat. Break it after "away." Now the leaving is the point. Break it after "without." Now there is a gap, a moment of not knowing what is missing. Same words, three poems. The line break is the poet’s most basic tool, and it is free.

The second tool is the : a picture in words that the reader can see, hear, smell or touch. Emily Dickinson opened a poem with "Hope is the thing with feathers / That perches in the soul." She does not explain hope. She hands you a bird. That is what an image does: it trades an abstract word for something the senses can hold, and it trusts you to make the connection.

A good rule for a first poem: no abstract words at all. Not "love," not "sad," not "memory." Only things: vinegar, a yellow wall, a finger tapping twice. If the images are right, the reader will feel the love and the sadness without the words. If you have to say "I was sad," the images have not done their job yet.

the place where a line of poetry ends, chosen by the poet to create a pause or emphasis

Check yourself

1. What does a line break do that a sentence in prose does not?

Why: Poets choose where lines end. Each ending is a pause that shapes how the reader hears the words.

2. Which of these is an image rather than an abstract statement?

Why: The bird on the wire can be seen. The other three name a feeling without showing anything.

33.10

Sound in a Poem

Main ideaPoems are built for the ear; rhythm, repeated sounds and rhyme make lines memorable and shape how they feel.

Read this aloud: "Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary." Edgar Allan Poe wrote that line in 1845, and you can hear it rock back and forth like a chair. The rhyme inside the line, "dreary" and "weary," and the steady beat make it stick in the ear. Poems are meant to be heard, and sound is a tool as real as the image.

Three sounds to know. is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables, the beat you tap your foot to. "ONCE up-ON a MID-night DREAR-y" has a strong beat on every other syllable. is the repeat of a beginning sound: "weak and weary," "Stacker of Wheat." is the matching of ending sounds, at the ends of lines or inside them. None of these is required. All of them are available.

Sound should match sense. A poem about running should move fast, with short words and hard sounds. A poem about a kitchen on Sunday should be slower, with longer vowels and softer sounds. Read your draft aloud and listen to where it drags or trips. Often the fix is a single word: a one-syllable word where you had three, or a soft "s" where you had a hard "k."

Rhyme is the sound students reach for first, and it is the easiest to do badly. If you twist a sentence into an odd order just to land a rhyme, the reader hears the twist, not the rhyme. If you must rhyme, let the rhyme be a word you would have chosen anyway. And remember that many great poems do not rhyme at all. Free verse gives up rhyme and keeps every other sound.

From the source
A Line You Can Rock To
Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary,
Edgar Allan Poe, "The Raven," 1845

Read it aloud and tap the beat. Find the rhyme inside the line and the alliteration. How does the sound match the mood of a tired man at midnight?

Words to know
rhythm
the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line, the beat of the poem
alliteration
the repetition of the same beginning sound in nearby words, such as 'weak and weary'
rhyme
the matching of ending sounds between words, at line ends or inside a line
Check yourself

1. 'Weak and weary' is an example of what?

Why: Both words begin with the same 'w' sound, which is alliteration.

2. What does 'sound should match sense' mean?

Why: A fast subject wants quick, hard sounds; a slow subject wants softer, longer ones.

3. What is the most common mistake with rhyme?

Why: Forced word order makes the reader hear the effort. A rhyme should be a word you would have chosen anyway.

33.11

Haiku, Free Verse and the Sonnet

Main ideaPoetic forms are sets of rules about lines, syllables and rhyme, and each form pushes the poet toward a different kind of attention.

A is a set of rules a poem agrees to follow. The rules sound like limits, and they are, but limits are useful. A basketball court has lines; the lines are what make the game. Three forms are enough to start. One is tiny, one is free, and one is four hundred years old.

The comes from Japan. In English it is usually written as three lines of five, seven and five syllables, seventeen in all, and it often holds one image from nature and a turn or surprise in the last line. Because it is so short, every syllable has to earn its place. "Yellow kitchen wall / vinegar and smoke, Sunday / the record skips, twice." Count it: five, seven, five. Writing a haiku is practice in cutting.

has no set rhyme or syllable count. It is what Jalen wrote. Its rules are the ones the poet makes up for that poem: where to break lines, what to repeat, when to stop. Walt Whitman made free verse famous in 1855 with lines that run long and then short. Free is not the same as easy. Without a form to lean on, every choice is yours, and the reader can tell when you were not paying attention.

The is fourteen lines, usually ten syllables each, with a set rhyme pattern, and often a turn in thought near the end. Shakespeare’s most famous one opens, "Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day? / Thou art more lovely and more temperate." You are not expected to write a sonnet this year. You are expected to recognize one, to count its lines, and to see that a form can hold a whole argument in a small box. Later, when you try one, you will find that the rhymes lead you to ideas you would never have had on your own.

form
a set of rules about lines, syllables, rhyme or shape that a poem follows
haiku
a short poem, in English usually three lines of five, seven and five syllables, often with a nature image
free verse
poetry with no set rhyme or syllable pattern, shaped by the poet's own line breaks
sonnet
a fourteen-line poem with a set rhyme pattern, usually ten syllables per line
Check yourself

1. What is the usual syllable pattern of a haiku in English?

Why: Three lines of five, seven and five syllables, seventeen in all.

2. How many lines does a sonnet have?

Why: A sonnet is fourteen lines, usually ten syllables each, with a set rhyme pattern.

3. Why is free verse 'not the same as easy'?

Why: Without set rules, the poet has to make and defend every decision, and careless choices show.
33.12

Reading Aloud and the Anthology

Main ideaReading your work aloud reveals what the page hides, and gathering a class's work into an anthology makes writing public and real.

The first time you read your own poem or story aloud to a room, your voice will shake. That is normal, and it is not a sign the work is bad. Reading aloud is the last step of revision and the first step of sharing. Your ear catches the sentence that trips, the line break that lands wrong, the word you used three times. Read it aloud twice, alone, before you read it to anyone.

When you read to others, slow down. Nervous readers rush, and rushed words vanish. Pause at line breaks and paragraph breaks; they are in the text for a reason. Look up at the end of a sentence you are proud of. If you lose your place, stop, find it, and go on. Nobody in the room is counting your mistakes. They are waiting to hear the kitchen.

When you listen, your job is just as real. Listen for one image or line that stays with you, and tell the writer afterward what it was. That single sentence, "the finger tapping twice stayed with me," is worth more to a writer than "good job." It tells them what worked, so they can do it again. Specific praise is a gift. Vague praise is a shrug.

An is a collection of writing by many authors in one book. A class anthology takes everyone’s best piece, edited and titled, and prints or posts it so that other people can read it. The moment your story sits between two classmates’ stories with your name on it, it stops being homework and becomes writing. That is the point of the whole unit. Not the grade. The book.

Think it through
After the Reading

Jalen finishes reading the kitchen poem. Three classmates speak. One says, "Good job." One says, "I liked it." One says, "When you said 'then walking away' and stopped, I thought about my grandpa, and I wanted the next line right away."

Which comment would help Jalen most, and why? What would you say about a piece you heard today, using one specific line?

Words to know
anthology
a collection of writing by many authors gathered into one book
specific praise
telling a writer exactly which line or image worked, rather than just 'good job'
Check yourself

1. Why should you read your work aloud before sharing it?

Why: Reading aloud exposes tripping sentences, wrong line breaks and repeated words.

2. Which response gives a writer the most useful feedback?

Why: Specific praise names what worked, so the writer can do it again.

3. What is an anthology?

Why: An anthology gathers many writers' pieces into one book, such as a class collection.
