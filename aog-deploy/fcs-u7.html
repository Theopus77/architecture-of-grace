Money and Choices

Consumer Skills

Two boxes of cereal sit on a shelf. One costs less. The other holds more. A ten-dollar bill sits in your hand after a morning of yard work. Friends are going for pizza, and there is a bike in a shop window. These are money choices, and people make them every single day. This unit is about making them on purpose.

First you will learn that a price is only half an answer. You will find the unit price by dividing, read the small tag on the shelf, and decide when the big box really is the better deal. You will compare a store brand to a name brand, test a coupon with math, sort needs from wants, and write a list that keeps you on budget.

Then you will follow money from a job to a goal. You will keep a record of what you earn, put savings in one place you can see, and figure how many weeks until the goal. You will learn what a bank does and what interest is, and you will split money into spend, save and share. By the end, you will be able to plan every dollar.

The big question

How do you get the most from the money you have, now and later?

Price Per Unit

Smart Shopping
Big questionHow do you know which box is really the better deal?
The story

Two Boxes of Cereal

Leo picked the cheaper box. His sister picked the other one. Only one of them did the math.

Leo and his big sister Nina went to the store with their dad on Saturday. Dad handed Leo the list and a job. "Pick the cereal," he said. "The best deal, not the best box." Leo walked down the cereal aisle. Every box had a bright color and a cartoon face. Two boxes were the kind the family liked.

The small box cost $3.00. The big box cost $4.50. Leo grabbed the small one right away. "This one is cheaper," he said. Nina picked up the big box and turned it sideways. "This one holds 18 ounces," she said. "Yours holds 12. You are paying less, but you are getting less, too."

Leo was not sure that mattered. Nina pointed at a tiny white tag on the shelf. Under the price, in small letters, it said 25.0 cents per ounce for the small box. Under the big box it said 25.0 cents per ounce, too. Leo frowned. "So they cost the same?" "Per ounce, yes," Nina said. "Now look at the store brand."

The store-brand box sat on the bottom shelf. It was plain and yellow. It held 18 ounces and cost $3.60. Its tag said 20.0 cents per ounce. Nina did the math on her fingers. "Three sixty divided by eighteen. Twenty cents. That is the real winner." Dad smiled. "A price is only half an answer," he said. "The size is the other half."

Leo put the store brand in the cart. On the way home he kept looking at the receipt. He wanted to know which of the other things they bought had been a good deal, and which had not.

Talk about itLeo thought the $3.00 box was the better deal. What was he forgetting? What did Nina look at that he did not?
Section 1

A Price Is Half an Answer

13.1

Price and Amount

Main ideaA price only makes sense when you also know how much you get for it.

Picture two bags of rice on a shelf. One bag costs $3.00. The other costs $4.00. Which is the better buy? You cannot tell yet. A is the money you pay. It is only half of the answer. The other half is the you get.

Now read the bags. The $3.00 bag holds 2 pounds. The $4.00 bag holds 5 pounds. The second bag costs more money. But it holds more than twice as much rice. For one more dollar, you get 3 more pounds. Now the $4.00 bag looks like the better deal.

Shoppers make one mistake more than any other. They look at the price and stop. A smart shopper asks two questions every time. How much does it cost? How much do I get? Only when you know both can you compare.

Think it through
Two Bags of Rice

A $3.00 bag of rice holds 2 pounds. A $4.00 bag of the same rice holds 5 pounds. A friend says the $3.00 bag is the better deal because it costs less.

What is your friend forgetting? How much more rice does the $4.00 bag hold?

Words to know
price
the amount of money you pay for something
amount
how much of something you get, such as ounces, pounds or pieces
Check yourself

1. A price tells you only half of the answer. What is the other half?

Why: To compare deals, you need both the price and the amount you get for it.

2. A $3.00 bag holds 2 pounds. A $4.00 bag holds 5 pounds. How much more rice is in the bigger bag?

Why: 5 pounds minus 2 pounds is 3 pounds more rice for one more dollar.

3. Which is the most common shopping mistake?

Why: Stopping at the price skips the amount, so you cannot tell which is the better deal.
13.2

Divide to Find the Unit Price

Main ideaUnit price is the price divided by the amount, and it lets you compare any two sizes.

Two bottles of juice sit side by side. One is 64 ounces for $3.20. The other is 32 ounces for $1.92. To compare them, find the . A unit price is the cost of one unit, such as one ounce or one pound. You find it by dividing the price by the amount.

Work it in cents so the math is cleaner. The big bottle: 320 cents divided by 64 ounces is 5 cents per ounce. The small bottle: 192 cents divided by 32 ounces is 6 cents per ounce. The big bottle costs 1 cent less for every ounce. That is the better deal.

The word means "for each." Five cents per ounce means 5 cents for each ounce. Once every item has a unit price, you can compare them like runners in a race. The lowest unit price wins. Check your division twice; one slip can flip the answer.

13.3

The Small Tag on the Shelf

Main ideaMost stores print the unit price on the shelf tag, so you can compare without doing the division yourself.

Look at the shelf under any box in a grocery store. There is a small paper tag. The big number is the price you pay. Look closer. In smaller print, the tag usually shows the unit price too. It might say "20.0 cents per oz" or "$1.60 per lb." The store did the division for you.

Here is the catch. You must check that both tags use the same unit. One box of crackers might show cents per ounce. The box next to it might show cents per pound or per 100 count. You cannot compare ounces to pounds straight across. If the units do not match, do the division yourself.

Some stores do not print unit prices at all. Then you bring the math. Read the on the package. That is the weight of the food inside, not the box. Divide the price by that number. A small notebook or a calculator on a phone works fine.

Think it through
Two Tags, Two Units

The tag under a box of oatmeal says "$3.00, 15.0 cents per oz." The tag under a bag of oatmeal next to it says "$4.80, $1.60 per lb." A shopper says the box is cheaper because 15 is less than 160.

Why is that comparison wrong? Hint: 16 ounces make a pound. What is the bag's price per ounce?

Words to know
shelf tag
the small label on the shelf edge that shows the price and often the unit price
net weight
the weight of the food inside a package, not counting the box or bag
Check yourself

1. Where do most stores show the unit price?

Why: The shelf tag under the item shows the price and, in small print, the unit price.

2. A bag of oatmeal costs $1.60 per pound. What is that per ounce?

Why: 16 ounces make a pound, so 160 cents divided by 16 is 10 cents per ounce.

3. Two tags show cents per ounce and cents per pound. What should you do?

Why: You can only compare unit prices that use the same unit.
Section 2

Comparing to Choose

13.4

Is Bigger Always Cheaper?

Main ideaBigger packages are often, but not always, cheaper per unit, so check the tag every time.

Many people believe the big package is always the better deal. Often it is. A big bag of rice usually costs less per pound than a small one. But not always. Stores know shoppers think this way. Sometimes the big size costs more per unit. Only the tag knows.

Try yogurt cups. A 6-pack costs $3.00. That is 300 cents divided by 6, or 50 cents per cup. A 12-pack costs $7.20. That is 720 divided by 12, or 60 cents per cup. The bigger pack costs 10 cents more per cup. Two 6-packs would be cheaper than one 12-pack.

There is one more thing to think about. A big bag of salad is cheaper per ounce. But if half of it wilts in the fridge before you eat it, you paid for food that went in the trash. A good deal is one you will actually use up. Buy the size your family will finish.

13.5

Brand Name or Store Brand

Main ideaA store brand often has the same ingredients as a name brand at a lower unit price, so read both labels.

Two jars of peanut butter sit on the shelf. One has a famous name and a bright label. The other has the store’s own name on it. That is the . Both jars hold 16 ounces. The name brand costs $3.20. The store brand costs $2.40.

Do the math. The name brand is 320 divided by 16, or 20 cents per ounce. The store brand is 240 divided by 16, or 15 cents per ounce. Now turn both jars around and read the . Often the lists are nearly the same. Peanuts, salt, maybe a little sugar. You are paying 5 cents more per ounce for the label.

Store brands are not always the same. Sometimes the taste is different, and your family may like one better. That is a fair reason to pick it. The rule is not "always buy the store brand." The rule is "compare, then choose." Try the store brand once. If it works, keep the savings.

Think it through
Two Jars, One Shelf

Name brand peanut butter: 16 ounces, $3.20, ingredients: peanuts, sugar, salt. Store brand peanut butter: 16 ounces, $2.40, ingredients: peanuts, sugar, salt. Your family eats one jar every two weeks.

How much would you save in a year (26 jars) by choosing the store brand? What might make you pick the name brand anyway?

Check yourself

2. Where do you look to see if two products have the same things in them?

13.6

Sales and Coupons

Main ideaA sale or coupon only helps if you needed the item, and the sale price still has to beat the unit price of the other choices.

A is a slip of paper or a code on a phone. It takes money off one item. A is a lower price for a short time. Both feel like winning. But a coupon on something you did not need is not saving. It is spending. Ask first: was this on my list?

Now do the math on a real one. A 20-ounce box of name brand cereal costs $3.00. You have a coupon for 60 cents off. The box now costs $2.40. That is 240 divided by 20, or 12 cents per ounce. The 20-ounce store brand is $2.20 with no coupon. That is 11 cents per ounce. The store brand still wins, even against the coupon.

Sometimes the coupon does win. A 60-cent coupon on a $2.40 item drops it to $1.80. That may beat the store brand. The point is to check, not to guess. Also watch "buy 2, get 1 free." Three boxes for $6.00 is $2.00 each. That is a real deal only if you will use three boxes before they go stale.

Think it through
The Coupon Test

Name brand cereal: 20 ounces, $3.00, with a 60-cent coupon. Store brand cereal: 20 ounces, $2.20, no coupon. Both are on your list. Step 1: subtract the coupon. Step 2: find each unit price. Step 3: pick the lower one.

Walk the three steps. Which box goes in the cart? What would the coupon need to be for the name brand to tie?

Words to know
coupon
a paper slip or phone code that takes a set amount off the price of one item
sale
a lower price on an item for a short time
Check yourself

1. A $3.00 box has a 60-cent coupon. What do you pay?

Why: $3.00 minus $0.60 is $2.40.

2. A 20-oz box costs $2.40 after a coupon. A 20-oz store brand costs $2.20. Which is cheaper per ounce?

Why: 220 divided by 20 is 11 cents; 240 divided by 20 is 12 cents. The store brand wins.

3. When is a coupon not really saving money?

Why: A coupon on something you did not need is extra spending, not saving.
Section 3

Before You Shop

13.7

Needs and Wants

Main ideaA need is something you must have to live and stay healthy; a want is something nice to have, and telling them apart guides every purchase.

Stand in the checkout line. Look in the cart. Milk, bread, eggs, apples. Those are . A need is something a person must have to live and stay healthy. Food, a safe place to live, clothes for the weather, medicine. Now look at the candy rack by the register. That is a .

A want is something nice to have but not needed. Wants are not bad. A treat, a game, a new pair of shoes when the old ones still fit. Every family enjoys some wants. The trouble comes when wants push out needs. If the candy means there is no money for milk, the order got mixed up.

Sometimes one item can be both. Shoes are a need. Shoes with a famous logo that cost twice as much are a want. The need part is "shoes that fit." The want part is "that brand." Smart shoppers pay for the need first. Then they decide if the want is worth the extra money.

13.8

Writing the List

Main ideaA good shopping list starts at home, in the cupboard and the fridge, and is written in the order of the store.

The list starts before the store. Open the cupboard. Open the fridge. What is almost gone? Write it down. What meals will the family eat this week? Write down what those meals need. Check again. Do not write "cereal" if there are two boxes on the shelf.

Next, put the list in order. Most stores have the same layout. Fruits and vegetables near the door. Milk, eggs and cheese along the back wall. Boxes and cans in the middle aisles. Frozen food last. If your list follows the store, you walk it once. You do not go back for the eggs.

Write the amount next to each item. "Milk, 1 gallon." "Apples, 6." "Rice, 5 pound bag." A list with amounts stops the guessing. It also helps you the total. If milk is about $3.00 and apples are about $4.00, you know before you go what you will spend.

Think it through

Check yourself

1. Where does a good shopping list start?

Why: Checking what you already have keeps you from buying it again.

2. Why put frozen food last on the list?

Why: Frozen food picked up last spends the least time thawing in the cart.

3. What should you write next to each item on the list?

Why: An amount, like 1 gallon or 6 apples, stops guessing and helps you estimate the total.
13.9

Sticking to the List

Main ideaStores are built to make you buy extra, and the list is your plan for walking past it.

Stores are designed by people who study shoppers. The candy sits by the register where you wait. The bright displays at the end of each aisle, called , hold things you did not plan to buy. The most expensive brands sit at eye level. The cheaper ones are on the bottom shelf. None of this is an accident.

The list is your plan. Hold it in your hand. Cross off each item as it goes in the cart. When something not on the list looks good, ask two questions. Do I need this? Is there money for it after the list? If the answer is no, walk past. Look at the bottom shelf, too. That is where the store brands often hide.

One more rule. Do not shop hungry. A hungry shopper buys more snacks. Eat first, then go. Keep a running total in your head or on paper. If milk is $3.00, bread is $2.50, eggs are $2.00 and apples are $4.00, the list so far is $11.50. If the family budget for this trip is $15.00, you know you have $3.50 left.

Earn, Save, Spend, Share

Money Basics
Big questionHow does money you earn today turn into something you want later?
The story

One Lawn at a Time

The bike in the window cost $120. Mateo had $6 and a lawn mower he was allowed to borrow.

The bike hung in the shop window all spring. It was blue with a black seat. The tag said $120. Mateo walked past it every day on the way home from school. He had $6 in a sock in his drawer. He also had a neighbor, Mrs. Ortiz, whose grass was getting tall.

"I could mow it," Mateo told his mom. "For money." His mom thought about it. "You can use our mower if I am there the first few times," she said. "Ten dollars a lawn is fair for a small yard. But you have to finish every job you start. Edges, too. And you put the mower back clean." Mateo said yes to all of it.

The first Saturday he mowed Mrs. Ortiz's yard. It took an hour and he was sweaty and tired. She handed him a ten-dollar bill. He held it for a long time. Then he did the math. Ten dollars a lawn. One hundred twenty dollars. "Twelve lawns," he said. "That is a lot of lawns."

His mom got out an empty jar. She taped a picture of the blue bike to the front. "Every lawn, the money goes in here," she said. "Not in the sock. Not in your pocket. In the jar, where you can see it." Mateo dropped in the ten and the six from his sock. Sixteen dollars. The jar looked very empty.

By June, two more neighbors had hired him. Some weeks he wanted to spend a ten on a game with his friends. Most weeks he did not. He put a mark on the calendar each time he mowed. In July, the jar held $120. He walked to the shop with his mom and a jar of small bills. The bike was still there.

Talk about itWhat made it hard for Mateo to keep saving? What helped him? Why did his mom want the money in a jar, not in his pocket?
Section 1

Money Comes In

14.1

Ways Kids Earn

Main ideaPeople earn money by doing work that someone else needs done, and the work comes first.

Picture a Saturday morning. One kid is walking a neighbor’s dog. Another is pulling weeds for a grandparent. A third is selling lemonade on the corner. Each one is money. To earn means to get money in return for work. The work comes first. The money comes after.

Kids earn in many ways. Mowing a lawn, raking leaves, shoveling snow, washing a car, watching a younger cousin while a parent is in the next room. Some families pay for extra chores. Some do not, and that is fair too. Regular chores are part of being in a family. Extra work for a neighbor is a job.

A job has rules. Agree on the pay before you start. Show up when you said you would. Finish the whole job, not most of it. Put the tools back. A kid who does all four gets asked back. That is how one lawn turns into three. Always ask a grown-up before you take a job for someone outside the family.

Which of the job rules did Jada break? What should she do now, and what might happen the next time she asks for work?

14.2

Counting What You Earned

Main ideaWrite down every job and every dollar, and subtract what the job cost you, so you know what you really made.

Mateo mows three lawns in one week at $10 each. How much did he earn? 3 times 10 is $30. But he also bought a box of lawn bags for $4 to hold the clippings. The bags were an , a cost of doing the job. What he kept is $30 minus $4, or $26.

Keep a . A record is a written list of what happened. Draw four columns on a page: date, job, money in, money out. Every time you get paid, write it down. Every time you spend on the job, write that too. At the end of the week, add each column. The difference is what you really made.

A record catches mistakes. Without one, kids often think they earned more than they did. "I mowed a lot" feels like $50 but might be $30. And a record shows a neighbor you are serious. A common mistake is writing the pay down only when you feel like it. Write it every single time.

14.3

A Want Now or a Goal Later

Main ideaEvery dollar spent on a small want today is a dollar that cannot go toward a bigger goal later.

Mateo has a ten-dollar bill in his hand. His friends are going for pizza and a game. It would cost about $8. He could go. He would have fun. But his is a $120 bike. A goal is something you decide to work toward. Every ten he spends is a lawn that did not count.

This is the hardest part of money. A want now feels big. A goal later feels far away. Both are real. Nobody has to say no to every small thing. But you have to choose on purpose. Ask: if I spend this, how much longer until my goal? For Mateo, one $8 pizza night is almost one whole lawn.

One trick helps. Look at the picture on the jar before you decide. Another trick is to give yourself a small spending amount each week, and no more. When it is gone, it is gone. Then a pizza night is planned, not stolen from the bike. The goal keeps moving forward.

Section 2

Saving for a Goal

14.4

The Jar With a Picture

Main ideaSaving works best when the money has one place to go and you can see the goal every day.

To means to keep money instead of spending it, so you can use it later. The simplest tool is a jar. Take a clear jar. Tape a picture of your goal on it. Write the goal amount on the tape: "$120." Put the jar where you will see it every day.

Why a jar and not a pocket? Money in a pocket gets spent. Money in a sock gets forgotten. Money in a clear jar with a picture on it has a job, and you can watch it grow. Every time you put money in, count it and write the new total on a card next to the jar.

Some families use an envelope, a box or a savings account instead. Any of these work. What matters is the same. One place. A picture or a name for the goal. A total you update. The mistake to avoid is "borrowing" from the jar. Once you start taking money out, the jar stops working.

Think it through

14.5

How Long Until the Goal

Main ideaDivide the goal by what you save each week to see how many weeks it will take.

A goal needs a number and a date. Mateo’s bike is $120. He earns about $30 a week from lawns. He decides to save $20 of it each week and keep $10 for spending. How many weeks until the bike? Divide the goal by the weekly savings. 120 divided by 20 is 6. Six weeks.

Now try it another way. If he saves all $30, then 120 divided by 30 is 4 weeks. If he saves only $10, then 120 divided by 10 is 12 weeks. The more you save each week, the sooner you get there. But the plan has to be one you can really keep. A plan you quit is slower than a smaller plan you finish.

Make a chart. Down the side, write the weeks. Next to each week, write the total in the jar. Week 1, $20. Week 2, $40. Week 3, $60. When you can see the finish line, it is easier to keep going. If a week goes wrong and you save less, do not quit. Just add one more week to the chart.

14.6

What a Bank Does

Main ideaA bank keeps money safe, keeps a record of it, and lets you take it out when you need it.

A jar works for $120. It does not work for $1,000. Money in a jar can be lost, stolen or burned in a fire. A is a business that keeps money safe. When you put money in, the bank writes it down in your . An account is the bank’s record of your money.

Putting money in is called a . Taking money out is called a withdrawal. The bank sends a statement, a list of every deposit and withdrawal, so you can check it against your own record. Kids can often open a savings account with a parent or guardian on it. Ask a grown-up at home.

The money in a bank is not sitting in a box with your name on it. The bank keeps careful records instead. That is why the statement matters. Read it. If a number looks wrong, tell a grown-up. And never share your account numbers with anyone outside your family.

14.7

Interest, Money for Saving

Main ideaA bank pays interest, a small amount added to your savings for leaving the money there.

Here is something a jar cannot do. A bank pays you for keeping your money there. That payment is called . Interest is extra money added to your account because you saved. The bank uses the money you deposit to make loans, and it shares a little of what it earns with you.

Interest is figured as a of what you saved. Percent means "out of one hundred." Here is an example with easy numbers. You put $100 in a savings account. The bank pays 2 percent interest for the year. Two percent of $100 is $2. At the end of the year you have $102. You did nothing but leave it alone.

Real interest rates change and are often smaller or larger than this example. The idea is what matters. Saving in a bank makes money grow a little on its own. The longer it sits, the more it earns. The mistake is expecting it to grow fast. Interest is slow and steady. Lawns are faster. Both together are best.

Section 3

A Plan for Every Dollar

14.8

Spend, Save, Share

Main ideaSplitting every dollar that comes in into spend, save and share gives each part of your money a job.

Picture three jars on a shelf. One says spend. One says save. One says share. When money comes in, it gets split three ways before any of it is used. Some for now, some for later, some for others. This is the simplest money plan there is, and many grown-ups still use it.

Try it with a $10 allowance. Put $5 in spend. Put $3 in save. Put $2 in share. That is one way. Another family might do $4, $4 and $2. There is no single right split. The rule is that all three jars get something every time. Spend is for small wants now. Save is for the goal. Share is for helping someone else.

The mistake most kids make is filling spend first and saving what is left. Usually nothing is left. Fill save first, then share, then spend. A person who can split $10 three ways can later split a paycheck three ways. The habit is the whole point.

Think it through

How would you split the $10 across the three jars? Show that your three amounts add to $10, and say why you chose them.

Words to know

money some families give a child on a regular schedule, like once a week
share
to give part of what you have to help someone else
Check yourself

1. What are the three jars for?

Why: Spend is for now, save is for the goal, share is for helping others.

2. $10 comes in. Spend gets $5 and save gets $3. How much goes to share?

Why: 5 + 3 = 8, and 10 minus 8 leaves $2 for share.

3. What is the mistake of filling the spend jar first?

Why: Saving what is left after spending usually means saving nothing; fill save first.
14.9

A Budget From an Allowance

Main ideaA budget is a written plan that gives every dollar coming in a place to go, and the parts must add up to the total.

A is a plan for money, written down before you spend it. It has two sides. Money in and money out. Money in is your , what you earn or receive. Money out is where each dollar goes. The rule is simple. The money out must add up to exactly the money in. Not more.

Here is a weekly example. Income: $10 allowance plus $10 from one lawn is $20. Money out: save $8, share $2, snacks $4, bus fare to the library $3, spending money $3. Add the money out: 8 + 2 + 4 + 3 + 3 = 20. It matches. Every dollar has a job. Nothing is missing and nothing is extra.

At the end of the week, compare the plan to what really happened. If snacks cost $6 instead of $4, something else got $2 less. That is normal. Fix the plan for next week. A budget is not a punishment. It is a map that shows where the money went, so you can steer it where you want.

14.10

Giving to Others

Main ideaSharing money, things or time with others is part of a healthy money plan, and even a small gift counts.

The share jar is the smallest, but it may matter the most. Sharing means giving part of what you have to help someone else. It can be money. It can be things, like a coat that no longer fits. It can be time, like helping at a food drive. All three are real ways to give.

Many families give to a place of worship, a food pantry, an animal shelter or a neighbor who is having a hard time. Kids can give too. A can of soup for the class food drive. Two dollars from the share jar for a school fundraiser. Sorting cans at a pantry with a grown-up. None of it has to be big.

Give carefully. Ask a grown-up before you give money to anyone, especially online or to a stranger who calls. Real groups will never rush you. Then notice how you feel after you give. Most people feel richer, not poorer. That feeling is why the share jar exists.
