The Ancient Eastern Cosmos

How the peoples of the ancient Near East pictured the world: a flat earth under a solid vault, with waters above and below. This page presents that geocentric understanding as it appears in the surviving texts and art, not as a modern scientific claim.

Sources

The picture reconstructed here is drawn from the literature and iconography of Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Hebrew Bible, roughly the second and first millennia BCE. It is not a single official doctrine but a shared set of assumptions visible across those cultures.

  • Enuma Elish (Babylonian creation epic, c. 12th–11th century BCE in its surviving form): Marduk splits the body of Tiamat (the salt-water chaos) to form the sky and the earth.
  • Genesis 1 (Hebrew Bible): God separates the waters above from the waters below with a firmament (raqia), gathers the lower waters so dry land appears, and sets the sun, moon, and stars in the vault.
  • Egyptian coffin texts and temple reliefs: the sky goddess Nut arched over the earth god Geb, with the sun traveling through her body or in a boat.
  • Assyrian and Babylonian astronomical texts and boundary stones that show the cosmos as ordered layers rather than an infinite space.

These texts describe how the world was ordered and maintained. They do not present a modern heliocentric or evolutionary account of planetary formation.

The layers

In the common ancient Near Eastern picture the cosmos is a series of stacked regions, all centered on the inhabited earth.

  • Waters above — a cosmic ocean held back by the firmament. Rain, snow, and the flood are pictured as openings in that barrier.
  • Firmament / vault — a solid or stretched dome (Hebrew raqia, “something hammered out”). The sun, moon, and stars are set in or move across it.
  • Air / atmosphere — the space between the vault and the earth where birds fly and weather occurs.
  • Earth — a disk or mound of dry land, often surrounded by a circular sea. Mountains at the edges or center can serve as pillars or meeting places of gods.
  • Waters below — the freshwater deep (Hebrew tehom, related to Tiamat) under the earth, source of springs and rivers.
  • Underworld — Sheol, the land of the dead, beneath or within the lower waters. In Mesopotamian texts the netherworld has its own gates and rulers.

The whole arrangement is static and centered on the human world. There is no empty outer space and no orbiting planets in the later Greek sense.

A geocentric view

“Geocentric” here means the inhabited earth is the fixed center of the ordered world. The lights in the sky move around or across it; the earth does not move.

This is not yet the later Greek mathematical geocentrism of epicycles and deferents. It is a cosmological picture in which:

  • The earth is stable (“founded upon the seas,” “set on pillars” in poetic language).
  • The sun, moon, and stars are lamps or beings placed in the vault to mark times and seasons.
  • Direction and value radiate from the center: temple, city, or cosmic mountain.
  • Chaos is the unbounded water outside the ordered structure, not a vacuum.

When Greek astronomers later calculated planetary positions with circles, they still placed the earth at or near the center. The ancient Eastern picture supplied the basic orientation—earth fixed, heavens moving—that those calculations refined.

Key texts

Enuma Elish

After defeating Tiamat, Marduk splits her body “like a shellfish.” One half he sets up as the sky, the other becomes the earth. He assigns stations to the gods, fixes the year, and establishes the paths of the celestial bodies. The ordered world is the result of a divine victory over watery chaos.

Genesis 1

The account begins with the earth formless and the deep covered in darkness. God speaks a firmament into the midst of the waters, separates the waters above from the waters below, and names the firmament “heaven.” On the second day the dry land appears; on the fourth the lights are set in the firmament to rule day and night and to mark seasons. The structure matches the wider Near Eastern layered cosmos, while the agent and the evaluation (“it was good”) are distinctive to the Hebrew text.

Egyptian imagery

Nut, the sky, is shown as a woman arched on hands and feet over Geb, the earth. The sun travels through her or sails in a boat. The picture is anthropomorphic, yet the geometry is the same: a covering sky over a stable earth, with waters at the edges.

Later models

Greek natural philosophers from the sixth century BCE onward proposed that the earth is a sphere and that the heavens are concentric spheres. By the second century BCE, Hipparchus and others were calculating lunar and solar positions with eccentric circles. Ptolemy’s Almagest (second century CE) gave a full geocentric system of deferents, epicycles, and the equant that could predict planetary positions for centuries.

That mathematical geocentrism is a different achievement from the ancient Eastern layered cosmos, but it preserves the same central commitment: the earth does not move, and the lights of heaven travel around it. The shift to a moving earth came much later, with Copernicus, Kepler, and Galileo.

This page describes historical cosmologies. It does not adjudicate modern scientific accounts of planetary formation or orbital mechanics.
