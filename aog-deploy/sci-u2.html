Light, Sound and Waves

Physical Science

Tap a drum. Feel the buzz under your hand. Hear the boom. Turn off the lamp. The room goes dark. Your shadow is gone. Sound and light are all around you, every day. This unit is about both of them.

You will find out what a sound really is. It is a wiggle. Something shakes, and the shake spreads to your ear. You will find out what light does. It shines from the sun or a bulb. It bounces off things and into your eyes.

By the end, you can explain a drum. Why does it stop when you touch it? You can explain a shadow. Why does it move? And you can send a secret message. Use claps, or blinks of a flashlight.

The big question

How do sound and light get from a drum or a lamp to you, and how can they carry a message?

How we figured it out
Wake up

An alarm buzzes. Something inside it is shaking fast. That shake is the sound.

Sunrise

Light comes in the window. Now you can see your room. The sun is a light source.

Breakfast

A spoon clinks on a bowl. The bowl vibrates. The sound spreads through the air.

Bus stop

Your shadow is long on the sidewalk. The sun is low in the sky.

Class

Hand on the drum machine. Feel the buzz. Loud hits make big wiggles.

Recess

A whistle blows. Everyone stops. The sound carried a message.

Noon

Shadows are very short now. The sun is high overhead.

Afternoon

You look through a window. Glass lets light through. The wall does not.

Dinner

A ship horn sounds on the lake in the fog. The horn says: I am here.

Dusk

The lighthouse beam turns on. Its light tells boats where the shore is.

Bedtime

You blink a flashlight to your sister. Two blinks mean no, one means yes.

Night

Lights off. The room is dark. You cannot see, but you can still hear.

Sound Is a Wiggle

Waves
Big questionWhat is a sound, and how does it get from a drum to your ear?
The story

Hand on the Drum

You can feel a sound before you can name it.

Mia stands at the drum machine. The big drum is quiet. She puts her hand flat on the drum skin. It feels smooth and still. Nothing is moving. Then her teacher taps the drum once with a stick.

Boom! Mia feels a buzz under her hand. The skin shakes very fast. It tickles her palm. The buzz gets smaller and smaller. Then it stops. The sound stops at the same time. Mia looks at her hand. It is not hurt. It just felt the drum wiggle.

She tries it again. This time she hits the drum herself. She hits it soft. The buzz is small. The sound is soft. She hits it hard. The buzz is big. The sound is loud. Mia grins. The drum wiggles, and that wiggle is the sound.

Now Mia puts her hand on her own throat. She hums. Her throat buzzes too, just like the drum. She stops humming. The buzz stops. Mia has found a rule. Where there is a sound, something is wiggling.

Talk about itMia felt the drum buzz and heard the sound at the same time. Why do you think the buzz and the sound stopped together?
Section 1

Things That Wiggle

3.1

The Drum Skin Shakes

Main ideaA sound comes from something that shakes back and forth very fast.

Tap a drum. The skin moves. It goes up and down very fast. This fast shaking is called a . You can feel it with your hand. You can hear it with your ear.

The shaking makes the sound. When the skin stops shaking, the sound stops. No shaking means no sound. Try it. Tap the drum. Then press your hand on it. The sound dies fast.

Think it through
Drum With a Hand on It

Look at the drum. Tap it and it goes boom. The sound rings for a while. Now tap it again. Right away, press your hand flat on the skin. The sound stops at once. Your hand made the skin still.

Why did the sound stop when the hand pressed the drum?

Words to know
vibration
a very fast shaking back and forth
Check yourself

1. What is a vibration?

Why: A vibration is a fast shaking back and forth. The drum skin vibrates when you tap it.

2. You press your hand on a ringing drum. What happens?

Why: Your hand stops the skin from shaking. No shaking means no sound.

3. What makes the drum's sound?

Why: The shaking skin makes the sound. The stick just starts the shaking.
3.2

Rubber Bands and Throats

Main ideaMany things vibrate to make sound, even your own throat.

Stretch a rubber band. Pluck it. It looks blurry for a second. It is shaking too fast to see. You hear a twang. Pinch the band. It stops. The twang stops too.

Put your hand on your throat. Say your name. Feel the buzz? Two small flaps in your throat shake. They make your voice. Whisper your name. The buzz is gone. Whispers do not use the flaps.

Think it through
Hum, Then Whisper

Put two fingers on the front of your neck. Hum a long note. Your fingers feel a buzz. Now whisper the same note. The buzz is gone. Now talk out loud. The buzz is back.

What do you think is shaking in your neck when you hum?

Words to know
pluck
to pull a string and let it go so it shakes
Check yourself

1. You pluck a rubber band. Why does it look blurry?

Why: The band moves back and forth so fast that your eyes see a blur.

2. When can you feel a buzz in your throat?

Why: Humming makes the flaps in your throat shake. Whispers do not shake them.

3. What happens if you pinch a twanging rubber band?

Why: Pinching stops the shaking. When the shaking stops, the sound stops.
Section 2

Loud, Soft, High, Low

3.3

Sound Moves Things

Main ideaSound can make other things vibrate too, like rice on a drum.

Put a few grains of rice on a drum. Do not touch the drum. Stand near it. Clap hard, or bang a pot. Watch the rice. It jumps! Your sound made the drum skin shake.

Sound is a wiggle that spreads out. It goes through the air. It reaches the drum. The skin wiggles a tiny bit. The rice hops. Sound can move things without a touch.

Think it through
Rice on a Drum

Ten grains of rice sit still on the drum. A boy bangs a pot lid two steps away. The rice jumps up and lands again. He bangs it softly. The rice barely moves. He bangs it very hard. The rice hops high.

What would happen to the rice if the boy stood far across the room and banged the lid?

Words to know
spread
to move out in every direction from one spot
Check yourself

1. Rice jumps on a drum when you clap. Why?

Why: The sound of the clap spread through the air. It made the skin vibrate, so the rice hopped.

2. The boy bangs the pot very hard. What does the rice do?

Why: A bigger sound makes a bigger wiggle in the drum. The rice hops more.

3. How does the clap sound get to the drum?

Why: The sound spreads out through the air and reaches the drum.
3.4

Loud and Soft

Main ideaA big wiggle makes a loud sound; a small wiggle makes a soft sound.

Tap a drum softly. The skin moves a little. The sound is soft. Hit it hard. The skin moves a lot. The sound is . A bigger wiggle makes a louder sound.

Loud sounds can hurt your ears. Fire trucks and lawn mowers are very loud. Cover your ears near them. A whisper is very soft. You must be close to hear it.

Sounds get softer as they spread out. A friend yells from across the field. It sounds soft. The same yell up close is loud. The wiggle gets weaker the farther it goes.

Read the numbers
How Loud Is It?

standard reference values, rounded

Which sound on the list should you cover your ears near? Which one is the softest?

Words to know
loud
a sound that is big and strong, easy to hear
Check yourself

1. How do you make a drum louder?

Why: A harder hit makes a bigger wiggle. A bigger wiggle is a louder sound.

2. Which sound is the softest?

Why: A whisper is a very small wiggle. You must be close to hear it.

3. A friend yells from far away. How does it sound?

Why: Sound gets weaker as it spreads out. Far away, the yell sounds soft.
3.5

High and Low

Main ideaFast wiggles make high sounds; slow wiggles make low sounds.

A bird’s chirp is . A big dog’s bark is . High and low is not about loud and soft. A high sound can be soft. A low sound can be loud.

Stretch a rubber band tight. Pluck it. The sound is high. Let it go loose. Pluck it. The sound is low. A tight band shakes faster. Faster shaking makes a higher sound.

Big things often make low sounds. A big drum goes boom. A tiny drum goes tap. Small things wiggle faster. Try tapping a big pot and a small cup.

Think it through
Two Glasses of Water

Two glasses sit on a table. One is almost full of water. One has just a little water. A girl taps each one with a spoon. The full glass makes a low clink. The glass with less water makes a higher clink.

What sound do you think an empty glass would make: higher or lower than the full one?

Words to know
high
a sound like a bird chirp or a whistle
low
a sound like a big drum or a big dog's bark
Check yourself

1. Which sound is high?

Why: A bird chirp is a fast wiggle, so it sounds high.

2. A rubber band is stretched tight. Its sound is:

Why: A tight band shakes faster. Faster shaking makes a higher sound.

3. Is a high sound always loud?

Why: High and low is about how fast the wiggle is. Loud and soft is about how big it is.
Section 3

Sound Travels

3.6

Through Air, Water and Wood

Main ideaSound can travel through air, through water and through solid things.

Sound needs something to move through. Air works. Water works. Wood works. Put your ear on a table. Have a friend tap the far end. You hear it clearly. The tap went through the wood.

Dip your ears under bath water. Tap the tub. You still hear it. Sound goes through water. Whales hear each other far away in the ocean this way.

Long ago, a scientist put a ringing bell in a jar. He pumped the air out. The ring got quiet. No air, no sound. Sound needs something to wiggle.

From the source
The Bell in the Jar
Around 1660, Robert Boyle hung a small watch with a bell inside a glass jar. When he pumped the air out of the jar, the bell could barely be heard. When he let the air back in, the sound came back.
Robert Boyle, air-pump experiments, about 1660 (retold) (paraphrased)

Why did the bell get quiet when the air was pumped out?

Words to know
travel
to move from one place to another
Check yourself

1. Your ear is on a table. A friend taps the other end. How did the sound reach you?

Why: Sound travels through solid things like wood. The tap moved through the table.

2. Can sound travel through water?

Why: Sound goes through water. Whales hear each other far away in the ocean.

3. The air was pumped out of the jar. What happened to the bell's ring?

Why: With no air in the jar, there was nothing to carry the wiggle out. The ring almost vanished.
3.7

The String Telephone

Main ideaA tight string can carry a voice from one cup to another.

Take two paper cups and a long string. Poke a hole in each cup. Tie the string to both. Pull it tight. Talk into one cup. Your friend puts the other cup to an ear.

Your voice shakes the cup. The cup shakes the string. The string shakes the other cup. Your friend hears you! Let the string go loose. Now it does not work. The wiggle needs a tight string.

Think it through
Tight String, Loose String

Two kids hold cups joined by a string. They step apart until the string is tight. One whispers a secret. The other hears it and laughs. Then they step closer. The string sags. The whisper is gone.

Why does the string telephone stop working when the string sags?

Words to know
tight
pulled straight with no sag or slack
Check yourself

1. What carries the voice between the cups?

Why: The tight string carries the wiggle from one cup to the other.

2. The string goes loose. What happens?

Why: A loose string cannot carry the wiggle. The voice does not get through.

3. What must you do to the string first?

Why: The string must be tight so the wiggle can travel along it.
3.8

Sending a Message

Main ideaPeople use sounds like bells and whistles to send messages far away.

A sound can carry a message. A school bell says, come in. A whistle at recess says, stop and look. A fire alarm says, get out now. You do not need words.

Long ago, church bells told a whole town the time. Ships blow horns in fog so they do not crash. Sounds can travel farther than your voice.

You can make your own sound code. One clap means yes. Two claps mean no. Try it with a friend across the room.

Think it through
Two Claps Mean No

A class makes a clap code. One clap means yes. Two claps mean no. Three claps mean come here. The teacher is across the gym. She claps three times. All the kids run to her.

What could you use instead of claps to send the same code even farther?

Words to know
message
information sent from one person to another
Check yourself

1. What does a fire alarm tell you?

Why: A fire alarm is a sound message. It means leave the building right away.

2. Why do ships blow horns in fog?

Why: In fog, ships cannot see each other. The horn sound tells them where a ship is.

3. Two claps mean no. Your friend claps twice. What did they say?

Why: In the code, two claps mean no. Sounds can carry messages without words.

Light and Seeing

Waves
Big questionWhy can you see some things and not others, and where does light come from?
The story

Behind the Bedsheet

A lamp, a sheet and two hands can make a whole show.

Leo and his sister hang a white bedsheet across the doorway. Behind it, they set a lamp on the floor. Grandma sits on the other side. The lights in the room go off. The sheet glows soft and white.

Leo puts his hands between the lamp and the sheet. On Grandma's side, a dark dog appears! Its ears wiggle. It opens its mouth. Grandma laughs. Leo's hands block the light. Where the light cannot go, the sheet stays dark.

His sister moves her hand close to the lamp. Her rabbit shadow grows huge. She moves it near the sheet. The rabbit shrinks and gets sharp. Then Leo turns the lamp off. Everything is black. No lamp, no shadows, no show.

Leo turns the lamp back on. He holds up a glass of water. The sheet shows a fuzzy, bright shape, not a dark one. Light goes through the glass. Leo thinks: light goes through some things and is stopped by others.

Talk about itThe rabbit shadow got bigger when the hand moved closer to the lamp. Why do you think that happened?
Section 1

Light Lets Us See

4.1

The Dark Room

Main ideaYou need light to see anything; in a truly dark room you see nothing.

Go into a closet. Shut the door. Wait. Can you see your hand? If no light gets in, you cannot. Your eyes need to see. No light means no seeing.

Open the door a crack. A little light comes in. Now you see shapes. Open it wide. Now you see colors too. More light lets you see more.

Think it through
The Closet Test

A girl goes into a closet with a red ball. She shuts the door. It is very dark. She holds the ball right in front of her face. She cannot see it at all. She opens the door a tiny bit. Now she sees a dark round shape. She opens it wide. Now the ball looks red.

Why could she not see the red ball with the door shut?

Words to know
light
what lets your eyes see; it comes from the sun, fire and bulbs
Check yourself

1. What do your eyes need to see?

Why: Eyes only work when light reaches them. In a truly dark room you see nothing.

2. The closet door opens a crack. What do you see first?

Why: A little light shows shapes. You need more light to see colors well.

3. A red ball is in a totally dark room. What color does it look?

Why: With no light, nothing can be seen, not even a bright red ball.
4.2

Where Light Comes From

Main ideaThe sun, fire, and light bulbs make light; most things only bounce it.

Some things make their own light. The sun does. A candle flame does. A light bulb does. Each one is a light . It shines.

Most things do not shine. A book does not. Your hand does not. You see them because light from a source hits them and bounces to your eyes. The moon does not shine on its own. Sunlight bounces off it.

The sun is our biggest light source. It lights the whole day. At night we use bulbs, flashlights and fires.

Think it through
Shines or Does Not Shine

Sort these into two piles. Pile one: things that make their own light. Pile two: things you only see because light bounces off them. Here is the list: a candle, a rock, the sun, a mirror, a flashlight, a shoe, a firefly, the moon.

Which pile does the moon go in? Why?

Words to know
source
a thing that makes its own light, like the sun or a bulb
Check yourself

1. Which one makes its own light?

Why: A flame is a light source. A book and a rock only bounce light.

2. Why can you see a book?

Why: A book does not make light. Light from a source hits it and bounces into your eyes.

3. Does the moon make its own light?

Why: The moon is a big rock. We see it because the sun's light bounces off it.
Section 2

Through, Blocked, Shadow

4.3

Light Goes Through Glass

Main ideaSome materials let light through, some let a little through, and some block it.

Hold a window glass up to a lamp. You see the lamp clearly. Glass lets light through. Now hold up wax paper. You see a glow, but no clear lamp. Wax paper lets a little light through.

Now hold up a wood board. You see nothing. Wood can light. Try more things: a plastic bag, a leaf, a shirt, a cup. Sort them into three groups: all, a little, none.

Think it through
Three Cups

Three cups sit in a row on a sunny windowsill. One is clear glass. One is frosty white plastic. One is a metal mug. Behind each cup is a toy. You can see the toy behind the glass. You see a fuzzy shape behind the white cup. You see no toy behind the mug.

Which cup blocks the light? Which lets a little light through?

Words to know
block
to stop light so it cannot go through
Check yourself

1. Which lets light through best?

Why: Clear glass lets almost all light through. You see the lamp clearly.

2. You hold wax paper up to a lamp. What do you see?

Why: Wax paper lets some light through, but it scatters it. You see a glow, not a clear shape.

3. What does a wood board do to light?

Why: Wood blocks light. No light gets through, so you see nothing behind it.
4.4

Shadows

Main ideaA shadow is the dark spot where something has blocked the light.

Stand in the sun. Look down. A dark shape is on the ground. It is your . Your body blocks the sunlight. The ground behind you stays dark.

Shadows change. Move close to a lamp. Your shadow grows big. Move away. It shrinks. Turn off the lamp. The shadow is gone. No light means no shadow.

In the morning, the sun is low. Shadows are long. At noon, the sun is high. Shadows are short. Chalk your shadow at recess. Check it after lunch.

Think it through
Chalk the Shadow

At 9 in the morning, a class draws chalk around a flagpole's shadow. It is long and points toward the school. At noon they look again. The shadow is short. It points a different way. At 3 in the afternoon, it is long again, pointing away from the school.

Why did the flagpole's shadow get short at noon?

Words to know
shadow
a dark spot made when something blocks light
Check yourself

1. What makes a shadow?

Why: A shadow is the dark place where light is blocked.

2. You turn off the only lamp. What happens to your shadow?

Why: No light means no shadow. A shadow is only a spot with light blocked.

3. When are shadows the shortest?

Why: At noon the sun is high in the sky. A high sun makes short shadows.
Section 3

Bouncing and Sending

4.5

Mirrors Bounce Light

Main ideaA mirror bounces light back so you can see yourself or send a beam.

A is very smooth and shiny. Light hits it and bounces straight back. That is why you see your face in it. The light from your face bounces to your eyes.

Shine a flashlight at a mirror. Tilt the mirror. The bright spot moves on the wall. You can aim light with a mirror. Kids can bounce a sun spot onto the ceiling.

Still water can act like a mirror. Look in a puddle. You see the sky. Wind makes ripples. Now the sky looks broken up.

Think it through
Sun Spot on the Ceiling

Sun comes in the window. A boy holds a small mirror in the sunbeam. A bright spot appears on the ceiling. He tilts the mirror a little. The spot slides across the ceiling. He tilts it more. The spot lands on the wall.

How could two kids with mirrors pass one sun spot from the window to the far corner?

Words to know
mirror
a smooth shiny surface that bounces light back
Check yourself

1. What does a mirror do to light?

Why: A mirror is smooth and shiny. Light bounces off it instead of going through.

2. You tilt a mirror in a sunbeam. What happens to the bright spot?

Why: Tilting the mirror changes where the light bounces. The spot moves.

3. Why does a puddle show the sky?

Why: Smooth, still water bounces light back like a mirror. Ripples break the picture up.
4.6

Blink a Message

Main ideaLight can carry a message, like a flashlight blink or a lighthouse beam.

You can talk with light. Make a code. One blink means yes. Two blinks mean no. Blink a flashlight to a friend across a dark yard. They blink back. No sound needed.

A is a tall tower with a big light. Its beam turns at night. Ships see it far out on the water. The light says: land is here, stay away from the rocks. Chicago has a lighthouse at its harbor on Lake Michigan.

Traffic lights send messages too. Red says stop. Green says go. You read the light before you cross.

Think it through
Blinks Across the Yard

Two friends live across a dark yard. They agree: one blink means yes, two blinks mean no, three blinks mean come over. One night, a flashlight in the window blinks three times. The other friend puts on shoes and runs over.

Why does a light code work at night but a wave of the hand does not?

Words to know
lighthouse
a tall tower with a bright light that warns ships
Check yourself

1. What does a lighthouse tell ships?

Why: The lighthouse beam warns ships that the shore and rocks are near.

2. Two blinks mean no. The flashlight blinks twice. The message is:

Why: In the code, two blinks mean no. Light can carry a message just like sound can.

3. Which light sends the message 'stop'?

Why: A red traffic light means stop. Green means go.
4.7

The Telescope Collects Light

Main ideaA telescope gathers lots of light so we can see faint, far things like the moon.

Stars are far away. Their light is faint. Your eye is small. It only catches a little light. A has a big lens or mirror. It catches much more light.

Look at the moon through a telescope. It looks bigger and brighter. You see bumps and holes. Those holes are craters. Long ago, Galileo did this. He was surprised the moon was not smooth.

The telescope on this site does the same job. It collects light. Then it sends it to your eye or a camera.

From the source
Galileo Looks at the Moon
In 1610, Galileo wrote that the moon, seen through his telescope, was not smooth and even as many had believed. Instead, he said, it was rough and uneven, full of hollows and bumps, like the face of the Earth with its mountains and valleys.
Galileo Galilei, Sidereus Nuncius (The Starry Messenger), 1610 (retold) (paraphrased)

What did the telescope let Galileo see that his eyes alone could not?

Words to know
telescope
a tool that collects light to show far-away things bigger
Check yourself

1. What does a telescope do with light?

Why: A telescope has a big lens or mirror that gathers more light than your eye can.

2. Through a telescope, the moon looks:

Why: A telescope collects more light and makes the moon look bigger and brighter.

3. What did Galileo see on the moon?

Why: Galileo saw the moon was rough, with bumps and hollows, not smooth.
