Twenty-three rooms taught you to see what a text was built to make you accept. This one asks you to build one — with somebody else's words in your hands, a claim of your own to defend, and nobody checking whether you applied your own rules. The work has four parts: find sources and judge them, make a claim the evidence can actually carry, quote in a way the person quoted would accept, and answer the strongest objection rather than the easiest. Four checks on one sentence

One sentence with a citation on it. It reads well, it names a real study, and every word in it is true.

Twenty-three rooms taught you to run one test on somebody else’s writing: what is this built to make me accept, and does it earn it. This room hands you the instruments and asks you to write. Four checks, in order: judge the source, size the claim to the evidence, quote so the author would accept your use of their words, and answer the objection you would raise against yourself. Each one is easy to skip, because skipping leaves no mark on the page.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

I cited it, so I am covered.

A citation says where the words came from. It says nothing about whether the source knew this subject, who paid for the study, how old it is, or whether the sentence you built on it is the size the study supports. A citation is the start of the work, not a receipt proving it was done.

A citation locates a source. It does not judge one.

⚠️ People often think…

The bigger claim is the stronger paper.

A claim can only be defended out to the edge of its evidence, so every inch past that edge is ground you have to give up the moment anybody pushes. “Cities” dies to one city where it failed; “four mid-sized cities over six years” does not die at all. The narrow claim is not the modest one — it is the one that survives contact.

Claim the ground you can hold. The rest is a gift to whoever disagrees.

⚠️ People often think…

I mentioned the other side, so I dealt with it.

Naming an objection and answering it are different acts, and the gap between them is where most papers quietly fail. Worse, the version easiest to answer is almost never the version anybody actually holds — so beating it proves nothing and signals to any informed reader that you did not understand the disagreement.

Answer the version your opponent would recognize as theirs.

⚠️ People often think…

Nobody is going to check any of this.

True, and that is the point of the room rather than an objection to it. Every instrument in this course was taught as a defense against writing somebody else aimed at you; from here on you are also the person aiming. The four checks are the only thing standing between an argument you believe and an argument you can defend — and you are the only one who will ever know whether you ran them.

Unchecked work is not faster. It is undefended.

Judge it, size it, quote it, answer it

1 Watch one

A student writes: “Research proves phone bans improve grades (Ortiz 2014).” Ortiz surveyed one school for one term and found a small rise in one subject. Repair it.

  1. Judge the source: 2014 is twelve years old on a question about phones, and one school for one term is a narrow base — usable, but only for a narrow sentence.
  2. “Research proves” claims a settled body of work. One survey is not a body of work, and surveys do not prove — they find.
  3. “Improve grades” widens one subject into all of them. Say the subject, and say the size: a small rise.
  4. Repaired: “In one school over one term, Ortiz (2014) found a small rise in math results after a phone ban.” Longer, narrower, and nothing in it can be taken from you.
2 Do one with me

Name the check each failure breaks.

  1. Quoting a study funded by the industry it clears, without saying so, fails
  2. Turning “in four cities” into “in cities” fails
  3. Cutting “in the short term” off the end of a quoted sentence fails
  4. Answering the weakest version of the disagreement fails

Helpful Hints

⚡ The one idea

You now hold every instrument this course taught you to catch. A nominalization hides an actor just as well in your essay as in a council report; an ellipsis removes a qualification just as cleanly from a source as from a press release. Nothing about the instruments changed — only who is holding them, and the fact that nobody is checking. The four checks below are what an honest argument does anyway, and they are also the only reason to trust the one you are about to write.

🪜 The four checks

  • 1 · The source. Is the expertise relevant, independent, current — and is this the original?
  • 2 · The claim. Is it the size of the evidence you actually have?
  • 3 · The quotation. Would the person quoted accept your version as fair?
  • 4 · The objection. Have you answered the strongest one, or the easiest?

⚖️ Sizing the claim

A claim's strength is the ratio between what it asserts and what you can support. Write the biggest claim you can defend, not the biggest one you believe — those are different, and the gap between them is where essays are lost. If your evidence is three studies in one country over two years, your claim is about that country and those years, and saying so is not timidity. It is the difference between a position and a target.

🛡️ The strongest objection

Find the best argument against your claim and answer that one. Answering a weak version is a straw man, a reader can see it, and it costs you the credit a concession was meant to buy. If the strongest objection turns out to be unanswerable, you have not wasted your time — you have found out that your claim was the wrong size, which is the most useful thing research can tell you.

🎯 How you will be asked

  • “Develop a position on this issue, supported by evidence.”
  • “Synthesize at least three of the provided sources.”
  • “Evaluate the reliability of Source B.”
  • “Address an objection to your own position.”
  • “Cite your sources accurately and consistently.”

✅ Before you hand it in, can you…

  • Say who funded each source, or that you could not find out?
  • Point at the words in your claim that limit it?
  • Show that every quotation would satisfy the person you took it from?
  • Name the strongest argument against you, in its best form?
  • Say what would change your mind?
