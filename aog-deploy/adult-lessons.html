From the K–12 rooms. Mirrors the Classroom Compact that opens every K-12 year — the room is made safe before any content, on the same four agreements.

Anchor idea · post it · The Compact

The Compact is the agreement we make about how this room will work. It is not a list of rules. It is the relational contract that makes the rest of the work possible. Five clauses. Co-authored. Posted in the room for all twelve sessions. 1. What is said here, stays here. The exceptions are the legal exceptions — imminent harm, abuse, court order — named at the start. 2. You may pass at any time, at any prompt, without explanation. 3. We do not give advice unless asked. We listen. 4. We do not narrate other people's stories. We speak only to our own. 5. The work is between you and the page. The room witnesses; it does not extract.

Before the session · facilitator preparation

Session 1 sets the tone for every session that follows. The first ten minutes are disproportionately load-bearing. Arrive in the room thirty minutes early. Make sure the chairs are arranged before participants enter — not while they are entering. Have water already poured, tissues already placed. The room should feel held when the first participant walks in, before you have said a word.

Many participants arrive at Session 1 with some combination of nervousness, defensiveness, skepticism, and quiet hope. None of these are problems. They are the normal arrival states for adults entering a group that asks them to do interior work. Do not try to talk anyone out of any of them. Do not over-promise what the group will deliver. Hold the structure; let the structure do the work.

If a participant does not show: do not call them mid-session. Brief acknowledgment to the group ("We're missing one. We'll begin without them and welcome them if they arrive"), then proceed. Make the touchpoint call within 24 hours after the session, not during.

The ninety minutes

  1. 01Arrival & Grounding8 min

    As participants enter, greet each by name if you know them; introduce yourself simply if you do not. Indicate where they may sit — any open chair. Do not direct seating beyond that. Wait until all expected participants are seated, then begin.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Welcome. My name is [name]. This is [co-facilitator's name]. We're going to begin in a moment. The first thing we'll do — and the first thing we'll do at every session — is take ninety seconds to arrive. You don't have to do anything. You just have to be here. Both feet on the floor if you can. One breath slower than usual. We'll do this together."

    Pause for ninety seconds. Time it. The temptation will be to cut it short. Do not.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Around the circle, one word — just one — for how you arrived today. There is no right word. Anything you say is welcome. You may pass if you don't have a word. I'll go first."

    Primary facilitator gives a real one-word answer (e.g., "Tired," "Curious," "Cautious," "Steady"). Co-facilitator follows. Then around the circle. Some participants will pass. Honor it without comment. The check-in is data, not performance.

    In-session check · First Check-In
    ObserveA participant who cannot make eye contact, whose word is barely audible, or who passes silently after a long uncomfortable pause.
    May indicateAcute social anxiety, dissociation, or a participant who is not sure they should be in the room. None of these are immediate concerns at S1. They are early data.
    ActionNote privately. Co-facilitator tracks this participant for the rest of the session. No public address.
  2. 02Program Orientation5 min
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Twelve sessions. Ninety minutes each. Once a week. Today is Session 1. The architecture is deliberate — each session builds on the last, and we don't reorder them or skip them. The structure is part of how this works. If you miss a session, we'll catch you up before the next one. The cohort is closed after today, which means no one new joins after Session 1, and we keep going as a group from here to Session 12."
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"This is not a substitute for therapy if you're in therapy. It's not a substitute for medication if you're on medication. It's not a substitute for AA, NA, SMART Recovery, or any other program you're already in. It's a structured space — twelve sessions, four phases — to do specific work on forgiveness, grace, and self-compassion. Some of that work will be about other people. Most of it, by Session 7, will be about you."

    Pause briefly. Let the last sentence settle. Then continue.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Before we go further, I want to name what this is not. This is not a place where you have to tell the story of your worst moment. We will never ask you to do that. The work we do here operates on what you've been carrying without ever requiring that you narrate exactly what put it there. You are welcome to share. You are never required to. Most of the writing we do in this group is private — you write, you keep what you wrote, and what you choose to share with the group is your choice every time."
  3. 03The Compact — Naming What This Room Is8 min

    Move physically to the wall where Anchor Card 1 is posted. The physical movement signals that the work is starting. Read the five clauses aloud, slowly, with eye contact around the room.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"This is the Compact. Five clauses. We co-author it today, and it stays posted on this wall through Session 12. Every session, when something gets hard or unclear, we come back to this card. Let me read what's on it, and then we'll add anything you think is missing."

    Read the five clauses aloud, one at a time. Pause briefly after each. After the fifth:

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Anything missing? Is there something you need from this room — from me, from each other — that isn't on this card?"

    Allow 60 seconds of silence if needed. Most groups will have one or two additions — common ones include "we don't fix each other" or "what's said in tears stays in the room." Add what is offered to the bottom of the printed Compact (use a marker; the addition is visible). Do not edit. The participants are co-authoring; respect their words.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Last piece. Confidentiality has limits — the same limits any clinical group has. If something comes up that suggests imminent harm to you, imminent harm to someone else, or abuse of a child or vulnerable adult, I'm required to act on it. That's the law and it's our standard. Outside those exceptions: what's said here, stays here. We protect each other's stories."
  4. 04The Four Foundational Distinctions12 min
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"The whole program rests on four distinctions. We'll meet each of them many times across the twelve weeks. Today I'm going to name them once, briefly, so you have a map of where we're going. You don't have to remember them. They'll come back."

    Write each on a flipchart or board as you say it. Leave them visible through the rest of Session 1.

    First. Identity is not behavior. A mistake is something I did. It is not who I am. The harm I caused is real, and what I did matters — and it does not exhaust who I am or determine what I am capable of. This is the bedrock of the program. We come back to it constantly.

    Second. Feelings are messengers. Every feeling carries information. Naming a feeling makes it workable. No feeling is bad — although what we do with a feeling can be helpful or unhelpful. Anger is a messenger. Shame is a messenger. Grief is a messenger. We learn to read what they are telling us before we decide what to do with what they are saying.

    Third. There is more than one voice inside. The voice that says "You'll never get this right" is not the only voice. The voice that says "That was the wrong choice and I can do better starting now" is also a voice. We get to choose which one we listen to. We don't get to silence either of them. We do get to choose which one we let lead.

    Fourth. You can put it down. Not forget it. Not pretend it didn't happen. Not minimize what was real. Choose, for today, not to carry it the same way you've been carrying it. We will spend most of Phase II naming what you are carrying. We will spend most of Phase III deciding what to do with each thing. Session 12 is the audit — what stays, what goes, what stays but in a different form.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"That's the map. We'll meet each of these many times. You don't have to remember them. The room will remind you."
  5. 05What This Group Will Ask of You — and Will Not8 min
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Three things this group will ask of you. First, that you show up. Twelve sessions; missing more than two breaks the architecture. Second, that you write — privately, in your workbook, whether or not anyone else ever sees it. The writing is where the work happens. Third, that you tell the truth in your writing. You can pass on sharing. You cannot productively lie on the page. The page is between you and you."
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Three things this group will not ask of you. We will not ask you to tell us your worst moment. We will not ask you to forgive someone you are not ready to forgive — forgiveness in this program is a process, not a performance, and we'll talk about what it actually means in Session 11. We will not ask you to reconcile with anyone you have decided not to reconcile with. Reconciliation is a separate question that we'll distinguish carefully from forgiveness."
    In-session check · The
    'Already Forgave Everyone' Participant
    ObserveA participant who, during this segment or at any point in S1, volunteers that they have already forgiven everyone — sometimes with a quick smile, sometimes with practiced spiritual language — and seems to want acknowledgment that they are 'ahead.'
    May indicatePerformative forgiveness, often pre-emptive, sometimes reinforced by the participant's faith community or twelve-step program in ways that have not yet metabolized. Not a problem; a clinical signal. The work of this program will, gently, surface what is underneath the performance.
    ActionNo public address. Note privately. Watch this participant especially in Sessions 6 (Compassionate Witness) and 7 (grief vs. resentment); these tend to be the points where performative forgiveness either deepens or breaks open.
  6. 06Practice — the Arrival Letter25 min
    The practice · The Arrival Letter

    Each participant has the workbook open to the Session 1 page. The prompt is at the top of the page. Twenty minutes of writing. Private. The letter is not collected, not read by anyone but the participant, and is sealed in the workbook envelope at the close of Session 1 to be reopened at Session 12 and not before. The prompt: "Write a letter to yourself — to the version of you who is sitting in this room right now, on Day One of this program. Tell that person what brought you here. Tell them what you are hoping for, even if you are afraid to hope. Tell them what you are afraid of. Tell them anything you want them to know on Day One that they may forget by Session 12. This letter is sealed today and reopened in twelve weeks. No one but you will read it. Tell the truth."

    Twenty minutes of protected writing time. The room is quiet. The two facilitators are seated, present, not writing themselves but visibly attentive. Tissues are within reach. Water is within reach. If a participant stops writing early, that is fine — they sit with what they wrote. If a participant cannot start, the co-facilitator approaches quietly:

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Even one sentence is enough. 'On Day One I was —' and then whatever the next word wants to be. You can write more later if you want."

    At minute 20: "Two minutes left." At minute 22: "Time to wrap up."

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Fold the letter in half. Place it in the envelope at the back of your workbook. Seal it. Write today's date on the outside of the envelope. We open these together — you open yours; I'm not reading it — at Session 12. Twelve weeks from today."

    This sealing-and-postponing ritual is doing several things at once. It is making the writing safe (no one will read it). It is creating a temporal anchor (the participant has now made a small commitment to be here at S12). It is establishing the program's relationship to time — that we are doing slow work, that what is done today is not finished today, that the audit at the end matters because the writing at the start was real.

  7. 07Discussion — One Sentence15 min
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"We don't share the letters. The letters are private. But around the circle, one sentence — not from the letter, not the content, just the experience: 'Writing the letter felt like —' and then whatever word or short phrase follows. You may pass. I'll go first."

    Primary facilitator goes first with a real one-sentence response (e.g., "Writing felt like beginning something I have been postponing"). Co-facilitator follows. Then around the circle. Hold the silence between speakers. Do not respond to each share with comment; nod, hold the room, let the next person speak.

    After the round: open three-to-five minutes for any reflections on Session 1 generally — the structure, the Compact, the four distinctions. Light touch. Do not let this become a debate. Do not let any participant dominate.

    In-session check · The Participant Who Has Already Started Doing the Work in the Letter
    ObserveA participant whose one-sentence response signals that the letter-writing went deeper than they expected — eyes are wet, voice is changed, breath is shorter.
    May indicateThe Arrival Letter has, for this participant, already begun to do what the letter is designed to do. This is not a problem. It is the program working faster than the average pace.
    ActionBrief eye contact, a small nod that signals 'I see you, I have you.' Do not extract more in the room. Co-facilitator approaches privately at the close: 'I noticed the writing landed. I'd like to check in briefly before next session — that's standard for me when I notice something landed hard.'
  8. 08Integration7 min
    Integration · what was done · what was not done

    WHAT WAS DONE TODAY: We arrived. We co-authored the Compact. We named the four foundational distinctions. We wrote a letter to ourselves and sealed it for twelve weeks. WHAT WAS NOT DONE TODAY: We did not begin the inward work. That starts in Session 2. We did not commit to anything except being here. We did not promise anyone we will or will not forgive anyone. We did not lock in any conclusions about what we will carry or put down — that work is across the next eleven sessions. WHAT YOU MAY NOTICE THIS WEEK: A small persistent quiet. The Arrival Letter may surface things you didn't know you were going to write. That is the page doing its work. You don't have to do anything about it. Just notice. We'll meet again next week.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"One sentence each, around the circle: 'I'm carrying out of this room — ' and then whatever word or short phrase follows. You may pass. I'll go first."

    Same closing round structure as the opening. Primary first. Around the circle. Pass is honored. The closing is shorter than the opening — it should feel like a clean exit, not a re-opening.

  9. 09Closing Exit2 min
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"What we did in this room stays in this room. What you carry out of this room is yours. The work continues — quietly — between now and next session. There is nothing you have to do with it. There is no homework. Just notice what stays with you. We meet again next week."

    The same words at every closing. By Session 4, the participants will be mouthing them with you. That is the intended shape of the holding.

Between sessions · facilitators

Within 24 hours: 20-minute co-facilitator debrief using the Facilitator Reflection Log (Appendix E). Cover every participant by name. Identify any clinical signals from the In-Session Scans. Note who passed at the opening and closing rounds. Note who appeared activated during the Arrival Letter. Within 48 hours: individual touchpoint phone call (5–10 min) for any participant flagged with a Tier 2 signal. Use the touchpoint script in Section 3.5. Document briefly in the participant's clinical record. Within one week: confirm Session 2 materials prepared. Anchor Concept Card 2 ("Identity Is Not Behavior") printed and ready to post. Read the Session 2 facilitator notes once before delivery, even if you have read them before. The protocol asks the facilitator to do the work it asks the participant to do — including arriving prepared each time.

Facilitator reflection · End of Session 1

Session 1 succeeds when, by the closing, every participant has spoken at least once, the Compact is posted with their additions, the Arrival Letters are sealed, and the room has registered — through some combination of silence, weight, and small unforced eye contact — that this is going to be different from a workshop. Session 1 fails (rare, but it happens) when one participant has dominated the room, when the Compact was a recitation rather than a co-authoring, when the facilitator over-talked and under-held. If S1 has not gone well, brief the consulting clinician within 24 hours. The intervention is recoverable from a difficult S1 only if the difficulty is named and addressed before S2. Either way: write your own one-sentence reflection. 'I am carrying out of this session — ' Date it. File it in the Facilitator Reflection Log. We meet again — with the participants — in seven days.

Anchor idea · post it · Identity Is Not Behavior

The mistake is something I did. It is not who I am. What I did matters. The harm was real. The accountability is real. And — none of it exhausts who I am or determines what I am capable of. When identity collapses into behavior, two things go wrong at once: I cannot be honest about what I did (because honesty would mean I am a permanent monster), and I cannot change (because what is essential cannot be revised). The distinction is not a trick. It is the bedrock. Every other piece of work in this program rests on it.

Before the session · facilitator preparation

This is the first session that asks participants to do real interior work. The work itself is not yet hard; the distinction is. Participants in clinical populations have often been trained — by their illness, by their family system, by the carceral or treatment systems they have moved through — to either collapse identity into behavior ("I am a junkie / a felon / a failure / an addict") or to defend against the collapse so vigorously that they cannot acknowledge the behavior at all ("none of that was really me").

Both moves are dead ends. The Identity / Behavior distinction is a third way: full ownership of the behavior, full preservation of the larger identity. Acknowledge what you did. Do not let it become who you are.

Watch in particular for the participant who, when invited to make this distinction, becomes anxious — sometimes hostile. Identity collapse is, paradoxically, an organizing principle for some participants. Releasing it can briefly destabilize. Hold the room. The destabilization passes.

If I am the harm I caused, then there is no version of me that could have done differently — and no version of me that can do differently going forward. The collapse protects us from accountability and prevents change at the same time. It is a closed loop."

  4. 04Direct Instruction — the Two Statements10 min
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"There are two ways to say what happened. They sound similar. They are operationally different."

    Write on the board, large:

    I-STATEMENT (identity collapse):

    "I am a liar." "I am a bad father." "I am an addict." "I am damaged."

    B-STATEMENT (behavior held without identity collapse):

    "I lied to my partner about the money." "I missed three of my son's birthdays." "I used cocaine for six years." "I survived something that hurt me."

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Notice what the B-statement does. It is more specific. It holds the behavior — the actual thing, with its actual contours — without converting the person into the thing. The I-statement seems harder, more honest. It is not. It is more crushing, but crushing is not the same as honest. The I-statement actually protects you from looking at what you did, because it converts the looking-at into self-loathing — and self-loathing is not the same as accountability."
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"This is not therapy-speak. It is operational. Recovery requires the B-statement. Twelve-step language sometimes uses the I-statement ("I am an alcoholic") as a shorthand — the meaning of that shorthand depends on the speaker; for some people it functions as a B-statement in disguise. We are not arguing with twelve-step language here. We are sharpening the distinction it sometimes blurs."
  5. 05Direct Instruction — When the Behavior Is Real10 min
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"The hardest part of this distinction is that the behavior we are trying to hold without collapsing into is sometimes — often — behavior we are not proud of. It is the thing we did. It is the thing we lost. It is the thing that hurt someone. The I-statement collapse is not random. It happens because the behavior was real, and because some part of us has decided that owning the full weight of it requires becoming it."
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"That move is understandable. It is also wrong. Becoming the behavior does not honor the harm. It removes you as a moral agent capable of doing differently. The person who caused the harm and the person who could do differently are the same person. If you erase that person — by collapsing them into the worst thing they did — you remove the only one who could repair anything."
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"This is why the bedrock matters. We hold the behavior, in its full weight, in its specific shape. We do not let it become the whole identity. Today's practice is in this exact direction."
  6. 06Practice — the I → B Translation25 min
    The practice · The I → B Translation

    Each participant writes privately. Two columns, drawn down a fresh page in the workbook. LEFT COLUMN: Three I-statements — three sentences that begin with 'I am — ' that name something you have been telling yourself about who you are. The harder ones. Not 'I am tired.' Closer to 'I am a failure as a parent.' 'I am someone who can't be trusted.' 'I am damaged goods.' Three of them. Five minutes. RIGHT COLUMN: For each I-statement, a B-statement. The behavior or experience underneath, named specifically, in its actual shape, without the identity collapse. 'I am a failure as a parent' becomes — for example — 'I missed my daughter's 14th and 15th birthdays. I have apologized twice. I have not changed the pattern.' Five minutes per translation; total practice is 20 minutes of writing. Underneath the right column, one more sentence: 'What the B-statement makes possible that the I-statement does not — is — '

    During the writing, the room is silent. The two facilitators are seated, attentive, not writing. Tissues within reach. If a participant freezes — pen down, eyes distant — the co-facilitator approaches quietly and says only:

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Even one is enough. Pick the lightest of the three. The hardest can wait."

Before the session · facilitator preparation

This session externalizes self-talk — the technical move underneath is straightforward CBT (cognitive defusion, in ACT terms). What makes it work in this curriculum is the framing: the voices are not symptoms to be eliminated; they are speakers in a long internal conversation. The Punisher (the harsh voice) is not the enemy. Some participants will arrive at this session having never paid sustained attention to their internal monologue. The practice meets each participant where they are.

Anchor idea · post it · Feelings as Messengers

Every feeling carries information. Naming a feeling makes it workable. No feeling is bad — although what we do with a feeling can be helpful or unhelpful. Anger is a messenger. Shame is a messenger. Grief is a messenger. Joy is a messenger. Even numbness is a messenger — it tells you something is being kept at a distance. The work is not to eliminate the feeling. It is to read what it is telling you, and then to choose what to do with the information.

Before the session · facilitator preparation

Phase II opens. The work turns inward. Session 4 establishes the basic operational stance toward emotional experience that the rest of Phase II will build on. Most adults arriving in clinical settings have one of two relationships to their emotions: they over-monitor and pathologize them (every feeling is a symptom), or they under-monitor and dismiss them ("I don't really do feelings"). Both stances make the inward work of Phase II difficult.

This session offers a third stance: the feeling is information. It is neither a symptom to be medicated away nor an inconvenience to be ignored. It is data — about what matters, about what is unresolved, about what is being approached or avoided. Read the data, then decide what to do.

This is not a session about emotion regulation per se. Regulation is downstream of naming. Most regulation strategies fail when they are applied to a feeling that has not yet been correctly named. The session focuses on the naming.

Anchor idea · post it · Grief vs. Resentment

Grief says: "I wish things had been different." It is a response to loss. Resentment says: "I blame." It is a response to perceived culpability — someone (sometimes me) is responsible, and the responsibility has not been resolved. Both can be appropriate. Both can be painful. They require different tools. Grief is moved through with mourning, time, witness, and ritual. Resentment is moved through with the work of forgiveness — which is different from reconciliation, and different from forgetting. When we apply the wrong tool — when we try to mourn what is actually resentment, or try to forgive what is actually grief — the work does not move. The weight does not lift. The distinction matters operationally, every day.

Before the session · facilitator preparation

The distinction is hard precisely because grief and resentment often coexist about the same object. A participant grieves the parent they lost AND resents the parent for what that parent did or did not do. Both are real. Both deserve their own tools. The work is not to choose between grief and resentment but to disentangle them so each can be addressed correctly.

Watch for two failure modes. First: resentment misnamed as grief. "I'm just so sad about what happened." If the sentence has a target — a specific other person whose specific actions are unresolved — it is at least partly resentment. Resentment named as grief stays stuck because mourning cannot dissolve culpability. Second: grief misnamed as resentment. "I'm so angry at her for dying." Sometimes this is real resentment about the dying. More often, especially with sudden or untimely death, the anger is grief that has not yet found its language. Grief named as resentment stays stuck because forgiveness work cannot resolve a loss that has not yet been mourned.

Anchor idea · post it · The Living Amend

An apology is a statement about the past. A living amend is a sustained behavioral commitment that addresses the pattern. The apology says: I am sorry for what I did. The living amend says: I am changing the pattern that produced what I did, and I am sustaining the change. Some harms are addressed by an apology. Patterns of harm are not. Patterns require living amends. The most important living amend, for many people, is the one owed to the self. Before the session · facilitator preparation

The Living Amend is borrowed in concept from the recovery community and adapted in this program for clinical use. The distinction between an apology and a living amend is operationally crucial: most adults who have caused harm in patterns have apologized many times, often sincerely, and have not changed the pattern. The repeated apology becomes itself a piece of the pattern — a way of acknowledging the harm without altering the conditions that produce it.

This session translates the inward work of Sessions 6–9 into outward, sustained behavioral commitment. The conceptual move is simple. The execution is hard. Many participants will, in the practice, articulate a living amend that is in fact another apology in disguise — "I'll really try harder this time." That is not a living amend. A living amend is a specific, measurable, sustained behavioral change. "I will not drink at family events for the next year, and I will leave any event where I cannot maintain that." That is a living amend. "I will speak to my daughter once a week, by phone or in person, for the next year, even when she does not return the contact." That is a living amend.

Watch for the participant whose living amend is grandiose ("I will become a different person"); too vague ("I will be more present"); or self-punishing ("I will deny myself X for Y years"). The Witness language helps here: a living amend is honest, fair, kind, without punishment — and it is specific enough that the participant will know whether they did it on any given day.

The most important pivot in this session is the living amend to the self. Adult clinical populations often have well-developed apology habits toward others and almost no concept of behavioral change toward themselves. The amend to the self is the operative form of the work this program has been building.

Anchor idea · post it · Forgiveness Is Not Reconciliation

Forgiveness is internal. It is the release of the verdict you have been holding against another person — or against yourself. Reconciliation is interpersonal. It is the restoration of a relationship. It requires conditions forgiveness does not — including the consent and capacity of the other person, and the safety of both parties. Forgiveness can happen without reconciliation. Reconciliation, to be real, requires more than forgiveness. Conflating the two has cost many people their safety, their integrity, or their forgiveness itself. The distinction is the difference between an inward gift you can give yourself and an outward arrangement that is not yours alone to make.

Before the session · facilitator preparation

This distinction is held until Session 11 by design. Earlier in the program, participants were not yet equipped to absorb that forgiveness can be complete without reconciliation, that reconciliation requires conditions forgiveness does not, that forgiveness is — first and most importantly — a gift to the self. The Compassionate Witness work in Sessions 6 and 8, the Living Amend in Session 10, are prerequisites for hearing this distinction without distortion.

Why participants need this distinction protected: many adults have been told — by family, by community, by spiritual or recovery frameworks — that forgiveness requires reconciliation. "You haven't really forgiven him until you can be in the same room with him." "Real forgiveness restores the relationship." These claims are sometimes well-meaning. They are also clinically dangerous, particularly for participants who have been harmed by people with whom reconciliation would be unsafe — survivors of intimate partner violence, abuse, sexual harm, sustained emotional abuse, betrayal that has not been repaired by the betrayer.

The clinical truth is operational. Forgiveness is the inward release of the verdict — it is something you can do alone, without the other person's participation, without their knowledge, sometimes after their death. Reconciliation is something you cannot do alone. Reconciliation requires the other person's accountability, capacity, safety, and good faith. Without those, reconciliation is not real reconciliation; it is a one-sided performance that puts the harmed party back in proximity to harm.

Watch for participants whose framework conflates the two. Hold the distinction firmly. Do not argue with their framework directly; let the operational distinction do its work in the practice.

Anchor idea · post it · The Audit

The Audit is the explicit, structured decision about what stays in the backpack going forward. Three categories: WHAT YOU PUT DOWN — items you are choosing not to carry the way you have been carrying them. WHAT YOU CARRY DIFFERENTLY — items you will continue to carry but in a changed form, with the Witness's voice, in the company of the work you have done. WHAT YOU CHOOSE TO CARRY — items that are appropriately yours and that you carry consciously, going forward, with the tools you have built. The Audit does not solve anything. It names what you have decided. The decision is the work. The Audit is followed by the reopening of the Arrival Letter — the version of you on Day One, met by the version of you twelve weeks later.

Before the session · facilitator preparation

Session 12 is the program's culmination. Everything Sessions 1–11 have built lands in the Audit. The architectural metaphor that has guided the program — the structure is the intervention — finds its closing form here. The Audit is not just a summary; it is the explicit, structured act of deciding. The decision matters more than what is decided.

Three things make Session 12 the highest-risk session of the program. First: the closure activates loss. For many participants, the cohort has been the most reliable structure in their lives for twelve weeks. Ending it produces grief. Some participants will recognize the grief; some will deflect it as anger or relief or numbness. Hold the room as the closure begins to land. Second: the Audit asks for explicit decisions about material the participant has been working with for weeks. Some decisions will be difficult or destabilizing. Third: the reopening of the Arrival Letter from Session 1 is, for many participants, an emotionally significant moment. The version of them on Day One will sometimes be barely recognizable. Sometimes the recognition is welcome and produces visible relief. Sometimes the recognition produces grief — for the version of themselves that has receded, or for the cost of the work they have done. Both responses are appropriate.

Read the Session 12 protocol before delivery. Time yourself in advance — the session has more components than any prior session and will run long if not held to time. Bring extra tissues. Have water visible in multiple locations. Have crisis resource cards visible. Have the post-program contact list pre-filled with each participant's named contact and the date of that contact.

Participate fully. The facilitator is present in the room as a participant in the closure — within the limits of the role. You may, in the closing round, name what you have carried out of leading the cohort. This is not over-sharing; it is appropriate to closure. Do not, however, surrender the structure. The structure is what allows the closure to be safe. Hold both.

  4. 04Practice — the Audit25 min
    The practice · The Audit

    Open the workbook to the Audit worksheet. The worksheet has three columns: PUT DOWN · CARRY DIFFERENTLY · CHOOSE TO CARRY. Two minutes to read the columns and to read your inventory once through. Item by item, place each on your inventory into one of the three columns. Use abbreviations or short phrases — the column placement is the operative decision; the language can be brief. Some items will go in more than one column, and that is fine — note both with a small notation indicating which is primary. Fifteen minutes. After every item is placed: read your three columns. Without changing anything, read them slowly, all the way through. Three minutes. Final question, one sentence per column: "The reason I am putting these down is —" "The reason I am carrying these differently is —" "The reason I am choosing to carry these is —" Five minutes.

    During the practice, both facilitators are scanning. The Audit is the most demanding piece of writing in the program. Many participants will encounter a moment in which they realize they cannot put down something they thought they would put down — or that they are putting down something they had not planned to release. Both are useful. Hold the room.

    In-session check · The Audit That Cannot Be Done
    ObserveA participant who, after fifteen minutes, has placed only one or two items, or who has placed every item in the same column.
    May indicateAcute closure-related distress, or a participant who cannot make the explicit decisions the Audit requires. Sometimes both.
    ActionCo-facilitator approaches gently after the first 10 minutes if the pen has been still. "You don't have to finish the Audit today. The decisions can continue after the program ends. Place what you can. The columns are patient." Tier 2 protocol if dissociation observed. Schedule individual touchpoint within 48 hours regardless.

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"In Session 1 — twelve weeks ago — you wrote a letter to yourself. You sealed the letter in the envelope at the back of your workbook. The envelope has been sitting there for twelve weeks. Today the version of you who wrote that letter meets the version of you who has been through this program."
    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Take out the envelope. Look at the date you wrote on it. Twelve weeks ago. When you are ready, open it. Read the letter, slowly, once through. Take your time. There is no rush."

    After seven minutes:

    The lesson · say it or adapt it"Fold the letter. Place it back in the envelope. The letter is yours. Carry it with you, or leave it in the workbook, or do something else with it altogether. That is your decision."
    In-session check · The Letter That Lands Hard
    ObserveA participant whose reading of the letter produces visible distress — sobbing, breathing changes, face in hands.
    May indicateReal recognition of distance between Day One and Day Eighty-Four. The recognition can land as relief, as grief, or as both. The activation is appropriate to the moment but may exceed the room's capacity if it continues.
    ActionCo-facilitator approaches quietly: "You're here. The letter is yours. Take whatever time you need." Tier 2 protocol if activation continues beyond regulation. Same-day clinician contact for any participant who continues to be acutely distressed at session close.
