Scenario Cards

Room 207 · Grades 11–12 · two cards a lesson

These scenario cards are designed for discussion, pair practice, and reflection across all units. Two cards accompany each lesson, so teachers can choose the situation that best fits the group — or use both.

No student is ever required to share personal content in response to a scenario card. The Reflection Pass is available at all times.

Some cards imagine the cohort a few years on — in college, a first job, a graduate program. Names you will not find in the novel (Layla, Jonah, Ryn, Nia, Fatima) are not in the cohort; they carry the adult scenarios.

★★ High-disclosure cards. Cards marked ★★ carry the highest disclosure risk. Review the standing safety note in the lesson plan and confirm counselor presence before using them.

THE EXIT INTERVIEW

B5-1A

THE EXIT INTERVIEW

The situation

Sofia is a semester away from graduation. Her college essay identified herself primarily by her academic achievements and extracurricular leadership. Now, sitting with the Exit Interview, she is being asked: 'Who were you becoming here — beneath the record?' She stares at the question. She has a very good answer about her record. She does not know how to answer about herself.

Discussion questions
  1. What is the Exit Interview asking that Sofia's application did not ask — and why is that harder to answer?
  2. Sofia has a clear answer about her record and almost no answer about herself. What has she been building — and what has she been neglecting?
  3. The Exit Interview asks you to name what you are releasing, carrying forward, and choosing to build. What is the purpose of each of those three things?
  4. Begin your own Exit Interview: what identity are you releasing as you leave this building — and why?

THE EXIT INTERVIEW

B5-1B

THE EXIT INTERVIEW

The situation

When Darius fills out his Exit Interview, the hardest section is 'what I am releasing.' He has been the class clown since sixth grade. It protected him from taking things seriously enough to be disappointed by them. He has used it to manage anxiety, deflect from vulnerability, and stay likable across social groups. 'I don't think that's who I want to be anymore,' he writes. 'But I don't know who I am without it.'

Discussion questions
  1. What function has the 'class clown' identity served for Darius — and what has it cost him?
  2. Darius says 'I don't know who I am without it.' What does that feel like — and what does it make possible?
  3. Releasing an identity is not the same as erasing it. What would it mean for Darius to carry the humor forward without being defined by it?
  4. What identity have you been performing in high school that you want to release or revise as you move forward?

Lesson 2

MAPPING YOUR VALUES

B5-2A

MAPPING YOUR VALUES

The situation

When Layla got into college, she posted a carefully worded, emotionally measured celebration on social media. Then she went to her room and cried with relief for twenty minutes. The post was real — she was grateful. But it was also curated — she didn't show the tears, the fear that had preceded the acceptance, or the complicated feelings about leaving. 'I showed them the best version of my real reaction,' she said. 'Is that still authentic?'

Discussion questions
  1. Layla showed a real reaction — just not all of it. Where is the line between appropriate curation and inauthenticity?
  2. Layla's question — 'is that still authentic?' — is genuinely complicated. What criteria would you use to answer it?
  3. What would radical authenticity have looked like for Layla in this moment — and would it have been better?
  4. As you approach your own significant transitions, what is the difference between sharing genuinely and sharing everything?

THE AUTHENTICITY SPECTRUM

B5-2B

MAPPING YOUR VALUES

The situation

Theo is discovering that he performs differently for each of the audiences in his life: more earnest with his parents, more ironic with his friends, more serious in academic settings, more relaxed with his girlfriend. Each version feels like him. None of them is all of him. 'I'm not being fake with anyone,' he says. 'But I'm not being fully real with anyone either.'

Discussion questions
  1. Is Theo's experience of performing differently across contexts inauthenticity — or sophistication?
  2. Theo says 'I'm not being fake but I'm not being fully real.' What is the difference between those two?
  3. Audience collapse — the collapse of all these separate performances into one unified self — is described as both terrifying and liberating. Why both?
  4. What would it mean for Theo to find one person with whom he is his full self — and what would that require?

VALUES UNDER FIRE

B5-3A

VALUES UNDER FIRE

The situation

Marcus's internship was at a company with a culture of constant availability — texts at 10pm, expectations of weekend responses, praise for those who never seemed to stop. He was good at the work. He also noticed that the people who had been there longest seemed depleted in a way he couldn't name. He asked a senior manager: 'How do you maintain yourself in this environment?' The manager laughed for a moment too long, then said: 'You learn to need less.'

Discussion questions
  1. What does 'you learn to need less' mean — and is it a sustainable answer?
  2. Marcus is observing something about this workplace culture from the outside. What is he seeing that long-timers may have stopped seeing?
  3. How do your Integrity Anchor values translate to an environment that might push against them?
  4. Design a set of personal professional non-negotiables — things you will not trade in any professional environment. Start with three.

VALUES UNDER WORKPLACE PRESSURE

B5-3B

VALUES UNDER FIRE

The situation

In her first professional role, Amara is asked to produce a report using data she knows to be selectively chosen. Her supervisor assures her this is normal — 'everyone does it.' Amara's Integrity Anchor includes honesty. She knows producing the report would not make her a liar. She also knows it would make her complicit. She asks for a meeting. Her supervisor is surprised anyone asked.

Discussion questions
  1. What is Amara's Integrity Anchor telling her — and what is the organizational pressure telling her?
  2. Amara knows the difference between being a liar and being complicit. Why does that distinction matter?
  3. She asks for a meeting. What does that move require — and what does it risk?
  4. Design the conversation Amara might have in that meeting — one that is honest, non-adversarial, and professionally competent.

VALUES IN THE WORKPLACE

B5-4A

VALUES IN THE WORKPLACE

The situation

Priya received the only rejection she has ever received — from her first-choice college. She has received acceptances from two excellent alternatives. She spends two weeks unable to feel the acceptances. 'I know the rejection is not a verdict about me,' she says. 'I cannot feel that it is not.' She has always known her worth and her achievements were different things. This is the first time the difference has had a cost.

Discussion questions
  1. Priya knows the rejection is not a verdict but cannot feel that. Why is the distance between knowing and feeling significant here?
  2. Priya's identity has been consistently confirmed by her achievements. What is being tested now — and what does it need?
  3. The rejection is from one program's selection process — not from who Priya is. What would it take for that distinction to land?
  4. Design a stable sense of identity that does not require confirmation from external outcomes. What does it rest on?

WORTH BEYOND ACHIEVEMENT

B5-4B

VALUES IN THE WORKPLACE

The situation

Jonah spent four years as the person who was going to be a doctor. It organized his choices, his courses, his identity in his family. In senior year he realizes — quietly, then with growing certainty — that he does not want to be a doctor. He wants to study urban planning. Saying it out loud feels like dismantling who he has been. 'I don't know who I am,' he says, 'if I'm not on the path I promised everyone I was on.'

Discussion questions
  1. What is Jonah confusing his identity with — and what is his actual identity beneath it?
  2. The 'path' organized Jonah's choices and relationships. What happens to those when the path changes?
  3. Jonah says he feels like he is dismantling who he has been. Is he — or is he discovering who he actually is?
  4. What would Jonah need to believe about himself for the change in direction to feel like growth rather than failure?

THE NEUROSCIENCE OF MORAL DEVELOPMENT

B5-5A

THE NEUROSCIENCE OF MORAL DEVELOPMENT

The situation

For three years, Ryn has been deferring to her parents on every significant decision. Not because she doesn't have opinions — she does — but because it's easier and because they're usually right. Now she has a decision they disagree with: she wants to take a gap year before college. She knows what they will say. She also knows, for the first time, that she is going to do it anyway. She is terrified by how confident she is.

Discussion questions
  1. What is the difference between Ryn's previous pattern (deferring) and what she is doing now?
  2. Ryn is 'terrified by how confident she is.' What does that suggest about what the adult voice feels like when it first appears?
  3. The adult voice is not rebellion and not compliance — it is conscious choosing. What makes Ryn's decision the third thing?
  4. Design the conversation Ryn has with her parents — one that honors both her relationship with them and her own voice.

THE ADULT VOICE

B5-5B

THE NEUROSCIENCE OF MORAL DEVELOPMENT

The situation

Marcus's coach wants him to train six days a week. Marcus knows — his body has been telling him clearly — that five is his sustainable level. He has tried six. He breaks down by week three, every time. His coach says: 'The athletes who get to the next level do six.' Marcus has to decide: is he listening to his coach, or to his body? He suspects both are right about different things.

Discussion questions
  1. Marcus says he suspects both are right about different things. What is each one right about?
  2. The adult voice requires holding relevant external input and personal truth simultaneously. What does Marcus know that his coach doesn't?
  3. Is listening to your body in training a form of self-knowledge or avoidance? How do you tell the difference?
  4. Design the conversation Marcus has with his coach — one that uses the adult voice: genuine consideration of input plus honest articulation of his own limit.

THE MORAL NORTH STAR

B5-6A

THE MORAL NORTH STAR

The situation

In college, Fatima is offered an internship at a company whose practices she has serious concerns about — the hiring process, the environmental record, the way junior employees are treated. The salary is three times what any comparable internship offers. Her advisor says: 'This is a significant opportunity.' Her Integrity Anchor says something different. She turns it down. She gets a worse internship and a better year.

Discussion questions
  1. What is Fatima's Moral North Star telling her — and what is the financial pressure telling her?
  2. Fatima's advisor is not wrong about the opportunity. How do you hold that alongside the clarity of the North Star?
  3. She turns it down and 'gets a better year.' What does that mean — and how do you measure a 'better year'?
  4. Design your own Moral North Star statement: the value you will never trade for success, approval, or belonging.

THE MORAL NORTH STAR

B5-6B

THE MORAL NORTH STAR

The situation

Theo has always been able to identify what he believes — and has almost never been able to hold it under social pressure. He knows his North Star: he will not participate in public cruelty, even when it is directed at someone he doesn't particularly like. He knows it. He has failed it three times this year. Each time he participated in the pile-on, he felt it cost him something he had to work to get back.

Discussion questions
  1. What does it mean to know your North Star and still fail to hold it?
  2. Theo says each failure 'cost him something he had to work to get back.' What did it cost — and what did the recovery require?
  3. Knowing a North Star is not the same as holding it. What builds the capacity to hold it under pressure?
  4. What is one situation in the coming year where Theo's North Star is likely to be tested — and what can he build now to hold it then?

UNIT REFLECTION — THE COMPASS OF INTEGRITY

B5-7A

UNIT REFLECTION — THE COMPASS OF INTEGRITY

The situation

When Amara finishes her Compass of Integrity, the West point — values still forming — is the longest. She has written three lines under it. The first is about who she wants to be in a career. The second is about who she wants to be in relationships. The third is about who she wants to be when no one is watching. 'The third one is the one that matters most,' she tells her pair. 'The other two are downstream of it. It is also the one I cannot claim yet.'

Discussion questions
  1. Why does Amara believe that who you are when no one is watching is downstream of everything else?
  2. Amara puts her most important value under West — still forming — rather than East — tested and held. What does that honesty make possible?
  3. The Compass of Integrity is designed to be carried into adulthood and returned to at 25, 35, 45. What makes a document like this worth returning to?
  4. Write your own third line — who you want to be when no one is watching. Then decide honestly: East or West?

THE COMPASS OF INTEGRITY

B5-7B

UNIT REFLECTION — THE COMPASS OF INTEGRITY

The situation

Jonah's Compass of Integrity is almost complete, but he is stuck on the East point — three values tested by pressure that held. He has written 'integrity' three times in different words. He knows this is too vague to be true. He tries to name a moment instead and gets stuck again. Finally he writes: 'I did not tell people that what they did was okay when it was not. I did not go along with quiet cruelty. I did not pretend to feel less than I felt.' Three sentences, three actual afternoons. These feel real.

Discussion questions
  1. What made Jonah's East point real when the vague version wasn't?
  2. Jonah wrote 'integrity' three times. What was missing from that version — and why does the Compass ask for values that were tested, not values that are admired?
  3. A value written as a specific behavior under specific pressure can be checked. Why does the Compass need entries you can check?
  4. Write your own East point: three values that were tested by real pressure and held. Name the pressure each time.

LIVING AMENDS

B5-8A

LIVING AMENDS

The situation

Layla has been apologizing to her younger sister for years — for the ways she treated her in middle school, for the jealousy and the cruelty that she is now embarrassed by. Her sister accepts the apologies and seems to hold no grudge. But Layla notices she keeps apologizing for the same things. Her sister finally says: 'I know you're sorry. You've told me a hundred times. What I notice is that you're still the one who interrupts me the most at the dinner table.'

Discussion questions
  1. What is the difference between what Layla has been doing and what her sister is asking for?
  2. Layla keeps apologizing for the same things. What does the repetition reveal?
  3. A living amend is sustained behavioral change over time. What would that look like specifically for Layla at the dinner table?
  4. Identify one relationship in your life where a living amend — not another apology — might be the appropriate response.

THE LIVING AMEND

B5-8B

LIVING AMENDS

The situation

Marcus has a long-standing habit of committing to things and following through on about 70% of them. He has told himself this is manageable. He has started to notice that the 30% he drops are the things other people were counting on, and that the pattern is eroding trust in a way that apologies haven't repaired. 'I know I keep saying I'm working on it,' he writes. 'At some point "working on it" has to become different behavior.'

Discussion questions
  1. Marcus has diagnosed his own pattern accurately. What is missing from the diagnosis?
  2. Why haven't Marcus's apologies and statements of 'working on it' rebuilt the trust the pattern has damaged?
  3. Design a living amend for Marcus: one specific, measurable behavioral change that would address the 70/30 pattern.
  4. Where in your own life is a living amend more appropriate than an apology — and what would it specifically require?

FORGIVING THE YOUNGER SELF

B5-9A

FORGIVING THE YOUNGER SELF

The situation

When Priya thinks about her sophomore self — the anxiety, the obsessive grade-tracking, the sleeplessness, the inability to enjoy anything — she feels a mix of compassion and frustration. 'I want to tell her it's going to be okay,' she says. 'And I'm also a little angry at her for not knowing that when I needed her to.' She realizes, as she says it, that being angry at her fifteen-year-old self for not having the perspective of her seventeen-year-old self is not entirely fair.

Discussion questions
  1. What is Priya being unfair about — and why does she already know it?
  2. Priya feels both compassion and frustration toward her younger self. Are both feelings valid?
  3. The Compassionate Witness offers something specific: what would it say to the fifteen-year-old who didn't have the perspective she needed?
  4. Write a letter to the version of yourself that most needs to be forgiven right now.

FORGIVING THE YOUNGER SELF

B5-9B

FORGIVING THE YOUNGER SELF

The situation

The thing Darius most needs to forgive his younger self for is the years he spent making himself invisible. He knows now it was a survival strategy. He was in a social environment that felt unsafe, and invisibility kept him protected. But it also meant he missed things — friendships, experiences, the chance to be known. 'I'm not angry at him,' Darius says. 'I'm sad for him. He didn't know there was a different option.'

Discussion questions
  1. What is the difference between being angry at your younger self and being sad for them?
  2. Darius says his younger self 'didn't know there was a different option.' Does not knowing make the strategy right — or just understandable?
  3. Forgiving the younger self is not pretending the survival strategy didn't have costs. What does it mean to honor both the cost and the reason?
  4. What would you say to Darius's younger self — the middle-schooler who chose invisibility — if you could?

B5-10A★

MENTAL HEALTH LITERACY & ADVOCACY

The situation

In his first semester of college, Marcus realizes he is not managing well. He knows it — the sleep pattern, the concentration, the gray afternoons. He has tried to muscle through it. He has told himself everyone feels this way. He finds himself at the student health center, then walking away without going in. Then going back. Then walking away again. On the third visit he goes in. The counselor inside says: 'It took courage to come here.' Marcus hadn't thought of it as courage. He'd thought of it as admitting defeat.

Discussion questions
  1. What has Marcus confused about what asking for help means?
  2. Marcus frames seeking support as 'admitting defeat.' Where did that frame come from — and is it accurate?
  3. The counselor reframes it as courage. What would Marcus need to believe for that reframe to land?
  4. Normalize the action: design a version of Marcus's decision that treats help-seeking as strength-based rather than deficit-based.

THE SUSTAINABLE SOUL

The situation

By senior spring, Priya has designed her daily practice: thirty minutes of reading that has nothing to do with school, a conversation every two days with someone she loves just to check in, and one morning per week where she deliberately does not check her phone until noon. These are small and unglamorous. They are also the things, she discovers, that keep her genuinely functional rather than technically operational.

  3. Design your own sustainable daily practice — not what sounds good, but what actually restores you. Be specific.
  4. What is the smallest version of this practice you could execute even on the hardest days?

THE SUSTAINABLE SOUL

FORGIVENESS FATIGUE

B5-12A

FORGIVENESS FATIGUE

The situation

The rejection from her first-choice college lands in March. Priya has been catching her Critic since she was eight — the same voice, the same verdict, caught and answered a thousand times. Hannah, her best friend since third grade, says: 'The Critic gets to have this for one day. Tomorrow we start the work of catching her on this one.' Priya's first honest reaction is not grief. It is exhaustion: 'I have done this work. Why am I doing it again?'

Discussion questions
  1. Priya's exhaustion is forgiveness fatigue, not resistance. What is the difference — and why does the distinction matter for what she does next?
  2. Hannah gives the Critic one day. What is that deadline protecting — and what would 'zero days' or 'no deadline' each cost?
  3. Priya is not being asked to catch the same thing again. She is being asked to catch it at a level she could not reach at fourteen. What does she bring now that she did not have then?
  4. Name one piece of forgiveness work — toward yourself or someone else — that keeps coming back. What would it mean to meet it honestly tired, rather than pretending you are fresh?

FATIGUE IS NOT RESISTANCE

B5-12B

FORGIVENESS FATIGUE

The situation

Darius has been forgiving his father for six years — in pieces. A piece after the apology in ninth grade. A piece the night his father showed up sober to the game. A piece at the vet, in October, when his father sat with him. Each time, some part of him says: 'I already did this. Why is there more?' By spring he can name the fatigue exactly: not anger at his father, but tiredness at having to keep choosing.

Discussion questions
  1. Darius's tiredness is not anger. What is it — and why is naming it accurately the first step the lesson asks for?
  2. 'I already did this. Why is there more?' What does the lesson say about work that comes back at a deeper level?
  3. Forgiving in pieces over six years is not a failure to forgive. What did each piece make possible for the next?
  4. Design the honest acknowledgment Darius could write in his journal — one that names the fatigue without pretending the earlier pieces did not count.

THE INHERITANCE OF GRACE

THE INHERITANCE OF GRACE

The situation

The acceptance letter comes on 15 December. Amara's grandmother makes tea, sits at the kitchen table, and says: 'I am going to miss you and be okay at the same time. Those are not in competition. I have raised you to leave well. The leaving well includes not worrying about me.' Amara has spent a year quietly planning how to stay close enough to look after her. She realizes she has just been given something she did not know she was allowed to receive.

Discussion questions
  1. What, exactly, has Amara's grandmother given her — and why does the lesson call this named grace?
  2. 'Those are not in competition.' What two things has Amara been treating as a competition — and what changes when they are not?
  3. Amara received this grace by name, from a person she can thank. How is that different from grace that arrived unnamed — a pattern, a habit, a way of being spoken to?
  4. Name one piece of grace you have received that you could trace to a specific person. What did it make possible in you?

THE LEADERSHIP PREREQUISITE

B5-15A

THE LEADERSHIP PREREQUISITE

The situation

A leader Amara has studied was once described by a subordinate as: 'She is the most effective person I have ever worked for because she is not trying to prove anything. She settled all of that before she got here.' The leader had spent years in a previous role working through a period of significant professional failure and personal loss. By the time she arrived in the leadership role, she was not leading to vindicate herself. She was leading to build something.

Discussion questions
  1. What does 'she settled all of that before she got here' mean — and what does it make possible?
  2. Unresolved bitterness, ambition tied to proving others wrong, and the need for vindication are identified as leadership liabilities. Why?
  3. Forgiveness — the release of resentment — is described as a leadership prerequisite. What does that mean in practice?
  4. Identify one place in your own life where unresolved bitterness is limiting your relational range or your leadership capacity.

FORGIVENESS AS LEADERSHIP CAPACITY

B5-15B

THE LEADERSHIP PREREQUISITE

The situation

Marcus noticed that the best student leaders at his school were not the loudest or most confident ones. They were the ones who seemed to genuinely not need the position for anything other than what it was for. 'The people who need the role,' a mentor told him, 'are usually the ones who use it for something other than the role.' Marcus understood this immediately. He had watched three student leaders use their position to manage old social wounds.

Discussion questions
  1. What does 'needing the role for something other than the role' look like in practice?
  2. Marcus's mentor is describing a pattern. What unresolved need is being managed through the leadership position?
  3. What does it mean to hold a leadership position genuinely — for what it is for, rather than what it can provide?
  4. Identify one situation where you have sought responsibility or visibility for something other than the genuine purpose of that responsibility.
