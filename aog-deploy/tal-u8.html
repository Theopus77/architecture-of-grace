How a Page Is Read

Method

A page of the Babylonian Talmud can look like a puzzle. There is a block of text in the middle. There are smaller columns wrapped around it. There are notes in tiny letters at the edges. In this unit you will learn how that page is built, who wrote each part, and how printers in Venice and Vilna set it in place.

Then you will learn how the words in the middle move. The Gemara asks questions, brings proofs, raises objections and gives answers. Sometimes it leaves a question open. You will meet the tools the rabbis used, like kal va-chomer and gezerah shavah, and famous pairs of voices, like Rav and Shmuel, Abaye and Rava.

By the end you will be able to find your way around a page, cite it the standard way, and trace the steps of a sugya. You will also be able to explain why the Talmud keeps the side that lost, and what its rulings protect, from the worker in the field to the person whose view was outvoted.

How is a page of Talmud built, and how does its argument move from question to answer?

Mishnah, Gemara, Rashi, Tosafot: The Vilna Page

The Page
Big questionHow does one printed page hold a conversation that lasted more than a thousand years?
The story

The Printer of Venice

A Christian printer in Venice decides where every commentary will sit, and the choice lasts five hundred years.

Venice, around 1520. Daniel Bomberg is not Jewish. He came from Antwerp, in what is now Belgium, and he prints books for a living. Printing is still a new craft. Most books in the world are still copied by hand. Bomberg has a plan that no one has finished before: print the whole Babylonian Talmud, every tractate, in one matching set.

The problem is not only the size of the text. The problem is what to put around it. For hundreds of years, Jewish students had read the Talmud with two companions. One was Rashi, a teacher from France who died in 1105. The other was the Tosafot, notes by Rashi's grandsons and their students. Handwritten copies often put these notes in the margins. Every copy did it a little differently.

Bomberg's Jewish editors and typesetters work out a design. The Mishnah and Gemara go in the middle of the page, in a large square block. Rashi's comments go in a narrow column on the inner side, next to the binding. The Tosafot go in a column on the outer side. All three columns start and end together, so the page looks like a frame around a window.

The shop also numbers the pages. Each leaf gets one number, and each leaf has two sides, a and b. Berakhot 2a means the front of the second leaf of tractate Berakhot. The first leaf is the title page, so the text begins on 2a. Almost every printing since then has kept the same page numbers, so a student in Chicago and a student in Jerusalem can open to the very same words.

Historians note that Bomberg had the pope's permission to print and that his editors included Jews and converts. His set was finished by about 1523. Later printers copied his layout. When the Romm family of Vilna printed their famous edition in the 1880s, they kept the frame, kept the numbers, and added more notes around the edges. The page you meet in this chapter is, in a real sense, Bomberg's page.

Section 1

The Frame Around the Text

15.1

The Center of the Page

Main ideaThe middle of a Talmud page holds the Mishnah and the Gemara, the two oldest layers of the text.

Open any tractate of the Babylonian Talmud and look at the middle of the page. You will see a large block of Hebrew letters. This block holds two texts, one inside the other. The older one is the , a law code that rabbinic tradition says Judah the Prince arranged around the year 200. The newer one is the , the record of how later rabbis discussed each Mishnah.

The two are not separated by color or by a different typeface. Instead, the word Mishnah, or a shortened form of it, marks where a Mishnah begins. The word Gemara marks where the discussion starts. A short paragraph of Mishnah can be followed by pages of Gemara. Sometimes a single Mishnah gets only a few lines.

Readers of the Talmud usually call the two layers together the . Scholars point out that the Mishnah is written mostly in Hebrew, while the Gemara is written mostly in Aramaic, the everyday language of Jews in Babylonia. So the center of the page is already a conversation between two centuries and two languages.

Think it through
Two Layers, One Block

Picture a page where the middle block begins with a short law: the time for saying the evening Shema starts when the priests come in to eat their portion. Right after it, without a new heading, a new voice asks why the law starts with the evening and not the morning. The first voice is the Mishnah. The second is the Gemara.

How could you tell, just from the way the two voices talk, which one is the older text and which is the commentary on it?

15.2

Rashi on the Inside

Main ideaRashi's commentary sits on the inner margin and explains the words and the flow of the Gemara.

Next to the binding, in a narrow column, sits the commentary of . Rashi is a short name made from the letters of Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki. He lived in Troyes, in northern France, from 1040 to 1105. He earned his living, according to later accounts, from wine. He wrote a commentary on almost the whole Babylonian Talmud.

Rashi’s comments are short. He picks a word or phrase from the Gemara, repeats it, and then explains it in a few words. He tells you who is speaking, what a hard Aramaic word means, and what the question is really asking. Students for centuries have said that without Rashi the page would be locked.

The letters in Rashi’s column look different from the letters in the center. Printers used a rounder, semi-cursive typeface for commentaries, and it became known as Rashi script. Historians point out that Rashi himself never wrote in this script. It is named after him only because his commentary was the first famous text printed in it.

Think it through
A Word and Its Gloss

Imagine a line of Gemara that says a certain rabbi 'went out to the courtyard.' Beside it, Rashi's column repeats the words 'to the courtyard' and adds: the place where the students waited, outside the study hall. That is the whole comment. It does not argue. It tells you what the words mean and where the scene happens.

15.3

Tosafot on the Outside

Main ideaThe Tosafot, on the outer margin, are questions and additions from Rashi's grandsons and their students in the 1100s and 1200s.

On the outer edge of the page sits a second column of commentary called the . The word means additions. These notes were written in France and Germany during the 1100s and 1200s by a group of scholars, not by one person. Several of them were Rashi’s own grandsons, including Rabbeinu Tam and Rashbam.

The Tosafot do something different from Rashi. Rashi explains the page in front of you. The Tosafot compare that page with other pages. A note might begin by quoting Rashi, then ask how his reading fits a passage in another tractate, then offer a new answer. They treat the whole Talmud as one book that must agree with itself.

Because the Tosafot ask hard questions, reading them takes practice. A note often opens with a word from the Gemara in bold, then a question, then two or three possible answers. Students in a , a study hall, often work through a Tosafot note with a partner, since one reader alone can miss a step.

Think it through
Rashi Reads a Page, Tosafot Read a Book

Two readers sit at one page. The first says: this word means a loan, and the rabbi speaking is Rav. The second says: yes, but in tractate Bava Metzia the same rabbi seems to say the opposite about loans, so either that page means something else or this one does. The first reader is doing Rashi's job. The second is doing the Tosafot's job.

Which of the two readers would you want first when you are new to a page? Which one helps you see the Talmud as a whole?

Section 2

Reading the Edges

15.4

The Small Print at the Edges

Main ideaAround the three main columns, later printers added short references that link a line to the Bible, to law codes and to other pages.

A standard page carries more than three texts. Around the edges, in very small type, sit helpers added by later editors. One helper points from a line of Gemara to the biblical verse it quotes. Another lists where the same teaching appears elsewhere in the Talmud. A third points to the place in the great medieval law codes where the ruling was settled.

These helpers have names. The list of parallel passages is called Masoret HaShas, the tradition of the Talmud. The pointer to the law codes is called Ein Mishpat, the well of judgment. Editions since Vilna also carry short notes by later rabbis, such as the comments of Rabbi Akiva Eger, printed in the margin.

None of this small print is older than the printing press, and much of it was added in the 1800s. Scholars describe the page as a set of layers laid down over time. The Mishnah is the oldest layer. The Gemara is next. Then Rashi, then the Tosafot, then the printers’ helpers. Reading a page well means knowing which layer you are in.

15.5

Two Languages on One Page

Main ideaThe Mishnah is mostly Hebrew and the Gemara mostly Aramaic, so reading the Talmud means moving between two related languages.

The Talmud switches languages without warning. The Mishnah is in Hebrew, the language of the Bible, though a later and simpler Hebrew. The Gemara is mostly in . Aramaic was the common language across much of the Middle East for centuries. Jews in Babylonia spoke it at home and in the market, so their study was recorded in it.

The two languages are cousins. They share an alphabet and many roots. A reader who knows Hebrew can often guess an Aramaic word, but not always. The Gemara also quotes earlier teachings in Hebrew, so a single line can begin in Aramaic, quote a Hebrew , and return to Aramaic. Rashi often marks these shifts.

Scholars note that the Jerusalem Talmud, made in the Land of Israel, uses a different western dialect of Aramaic. The Babylonian Talmud uses an eastern dialect. Readers today learn a short list of common Aramaic words first, such as the words for ’he said,’ ’a difficulty,’ and ’come and hear.’ With those, the page starts to open.

15.6

Studying Aloud, in Pairs

Main ideaJewish tradition studies the page with a partner, out loud, which is one reason the page keeps so many voices.

The page was built to be argued over, not read silently. In a traditional beit midrash, students learn with a , a study partner. One reads a line aloud. The other stops and asks what it means. They check Rashi. They try the Tosafot. They disagree, and then they move on. The room is loud.

A saying in Pirkei Avot, a tractate of the Mishnah, advises a student to get a teacher and acquire a companion. Rabbinic readers have long taken this to mean that Torah is studied with others, not alone. The Gemara itself, in tractate Taanit, praises scholars who sharpen one another, like iron on iron, and warns against studying by oneself.

The page’s layout fits this way of learning. A pair can point to the center, then to Rashi’s column, then to a Tosafot note, without turning a page. Modern classrooms in Chicago and elsewhere that teach Talmud, whether in day schools or in university courses, often keep the pair method even when the students read in English.

Section 3

From Manuscript to Print to Screen

15.7

Bomberg's Venice Edition

Main ideaDaniel Bomberg's Venice printing of 1520–23 fixed the page layout and page numbers that nearly every edition since has followed.

Before printing, every Talmud was a handwritten manuscript. Copies were rare and costly. Scholars note that the Talmud was also attacked: in Paris in 1242, cartloads of manuscripts were burned after a public trial. Very few complete medieval manuscripts survive. The best known is the Munich manuscript, copied in 1342, the only complete handwritten Babylonian Talmud that still exists.

Printing changed everything. , a Christian printer from Antwerp working in Venice, produced the first complete printed Babylonian Talmud between about 1520 and 1523. His editors set the Mishnah and Gemara in the center with Rashi inside and Tosafot outside. They gave each leaf a number, and each leaf has a front side, a, and a back side, b.

Later printers, Jewish and Christian, copied Bomberg’s layout and his numbering. That is why a citation like Berakhot 2a means the same page in a Venice edition, a Vilna edition and a modern one. Historians call this a rare case where one printer’s design became the standard for a whole literature for five centuries.

15.8

The Vilna Shas

Main ideaThe Romm press in Vilna printed the edition of 1880–86 that became the standard look of the Talmud page.

In the 1800s the city of Vilna, now Vilnius in Lithuania, was a center of Jewish learning and printing. The Romm family ran a large press there. Between 1880 and 1886 they printed a new edition of the whole Babylonian Talmud. It is known as the . Shas is a short form of the Hebrew words for six orders, the six divisions of the Mishnah.

The Vilna edition kept Bomberg’s frame and numbering. It added more commentaries around the edges and at the back of each volume, cleaned up many printing errors, and used clear type. Scholars say its editors gathered readings from manuscripts and earlier printings. It was not the first Talmud, but it became the one that most later editions copied, even photographically.

When people today say ’the Talmud page,’ they usually mean the Vilna page. Its look is so fixed that a student who learns one page can find their way around any other. Yet the layout is a printer’s choice, not part of the text. Manuscripts before 1500 looked quite different, and digital editions today can look different again.

15.9

Editions and Translations Today

Main ideaModern editions and translations, in print and online, open the page to readers who do not know Hebrew or Aramaic.

For most of history, reading the Talmud meant reading Hebrew and Aramaic. In the 1900s, translations began to change that. The Soncino Press in London published a complete English translation between 1935 and 1952. Earlier, Michael Rodkinson had printed a partial and abridged English version in the United States between 1896 and 1903; scholars treat it with care because it leaves out and reshapes much.

Rabbi Adin Steinsaltz began a Hebrew edition in 1965 that added vowels, punctuation and a running commentary; an English version followed. The Schottenstein edition from ArtScroll, finished in 2005, gives an English translation phrase by phrase, with notes. Each edition makes choices about what to explain, and readers in different communities prefer different ones.

Online, the site , founded in 2011, offers the Hebrew and Aramaic text with an English translation and links to Rashi, Tosafot and thousands of other texts. Many public schools, colleges and libraries use it because it is free. The Vilna page, the Steinsaltz page and a Sefaria screen all carry the same words. What changes is how much help surrounds them.

Finding Your Way

15.10

How to Cite a Page

Main ideaA Talmud citation names the tractate, the leaf number and the side; a Mishnah citation names the tractate, chapter and mishnah.

A is the short address of a passage. For the Babylonian Talmud it has three parts: the tractate, the leaf number and the side. Berakhot 2a means tractate Berakhot, leaf 2, front side. Bava Metzia 59b means tractate Bava Metzia, leaf 59, back side. Because every standard edition shares Bomberg’s numbering, this address works everywhere.

The Mishnah is cited differently, by chapter and paragraph. Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 means tractate Sanhedrin, chapter 4, mishnah 5. Pirkei Avot is usually cited without the word Mishnah, as Avot 1:14. The Jerusalem Talmud is often cited by chapter and law, or by the page of its Venice printing, and scholars sometimes add the letter y. in front of the tractate name.

Learning to cite well matters in this course. When you write about a passage, give its address so a reader can find the very words. Give the translation you used and its year, since translations differ. A citation is a small act of honesty: it lets someone check you.

15.11

The Six Orders and Their Tractates

Main ideaThe Mishnah is arranged in six orders, and each order holds tractates on related subjects.

The Mishnah has 63 tractates, grouped into six . Rabbinic tradition names them by topic. Zeraim, Seeds, covers blessings and farming laws. Moed, Appointed Time, covers Shabbat and the festivals. Nashim, Women, covers marriage and vows. Nezikin, Damages, covers property, courts and injury. Kodashim, Holy Things, covers the Temple service. Tohorot, Purities, covers ritual purity.

The Babylonian Gemara does not cover every tractate. It discusses about 37 of the 63, mostly in Moed, Nashim, Nezikin and Kodashim, plus Berakhot from Zeraim and Niddah from Tohorot. Scholars suggest that the Babylonian academies focused on laws that still applied in daily life after the Temple was gone.

The names of tractates are often the first thing a student learns. Berakhot means Blessings. Shabbat is the Sabbath. Bava Metzia, the Middle Gate, is about lost objects, loans and wages. Sanhedrin is about courts. Avot, Fathers, is the tractate of sayings. Knowing the name tells you the subject before you read a word.

15.12

Where the Page Protects Someone

Main ideaEven a lesson on layout leads to the Talmud's purpose: rules that protect the worker, the owner of a lost object and the person at risk.

The page is a tool, not the point. The point is the questions the text keeps asking. Take the order Nezikin. Its tractate Bava Metzia opens with two people holding one cloak, each claiming it. The Mishnah rules that they divide it and each swears an oath. The Gemara then spends pages asking why an oath is needed and what happens if one of them is lying.

Further on, the same tractate turns to lost objects. Who must return them? What if the finder cannot tell whose it is? The Mishnah lists objects that a finder may keep and objects that must be announced. Rashi explains the terms. The Tosafot compare the rule with a passage in another tractate. Every layer of the page is working on one question: how do you treat what is not yours?

This course follows two threads through the Talmud, argument and mercy. Argument is how the page moves. Mercy is what many rulings protect: the day laborer waiting for wages, the stranger, the person who might be shamed in public. The Moves of the Gemara: Question, Proof, Objection, Answer

Big questionWhy does the Talmud keep the whole argument instead of just writing down the answer?
The story

Five Objections to One Rule

A seventh-grade class tries to write its own page of Talmud about phones.

On Monday, Ms. Rivera wrote one line on the board: "No phones in class." Then she said, "Today this is our Mishnah. Your job is to be the Gemara." The class looked at her. Deshawn raised his hand first. "What counts as a phone?" he asked. "Is a smartwatch a phone?" Ms. Rivera wrote his question under the rule and labeled it Question.

Maya offered a proof. "The school handbook says no devices that can send messages. A smartwatch can send messages. So it counts." Ms. Rivera wrote that down as Proof. Then Omar objected. "But the handbook also lets students use a calculator. Some calculators have screens too." The room got louder. Ms. Rivera smiled and wrote Objection.

By the end of the period the board was full. There were five objections. One asked about a student who needs a phone to check blood sugar. Another asked about an emergency call from home. The class agreed that the rule must bend to protect health and safety. They did not agree about the smartwatch, so Ms. Rivera wrote one word next to it: teyku, "let it stand."

"Now look," she said. "If I erased everything but the answer, what would you lose?" Priya said, "We would lose why." Ms. Rivera nodded. "The Talmud keeps the why. That is what we will study this week."

The Shape of a Sugya

16.1

A Unit of Discussion

Main ideaA sugya is one complete discussion in the Gemara, and it usually moves through question, proof, objection and answer.

Open the Babylonian Talmud to Berakhot 2a, the first page of the first tractate. The Mishnah asks a plain question: from what time may the evening Shema be said? Right after it, the Gemara begins its own discussion. That discussion, from its first question to its last answer, is called a .

Scholars describe a sugya as a unit of argument. It often starts with a Mishnah. Then a rabbi asks about it. Someone brings a proof. Someone else raises an objection. The discussion ends with an answer, or sometimes with no answer at all. A single page can hold several sugyot, and one sugya can run for many pages.

The word sugya comes from Aramaic and is often explained as "a going" or "a path." Teachers like that meaning. A sugya is a path the reader walks, step by step. Learning Talmud, many teachers say, means learning to walk that path and see where each step leads.

Think it through

Step 1: a Mishnah states a rule. Step 2: a rabbi asks, "What is the reason?" Step 3: another rabbi quotes a verse as proof. Step 4: someone objects that the verse could mean something else. Step 5: an answer explains why the verse still works.

Label each step with one word: rule, question, proof, objection or answer. Which step do you think takes the most skill?

16.2

What Is the Reason?

Main ideaThe Gemara often opens by asking "mai ta'ama?" (what is the reason?), because it wants to know why a rule is true, not only what it says.

Again and again the Gemara asks two Aramaic words: mai ta&#x27;ama, "what is the reason?" A Mishnah may state a rule in one short line. The Gemara wants to know what stands behind it. Is it a verse from the Torah? Is it a teaching from an earlier rabbi? Is it common sense?

Knowing the reason matters for new cases. Suppose a rule says a finder must announce a lost coat. If the reason is that the owner can prove the coat is theirs by a mark, then the rule might change for an object with no mark. The Gemara reasons this way in Bava Metzia, the tractate about lost objects and business.

So the question "what is the reason?" is not rude. In the Talmud it is a sign of respect for the rule. The rabbis treat a rule as something that can be understood. Once they understand it, they can apply it to cases the Mishnah never mentioned.

16.3

Proof from a Verse

Main ideaA common move in the Gemara is to support a rule with a verse from the Hebrew Bible.

On Berakhot 2a the Gemara asks why the Mishnah speaks of the evening Shema before the morning one. Its first answer points to a verse. Deuteronomy 6:7 says to speak these words "when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." Lying down comes first in the verse, so evening comes first in the Mishnah.

Then the Gemara offers a second answer. Genesis 1:5 describes creation with the words "there was evening and there was morning." In the order of creation, too, evening comes before morning. The Gemara keeps both answers side by side. It does not erase the first when it finds the second.

A proof from a verse is a basic move in the Talmud. Rabbinic tradition holds that the Written Torah and the rabbis’ teaching fit together. So a rabbi who wants to support a rule often looks for words in the Bible that point to it. Other rabbis then test the proof to see if the verse really says what is claimed.

Section 2

Objections and Answers

16.4

The Kushya

Main ideaA kushya is an objection or difficulty, and raising one is how the Gemara tests every claim.

In the Gemara, a claim is never safe just because a great rabbi said it. Someone may point out a problem. The problem might be a verse that seems to say the opposite. It might be a Mishnah that disagrees. It might be a case where the claim gives a strange result. That problem is called a , a difficulty or objection.

The Gemara has set phrases for objections. One is "meitivi," meaning "they raised an objection." Another is "ve-ha-tanya," "but was it not taught…?" These phrases tell the reader that a challenge is coming. Students learn to spot them, because they mark a turn in the path.

Teachers who run a , a study pair, often tell students to play the objector on purpose. One partner reads a claim. The other looks for the kushya. Many readers of the Talmud say this is not about winning. A claim that has faced hard objections and survived is a claim you can trust more.

16.5

The Terutz

Main ideaA terutz is an answer that resolves an objection, often by showing that two sources speak about different cases.

After a kushya comes the search for a , an answer. One of the most common kinds of answer is a split between cases. The Gemara says, in effect, "This source is talking about one situation, and that source is talking about another." Suddenly two texts that seemed to clash can both be true.

Here is how that looks. Suppose one teaching says a worker may eat from the crop he harvests. Another seems to say he may not. The Gemara might answer that one teaching speaks while he is working and the other speaks after he has finished. The Mishnah in Bava Metzia 7:2 does draw lines like these about when a worker may eat.

Some answers are strong. Others the Gemara itself calls weak or forced. Rabbinic readers and modern scholars both notice this. The Talmud does not pretend every answer is equally good. It often signals when an answer is only a way to keep a view alive.

16.6

Teyku: Let It Stand

Main ideaSometimes the Gemara ends a question with teyku, "let it stand," and leaves it unresolved.

Not every sugya ends with a neat answer. Sometimes the rabbis ask a question, try several answers, and none of them works. Then the Gemara writes one word: . It means "let it stand." The question stays open.

Many teyku questions are detailed puzzles about unusual cases. The Gemara in Bava Metzia, for example, raises cases about lost objects and ownership that it cannot settle. A later popular tradition reads the letters of teyku as a short form of a Hebrew phrase: Elijah the prophet will one day answer the questions. Scholars note that this reading came later; the word itself simply means the question stands.

Teyku teaches something about the Talmud’s stance. The rabbis were willing to write down what they did not know. They did not hide an open question to look wise. For students, teyku is a model of honest study: say what the evidence shows, and admit where it runs out.

Section 3

Tools for Reasoning

16.7

From Light to Heavy

Main ideaKal va-chomer is an argument from a lesser case to a greater one: if a rule holds in the easy case, it holds even more in the hard case.

means "light and heavy." It is an argument from the smaller case to the bigger one. If a rule is true where it matters less, it must be true where it matters more. English speakers call this an argument a fortiori.

Rabbinic tradition finds examples in the Torah itself. In Numbers 12:14, after Miriam is struck with a skin disease, God tells Moses that if her father had spit in her face, she would hide in shame seven days. How much more, the verse implies, should she be shut out after this. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 25a points to this verse as a model of kal va-chomer.

Kal va-chomer appears among the seven rules of reasoning linked with Hillel, who taught around the turn of the Common Era. Later lists expanded them to thirteen, linked with Rabbi Ishmael. The rabbis also knew the tool’s limits. A kal va-chomer can be refuted if someone shows that the "light" case has a feature the "heavy" case lacks.

16.8

A Matching Word

Main ideaGezerah shavah links two laws because the Torah uses the same word in both places.

means "an equal decree." It connects two passages of the Torah that share a word or phrase. What is clear in one passage is then carried over to the other. It is another of the rules of reasoning linked with Hillel.

The Gemara in Pesachim 66a tells a famous story. One year the eve of Passover fell on the Sabbath. The leaders did not know if the Passover offering could be made that day. Hillel argued that the daily Temple offering is made on the Sabbath, and the Torah uses the phrase "in its appointed season" for both offerings. So the Passover offering, too, could be made on the Sabbath.

The rabbis were careful with this tool. The Gemara teaches that a person may not invent a gezerah shavah on their own; it must be received from a teacher. Rabbinic readers explain that this rule kept the tool from being used to prove anything at all. It shows the rabbis thinking about the limits of their own methods.

16.9

A Teaching from Outside

Main ideaA baraita is a teaching from the time of the Mishnah that was left out of it, and the Gemara often quotes one as a proof or an objection.

The rabbis of the Mishnah are called , "repeaters" or teachers. The rabbis of the Gemara are called , "speakers" or explainers. The Mishnah did not hold everything the tannaim taught. Teachings left outside it are called baraitot, from an Aramaic word for "outside."

In the Gemara a baraita is often introduced by "tanu rabbanan," "our rabbis taught," or "tanya," "it was taught." A baraita carries weight because it comes from the earlier generation. So when an amora makes a claim, another amora may quote a baraita against it. That is a strong objection.

The answer sometimes becomes one of the most common moves in the Talmud: the baraita and the claim speak about different cases. Readers learn to ask of every quotation, "Who is speaking here, a tanna or an amora?" The answer tells you how much authority the words carry in the argument.

Section 4

Voices and Why They Stay

16.10

Rav and Shmuel

Main ideaRav and Shmuel were early amoraim in Babylonia whose disagreements run through the Talmud.

In the early 200s CE, a rabbi called Rav came to Babylonia from the Land of Israel. Tradition says he founded an academy at Sura. His colleague Shmuel led the Jewish community’s learning at Nehardea. Both belong to the first generation of Babylonian amoraim.

The Gemara often sets their views side by side: "Rav said… and Shmuel said…" Rabbinic tradition sums up a pattern. In matters of ritual law the ruling usually follows Rav. In matters of civil law, such as money and damages, it usually follows Shmuel. Shmuel was also known for skill in astronomy and medicine.

Shmuel is also linked with a famous principle, found in several tractates: "the law of the kingdom is the law." It means Jews should follow the fair civil laws of the country where they live. Readers across the centuries have discussed how far that principle reaches.

Main ideaAbaye and Rava were fourth-century amoraim whose debates became a symbol of Talmud study itself.

About a hundred years after Rav and Shmuel, two more rabbis became famous as a pair. Abaye led the academy at Pumbedita. Rava taught at Mahoza. Both lived in the 300s CE. Their debates fill so many pages that "the discussions of Abaye and Rava" became a way to describe Talmud learning.

In most of their disputes the halakhah, Jewish law as the Talmud develops it, follows Rava. The Gemara in Bava Metzia 22b lists six exceptions where it follows Abaye. Students remember the six by a memory word, Ya’al Kegam, made from the first letters of the six cases.

The pair is a good example of how the Talmud treats the side that did not win. Abaye’s arguments are kept in full. Students study them as carefully as Rava’s. A learner cannot understand Rava’s answer without knowing what Abaye asked.

16.12

Why the Minority Is Kept

Main ideaThe Mishnah and the Gemara record views that lost, and they give reasons for doing so.

Mishnah Eduyot 1:5 asks a sharp question. If the law follows the majority, why write down the view of a single rabbi who was outvoted? Its answer: so that a later court, if it sees reason, may rely on that view. A minority opinion is saved for a future that may need it.

The Gemara in Eruvin 13b tells of a three-year dispute between the House of Hillel and the House of Shammai. A heavenly voice declares that both views are "the words of the living God," and that the law follows Hillel’s house. Why Hillel’s? The Gemara says they were kind and humble, and they taught Shammai’s view before their own.

Readers have taken this in different ways. Orthodox teachers often stress that both sides were seeking truth within the tradition. Reform and Conservative teachers often point to it as a model for respectful disagreement. Academic scholars study how the editors shaped the story. All of them notice that the Talmud keeps the losing side.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. What sits at the center of a Vilna Talmud page?

Why: The center block holds the Mishnah and the Gemara.

2. Where is Rashi's commentary printed on the standard page?

Why: Rashi sits on the inner margin, next to the binding; Tosafot sit on the outer margin.

3. Who wrote the Tosafot?

Why: The Tosafists of the 12th–13th centuries included Rashi's grandsons and students.

4. What does the citation Berakhot 2a point to?

Why: Talmud citations name the tractate, the leaf (daf) and the side, a or b.

5. Who printed the first complete Talmud, in Venice in 1520–23?

Why: Daniel Bomberg, a Christian printer, produced the first full printed Talmud in Venice.

6. What is the Vilna Shas?

Why: The Romm press in Vilna printed the edition whose layout most students use today.

7. In a sugya, what is a kushya?

Why: A kushya is a difficulty raised against a claim.

8. Which word shows that a question was left open?

Why: Teyku means "let it stand."

9. "If this is true in the small case, how much more in the big case." Which tool is this?

Why: Kal va-chomer argues from the lighter to the heavier case.

10. What did Hillel prove in Pesachim 66a using a gezerah shavah?

Why: Hillel linked "in its appointed season" in two laws to show the Passover offering overrides the Sabbath.

11. Which phrase introduces a baraita?

Why: Tanu rabbanan signals a teaching of the tannaim from outside the Mishnah.

12. Which pair were first-generation Babylonian amoraim?

Why: Rav and Shmuel taught in the early 200s CE, the first generation of Babylonian amoraim.

13. According to Bava Metzia 22b, in how many cases does the law follow Abaye over Rava?

Why: The memory word Ya'al Kegam stands for the six cases.

14. Why does Mishnah Eduyot 1:5 record a minority view?

Why: Eduyot 1:5 says a lone view is kept so a later court may rely on it.

15. Which of these modern editions is a free online library of the Talmud?

Why: Sefaria offers the Talmud online; the Vilna and Venice editions are printed books.

Write it

Does the Talmud care more about the answer or about the argument? Cite two or three passages from this unit, such as Berakhot 2a, Eruvin 13b, Mishnah Eduyot 1:5 or Bava Metzia 22b. Explain what each passage says and how different readers have understood it.

  • Name the tractate and page for every passage you cite.
  • Say who is speaking: the Mishnah, the Gemara, a named rabbi, or a later reader.
  • Use the unit's terms, like sugya, kushya, terutz and teyku.
  • Compare readings without saying which one is true.
  • Point to what a ruling protects, such as a worker or a minority view.
