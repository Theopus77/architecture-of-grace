Greetings and Introductions

Meeting People

Every conversation starts the same way: someone says hello. In Spanish, that first moment carries a lot. The greeting changes with the time of day, from buenos días (good morning) to buenas noches (good evening). The word for you changes with the person, from tú for a friend to usted for a teacher. A handshake, a hug or a cheek kiss may come with it.

This unit follows students meeting new people in Chicago, from Pilsen to Humboldt Park. You will greet and say goodbye, introduce a friend, and ask ¿Cómo te llamas? (What's your name?). You will spell names aloud with the 27 letters of the Spanish alphabet, and learn why many people carry two last names.

By the end, you will be able to meet someone new in Spanish, politely and correctly. You will ask where they are from with ¿De dónde eres?, name a nationality that matches the person, and write names with every accent mark in place. Those small marks matter: papá is dad, but papa is potato.

The big question

How do the first words we say to someone show who we are and how much we respect them?

Hola and Hasta Luego

Greetings
Big questionHow does the way you say hello and goodbye show respect for the person in front of you?
The story

First Day in Pilsen

A new seventh grader learns that one hello does not fit every person.

Daniela walked into her new middle school in Pilsen, a Chicago neighborhood where many families have roots in Mexico. It was eight in the morning. A woman at the front desk smiled and said, "Buenos días" (good morning). Daniela answered, "Buenos días," and felt a little braver.

In the hallway, a boy with a soccer ball under his arm waved at her. "¡Hola! ¿Qué tal?" he said (Hi! How's it going?). Daniela laughed. "Bien, ¿y tú?" (Good, and you?). His name was Tomás. He pointed down the hall and said, "Vamos, la clase es allí" (Come on, the class is there).

Before class, Tomás stopped next to a tall man with gray hair. "Señor Ramírez, le presento a Daniela," he said (Mr. Ramírez, let me introduce Daniela to you). The teacher shook her hand. "Mucho gusto, Daniela" (Nice to meet you). Daniela noticed that Tomás used a more formal way to talk to the teacher than he used with her.

At three o'clock, the bell rang. Tomás gave her a quick wave. "¡Chao, Daniela! ¡Hasta mañana!" (Bye, Daniela! See you tomorrow!). At the door, Daniela turned to Señor Ramírez. "Hasta luego, señor" (See you later, sir). He smiled. "Hasta luego, Daniela. Buenas tardes" (Good afternoon).

Section 1

Hello by Time of Day

17.1

Buenos Días

Main ideaSay buenos días (good morning) from when you wake up until about noon.

Picture walking into school at eight in the morning. The office worker looks up. In Spanish, the polite hello at this hour is (good morning). It literally means "good days," in the plural. English speakers often say "buen día" or "bueno día" by mistake. Many people say buen día in some countries, but buenos días is the standard greeting everywhere.

Look at the pieces. Buenos is an adjective, a word that describes a noun. Días is a noun, a word for a thing. Día (day) is a masculine noun even though it ends in -a. So the adjective takes the masculine plural ending -os: buenos días. You will meet this odd noun again: el día (the day).

Use buenos días with anyone: a teacher, a bus driver, a friend’s grandmother. It works from waking up until lunch time, around noon. You can add a name or a title: "Buenos días, señora López" (Good morning, Mrs. López). The word (ma’am, Mrs.) shows respect.

17.2

Buenas Tardes and Buenas Noches

Main ideaSay buenas tardes in the afternoon and buenas noches at night, both as hello and as goodbye.

Picture yourself arriving at your aunt’s house at four in the afternoon. The greeting now is (good afternoon). La tarde (the afternoon) is a feminine noun, so the adjective is buenas, with -as. Many people use buenas tardes from lunch until it gets dark, so early evening can still be tarde.

When it is dark, the greeting becomes (good evening, good night). La noche (the night) is also feminine, so it is buenas noches. English has two phrases here, good evening and good night, but Spanish uses one. That means buenas noches works when you arrive at a party at nine and when you go to bed.

Watch the pattern: buenos días, buenas tardes, buenas noches. Only the first one uses -os, because only día is masculine. A common mistake is to copy the first one and say "buenos tardes." Check the noun first, then choose the ending. In quick, casual speech, some people just say "¡Buenas!" for any of these.

17.3

Hola and ¿Qué Tal?

Main ideaHola is a friendly hi for any time of day, and ¿Qué tal? asks how things are going.

Picture seeing your best friend in the lunch line. You do not need to check the clock. You just say (hi, hello). The h in hola is silent, so it sounds like "OH-lah." English speakers often pronounce the h, but in Spanish the letter h makes no sound.

Friends often add a quick question: (How’s it going? What’s up?). Notice the upside-down question mark ¿ at the start. Spanish marks the beginning and the end of every question. Qué has an accent mark because it is a question word here.

Hola is friendly and works with almost everyone. With an adult you do not know well, pair it with a time greeting: "Hola, buenos días." That sounds warm and polite at the same time. A fast answer to ¿Qué tal? is "Bien, ¿y tú?" (Good, and you?).

Think it through
Lunch Line Hello

Ana: ¡Hola, Marco! ¿Qué tal? Marco: Bien, ¿y tú? Ana: Muy bien, gracias. (Hi, Marco! How's it going? / Good, and you? / Very good, thanks.) Notice: ¡ opens the exclamation, ¿ opens the question, and the h in hola stays silent.

Act out this dialogue with a partner. Then change it so one person is tired and answers differently.

17.4

Answering ¿Cómo Estás?

Main ideaAnswer ¿Cómo estás? with bien, muy bien, más o menos or mal, and ask back with ¿y tú?

Picture your cousin calling you after a long test day. She asks, "¿Cómo estás?" (How are you?). This question asks how you feel right now. The answer starts with a word for your mood, and you can add "gracias" (thanks) to be polite.

Here is a scale from best to worst. means very well. means well or fine. means so-so, literally "more or less." Mal means bad or not well. A full answer can use estoy (I am): "Estoy bien." You can also say just "Bien, gracias."

Do not stop there. Ask back: "¿Y tú?" (And you?) to a friend. English speakers sometimes answer "Bien" and then walk away, which can sound cold. Another mistake is to say "Soy bien." For how you feel, Spanish uses estoy, not soy. You will study that difference in a later unit.

Think it through
Four Answers

¿Cómo estás? After a great game: "¡Muy bien, gracias!" On a normal day: "Bien, ¿y tú?" With a small headache: "Más o menos." With a bad cold: "Estoy mal." Each answer names the feeling first, then can add gracias or ¿y tú?

Section 2

Saying Goodbye

17.5

Adiós and Hasta Luego

Main ideaAdiós means goodbye, and hasta luego means see you later.

Picture the bell ringing at the end of the day. You leave the classroom. The simplest goodbye is (goodbye). It has a written accent on the o: adiós. Without the accent, the word is spelled wrong. The stress falls on the last syllable, "ah-DYOHS."

Many people prefer (see you later). Hasta means until, and luego means later, so it is literally "until later." It sounds friendly because it expects to see the person again. You can say it to a teacher, a store clerk or a friend.

Some English speakers think adiós means goodbye forever. It does not. People say adiós every day. Still, hasta luego is very common when you will see someone soon. You can even use both: "Adiós, hasta luego." Remember that the h in hasta is silent: "AHS-tah LWEH-go."

Think it through
Leaving the Store

Cashier: Aquí tiene. (Here you go.) Luis: Gracias. Adiós. Cashier: Hasta luego. Luis says a simple goodbye. The cashier answers with see you later, a friendly way to expect him back.

17.6

Hasta Mañana and Chao

Main ideaHasta mañana means see you tomorrow, and chao is a casual bye among friends.

Picture Friday afternoon versus Thursday afternoon. On Thursday, you know you will see your friends the next day. So you say (see you tomorrow). Mañana means tomorrow, and it has the letter ñ. The ñ sounds like the ny in canyon: "mah-NYAH-nah."

Among friends, many people say (bye). It comes from the Italian word ciao, and it is common in much of Latin America. Some people spell it chau. Use chao with friends and family, not in a formal moment, like leaving a job interview.

Here is the pattern: hasta + a time word. Hasta luego is until later. Hasta mañana is until tomorrow. A mistake English speakers make is writing manana without the tilde. Without the ñ, the word is misspelled, and the sound changes. Always type ñ.

17.7

Choosing the Right Goodbye

Main ideaPick a goodbye by asking when you will meet again and how close you are to the person.

Picture three people you say goodbye to on one day: your little brother, the school nurse and a new friend from art class. Each goodbye can fit a different situation. Two questions help you pick. When will I see this person again? How formal is this moment?

If you will meet tomorrow, say hasta mañana. If you will meet later today or soon, say hasta luego. If you do not know, adiós always works. At night, buenas noches can also mean goodbye. With friends and family, chao is quick and warm.

Many speakers also say (see you, literally we see each other). It is friendly and fits most people you know. A common learner mistake is to say "buenos días" when leaving in the morning. Some people do use it that way, but hasta luego is clearer for goodbye.

Section 3

Tú, Usted and Introductions

17.8

Tú Versus Usted

Main ideaUse tú with friends, family and kids, and usted with adults you should show respect to.

Picture Tomás talking to Daniela and then to his teacher. English has one word for you. Spanish has two for one person. (you) is informal, used with friends, classmates, family and children. (you) is formal, used with a teacher, a principal, a doctor or an older stranger.

The verb changes too. With tú, the verb usually ends in -s: "¿Cómo estás?" With usted, the verb matches él and ella: "¿Cómo está usted?" So estás goes with tú, and está goes with usted. Listen for that final -s to know which one someone used.

Customs differ. In some families, kids use usted with their own parents or grandparents. In other places, almost everyone uses tú. When you are not sure, start with usted. If the person says "Háblame de tú" (talk to me with tú), you can switch. Tú has an accent; tu without an accent means your.

Think it through
Same Question, Two Ways

To a classmate: "Hola, Daniela. ¿Cómo estás?" To the principal: "Buenos días, señora Ortiz. ¿Cómo está usted?" The question is the same: How are you? The verb changes from estás (tú) to está (usted), and the greeting gets more formal.

Ask your grandmother and your best friend how they are. Write each question and underline the verb.

17.9

Handshakes and Cheek Kisses

Main ideaMany Spanish speakers greet with a touch: a handshake, a hug or a light kiss on the cheek.

Picture arriving at a family party. Your cousin’s friends lean in and touch your cheek. In many Spanish-speaking places, people greet with un beso (a kiss) on the cheek. It is usually a light touch of cheeks with a kissing sound, not a real kiss. In much of Latin America it is one cheek, and in Spain it is usually two.

In school or business, a firm handshake is common, especially between people who just met. Close friends and family often hug, called un abrazo (a hug). Many people greet everyone in the room when they arrive and say goodbye to each person when they leave.

Customs change by country, family and age. Some people prefer not to be touched at all, and that is fine. Watch what others do, and follow the other person’s lead. A smile and a clear "Mucho gusto" (nice to meet you) are always welcome.

17.10

Te Presento a… and Mucho Gusto

Main ideaIntroduce someone with te presento a (informal) or le presento a (formal), and answer mucho gusto.

Picture introducing your new friend Daniela to your cousin. You say, "Te presento a Daniela" (Let me introduce Daniela to you). The phrase is the tú way. Te means to you, informal. The little word a comes before a person’s name, so do not drop it.

To introduce someone to a teacher, switch to the usted way: "Le presento a Daniela." Le means to you, formal. The rest stays the same. This is the same choice as tú versus usted, now inside a phrase.

The new people answer (nice to meet you, literally much pleasure). The other person can say "Igualmente" (likewise) or "El gusto es mío" (the pleasure is mine). A mistake English speakers make is saying "Mucho gusto" to someone they already know. It is only for a first meeting.

The Alphabet, Names and Origins

Alphabet and Origins
Big questionWhat can a person's name, and the way they spell it, tell you about who they are and where they come from?
The story

A Name with Two Last Names

A new classmate spells her full name, and the class learns why it is so long.

On Monday, a new student stood at the front of Señor Ramírez's class. "Me llamo Valentina Gómez Ortiz," she said (My name is Valentina Gómez Ortiz). A boy in the back whispered, "Two last names?" Valentina heard him and smiled. "Sí, dos apellidos" (Yes, two last names).

Señor Ramírez handed her a marker. "¿Cómo se escribe?" he asked (How is it spelled?). Valentina spelled her first name out loud with Spanish letter names: "ve, a, ele, e, ene, te, i, ene, a." The class wrote along. Some students wrote a b at first, because in Spanish b and v sound almost the same.

Then she explained. "Gómez es el apellido de mi papá. Ortiz es el apellido de mi mamá" (Gómez is my dad's last name. Ortiz is my mom's last name). In many Spanish-speaking families, a person carries both. At school in Chicago, her records only had room for one, so sometimes teachers called her "Valentina Ortiz" by mistake.

"¿De dónde eres?" asked Tomás (Where are you from?). "Soy de Chicago, pero mi familia es de Puebla, México" (I'm from Chicago, but my family is from Puebla, Mexico). She wrote Gómez on the board and pointed to the accent mark on the o. "No olviden el acento" (Don't forget the accent), she said, and the class laughed.

The Spanish Alphabet

18.1

Twenty-Seven Letters

Main ideaThe Spanish alphabet has 27 letters: the 26 English letters plus ñ.

Picture a class roster in Spanish with the names Nuñez and Nuto. Which comes first? The Spanish (alphabet) has one letter English lacks: , the letter ñ. It comes right after n. So Nuto comes before Nuñez, because n comes before ñ. Words with ñ get their own place in the dictionary.

The Real Academia Española (Royal Spanish Academy), called the RAE, is based in Madrid. It works with language academies across the Spanish-speaking world. In 2010 their spelling guide set the alphabet at 27 letters. Before that, many classrooms taught ch and ll as separate letters.

Today ch and ll are called digraphs, pairs of letters that make one sound. They are still used all the time, as in chocolate and llamar (to call). But they are not counted as their own letters. So in a dictionary, chico is filed under c, between words starting with ce and ci.

18.2

The Names of the Letters

Main ideaEach Spanish letter has a name, like be, ce, hache and jota, that you use to spell aloud.

Picture spelling your email address to a receptionist in Spanish. You need the letter names. Vowels are simple: a, e, i, o, u, each said with one clear sound. Many letters add an e: be (b), ce (c), de (d), ge (g), pe (p), te (t). Others start with e: efe (f), ele (l), eme (m), ene (n), ese (s).

Some names surprise English speakers. H is , even though h is silent. J is , said with a sound from the throat. G is ge, which sounds like the Spanish j when it comes before e or i. Q is cu, R is erre, X is equis and Z is zeta.

Three letters have more than one common name. For v, many people say uve, and in much of Latin America people say ve or ve corta. W is uve doble or doble ve. Y is ye, and older speakers may call it i griega (Greek i). All of these are correct, so listen to what the speaker uses.

18.3

¿Cómo Se Escribe?

Main ideaAsk ¿Cómo se escribe? to learn how a word is spelled, and say con acento for an accent mark.

Picture a new classmate named Ximena. You want to write her name on a card. You ask, (How is it spelled? literally how is it written?). She answers, "equis, i, eme, e, ene, a." Asking is polite, and it keeps you from guessing wrong.

Spelling aloud has helpers. For a capital letter, say (capital). For an accent mark, say con acento or con tilde after the letter: "e con acento." Double letters can be said as dos: "dos erres" (two r’s) or "doble ere." So Ramírez is "erre mayúscula, a, eme, i con acento, erre, e, zeta."

Watch for letters that sound alike in Spanish. B and v sound almost the same, so people ask, "¿Con be o con uve?" S, z and c before e or i sound the same in most of Latin America. That is why spelling a name aloud matters, even for native speakers.

Think it through
Spelling Ramírez

Student: ¿Cómo se escribe su apellido, profesor? Sr. Ramírez: erre mayúscula, a, eme, i con acento, erre, e, zeta. Student: ¿Con zeta o con ese? Sr. Ramírez: Con zeta. Letters: R-a-m-í-r-e-z.

Spell the last name of someone in your family aloud, including capitals and accents.

Section 2

Names and Last Names

18.4

Me Llamo and ¿Cómo Te Llamas?

Main ideaAsk ¿Cómo te llamas? (tú) or ¿Cómo se llama usted? and answer Me llamo plus your name.

Picture the first minute of class. You turn to the student next to you and ask, (What’s your name? literally how do you call yourself?). She answers, Valentina (my name is Valentina). The verb is llamarse, to be called.

Look at the pattern. Me llamo is I call myself. Te llamas is you call yourself, for tú. Se llama is he, she or you (usted) call yourself. So you ask a teacher, "¿Cómo se llama usted?" The ending changes with the person: -o for yo, -as for tú, -a for él, ella and usted.

A common mistake is saying "Me llamo es Valentina," copying English "my name is." Llamo is already the verb, so es is extra. You can also say "Mi nombre es Valentina" (my name is Valentina) or "Soy Valentina" (I’m Valentina). All three are correct.

Think it through
Three Ways to Say Your Name

Classmate: ¿Cómo te llamas? You: Me llamo Leo. Teacher: ¿Cómo se llama usted? Parent: Me llamo Rosa Díaz. Other correct answers: Mi nombre es Leo. / Soy Leo. Not correct: Me llamo es Leo.

Write the question you would ask a new classmate and the one you would ask the new principal.

What's your name? — formal, for usted

Check yourself

1. Which sentence is correct?

Why: Me llamo already contains the verb, so no es is needed.

18.5

Two Last Names

Main ideaMany Spanish speakers traditionally carry two last names: first the father's, then the mother's.

Picture Valentina Gómez Ortiz. Her first name is Valentina. Her apellidos (last names, surnames) are Gómez and Ortiz. Traditionally, the first comes from the father, and the second comes from the mother. This custom is common in Spain and much of Latin America.

Walk through it with her parents. Her father is Javier Gómez Ruiz. Her mother is Laura Ortiz Vega. Valentina takes Gómez, her father’s first last name, and Ortiz, her mother’s first last name. So she is Valentina Gómez Ortiz. Her brother Mateo is Mateo Gómez Ortiz too.

In daily life, many people use only the first last name: "Valentina Gómez." A mistake English speakers make is thinking the second last name is the main one, and calling her "Ms. Ortiz." In some countries, laws now let parents choose the order, so families can differ. When in doubt, ask.

Think it through

18.6

Señor, Señora and Señorita

Main ideaUse señor, señora or señorita with a last name to address adults with respect.

Picture meeting your friend’s mother, Laura Ortiz Vega. You want to be polite. You say, "Buenos días, señora Ortiz." (Mr., sir) and señora (Mrs., ma’am) go before the last name. With two last names, use the first one: señora Ortiz, not señora Vega.

Señorita (Miss) is used for a young woman or girl. Some adults now prefer señora or just their name, so follow their lead. Written, these titles are often shortened with a capital: Sr., Sra., Srta. When you speak directly to the person, the title starts with a small letter in the middle of a sentence: "Gracias, señor Ramírez."

Teachers are often called (male teacher) or profesora (female teacher), sometimes with the last name. In many schools students also say maestro or maestra. A common mistake is to say "señor" plus a first name to a teacher, like "señor Luis," when the school uses last names. Listen to what classmates say.

Think it through
Who Is Who?

Section 3

Where You Are From

18.7

¿De Dónde Eres?

Main ideaAsk ¿De dónde eres? and answer Soy de plus a city or country.

Picture a new friend at a summer program. You want to know where she is from. You ask, (Where are you from?). De means from, dónde means where, and eres means you are. Spanish puts de at the front of the question. English leaves "from" at the end.

She answers, Chicago (I’m from Chicago). Soy means I am. For a city or a country, just add the name: "Soy de Puerto Rico." "Soy de Guatemala." With usted, ask "¿De dónde es usted?" The answer still starts with soy.

Two common mistakes come from English. One is "¿Dónde eres de?" with de at the end. The other is "Estoy de Chicago." For where you are from, Spanish uses ser (soy, eres, es), not estar. Dónde has an accent because it is a question word.

Think it through
At a Summer Program

Leo: Hola, ¿de dónde eres? María: Soy de Chicago. Mi familia es de Guatemala. ¿Y tú? Leo: Soy de Aurora, pero mis abuelos son de Puerto Rico. (my grandparents) Notice: soy, es and son all come from ser, the verb for origin.

Answer ¿De dónde eres? for yourself, and add where one person in your family is from.

Why: Spanish puts de first, and dónde has an accent: ¿De dónde eres?

2. Which answer is correct?

Why: Origin uses ser: Soy de Chicago.

3. You ask an older visitor where she is from. What do you say?

Why: With an older visitor use usted and its verb es.
18.8

Twenty Spanish-Speaking Countries

Main ideaSpanish is an official language in 20 countries across the Americas, Europe and Africa.

Picture a world map with pins in every country where Spanish is an official language. You would count 20 countries. One is in North America: México. Six are in Central America: Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Panamá. Two are island countries in the Caribbean: Cuba and the Dominican Republic, in Spanish la República Dominicana.

Nine are in South America: Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Perú, Bolivia, Chile, Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay. In Europe there is España (Spain). In Africa there is Equatorial Guinea, called Guinea Ecuatorial. Notice the accents in México, Panamá, Perú and España’s ñ.

Puerto Rico is not on this list of countries, because it is a territory of the United States. But Spanish is an official language there, next to English. The United States also has millions of Spanish speakers, including many families in Chicago. Each place has its own history, foods and ways of speaking.

18.9

Nationalities and Agreement

Main ideaNationality words change for gender: mexicano, mexicana; español, española.

Picture two cousins from Mexico, a boy and a girl. He says, "Soy ." She says, "Soy mexicana." A nationality word is an adjective, and in Spanish most adjectives match the person. Words ending in -o switch to -a for a female: colombiano, colombiana; peruano, peruana.

Words ending in a consonant add -a for a female: español, española; inglés, inglesa. Notice that inglés loses its accent in inglesa. Words ending in -e stay the same for everyone: (from the United States), costarricense (Costa Rican), nicaragüense. For a group, add -s or -es: mexicanos, españoles.

Two mistakes are common. English speakers capitalize nationality words, but Spanish writes them in lowercase: "Soy mexicana," not "Soy Mexicana." Country names still get a capital: México. The other mistake is using the -o form for everyone, as in "Ella es mexicano." Check who you are describing.

Think it through
Matching the Person

Carlos es de Colombia → Carlos es colombiano. Ana es de Colombia → Ana es colombiana. Pablo es de España → Pablo es español. Lucía es de España → Lucía es española. Sam y Mia son de Estados Unidos → Son estadounidenses.

Your friend Elena is from Perú. Write one sentence saying her nationality.

Words to know
mexicano, mexicana
Mexican — -o for a male, -a for a female
español, española
Spanish (from Spain) — add -a for a female
estadounidense
from the United States — same form for males and females
la nacionalidad
the nationality — written in lowercase in Spanish: cubano, cubana
Check yourself

1. Lucía is from Spain. Which sentence is correct?

Why: A consonant-ending nationality adds -a for a female, and nationalities are lowercase.

2. Which nationality word is the same for males and females?

Why: Words ending in -e, like estadounidense, do not change for gender.

3. Which sentence has the correct capitalization?

Why: Nationalities are lowercase in Spanish, but country names are capitalized.
18.10

Accent Marks Change Meaning

Main ideaA written accent can change a word's meaning: papá is dad, but papa is potato.

Picture writing a birthday card: "Feliz cumpleaños, papa." Without the accent, you just wished a happy birthday to a potato. (dad) has an accent on the last a. Papa, with no accent, means potato in much of Latin America. The accent mark, or la tilde, changes both the stress and the meaning.

An accent shows which syllable is stressed when the normal rule does not apply. In papá, the stress falls on the end: pa-PÁ. In papa, it falls on the first syllable: PA-pa. You saw this in adiós and Gómez too. Leave the accent out, and a reader may stress the wrong part.

Some short words use an accent only to show a different meaning. Tú (you) and tu (your). Él (he) and el (the). Sí (yes) and si (if). English speakers often skip these marks because English has none. In Spanish, a missing accent is a spelling error, just like a missing letter.

Think it through
Pairs That Differ by One Mark

papá (dad) / papa (potato) tú (you) / tu (your) él (he) / el (the) sí (yes) / si (if) Try it: "Él es mi papá" means He is my dad. "El papa" without accents on either word would be the potato.

Write one sentence that uses both tú and tu correctly.

Words to know
el acento
the accent mark — also called la tilde; shows stress or a different meaning
papá
dad — stress on the end; papa without the accent means potato
sí
yes — si without the accent means if
Check yourself

1. What does papa (no accent) mean in much of Latin America?

Why: Papa without an accent means potato; papá with the accent means dad.

2. Which sentence means "Yes, he is my friend"?

Why: Sí (yes) and él (he) both need their accent marks.

3. Why does Gómez have an accent mark?

Why: The accent tells the reader where the stress falls when the normal rule would put it elsewhere.
