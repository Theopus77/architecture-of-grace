Nutrition

Two granola bars sit side by side with the same green box, the same oats on the front and the same price, and one has four times the added sugar of the other. Every one of these is a nutrition problem, and none of them is solved by knowing that vegetables are good for you. They are solved by reading a label, costing a menu and planning a week.

The story

Two Bars, One Shelf

Two granola bars promise the same thing on the front, and the back of the box tells a different story.

Maya had ten minutes before practice and two dollars in her pocket. The corner store by school kept the granola bars on the shelf nearest the door, and two boxes sat side by side. Both were green. Both said "whole grain" in big letters, both showed oats spilling out of a bowl, and both cost the same. She grabbed one, then stopped, because Mr. Alvarez had spent all of third period saying the front of a box is an advertisement and the back is a fact sheet.

She turned both boxes around. The panels looked almost identical at first: black lines, bold numbers, the words Nutrition Facts across the top. Then she started reading line by line the way he had shown them. The first box said one bar was a serving and had 190 calories. The second box said one bar was a serving and had 140 calories. Fine. Then she found the line that mattered. Added Sugars: 12 g on the first, 3 g on the second.

Below that, the percent column told her how big those numbers really were. The first bar's added sugars were 24% of the Daily Value, almost a quarter of a whole day's limit in one snack. The second bar's were 6%. She flipped to the ingredient lists. The first bar's list started with oats, then sugar, then corn syrup, then honey, three kinds of sugar in the first four ingredients. The second started with oats, then peanuts, then dates.

Maya bought the second bar. It was chewier and less sweet, and she noticed she was not hungry again twenty minutes into practice the way she usually was. The next day she told Mr. Alvarez, and he asked the class the question he had been building toward all week: if two foods look the same on the front, what six things should you check on the back before you decide? By the end of the chapter, every student could answer it.

The Six Nutrients

31.1

Carbohydrates and Fiber

Main ideaCarbohydrates are the body's main fuel, and the fiber in whole grains, fruits, vegetables and beans slows that fuel down and keeps digestion moving.

Sit down to a bowl of oatmeal, a slice of toast or a plate of rice and you are eating mostly . A carbohydrate is a nutrient made of sugar units, and the body breaks it into glucose, the sugar that muscles and the brain burn for energy. Each gram of carbohydrate gives the body about 4 calories. A is simply a unit of food energy, the same way an inch is a unit of length. Carbohydrates come in two forms that behave differently once they are inside you.

Simple carbohydrates are single or paired sugar units: the sugar in a soda, honey, fruit juice and the natural sugar in milk and fruit. They digest fast and hit the bloodstream quickly. Complex carbohydrates are long chains of sugar units called starch, found in bread, pasta, rice, potatoes, corn and beans. They take longer to break down, so their energy arrives more slowly and lasts longer. A bowl of oatmeal at seven keeps you going through third period in a way a doughnut does not, and the difference is mostly this.

is a carbohydrate the body cannot digest at all, and that is exactly why it matters. It comes from the walls of plant cells: the bran on a whole grain, the skin of an apple, the inside of a bean. Because it passes through undigested, it slows the absorption of everything else, helps you feel full, and keeps food moving through the intestines. The Nutrition Facts label lists Dietary Fiber on its own line. A common mistake is to assume brown-colored bread is whole grain; check the ingredient list for the word whole, as in whole wheat flour, as the first ingredient.

The rule is this: make about half your grains whole grains, and get most of your carbohydrates from foods that carry fiber with them. That does not make a cookie or a white roll bad; it makes them a smaller part of the day. When someone says they are cutting out carbs, they are cutting out the body’s main fuel source, and the usual result is feeling tired and foggy. The better move is choosing which carbohydrates, not whether.

31.2

Proteins and Fats

Main ideaProteins build and repair the body, fats carry energy and certain vitamins, and both are needed every day in the right amounts.

Scrape your knee and a week later the skin is whole again. That repair job is done with , the nutrient the body uses to build and fix muscle, skin, blood, hair and every organ. Protein is made of smaller units called amino acids, and the body needs about twenty of them. It can make some on its own; the rest, called essential amino acids, have to come from food. Each gram of protein gives about 4 calories, the same as a carbohydrate, but the body would rather use it for building than for fuel.

Animal foods such as meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy carry all the essential amino acids together and are called complete proteins. Plant foods such as beans, lentils, nuts, seeds and grains each carry some. Eat a variety of them across a day and the body gets the full set. Rice and beans, peanut butter on whole wheat bread, hummus and pita: these pairs have fed families for centuries because together they are complete. A vegetarian does not need to combine them in the same meal, only across the day.

has a bad reputation it does not fully deserve. Fat gives 9 calories per gram, more than twice what carbohydrate or protein gives, so a little goes a long way. The body uses it to store energy, insulate against cold, protect organs and carry the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. The kind of fat matters. , found in olive oil, canola oil, nuts, avocados and fish, is liquid at room temperature and is the kind to lean toward. , found in butter, lard, fatty meat and full-fat dairy, is solid at room temperature and is listed on the label so you can keep it moderate.

One kind of fat to avoid is trans fat, made by pumping hydrogen into vegetable oil to make it solid; it appears on the label as Trans Fat and in the ingredients as partially hydrogenated oil. Most of it has been removed from the food supply, but the line is still there. A common mistake is cutting all fat from a meal and then eating extra sugar to make up for the missing flavor and fullness. A tablespoon of olive oil on a salad helps the body absorb the vitamins in the vegetables. Fat is not the enemy; the amount and the kind are the whole question.

31.3

Vitamins, Minerals and Water

Main ideaVitamins and minerals give no calories but run the body's chemistry, and water, the sixth nutrient, is needed in larger amounts than any of the others.

Slice an orange and you are holding vitamin C. Pour a glass of milk and you are holding calcium and vitamin D. are chemicals the body needs in tiny amounts to make its reactions work: seeing in dim light, healing a cut, turning food into energy. They give no calories. They split into two groups. Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat and can be stored in the body. Water-soluble vitamins, the B vitamins and vitamin C, dissolve in water, are not stored well, and need to come in regularly.

are elements the body needs, also with no calories: calcium for bones and teeth, iron to carry oxygen in the blood, potassium for the heart and muscles, sodium to balance fluids, and others. The current Nutrition Facts label lists four of these because many people do not get enough: vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium. Sodium is also listed, for the opposite reason. Iron is worth a special mention for teenagers, especially girls, because low iron is one of the most common nutrient shortfalls and shows up as tiredness. Red meat, beans, spinach and fortified cereal all carry it.

Water is the sixth nutrient and the one people forget. More than half of your body weight is water. It carries nutrients to cells, carries waste away, cools you through sweat and cushions your joints. You lose it constantly, breathing, sweating and using the bathroom, so it has to come back in constantly. Drink when you are thirsty, drink more when it is hot or when you are exercising, and check the simplest signal there is: urine that is pale yellow means you are keeping up, and dark yellow means you are behind.

A common mistake is to think a vitamin pill can replace vegetables. A pill carries the vitamins but not the fiber, the water, the minerals and the hundreds of other plant compounds a carrot carries. Another mistake is the belief that more is always better; fat-soluble vitamins can build up to harmful levels from big supplement doses. The safe rule for a healthy person is variety: a range of colors on the plate across the week covers the vitamins and minerals without a chart.

MyPlate

31.4

Five Groups on One Plate

Main ideaMyPlate turns nutrition science into a picture: fruits and vegetables on half the plate, grains and protein foods on the other half, and dairy on the side.

Picture a dinner plate with a line down the middle. On the left, fruits and vegetables, with vegetables taking a bit more room. On the right, grains and protein foods, with grains taking a bit more room. Beside the plate, a small circle for dairy. That picture is , the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s guide to a balanced meal, and it replaced the old food pyramid in 2011. Its point is that you do not need a calculator at dinner; you need to look at the plate and check the shares.

The five groups each supply something the others do not. Vegetables bring fiber, vitamins A and C, potassium and folate; eat a variety, including dark green, red and orange ones, and beans and peas count here or as protein. Fruits bring fiber, vitamin C and potassium; whole fruit beats juice because the fiber is still there. Grains bring carbohydrate for energy and B vitamins, and at least half should be whole grains. Protein foods, which include meat, poultry, seafood, eggs, beans, nuts and seeds, bring protein, iron and zinc. Dairy brings calcium, vitamin D and protein; fortified soy milk counts.

Building a plate is a matter of looking at what is there and moving things around. A plate that is three-quarters pasta with a spoon of sauce is mostly grains. Cut the pasta share to a quarter, add a big scoop of sautéed peppers and zucchini, add a piece of chicken or a scoop of white beans, put a glass of milk beside it, and the same dinner now covers all five groups. The pasta did not become bad; it became the right size.

A common mistake is to treat MyPlate as a rule for every single meal. It is a pattern for the day. Breakfast might be heavy on grains and dairy, lunch heavy on vegetables and protein, and the day still balances. Another mistake is forgetting that many family dishes mix groups in one pot: a stir-fry, a stew, a rice bowl, a taco. Look at what is in it and count the groups; a bowl of chili with beans, tomatoes, onion and a sprinkle of cheese over rice covers four of five.

31.5

Portions and Servings

Main ideaA serving is a standard amount printed on a label; a portion is what you actually put on the plate, and the two are often very different.

Pour cereal into a bowl the way you normally do, then measure it. Most people pour two to three times the serving printed on the box. That gap has a name. A is a standard measured amount, the one the Nutrition Facts label uses. A is the amount you choose to eat. The label’s numbers are true for the serving, so if your portion is double, every number on the label doubles too: calories, sodium, added sugars, all of it.

Measuring every meal is not realistic, so learn a few hand and object comparisons for common foods. A cup of cooked rice, pasta or cereal is about the size of a fist. Three ounces of cooked meat, one common serving, is about the size of a deck of cards or the palm of your hand. An ounce of cheese is about the size of your thumb, or four dice. A tablespoon of peanut butter or oil is about the size of the tip of your thumb. A medium piece of fruit is about the size of a tennis ball.

Restaurant and packaged portions have grown over the decades, and plates and cups have grown with them. A twenty-ounce soda bottle is two and a half servings, and the label now often shows both the per-serving and the per-bottle numbers side by side for that reason. A common mistake is reading a label, seeing 150 calories, and eating the whole bag without checking Servings Per Container, which might say four. That bag was 600 calories, not 150.

None of this means eating tiny amounts. An athlete or a growing teenager needs more than a small child, and hunger is real information. The point is to know what you are eating. Serve food onto a plate instead of eating from the bag, use a smaller plate when you want to eat less, and fill the first half of the plate with vegetables and fruit before you add the rest. Then eat until you are satisfied and stop, which is a skill that takes practice.

Reading the Label

31.6

Serving Size and Calories

Main ideaRead a Nutrition Facts label from the top: the serving size and servings per container set the scale for every number below them.

Turn a box of crackers around and find the black-bordered panel headed Nutrition Facts. Every packaged food sold in the United States carries one in the same order, so once you can read one, you can read them all. Start at the top. The first lines are Servings Per Container and , written in a household measure and in grams, for example "16 crackers (30g)". This is the amount every number below describes. If you eat 32 crackers, double everything.

The next line, in large bold type, is per serving. The type is big on purpose: the 2016 label redesign made calories and serving size the most visible things on the panel. A calorie count on its own is neither good nor bad. A 400-calorie meal is a reasonable lunch; a 400-calorie handful of candy is a lot of energy for very little else. What the calories come with is what the rest of the label tells you, which is why you keep reading instead of stopping here.

Serving sizes are set by the government to reflect what people typically eat, not what a company wishes you ate, and they were updated in the redesign. A pint of ice cream, once labeled as four servings, is now labeled as three because that is closer to real portions. Packages that could be one or two servings, like a 20-ounce drink, often show two columns, per serving and per package. Read both column heads before you compare anything.

The most common label mistake is comparing two foods with different serving sizes as if they were the same. Cereal A lists 120 calories for a ¾-cup serving; cereal B lists 160 calories for a 1-cup serving. Cereal B is not more caloric; per cup, A is 160 too. Before you compare any two labels, check that the serving sizes match, and if they do not, do the arithmetic to a common amount. Then the comparison means something.

31.7

Percent Daily Value

Main ideaThe percent Daily Value column tells you whether a serving is high or low in a nutrient: 5% or less is low, 20% or more is high.

Look at the right edge of the label and you will find a column of percentages headed % Daily Value, or % DV. This column is the shortcut. Each Daily Value is the total amount of a nutrient to aim for, or to stay under, in a whole day, based on a 2,000-calorie diet. The percentage tells you how much of that day’s amount one serving supplies. If sodium says 15% DV, one serving gives you 15 percent of a day’s sodium limit, and you have 85 percent left for everything else you eat.

The FDA gives a simple rule for reading the column. Five percent or less is in that nutrient. Twenty percent or more is . For nutrients you want more of, fiber, vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium, look for high numbers. For nutrients to limit, saturated fat, sodium and added sugars, look for low numbers. A can of soup at 40% DV sodium is high, and that is one serving; many cans hold two.

Percentages let you compare foods without knowing any of the actual gram or milligram amounts. Two frozen dinners: one shows 35% DV sodium and the other 18%. You do not need to remember that the Daily Value for sodium is 2,300 mg to see which is the better choice on sodium. Protein and total sugars usually have no % DV listed, protein because it is rarely short in the U.S. diet, and total sugars because no Daily Value has been set for natural sugars. Added sugars do have one.

A common mistake is reading the percentage as a share of the food, as if 15% DV sodium meant the food is fifteen percent salt. It means fifteen percent of a day. Another mistake is applying the 2,000-calorie basis to everyone. A small child needs less and a large athlete may need more, so the column is a scale for comparing foods, not a prescription. Use it to pick the higher-fiber cereal and the lower-sodium soup, and let hunger and activity set the totals.

31.8

Sodium, Added Sugars and Ingredients

Main ideaThe sodium and added sugars lines show the two things most packaged foods have too much of, and the ingredient list shows where they came from.

Find the Sodium line about a third of the way down. is the part of salt the body needs a little of and most people get far too much of, mostly from packaged and restaurant food rather than the salt shaker. The Daily Value is 2,300 milligrams, about one teaspoon of salt, and the label shows milligrams and the percentage. Bread, cheese, deli meat, canned soup, frozen meals, pizza and sauces are the big sources, and none of them taste especially salty, which is exactly why the line is there.

Below Total Carbohydrate, indented, are Dietary Fiber, Total Sugars and, indented again, Includes Added Sugars. are sugars put in during processing, sugar, corn syrup, honey, agave, fruit juice concentrate, as opposed to the sugars naturally in milk or fruit. This line was new in the 2016 redesign and it is the one that lets you tell a sweetened yogurt from a plain one, or a granola bar that is mostly oats from one that is mostly syrup. The Daily Value is 50 grams, and one 12-ounce can of regular soda uses most of it.

Under the panel is the , in order by weight from most to least. The first three ingredients are most of the food. If sugar or a syrup is in the first three, the food is sweet whatever the front says. Sugar wears many names: corn syrup, high-fructose corn syrup, dextrose, cane juice, honey, maltose, rice syrup, and anything ending in -ose. Manufacturers can split sugar into several kinds so no single one lands first; count them all. A short list of things you recognize is usually a good sign.

A common mistake is trusting the front of the package. "Made with real fruit" can mean a few drops of juice concentrate. "Natural" has no fixed legal meaning for most foods. "Lightly sweetened" is not a defined term. The words on the front are marketing; the numbers on the back are regulated. Maya’s two granola bars in the chapter story had the same front and very different backs, and the back is where she found the truth: 12 grams of added sugar against 3, and three sugars in the first four ingredients against none.

Different Bodies, Different Needs

31.9

Allergies and the Label

Main ideaA food allergy can be life-threatening, and the law requires the nine major allergens to be named plainly on every packaged food label.

A friend at the lunch table takes one bite of a cookie and her lips start to tingle and swell. That is a : the immune system treating a harmless protein in food as an attacker. Reactions range from hives and an itchy mouth to , a severe whole-body reaction that closes the airway and drops blood pressure and can kill within minutes. Someone having anaphylaxis needs epinephrine, usually from an auto-injector, right away, and a call to 911 even if the injector works.

U.S. law requires packaged food labels to name nine major allergens in plain English: milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame. Sesame was added in 2023. The name appears either in the ingredient list itself, for example "whey (milk)", or in a Contains statement right under the list: "Contains: wheat, milk, soy." Look for the Contains line first; if there is none, read the whole list. Some labels add a voluntary "may contain" or "made in a facility that also processes" warning for cross-contact, and someone with a severe allergy takes those seriously.

An is different. Lactose intolerance, for example, means the body lacks the enzyme to digest milk sugar, so milk causes stomach pain and gas but not an immune reaction. Uncomfortable, not dangerous. Celiac disease is different again: an immune disease in which gluten, the protein in wheat, barley and rye, damages the small intestine. People with celiac disease must avoid gluten entirely, and the label term to look for is gluten-free, which the FDA defines.

If you cook for other people, allergies are part of the job. Ask before you serve. Read every label, including on foods that seem safe; soy sauce contains wheat, many chocolates contain milk, some pesto contains tree nuts. Wash cutting boards, knives and hands between an allergen and the food for the person avoiding it, the same cross-contact rule you learned for raw chicken. A common mistake is assuming a little bit is fine. For a true allergy, a trace can be enough.

Main ideaSome people plan around a medical need and some around a belief or a choice; either way, the same six nutrients still have to show up.

Water and What You Drink

Main ideaWater is the everyday drink; sugary drinks are the largest single source of added sugar for teenagers, and the label tells you exactly how much.

The calories do not fill you up the way food does.

Water does the job the body actually needs done. It carries nutrients, cools you, keeps joints moving and keeps your brain sharp; even mild shows up as a headache, tiredness and trouble concentrating. 32.7

A Sample Weekly Food Budget

Main ideaA food budget divides a fixed amount across the week's meals in advance, so the receipt is a check on the plan instead of a surprise.

A is an amount of money set aside for food for a period, and the plan for how to spend it. Every figure in this lesson is an example. Say a family of four has $200 for the week. Before anyone shops, that $200 gets divided on paper. One way: $110 for dinner ingredients, $35 for breakfasts, $40 for lunches and snacks, and $15 held back for the thing you forgot, milk on Thursday or a dozen eggs. Written down, the numbers force choices before the store does.

Now cost the dinners. Sunday, a whole chicken at $1.49 a pound, 5 pounds, $7.45, plus 3 pounds of potatoes at $0.89, $2.67, and carrots, $1.29: $11.41. Monday’s chicken soup uses the carcass and leftover meat, an onion, $0.60, and a cup of rice from the bag, call it $0.40: $1.00. Tuesday, a pound of dried black beans, $1.49, rice, $0.80, tortillas, $2.29, cheese, $3.49: $8.07. Keep going through the week and add it up. If the total for seven dinners is $118, it is $8 over the $110 line, and you go looking for the $8 before you shop, not after.

Finding the $8 is usually easy once you can see the numbers. Swap one meat dinner for an egg or bean dinner and save five or six dollars. Drop the pre-shredded cheese for the block and save a dollar. Use frozen vegetables instead of fresh on two nights. What you should not do is cut the vegetables out altogether or shrink the portions until people are hungry; the goal of the budget is to feed the family well, and the numbers serve that goal, not the other way around.

After the trip, read the against the plan. Tasha’s family in the chapter story came home at $168.40 against $200, and the $31.60 difference went into an envelope for next week. Over a month, that habit adds up to a cushion for the week the car needs a tire. A common mistake is having a budget and never checking the receipt against it; the numbers only help if they are compared. Keep the receipts in a drawer or take a photo, and add them up on Sunday when you plan the next week.

Stretching the Dollar

Main ideaThe cheapest nutritious foods in the store are dried beans, rice, oats, eggs, frozen vegetables, bananas and whole chickens; build around them and the budget stretches.

Walk the store with a calculator and a pattern appears. Some of the cheapest foods per pound are also among the most nutritious: dried beans and lentils, rice, oats, eggs, peanut butter, potatoes, cabbage, carrots, bananas, frozen vegetables, canned tomatoes, whole chickens and chicken thighs, and store-brand milk. A pound of dried beans, about $1.50, makes six cups cooked, enough protein for a family dinner and more. A dozen eggs makes two dinners. These are the foods that families all over the world have built meals around for centuries, for exactly this reason.

A whole chicken costs less per pound than any of its parts and gives a dinner, a soup and a stock. Ground turkey is often cheaper than ground beef. Eggs and beans are the least expensive proteins of all, and a week with two meatless dinners, a frittata and a chili, can save ten dollars without anyone feeling short. Mixing works too: a half pound of ground beef stretched with a can of beans feeds the same table as a full pound.

32.9

Storing Food Safely

Main ideaWhere and how food is stored decides whether the money spent on it is eaten or thrown away: cold food at or below 40 °F, frozen at 0 °F, and raw meat on the bottom shelf.

Bags on the counter, cold things first. Meat, poultry, dairy and eggs go into the refrigerator before anything else is unpacked, and the refrigerator must hold at or below 40 °F. Put a cheap refrigerator thermometer on the middle shelf and check it; a refrigerator running at 45 °F is quietly spoiling everything in it. The runs at 0 °F. Raw meat and poultry go on the bottom shelf, in a dish or a bag, so their juices cannot drip onto the vegetables or the leftovers below. That one habit prevents most cross-contamination in a home kitchen.

The refrigerator door is the warmest part, so it holds condiments, not milk. The crisper drawers hold produce; many have a humidity setting, higher for leafy greens, lower for fruit. Some produce belongs out of the refrigerator: potatoes, onions, winter squash and garlic keep for weeks in a cool, dark cupboard, and refrigerating them makes potatoes sweet and onions soft. Tomatoes and bananas lose flavor in the cold. Bread keeps a few days on the counter and months in the freezer.

Dates on packages confuse people. "Sell by" is a note for the store. "Best by" or "best if used by" is about peak quality, not safety. "Use by" is the last date recommended for use at peak quality, and only on infant formula is it a safety date. Most foods are fine after these dates if they were stored properly; the real test is smell, look and texture. What does matter is the refrigerator clock: raw ground meat and poultry within one to two days, raw whole cuts within three to five days, cooked leftovers within three to four days. Freeze anything you will not reach in time.

Label everything you freeze with the name and the date, because a frozen block with no label is a mystery nobody will thaw. Freeze in meal-sized portions, flat in bags so they stack and thaw fast. Thaw in the refrigerator, in cold water changed every 30 minutes, or in the microwave if cooking right away; never on the counter, where the outside sits in the danger zone while the middle is still frozen. A common mistake is a freezer packed with unlabeled food that is never eaten; a freezer is a savings account only if you make withdrawals.

Main ideaOn a night with fifteen minutes, a stocked pantry, a few frozen staples and three fast patterns beat takeout on cost, nutrition and often on time.

It is 6:40, practice ran late, everyone is hungry, and the question is whether dinner is cooked or ordered. The answer was decided a week ago, when the pantry was stocked. A kitchen that always holds eggs, pasta, rice, canned beans, canned tomatoes, frozen vegetables, tortillas, cheese, onions, garlic and oil can produce a real dinner in fifteen minutes without a trip to the store. These are : the foods you rebuy before they run out, not when a recipe calls for them.

Three fast patterns cover most nights. The egg dinner: a frittata or scrambled eggs with whatever vegetables are around, toast on the side, eight minutes. The bean dinner: a can of beans, drained and rinsed, warmed with salsa and a handful of frozen corn, folded into tortillas with cheese, ten minutes. The pasta dinner: pasta boiling while a bag of frozen broccoli goes into the same pot for the last three minutes, tossed with olive oil, garlic and cheese, or with a jar of sauce, twelve minutes. Each one has a protein, a grain and a vegetable.

The microwave and the freezer are the fast cook’s friends. A baked potato takes an hour in the oven and seven minutes in the microwave; top it with beans and cheese or leftover chili. Frozen vegetables steam in the microwave in four minutes. Batch-cooked rice reheats in two. A sheet pan of chicken thighs and vegetables is not fifteen minutes, but it is five minutes of work and thirty-five of walking away, which on some nights is the better kind of fast.

Takeout is not a failure and every family orders sometimes. The point is that fast is a skill, and the skill is stocked on Sunday. 32.11

Cutting Food Waste

Main ideaMost household food waste comes from buying too much, storing it wrong and forgetting it; a plan, a first-in-first-out fridge and a Friday clean-out soup fix most of it.

Open the refrigerator on a Saturday and look at the back of the bottom shelf: the bag of spinach that turned to liquid, the half lemon, the yogurt three weeks past its date, the container of something from two Sundays ago. That is , and in the United States a large share of the food bought by households is thrown away uneaten. In a $200 week, wasting a fifth of the food is $40 in the trash, more than the entire held-back line in the budget. Waste is a nutrition problem and a money problem at the same time.

Waste has three main causes and each has a fix. Buying too much: the list with quantities on it, drawn from a plan, is the fix, and so is buying fragile produce for the first three days and sturdy produce for the last four. Storing wrong: the refrigerator at 40 °F, produce in the crisper, bread in the freezer, herbs in a glass of water. Forgetting: , the rule every restaurant kitchen uses, means new food goes to the back and older food moves to the front where it will be seen and used. An "eat me first" bin on the top shelf does the same job at home.

Plan a clean-out meal near the end of the week. Friday’s dinner is a soup, a stir-fry, a frittata or a fried rice built from whatever is left: the last of the vegetables, the rest of the roast chicken, the half can of beans. These dishes were invented for exactly this purpose in kitchens all over the world, and fried rice made from cold day-old rice is better than fried rice made from fresh. Overripe bananas become banana bread or go into the freezer for smoothies. Stale bread becomes croutons or breadcrumbs.

Know what the dates mean so good food does not go out with the bad. A yogurt a few days past its best-by date that smells and looks normal is fine. Milk that smells sour is not, whatever the date. Trust your senses over the print. A common mistake is the opposite one too: keeping cooked leftovers for a week because they look fine. Three to four days is the rule, and after that they go, or they should have gone into the freezer on day one. Between the plan and the freezer, most waste never happens.
