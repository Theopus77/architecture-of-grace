Festivals and Everyday Life

Practice

Many families in Illinois keep festivals that are thousands of years old. They sweep ancestors' graves in spring. They share mooncakes under the fall moon. They cheer for lion dancers in Chicago's Chinatown at Lunar New Year.

In this unit you will learn how these festivals work and where they come from. You will see how Confucius's teaching about family shows up in them. You will visit temples, learn about old exams, and walk through Chinatown.

By the end, you will be able to name the main festivals and explain what families do. You will be able to tell how people of many traditions keep them. And you will know how to visit a temple or festival with respect.

How do festivals, temples and neighborhoods keep old teachings part of everyday life?

The story

Brooms on the Hill

A family climbs a hill on a spring morning to care for the graves of people they love.

It is early April. Mei and her family drive out of the city to a quiet hillside cemetery. Her father carries a broom. Her grandmother carries a bag of oranges and a bunch of white flowers.

They stop at a stone with carved Chinese letters. This is the grave of Mei's great-grandparents. Mei never met them, but she knows their names. Her grandmother says them out loud as they begin to work.

Everyone has a job. Father sweeps away old leaves. Mei pulls weeds from the edges. Her brother wipes the stone with a wet cloth until the letters shine. Then Grandmother sets out the fruit and the flowers.

The family stands together and bows three times. Grandmother tells a story about how her father walked miles to school as a boy. Mei listens closely. On the drive home she thinks, "Now I know a little more about where I come from."

A Calendar That Follows the Moon

13.1

Months by the Moon

Main ideaThe traditional Chinese calendar counts months by the moon, so festival dates move on the everyday calendar.

Look up at the moon for a month. It grows from a thin sliver to a full circle and back again. That trip takes about 29 and a half days. The traditional Chinese calendar uses this trip to count months.

A calendar that counts by the moon is called a . The Chinese calendar also watches the sun, so the seasons stay in place. Because it uses both, scholars call it a lunisolar calendar. Each new month starts on the night of the new moon.

Most people in China today use the same calendar as Illinois for school and work. Families use the old calendar for festivals. So Lunar New Year can fall on a different day in January or February each year.

13.2

Twelve Animals, Twelve Years

Main ideaEach year in the Chinese calendar has one of twelve animals, and the list repeats every twelve years.

Ask a friend who celebrates Lunar New Year, "What is your animal?" They may say the Tiger or the Rabbit. Each year in the Chinese calendar is named for one of twelve animals. This set of animals is called the .

The twelve animals go in order: Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit, Dragon, Snake, Horse, Goat, Monkey, Rooster, Dog and Pig. After the Pig, the list starts again with the Rat. So each animal comes back every twelve years.

A well-known folk story tells how the animals raced across a river. The order they finished became the order of the years. The clever Rat rode on the Ox’s back and jumped ahead at the end. Families tell this story to children at New Year.

13.3

The Biggest Holiday of the Year

Main ideaLunar New Year is a time for family meals, cleaning, red decorations and good wishes.

Before Lunar New Year, many families clean their homes from top to bottom. People say this sweeps out the old year. Then they hang red paper with wishes for luck and health. Red is a color of joy in Chinese culture.

On New Year’s Eve, families share a big . People travel far to be home for it. Children often get red envelopes with money inside from older relatives. Many families also honor their ancestors with food and bows.

The celebration lasts about fifteen days. It ends with the on the first full moon of the year. Families hang lanterns and eat sweet rice balls. Round foods and a round moon stand for a family together.

Tomb-Sweeping Day

Main ideaOn Qingming, families clean the graves of their ancestors and bring gifts of food and flowers.

Mei’s family went to the cemetery on . The name means "clear and bright." It falls in early April, around April 4 or 5. In English it is often called Tomb-Sweeping Day.

On this day families visit the graves of their . They sweep, pull weeds and wipe the stones. Then they set out food, tea and flowers. Many bow or burn incense. Some families burn paper gifts for those who have died.

13.5

Respect for Family

Main ideaConfucius taught that caring for parents and honoring ancestors is an important part of being a good person.

Why do families work so hard on Qingming? Part of the answer comes from Confucius. He was a teacher in China about 2,500 years ago. His students wrote down his words in a book called the Analects.

Confucius taught (shyow). It means respect and care for your parents and family. In Analects, book 2, he says children should serve their parents with respect while they live. When parents die, children should bury them and honor them with proper rites.

Many families say Qingming puts this teaching into action. They remember who came before them. Not every family who keeps Qingming thinks of Confucius. For many, it is simply what their family has always done.

Dragon Boats and a Poet

People tell that the festival remembers (choo yuen). He was a poet and royal adviser who lived about 2,300 years ago. The story says he was sent away by his king, and he drowned himself in a river in sorrow for his land.

In the story, people raced out in boats to search for him. They threw rice into the water. Today people eat , sticky rice wrapped in leaves. Dragon boat races now take place around the world, including in Chicago.

13.8

The Lady in the Moon

Main ideaFamilies tell the old story of Chang'e, who floated up to live on the moon.

At the Mid-Autumn Festival, grandparents often tell the story of Chang&#x27;e (chahng-uh). Long ago, the story says, ten suns burned the earth. A great archer named Hou Yi shot down nine of them and saved the people.

As a reward, Hou Yi was given a potion that would let him live forever. Chang’e drank it. Tellings differ on why. Some say she was protecting it from a thief. She floated up to the moon and lives there still, with a white rabbit.

There are many versions of this . Families tell the one they learned as children. Looking at the full moon, children try to spot the rabbit’s shape. The story makes the moon feel like an old friend.

Why the Lion Bows

A boy in Chicago's Chinatown watches a lion dance stop at every shop door.

Drums boom along Wentworth Avenue in Chicago's Chinatown. It is Lunar New Year. Leo holds his aunt's hand and stands on his toes. A lion with a huge red and gold head dances down the street.

Two dancers are inside the lion. One works the head, and one is the back legs. The lion blinks its big eyes and shakes its fur. Cymbals crash each time it jumps.

Then the lion stops at a bakery door. It bows low, three times. The owner hangs a red envelope and some green lettuce by the door. The lion reaches up, takes them, and scatters the leaves in the street.

"Why does the lion bow at the doors?" Leo asks. His aunt smiles. "It is saying hello with respect," she says. "And it brings good wishes to each shop for the new year. The shop thanks the dancers with the envelope."

Temples for a Teacher

14.1

Confucius's Hometown Temple

Main ideaIn Qufu, Confucius's hometown, a large temple has honored him for more than 2,000 years.

Confucius was born in a place now called (choo-foo), in eastern China. After he died, people honored him at his old home. Over many centuries, rulers made the site larger and grander.

Today the Temple of Confucius in Qufu has many halls, courtyards and old trees. Its main hall has yellow roof tiles, a color once used for emperors. Stone tablets in the courtyards record gifts and visits by rulers over the centuries.

Near the temple stand the Kong family mansion and the Kong family cemetery. Confucius’s family name was Kong. In 1994, UNESCO named the temple, mansion and cemetery a World Heritage Site.

14.2

A Day for Teachers

Main ideaIn Taiwan, Teachers' Day falls on Confucius's traditional birthday and opens with a ceremony in his honor.

Confucius is honored as a great teacher. Tradition dates his birthday to about 551 BCE. In Taiwan, September 28 is kept as his birthday. It is also Teachers&#x27; Day.

Early that morning, a ceremony is held at the Taipei Confucius Temple. Musicians play old instruments. Young dancers move slowly in rows while holding feathers and flutes. Officials offer gifts and bow. This follows steps that are many centuries old.

Around the country, students thank their teachers with cards and flowers. Other places mark the day too. Qufu holds a large celebration each fall. China keeps its own Teachers’ Day on September 10.

14.3

Daoist Temples and Priests

Main ideaDaoist temples are living places where priests lead ceremonies and visitors light incense.

Some temples belong to . Daoism is a Chinese tradition that follows the Dao, or the Way. Many Daoists honor Laozi, the old teacher linked to a book called the Daodejing. Some honor him as a deity.

Step into a Daoist temple and you may smell incense. Visitors light sticks, hold them up, bow and place them in a large pot. Many are asking for health or blessings for family. Statues of deities sit in the halls.

Daoist wear long robes and often keep their hair in a knot. They chant old texts, play instruments and lead ceremonies for festivals, blessings and funerals. For many families, the temple is part of everyday life.

14.4

The Great Exams

Main ideaFor about 1,300 years, people in China took hard exams on the classics to become government officials.

Imagine a test so important that it could change your whole family’s life. In old China, men who wanted government jobs took the . These began around 605 CE and ended in 1905.

The exams tested the . These are old, honored books, like the Analects of Confucius. Students spent years memorizing them and writing essays about them. Test-takers sat alone in tiny rooms for days.

Passing brought honor to a whole family and village. Few passed the highest level. So the classics sat at the heart of schooling for about 1,300 years. That is why Confucius’s words spread so widely.

14.5

Learning Like Confucius

Main ideaConfucius taught that learning and practice go together and that anyone can be a teacher to you.

The Analects begins with a question about school. Confucius asks: is it not a joy to learn something and practice it often? He links learning with doing again and again.

Confucius also taught that we can learn from people around us. In Analects, book 7, he says that when he walks with two others, one can surely teach him. He copies their good ways and fixes in himself what is not good.

Students in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam memorized lines like these. Many families today still say Confucius’s ideas shape how much they value school. Others point out that people value learning for many reasons.

Ask ten Chinese American families about religion and you may hear ten answers. One family goes to a Christian church on Sunday. Another visits a Buddhist temple. Another keeps an ancestor shelf at home.

In China, people often speak of the : Confucian, Daoist and Buddhist. Many people draw on more than one. A family might honor ancestors in a Confucian way and visit a Daoist temple for a blessing.

Some families say they have no religion but still keep Qingming and New Year. Scholars even debate whether Confucianism is a religion. Some call it a way of life. The best way to learn is to listen to each family.

14.8

Visiting With Respect

Main ideaVisitors to temples and festivals show respect by watching quietly, asking first and following the host's lead.

Temples welcome visitors, but they are places of worship. People may be praying or making offerings. A respectful speaks softly and walks around people who are bowing.

It helps to ask before you take photos, especially of people or altars. Do not touch statues or offerings. If you are not sure what to do, watch quietly or ask a helper at the temple.

At a parade or festival, you can clap, cheer and enjoy the food. Learning a greeting, like "Happy New Year," shows care. Visitors do not have to join any ceremony. Watching with is enough.
