Colonies to a New Nation

U.S. History

In 1740, a girl on a Pennsylvania farm and a boy on a South Carolina rice plantation woke up under the same king. One would grow up free. One would not. A century later, a family floated everything they owned down the Ohio River toward Illinois, past a slave state on one bank and a free state on the other. This unit is the story of how those two worlds became one country, and how the argument between them grew as the country grew.

You will see how land shaped the thirteen colonies, who worked and who was free, and how colonists got used to running their own towns. You will follow the fight with Britain from a tax on paper to a war, and watch fifty-five sweating men in a shut-up room build a new kind of government. Then you will ride the steamboats and rails west, watch Illinois become a state, and learn what that growth cost the people who were already here.

Along the way you will read the actual words of the Declaration, the Constitution and the law that made Illinois free. You will look at numbers from ship records and the census. And you will keep asking the unit's big question: the founders promised liberty for all. Who got it, when, and what did it take?

The big question

The founders promised liberty and equality. Who got it, who was left out, and how did the promise get stretched?

Thirteen Colonies

Colonial Life
Big questionHow did land, work and law shape life in the thirteen colonies, and who was free?
The story

Two Mornings in 1740

Same sunrise, same king, same year. Two children wake up to very different days.

The sun comes up over a farm near Lancaster, Pennsylvania. Anna, age nine, hears the rooster before she opens her eyes. Her father is already outside with the cows. Her mother is stirring a pot of cornmeal mush. Anna pulls on her wool dress and goes to gather eggs. The barn smells like hay and cold air.

After breakfast Anna's older brother walks to the mill with a sack of wheat. Anna churns butter until her arms ache. Later her mother will teach her to read from the family Bible. Nobody in the house is rich. But the land is theirs, and the food on the table is food they grew.

Five hundred miles south, the sun comes up over a rice plantation near Charleston, South Carolina. Cato, age nine, wakes on a dirt floor in a small wooden cabin. His mother is already gone to the fields. The rice fields are wet and full of mosquitoes. Cato's job is to scare birds from the young plants.

Cato was born on this plantation. His grandmother was born in Africa. She was taken across the ocean on a ship, chained below the deck. Cato and his family cannot leave. They are not paid. A man who lives in the big house says he owns them. No law in South Carolina says he is wrong.

Anna and Cato will never meet. But they live in the same British colonies, under the same king, in the same year. Their mornings show a hard truth. In the colonies, where you were born, and to whom, decided almost everything about your life.

Talk about itAnna and Cato both work hard. What is the biggest difference between their mornings? Why does it matter?
Section 1

Three Kinds of Colonies

13.1

New England: Rocky Soil, Busy Harbors

Main ideaNew England's thin soil and good harbors pushed people toward fishing, shipbuilding and trade.

A is a place ruled by a faraway country. By the 1730s, Britain had thirteen colonies along the Atlantic coast. The four northern ones were called New England: Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Connecticut and Rhode Island. Winters were long and cold. The soil was thin and full of rocks.

Farmers in New England could feed their families, but not much more. So many people turned to the sea. Cod fish were thick off the coast. Tall forests gave wood for ships. Boston became a busy , a safe place where ships load and unload. Ships from Boston carried fish, lumber and rum across the ocean.

We know this from ship records, tax lists and letters. A merchant’s account book from Boston lists barrels of fish sent to Spain and the West Indies. Geography did not force people to fish. But it made fishing and trade the smart choice. That is how land shapes life.

13.2

The Middle Colonies: Breadbasket

Main ideaRich soil and wide rivers made the Middle Colonies grow grain and welcome many kinds of people.

South of New England lay the Middle Colonies: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware. Here the soil was deep and dark. Winters were shorter. Farmers grew so much wheat, corn and rye that people called this region the of the colonies. Anna’s family in the story lived here.

Two big rivers, the Hudson and the Delaware, worked like highways. Farmers floated grain to New York City and Philadelphia. Mills along the rivers ground wheat into flour. By 1750, Philadelphia was one of the largest cities in the colonies. Its streets were laid out in a neat grid.

The Middle Colonies were also the most mixed. Pennsylvania was started in 1681 by William Penn, a . Quakers believed every person had a light from God inside. Penn promised freedom of worship. Germans, Dutch, Scots-Irish, Swedes and English all came. On one street in Philadelphia, you might hear four languages.

13.3

The Southern Colonies: Cash Crops

Main ideaWarm weather and rich lowlands let the Southern Colonies grow crops to sell, using enslaved labor.

The Southern Colonies were Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia. Summers were long and hot. Rivers ran slow through flat, wet land. This was perfect for , crops grown to sell rather than eat. Virginia and Maryland grew tobacco. South Carolina grew rice and indigo, a plant used for blue dye.

A large farm that grew cash crops was called a . Tobacco and rice took huge amounts of hand labor. Planters wanted workers who could not quit. So they bought enslaved Africans. By 1740, more than half the people in South Carolina were enslaved. Cato in the story was one of them.

There were few big cities in the South. Charleston was the main one. Most people lived spread out on farms. Rich planters lived in brick houses and sent their sons to England for school. Small farmers lived in one-room cabins. We know the numbers from colonial tax lists, which counted enslaved people as property.

Section 2

Everyday Life

13.4

Farms, Towns, Ships and Shops

Main ideaMost colonists farmed, but towns held the shops, mills and docks that connected farms to the world.

About nine out of ten colonists lived on farms. A farm family made most of what it used. They grew food, spun wool, made soap and candles. But no farm could make everything. For nails, salt, cloth and tools, families went to town.

A colonial town had a blacksmith who shaped iron, a miller who ground grain and a cooper who made barrels. A bought and sold goods from far away. In port towns, ships brought tea, sugar and cloth. They left with fish, flour, tobacco and lumber.

Trade connected everyone. A Pennsylvania farmer’s wheat became bread in the West Indies. Sugar from there became rum in Rhode Island. Enslaved people were bought and sold in this same trade. A Boston merchant’s ledger from the 1700s shows all of it on one page: barrels of rum, bolts of cloth and human beings.

13.5

A Colonial Child's Day

Main ideaColonial children worked from a young age, and their jobs depended on where they lived and who they were.

There was no such thing as a lazy morning. Colonial children worked. On a farm, a six-year-old fed chickens and carried water. By ten, boys plowed and girls spun thread. Anna churned butter. A girl might sew a , a cloth stitched with letters to practice sewing and reading.

In towns, a boy of twelve might become an . He lived with a craftsman for several years and learned the trade. Benjamin Franklin was an apprentice in his brother’s print shop in Boston in the 1720s. Girls learned cooking, spinning and child care at home.

Enslaved children worked too, but for no wage and no future choice. Cato scared birds from rice. Older children carried water to field workers. They could be sold away from their parents at any time. Free and enslaved children might live a mile apart and have completely different lives.

13.6

Church and Schoolhouse

Main ideaReligion was central to colonial life, and in New England it led to the first public schools.

In most colonies, the church was the center of town life. In New England, the Puritans built a in the middle of each town. Families sat through long sermons on hard benches. In Pennsylvania, Quakers met in plain rooms with no minister. In the South, Anglican churches followed the Church of England.

Puritans believed everyone should read the Bible. So in 1647, Massachusetts passed a law: any town with fifty families had to hire a teacher. Towns with one hundred families needed a grammar school. This was the first law in the colonies requiring schools.

Children learned from a , a wooden paddle with the alphabet under a thin sheet of horn. Later they used the New England Primer, a small book of rhymes and prayers. Outside New England, schooling was spotty. Rich children had tutors. Poor children often had none. Teaching enslaved people to read was against the law in some colonies.

Section 3

Who Was Free

13.7

Indentured Servants

Main ideaIndentured servants traded years of unpaid work for the trip to America, and then went free.

The trip from Europe cost more than most poor people had. So many signed an , a contract. A ship captain or planter paid for the trip. In return, the person worked for four to seven years without pay. This person was an . Perhaps half of all Europeans who came to the colonies before 1776 came this way.

Life as a servant was hard. Masters could whip servants and add years for running away. Many died of fever before their time was up. But the contract had an end. When the years were done, the servant was free. Some got land, tools or a new suit of clothes.

Here is the key difference. An indentured servant’s contract ended. Slavery did not. By 1700, colonial laws said enslaved people were property for life, and their children were too. Both groups worked without pay. Only one group had a way out.

13.8

The Middle Passage

Main ideaMillions of Africans were forced across the Atlantic in chains, and hundreds of thousands were brought to the colonies.

Cato’s grandmother was taken from West Africa. Traders marched captured people to the coast and sold them to ship captains. The ocean voyage was called the . It was the middle leg of a trade route between Europe, Africa and the Americas.

Below deck, people were chained side by side with barely room to turn. The trip took about two months. The air was foul. Disease spread fast. Roughly one in six or seven people died before reaching land. Ship records kept by the traders themselves tell us these numbers.

Over about three hundred years, roughly twelve million Africans were forced onto ships. Most went to Brazil and the Caribbean. About four hundred thousand were brought to what became the United States. Olaudah Equiano, who was taken as a boy in the 1750s, later wrote a book about it. His words are one of the few accounts we have from someone who survived the voyage.

From the source
A Survivor Remembers
Olaudah Equiano wrote that when he was carried aboard the ship, he was sure he was going to be killed. Below deck, he said, the heat and the crowding were almost unbearable, the air was hard to breathe, and the cries of the sick and dying made the whole scene one of horror.
Olaudah Equiano, from his life story, published 1789 (paraphrased) (paraphrased)

Section 4

Governing Themselves

13.9

Town Meetings and Assemblies

Main ideaColonists began governing themselves through town meetings and elected assemblies.

In New England, the men of a town gathered in the meetinghouse for a . They voted on roads, schools, fences and taxes. Anyone could speak. Decisions were made by majority vote. Town meetings still happen in some New England towns today.

Each colony also had an , a group of elected men who made laws for the whole colony. Virginia’s assembly, the House of Burgesses, first met in 1619. It was the first elected lawmaking body in the colonies. Assemblies decided how tax money was spent. That gave them real power.

Who could vote? Mostly white men who owned land. Women could not vote. Enslaved people and most free Black people could not. Native people could not. Still, more colonists could vote than people in most of Europe. This habit of self-rule would matter a great deal when Britain started to push.

13.10

A Faraway King

Main ideaBritain ruled the colonies but mostly left them alone, so colonists got used to running things themselves.

London was three thousand miles away. A letter took six weeks or more to cross the ocean. The king sent a to most colonies, but the governor could not do much without the assembly, which controlled the money. Many governors just tried to get along.

Britain cared most about trade. Laws said colonists should ship goods on British ships and sell certain crops only to Britain. But there were few officials to enforce this. Smugglers sneaked in sugar and tea from other countries. Britain mostly looked the other way. Historians call this rule.

For about a hundred years, this worked. Colonists thought of themselves as loyal British subjects with British rights. They also thought of themselves as people who made their own laws. When Britain later tried to tighten its grip, colonists were shocked. They had grown up free of it.

Revolution and Constitution

Founding
Big questionWhy did the colonies break from Britain, and what kind of government did they build instead?
The story

Windows Shut in the Heat

Philadelphia, summer 1787. Inside a locked room, fifty-five men argue about the future.

It is late May 1787 in Philadelphia. The air is thick and hot. Flies buzz over the cobblestones. Inside the State House, the same building where the Declaration of Independence was signed, men in wool coats file into a room upstairs. Then they shut the windows. Every one of them.

The windows stay shut all summer. The delegates have agreed that nothing said in this room will leave it. No reporters. No crowds. Guards stand at the door. The men sweat through their shirts. Some are from Georgia, some from New Hampshire. They have come to fix a government that is falling apart.

George Washington sits at the front in a tall chair. He rarely speaks, but everyone watches him. Benjamin Franklin, eighty-one years old, is carried in by prisoners from the local jail because his legs hurt too much to walk. James Madison of Virginia sits near the front and writes down every word.

They argue for nearly four months. Big states want more votes. Small states want equal votes. Northern delegates and Southern delegates fight over slavery. Some men walk out. Twice the whole thing nearly collapses. Franklin suggests they pray. Instead, they keep talking.

On September 17, they sign a paper only four pages long. It begins with three words: We the People. It is not perfect. It leaves millions of people out. But it will become the oldest written national constitution still in use in the world. And it started behind shut windows in the heat.

Section 1

Trouble With Britain

14.1

Taxes Without a Vote

Main ideaAfter 1763, Britain began taxing the colonies directly, and colonists protested that they had no vote in Parliament.

For a hundred years, Britain mostly left the colonies alone. Then, in 1763, Britain won a long and costly war with France. It had huge debts. It also had thousands of soldiers stationed in America. Parliament, Britain’s lawmaking body, decided the colonists should help pay.

In 1765, Parliament passed the . Colonists had to buy a special stamp for newspapers, legal papers, even playing cards. This was a , money paid to the government. Colonists were furious. Not because the tax was big. Because they had never voted for it. No colonist sat in Parliament.

Their slogan became no taxation without representation. Colonial assemblies had always decided their own taxes. Now a body across the ocean was doing it. Colonists refused to buy British goods. Crowds threatened stamp sellers. Within a year, Parliament backed down and repealed the Stamp Act. But it did not give up the right to tax.

14.2

Blood on King Street

Main ideaBritish soldiers in Boston fired on a crowd in 1770, and both sides used the event to argue their case.

Parliament passed new taxes in 1767 on glass, paint, paper and tea. Boston protested loudest. So Britain sent soldiers to Boston to keep order. Townspeople hated it. Soldiers took part-time jobs and pushed down wages. Fights broke out in the streets.

On the cold night of March 5, 1770, a crowd gathered around a soldier on King Street. People threw snowballs, ice and insults. More soldiers came. Someone yelled fire. The soldiers shot into the crowd. Five colonists died. One was Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native descent. Colonists called it the .

Was it a massacre or self-defense? A silversmith named Paul Revere made a picture showing soldiers firing in a neat line at peaceful people. It was , made to stir anger. At the soldiers’ trial, witnesses told a messier story. The lawyer who defended the soldiers was John Adams, a patriot. Most were found not guilty. Both sides used the same night as evidence.

14.3

Tea in the Harbor

Main ideaThe Boston Tea Party and Britain's harsh punishment pushed the colonies together toward war.

By 1773, only one tax was left: the tax on tea. Parliament kept it to prove it still had the right. Then it let one British company sell tea cheaply in the colonies. Colonists saw a trick. Cheap tea would get them to pay the tax without noticing.

On the night of December 16, 1773, about one hundred men, some dressed as Native Americans, climbed onto three ships in Boston Harbor. They broke open 342 chests of tea and dumped them into the water. It took about three hours. No one was hurt. People called it the .

Britain was furious. In 1774, Parliament closed Boston Harbor until the tea was paid for and took away much of Massachusetts’s self-government. Colonists called these laws the Intolerable Acts. Instead of scaring the colonies, the punishment united them. Delegates from twelve colonies met in Philadelphia that fall. In April 1775, shots were fired at Lexington and Concord. The war had begun.

Declaring Independence

14.4

The Big Idea

Main ideaThe Declaration of Independence said that all people have rights and that governments get their power from the people.

In the spring of 1776, a cheap pamphlet called Common Sense sold by the tens of thousands. Its author was Thomas Paine. He argued in plain words that a small island should not rule a continent. Many colonists who had wanted to stay British began to change their minds.

In June, Congress asked a Virginian named Thomas Jefferson to write a statement. He was thirty-three. He wrote that all men are created equal. He wrote that every person has that cannot be taken away. Among them are life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. He wrote that governments get their power from the consent of the governed.

That last idea was the big one. Kings had always claimed their power came from God. Jefferson said it came from the people, and that people could change a government that hurt them. On July 4, 1776, Congress approved the . It was a breakup letter to the king. It was also a promise that Americans would spend the next two centuries trying to keep.

Main ideaThe war was won by ordinary people who suffered through hunger, cold and defeat under George Washington's leadership.

The was not much to look at. Farmers, apprentices, fishermen and free Black men signed up. Some were fourteen. Many had no shoes. They faced the strongest army in the world. Congress chose George Washington of Virginia to lead them. He lost more battles than he won, but he never quit.

The worst time came in the winter of 1777 to 1778 at , Pennsylvania. About twelve thousand soldiers camped in log huts. Food ran out. Men wrapped rags around bare feet. Roughly two thousand died of disease and cold. Washington wrote letter after letter begging Congress for supplies.

Main ideaFrance joined the war after the American victory at Saratoga, and French help made the final victory at Yorktown possible.

14.7

A Loose Team of Thirteen

Main ideaThe Articles of Confederation made a central government too weak to tax, keep order or settle disputes.

After breaking from a king, Americans feared strong government. So their first plan, the , made the states almost separate countries. Congress could declare war and make treaties. But it could not collect taxes. It could not raise an army on its own. There was no president and no national court.

Problems piled up. Congress could not pay soldiers who had fought the war. States printed their own money and taxed each other’s goods. Nine of thirteen states had to agree to pass any law. All thirteen had to agree to change the Articles. Almost nothing got done.

In 1786, farmers in Massachusetts, many of them war veterans, could not pay their debts. Led by Daniel Shays, they shut down courts with guns. The national government had no army to send. Shays’s Rebellion scared leaders across the country. Washington wrote that the union was falling apart. In 1787, states sent delegates to Philadelphia to fix it.

14.8

Three Branches, Checked and Balanced

Main ideaThe Constitution split power into three branches, each able to check the others, so no one could rule alone.

The delegates in Philadelphia threw out the Articles and wrote a new , a written plan for government. The big fight was over votes. Big states wanted votes by population. Small states wanted equal votes. The answer was a deal: a Congress with two houses. In the House of Representatives, big states get more seats. In the Senate, every state gets two.

The plan splits power into three . Congress, the legislative branch, makes laws. The president, the executive branch, carries them out. The courts, the judicial branch, decide what the laws mean. Each branch can stop the others. The president can veto a law. Congress can override the veto. Courts can strike down laws. This is called .

The idea came from experience. A king with all the power had been the problem. So the delegates made sure power was divided. They also made the Constitution changeable. It can be amended, but only with wide agreement. That has happened only twenty-seven times in over two hundred years.

14.9

The Bill of Rights

Main ideaMany Americans would not accept the Constitution without a list of rights, so ten amendments were added in 1791.

Writing the Constitution was only half the job. Nine states had to approve it. Many people said no. Where, they asked, was the list of rights? What stopped this new government from jailing people for speaking out, as the king had? Supporters promised to add a list. Only then did enough states say yes.

In 1791, ten , or changes, were added. Together they are the . The First Amendment protects freedom of religion, speech, the press and the right to gather and protest. Others protect against unfair searches, promise a fair trial and forbid cruel punishment.

These rights came from real memories. Soldiers had been housed in colonists’ homes, so the Third Amendment forbids it. Officials had searched homes without cause, so the Fourth Amendment requires a good reason. The Bill of Rights is short. But every day, in courts across the country, people still argue over what its words mean.

Section 4

The Promise and the Gap

14.10

Who Was Left Out

Main ideaThe Constitution protected slavery and did not give rights to women, Native people or most Black Americans.

The Declaration said all men are created equal. The Constitution said We the People. But who counted? In 1787, about one in five people in the new nation was enslaved. Women could not vote. Native nations were treated as outsiders. Most states let only white men with property vote.

The delegates in Philadelphia argued about slavery and then made deals. Enslaved people would count as three-fifths of a person when deciding how many seats a state got in Congress. That gave slaveholding states more power. The slave trade from Africa could continue until 1808. Runaways had to be returned. The word slavery never appears, but the deals are there.

Some delegates were troubled. Others owned enslaved people themselves. Washington and Jefferson both did. Free Black Americans noticed the gap right away. In the 1790s, they wrote petitions to Congress asking why liberty did not include them. Abigail Adams told her husband John to remember the ladies. The promise was written down. It was not yet kept.

14.11

Stretching the Promise

Main ideaOver two centuries, amendments and movements extended the Constitution's rights to people first left out.

The delegates built a door into the Constitution: it could be amended. That door has been used to widen who counts. After the Civil War, the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery in 1865. The Fourteenth, in 1868, made everyone born here a citizen. The Fifteenth, in 1870, said the vote could not be denied because of race.

Women won the vote with the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920, after decades of marches and arrests. Native Americans were made citizens by law in 1924. In 1971, the Twenty-sixth Amendment lowered the voting age to eighteen. Each change came because people pushed, often for a lifetime.

Words on paper did not do this alone. Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery, said the Constitution’s promises belonged to Black Americans too. Illinois sent Abraham Lincoln to the White House. Chicago women marched for the vote. The gap between the promise and the practice is where much of American history happens. Every has argued about it.

The story

Everything We Own, Floating

A family, a cow, a plow and a rooster on a raft, drifting down the Ohio River toward Illinois in 1830.

The flatboat is forty feet long and looks like a barn on a raft. Sarah, eleven, sits on the roof of the little cabin and watches Pennsylvania slide by. Below her are two beds, a table, an iron kettle, a plow, a spinning wheel, seed corn, a cow named Bess and a very angry rooster. Everything her family owns is on this boat.

Her father bought the flatboat for thirty dollars at Pittsburgh. He steers with a long oar at the back. Her older brother pushes off from sandbars with a pole. Her mother cooks over a box of sand with a fire in it. They travel only by day. At night they tie up to a tree. The river is wide and brown and never stops moving.

They pass Cincinnati, a real city with brick buildings and steamboats puffing black smoke. One steamboat churns past them going upriver, against the current. Sarah's father shakes his head. Ten years ago, he says, nobody could go up this river. Now they go up faster than we go down.

They pass Kentucky on the left, where Sarah sees enslaved people working in a field by the water. They pass Indiana on the right, a free state. At Shawneetown, in Illinois, they will sell the boat for its lumber and buy a wagon. Sarah's father has a paper from the land office: eighty acres of prairie, bought for one hundred dollars.

Sarah does not know that the prairie was Potawatomi and Kickapoo land a few years before. She does not know that the rooster will die in the first frost, or that her family will plant corn in soil so rich it will make them, in twenty years, comfortable. Right now she knows the river, the roof and the sky. The whole country is moving, and she is on top of it.

15.6

Steam on the Rivers

Main ideaSteamboats let people and goods travel upstream, turning rivers into two-way highways.

Before 1810, going down a river was easy and going up was nearly impossible. Flatboats like Sarah’s drifted down and were sold for lumber. Then the crew walked home, sometimes a thousand miles. That is why Sarah’s father was so amazed by the steamboat.

In 1807, Robert Fulton’s chugged up the Hudson River from New York to Albany. A steam engine turned a big paddle wheel. Within twenty years, hundreds of steamboats worked the Ohio and Mississippi. A trip from New Orleans to Louisville that once took months now took about a week.

River towns boomed. Cincinnati, Louisville, St. Louis and Alton, Illinois, grew fast. Farmers could send corn and pork south and get sugar, coffee and cloth back. Steamboats were also dangerous. Boilers exploded. Snags ripped hulls. But the river had become a road that ran both ways.

15.8

The Trail of Tears

Main ideaThe Indian Removal Act forced Native nations off their homelands, and thousands of Cherokee died on the march west.

Every acre Sarah’s family bought had belonged to someone. As settlers pushed west, the government pressured Native nations to sign treaties giving up land. In Illinois, the Sauk leader Black Hawk tried to return to his village in 1832. Soldiers and militia drove his people out. Within a few years, most Native people in Illinois had been forced west of the Mississippi.

In the South, the had done everything settlers said they should. They farmed, built schools, wrote a constitution and printed a newspaper in their own alphabet. It did not matter. Georgia wanted their land. In 1830, Congress passed the . President Andrew Jackson signed it.

The Cherokee fought in court and won. The Supreme Court said Georgia had no right to their land. Jackson ignored the ruling. In 1838, soldiers rounded up about sixteen thousand Cherokee and marched them about eight hundred miles to what is now Oklahoma. Roughly four thousand died of cold, hunger and disease. The Cherokee call it the trail where they cried. We call it the .

15.9

Machines Change Work

Main ideaNew machines like the cotton gin, the reaper and the steel plow made work faster and changed who did it.

In 1793, a young teacher named Eli Whitney built a machine called the . It pulled the sticky seeds out of cotton. By hand, one person cleaned about a pound a day. With a gin, one person could clean fifty pounds. Cotton became the South’s great cash crop.

Here is the cruel twist. The machine made cotton cheap to clean, so planters wanted more cotton, so they wanted more enslaved people to plant and pick it. The number of enslaved people in the United States roughly quadrupled between 1790 and 1860. A machine meant to save work made slavery grow.

On the prairie, the problems were different. Prairie sod was so thick and sticky that iron plows clogged. In 1837, a blacksmith named John Deere in Grand Detour, Illinois, made a plow of polished steel that cut clean. In 1831, Cyrus McCormick built a that cut grain many times faster than a man with a blade. In 1847 he moved his factory to Chicago. Illinois soil plus Illinois machines fed the country.

15.10

A Bigger Country, a Bigger Argument

Main ideaEvery new state raised the question of slavery, and the Missouri Compromise only delayed the fight.

By 1819 there were twenty-two states: eleven that allowed slavery and eleven that did not. Each side had the same number of senators. Then Missouri, carved from the Louisiana Purchase, asked to join as a slave state. That would tip the balance. Congress argued for months.

The answer was the of 1820. Missouri came in as a slave state. Maine came in as a free state. That kept the balance. And a line was drawn across the rest of the Louisiana Purchase. North of it, no slavery. South of it, slavery allowed. Illinois sat north of that line.

Thomas Jefferson, old and worried, wrote that the news scared him like a fire bell in the night. He saw what was coming. Every new state and every new railroad west would raise the same question. In 1830 Sarah’s flatboat passed between a slave state and a free state. Thirty years later, that river would be a battle line. An Illinois lawyer named Abraham Lincoln would be president.

Think it through
The Line on the Map

Picture the map of 1820. A line runs west from Missouri's southern border across the empty plains. Above the line, states will be free. Below it, states may allow slavery. Missouri itself pokes above the line as a slave state, the one exception. Every year, settlers pour in on both sides.

Why would a line on a map calm the argument for a while? Why could it not settle the argument for good?
