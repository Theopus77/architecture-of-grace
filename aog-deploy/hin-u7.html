Beliefs and Practice

Hindu texts are full of stories. They are also full of big ideas about how to live. In this unit you will learn four of those ideas: dharma, karma, samsara and moksha. You will see how Hindu families connect them to stories you already know.

Then you will follow a year of Hindu festivals, from Pongal in January to Diwali in the fall. You will learn what happens in puja at home and in a temple. You will also visit, in words, the Hindu Temple of Greater Chicago in Lemont, Illinois.

By the end, you will be able to explain each key idea in your own words. You will name the stories festivals remember. You will also know how to be a respectful guest at a place of worship.

The story

Every Action Is a Seed

A girl plants a seed, and her grandfather tells her what his teachers told him.

Meera kneels in her grandfather's backyard in Naperville. It is May, and the soil is cool. She pushes a bean seed into the dirt with one finger. Then she pats the soil flat and pours water from a small green can.

Her grandfather, Dada, watches from a lawn chair. "Do you know what my teachers said in India?" he asks. "They said every action is a seed too. A kind action grows into something. So does an unkind one." Meera looks at the wet soil. "Even little things?" she asks.

Dada nods. "That idea is called karma," he says. "Many Hindus believe our actions have results, now or later. Some believe the results can reach into another life." He tells her the Gita says to do your work well without worrying about the prize.

Meera thinks about the bean. She cannot see it now, but she knows it is there. She decides to water it every morning. "Then I will plant good seeds," she says. Dada smiles. "That is how my mother taught it to me," he says. "With a garden."

Section 1

Duty and Action

13.1

Dharma: The Right Way to Live

Main ideaHindus use the word dharma for duty and the right way to live, and it can be different for each person.

In the Ramayana, Prince Rama is told he must leave home for fourteen years. He could argue. Instead, he goes, because he wants to keep his father’s promise. Many Hindus see Rama as a model of .

Dharma is a Sanskrit word. It means duty, what is right, and the order that holds life together. Hindus believe dharma is not the same for everyone. A student, a parent and a ruler each have their own duties.

Hindu texts give many examples of dharma. Some are clear, like telling the truth and not hurting others. Some are hard. In the Mahabharata, good people argue about what dharma asks. The texts show that finding the right thing can take careful thought.

13.2

Karma: Actions Have Results

Main ideaKarma means action, and Hindus believe every action has results.

Think of Meera’s bean seed. She plants it now, and it grows later. Many Hindus explain the same way. The word karma means action. Hindus believe good actions bring good results and harmful actions bring harmful ones.

Hindus believe the results of karma can come soon or much later. Many believe they can even come in a future life. This is why many Hindu families teach children to be kind, honest and careful in small things as well as big ones.

The Bhagavad Gita adds another idea. In it, Krishna tells Arjuna to do his duty but not to be greedy for the reward. Many readers take this to mean: do good work because it is right, not only for a prize. Scholars call this idea acting without attachment.

13.3

Four Aims of Life

Main ideaHindu texts name four aims of a good life: dharma, artha, kama and moksha.

Hindu texts say a full life has four aims. They are called the purusharthas. The first is dharma, doing what is right. The second is , earning a living and caring for a home. The third is , enjoying good things like music, food and love.

The fourth aim is moksha. Many Hindus believe it is freedom from the cycle of rebirth. The texts say the first three aims are good, but they should follow dharma. You should earn money honestly, for example, and enjoy life without hurting others.

The four aims show that Hindu texts do not ask everyone to give up the world. Many people work, raise families and celebrate festivals. The texts describe all of these as part of a good life when they are guided by dharma.

Rebirth and Freedom

13.4

Samsara: The Wheel of Rebirth

Main ideaMany Hindus believe in samsara, a cycle of birth, death and rebirth.

In the Gita, Krishna gives Arjuna a picture. A person takes off worn-out clothes and puts on new ones. Krishna says the self does the same with bodies. Many Hindus read this verse as a teaching about rebirth.

The belief in rebirth is called . Samsara means wandering or flowing on. Many Hindus believe the self is born, dies and is born again, many times. They believe karma helps shape each new life.

Samsara is often pictured as a wheel that keeps turning. Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs also speak of rebirth, in their own ways. Hindu schools explain samsara differently, but many share this picture of a long journey through many lives.

13.5

Moksha: Freedom

Main ideaMany Hindus believe the highest goal is moksha, freedom from the cycle of rebirth.

Picture a bird in a cage. The door opens, and the bird flies free. Some Hindu teachers use pictures like this to describe . Moksha means freedom or release.

Many Hindus believe moksha is freedom from samsara, the cycle of rebirth. They describe it as deep peace. Different Hindu schools describe it in different ways. Some say the self finds it is one with ultimate reality. Others say the soul lives forever in loving closeness to God.

Hindu texts give several ways to seek moksha. Some teach knowledge, some teach selfless action, and some teach loving devotion. You will learn about these three paths later in this chapter.

13.6

Karma in a Story You Know

Main ideaHindu stories often show how actions lead to results, which helps readers remember karma.

Remember the Ramayana. When Rama is in the forest, a bird named Jatayu tries to save Sita from Ravana. Jatayu is badly hurt. Rama later honors the brave bird with care and funeral rites. Many readers see Jatayu’s brave act as good karma.

Ravana’s story shows the other side. He takes Sita away by force. In the end he loses his kingdom and his life. Many Hindu tellers use Ravana to show that harmful actions bring harmful results.

Stories like these helped families teach karma long before most children could read. A grandmother telling the Ramayana could point to each character and ask, what did this action lead to? Many families still teach this way today.

One Reality, Many Paths

13.7

Brahman: One Reality, Many Forms

Main ideaMany Hindus speak of one ultimate reality, Brahman, honored in many forms such as Vishnu, Shiva and Devi.

Walk into a Hindu temple and you may see many murtis, or sacred images. There may be Vishnu, Shiva, Devi the Goddess, Ganesha and Hanuman. A visitor may wonder how one religion honors so many.

Many Hindus explain it this way. They speak of one ultimate reality called . They believe Brahman can be known through many forms. A very old verse of the Rig Veda says the wise call the one by many names (Rig Veda 1.164.46).

Hindus show this idea in different ways. Vaishnavas honor Vishnu as the Supreme. Shaivas honor Shiva. Shaktas honor Devi, the Goddess. Many families honor several forms at one home shrine. These traditions are all part of Hinduism.

Main ideaDharma, karma, samsara and moksha fit together as one picture of life in many Hindu texts.

Think back to Meera’s garden. Dharma is like knowing how to care for the plant. Karma is like the seeds she plants each day. Samsara is like the seasons that keep coming back.

Many Hindus say these ideas are not just for thinking. They shape daily choices, like being honest, helping at home and praying at a home shrine. Learning the words helps you understand what Hindu classmates and neighbors may mean.

The story

Shoes at the Door

A fourth-grade class visits a Hindu temple in Lemont, Illinois.

The school bus climbs a hill in Lemont, southwest of Chicago. At the top stands a white temple with carved towers. It is the Hindu Temple of Greater Chicago. The class steps off the bus, and the air smells like flowers and warm oil.

A volunteer named Mrs. Rao meets them at the door. "Before we go in, we take off our shoes," she says kindly. "It shows respect, like wiping your feet at a friend's house." The students line their sneakers in neat rows. Some socks have holes, and everyone laughs quietly.

Inside, the floor is cool. Mrs. Rao leads them to a shrine where murtis of Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman stand dressed in bright cloth. A priest waves a small lamp in circles while a bell rings. Families stand with their hands pressed together.

"This is called arati," Mrs. Rao whispers. "People are showing love and respect to God." On the way out, she gives each student a small sweet in a paper cup. "This is prasad," she says. "It was offered first, and now it is shared."

A Calendar of Moon and Sun

14.1

Why the Dates Move

Main ideaMost Hindu festivals follow a calendar based on both the moon and the sun, so their dates change each year.

Look at a wall calendar. Diwali might fall in October one year and November the next. Why does it move? Most Hindu festivals follow a calendar, which counts months by the moon.

A moon month runs from one new moon to the next. Twelve moon months are a little shorter than a sun year. So Hindu calendars add an extra month every few years to keep in step with the seasons. Calendars like this are called lunisolar.

India has several regional calendars, so some dates differ from place to place. A few festivals, like Pongal, follow the sun and fall on nearly the same date each year. Temples in Illinois post each year’s festival dates for families.

Pongal gives thanks to Surya, the sun, for the harvest. The name of the festival is also the name of the sweet rice dish. The overflowing pot is a sign of plenty. Families also honor the cows and bulls that help on farms.

Tamil families in Illinois celebrate Pongal too, often in their kitchens and at temples. Other parts of India hold harvest festivals at the same time with different names, such as Makar Sankranti.

Puja at Home

Main ideaPuja is Hindu worship, and many families do it every day at a home shrine.

Early in the morning, a grandmother stands at a small shrine in her kitchen. She lights a lamp, rings a little bell and offers a flower. This is , Hindu worship.

A home shrine may hold murtis or pictures of Ganesha, Krishna, Shiva, Lakshmi or others. Families offer water, flowers, fruit, incense and light. Many say prayers or sing. Each family does puja in its own way.

Inside a Temple

Main ideaIn a Hindu temple, priests care for the murtis and lead worship such as arati.

A Hindu temple is called a mandir in Hindi. Inside are shrines, each with a murti. Hindus believe the deity is present in the murti in a special way, so it is bathed, dressed and offered food with great care.

Priests lead worship several times a day. In , a priest waves a lamp in circles before the murti while people sing and a bell rings. Afterward, many people pass their hands over the flame and touch their eyes.

Seeing the murti is itself important. Hindus call this , which means seeing and being seen by the deity. Many visitors come just for darshan, then share prasad and go home.

14.10

The Temple in Lemont

Main ideaThe Hindu Temple of Greater Chicago in Lemont, Illinois, has a Rama temple and serves many families.

The Hindu Temple of Greater Chicago stands on a hill in Lemont, Illinois. Its main shrine honors Rama, with Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman. Another building has shrines to other deities, including Ganesha, Shiva and Durga.

Families come from all over the Chicago area. They come for weekly worship, weddings, festivals like Diwali and Navaratri, and classes. The temple helps Hindu Americans keep their traditions and teach them to children.

Visitors are often welcome. Guests take off their shoes, dress modestly, speak quietly and do not touch the murtis. It is polite to ask before taking photos. These simple steps show respect for a place that is holy to others.
