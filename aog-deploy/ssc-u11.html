Classical Worlds

World History

Between about 500 BCE and 500 CE, people on three sides of the world built states larger and more lasting than anything before. Greek city-states invented citizen government and the habit of asking why. Rome turned a hill town into an empire of sixty million people, held together by roads, law and Latin. India's emperors and China's dynasties governed tens of millions with written edicts, examinations and a shared script. Historians call these the classical worlds because later ages kept returning to them for models.

This unit follows a potter climbing to the Athenian Assembly, a baker in Pompeii on the morning Vesuvius woke, and a caravan leaving Chang'an with silk. It asks how each society decided who could rule and who was left out, how it fed and defended itself, what it believed, and what happened when it fell. It also follows the threads that tied them together: Alexander's road to India, Greek styles on Buddhist statues, Indian numbers on Roman shores, and plague on the Silk Roads.

By the end you will be able to explain where democracy, republics, juries, paper, zero, the Senate and the civil service came from, and to argue, with evidence, which classical legacy matters most in your own life in Illinois.

The big question

What did the classical worlds of Greece, Rome, India and China build that we still live with, and what did those achievements cost?

The story

The Rope and the Hill

On a morning in Athens around 430 BCE, a potter drops his work and hurries uphill, because today the city will decide about war.

The sun is barely over the hills when Demos, a potter, wipes the clay from his hands and leaves his shop. He is not in trouble and he is not late for work. He is going to vote. Today the Assembly of Athens meets on the Pnyx, a bare rock hill west of the Acropolis. Any male citizen over eighteen may come, speak and raise his hand. Demos has never been rich, but here his hand counts the same as a nobleman's.

In the marketplace, city slaves stretch a long rope dipped in red paint and sweep it across the crowd. Any man caught with red on his cloak was dawdling instead of going to the Assembly, and he can be fined. Demos hurries ahead of the rope. He climbs the stone steps with farmers, sailors, shopkeepers and a few wealthy men in fine wool. Perhaps six thousand people crowd onto the slope, facing a carved stone platform where speakers stand.

A herald calls out, "Who wishes to speak?" One man argues that Athens should send ships to help an ally. Another warns that the treasury is low and the enemy is strong. Demos listens and thinks about his brother, who rows in the fleet. No one asked Demos to be an expert. The Athenians believed that ordinary people, hearing both sides, could judge well enough.

Not everyone is on the hill. Demos's wife, Melitta, is at home; women cannot vote. His neighbor Nikias, a skilled metalworker born in another city, pays taxes but has no vote either. The enslaved man who grinds Demos's clay has no say at all. Athenian democracy was real, and it was also narrow. Out of perhaps three hundred thousand people in Athens and the land around it, only about thirty to forty thousand were voting citizens.

When the speeches end, the herald calls for a show of hands. Officials count roughly by eye. Demos raises his hand with the majority, and the matter is settled. He walks back down to his shop knowing that he, a potter, helped decide the policy of a great city. Two and a half thousand years later, that idea still seems remarkable.

Talk about itAthens let a potter vote on war but not his wife or his foreign-born neighbor. Was Athens a democracy? What would you need to know to decide?
Section 1

Land and City-States

23.1

Mountains, Sea and the Polis

Main ideaGreece's mountains and coast split its people into small, independent city-states that shared a language and gods.

Look at a map of Greece and you see a ragged land. Mountains cover most of it. The coast twists into bays and peninsulas, and hundreds of islands dot the Aegean Sea. Farmland lies in small valleys cut off from each other by rock. The soil is thin and dry. Wheat grows poorly, but olives and grapes do well on the hillsides, and the sea is never far away.

This geography shaped Greek life. A valley could not easily rule the next valley over the mountains, so each one became its own tiny country. The Greeks called such a community a , which we translate as city-state. A polis was a town, the farmland around it and the people who belonged to it. There were hundreds of them, most with only a few thousand people. Each had its own laws, army and coins.

Because farming was hard, Greeks became sailors and traders. They sailed to Egypt for grain and to Italy for metals. By about 600 BCE they had planted colonies, new city-states, around the Black Sea and along the coasts of Italy, France and North Africa. A colony kept the mother city’s gods and customs but ruled itself.

The city-states fought each other often, yet they knew they were all Greek. They spoke the same language, worshiped the same gods and gathered every four years at Olympia for athletic games in honor of Zeus. Wars stopped for the games. A Greek from Sparta and a Greek from Athens might be enemies, but both called outsiders , people whose speech sounded like "bar-bar" to them.

23.2

Athens and Sparta

Main ideaAthens and Sparta were both Greek, but one built a trading democracy and the other a military state.

The two most powerful city-states could hardly have been more different. sat near the sea in the region of Attica. It grew rich from trade, silver mines and the olive oil it shipped abroad. Athenians prized speaking well, building beautifully and arguing about ideas. By the fifth century BCE, its citizens governed themselves in an assembly.

lay inland in a valley of the Peloponnese, the southern peninsula. Long before, the Spartans had conquered their neighbors and turned them into , farm workers who belonged to the state. Helots outnumbered Spartans many times over. To keep them down, Sparta became an army. Two kings led it in war, and a council of elders and a small assembly ran the city.

A Spartan boy left his family at age seven to live in barracks. He learned to endure cold, hunger and beatings. Every Spartan man was a full-time soldier until age sixty, and the helots did the farming. Spartan women had more freedom than Athenian women. They ran the households, owned land and trained in sports so they would have strong children. Spartans distrusted trade, coins and new ideas.

Athenians thought Spartans were grim and closed-minded. Spartans thought Athenians were soft and talked too much. Yet the Greek world respected Sparta’s soldiers and Athens’s ships. When a great danger came from the east, the two rivals would have to fight side by side.

Democracy and War

23.3

How Athens Became a Democracy

Main ideaOver about two centuries, reformers gave ordinary Athenian citizens the power to make laws and judge cases.

Athens was not always a democracy. Around 600 BCE, nobles ran the city and poor farmers who fell into debt could be sold as slaves. Anger grew. In 594 BCE the Athenians chose a respected leader named to fix things. He canceled the debts, freed those enslaved for debt and let all citizens, not just nobles, sit in the assembly and serve on juries. Nobles still held most offices.

Later, a strongman named Peisistratus seized power as a . To the Greeks that word meant a ruler who took power by force, not always a cruel one. His sons ruled after him until they were driven out. Then, in 508 BCE, a noble named rebuilt the government. He broke up the old family power blocs and mixed citizens from different regions into ten new tribes. Each tribe sent fifty men, chosen by lottery, to a Council of Five Hundred that prepared business for the assembly.

The Athenians called their system , from demos, the people, and kratos, power. Its heart was the Assembly, where every citizen could speak and vote directly. Most officials were picked by drawing lots, on the idea that any citizen was fit to serve for a year. Only generals were elected, since war needed skill. Large juries of hundreds of citizens decided court cases with no judge to overrule them.

The Athenians even had a way to protect democracy from an ambitious man. Once a year the Assembly could vote to banish someone for ten years. Citizens scratched a name on a piece of broken pottery called an ostrakon. If enough named the same person, he had to leave. Our word ostracize comes from this. Archaeologists have dug up thousands of these pottery votes with real names on them.

23.4

Who Was Left Out

Main ideaAthenian democracy included every male citizen but shut out women, foreigners and the enslaved, who together were most of the population.

Athenian democracy was direct: citizens did not send representatives, they showed up and voted. But who counted as a citizen? By the middle of the fifth century BCE, a man had to be born to an Athenian father and an Athenian mother, be free and be at least eighteen. Scholars estimate the citizens numbered roughly thirty to forty thousand adult men, out of a total population in Attica of perhaps three hundred thousand.

Women of citizen families were expected to stay mostly at home, weave cloth and manage the household. They could not vote, hold office or own much property in their own name. A woman’s father or husband spoke for her in court. Poorer women did work outside, selling in the market or farming, but still had no political voice.

were free people from other places who lived in Athens. Many were skilled craftsmen, merchants and bankers. They paid a special tax and served in the army, but they could not vote or own land. The philosopher Aristotle himself lived in Athens as a metic. Below them were the enslaved. Perhaps one in three people in Athens was enslaved. They worked in homes, workshops, farms and, worst of all, the silver mines. Athens’s wealth rested on their labor.

So the same city that invented rule by the people also ruled over most of its people without their consent. Historians point this out not to dismiss Athens but to be accurate. The Athenians opened a door that had never been opened. They also decided, deliberately, how narrow the doorway would be. Every democracy since has had to answer the same question: who counts?

23.5

The Persian Wars

Main ideaOutnumbered Greek city-states twice turned back the huge Persian Empire, at Marathon and then at Salamis and Plataea.

To the east of Greece lay the largest empire the world had yet seen. The stretched from Egypt to the edge of India. Its king, Darius, ruled millions of people. When Greek cities on the coast of Asia Minor rebelled against him, Athens sent ships to help. Darius crushed the revolt and decided to punish Athens.

In 490 BCE a Persian army landed at the plain of , about twenty-six miles from Athens. The Athenians, with help from one small ally, marched out to meet them. The Greeks fought as , heavily armored foot soldiers who locked shields in a tight block called a phalanx. They charged at a run and broke the Persian line. The story that a runner raced to Athens with news of victory gave the marathon race its name and distance.

Ten years later Darius’s son Xerxes came back with a far larger force by land and sea. At the narrow pass of Thermopylae, a small Greek army led by three hundred Spartans held the road for days until they were surrounded and killed. The Persians burned Athens. But the Athenian leader Themistocles had persuaded his city to build a navy with its silver. In 480 BCE, in the narrow strait of , the Greek ships rammed and sank the crowded Persian fleet while Xerxes watched from shore.

The next year a united Greek army defeated the Persians at Plataea, and the invasion was over. How do we know all this? Mostly from one man, the historian Herodotus, who traveled, asked questions and wrote it down a few decades later. The victory left Athens with the strongest navy in Greece and a new confidence. Its greatest age was about to begin.

The Golden Age

23.6

The Parthenon and the Theater

Main ideaIn the fifty years after the Persian Wars, Athens used its wealth to build the Parthenon and to invent drama.

After the Persians left, Athens led a league of Greek cities that paid money into a common fund for defense. Under the leader , Athens moved that treasury to its own city and spent much of it at home. Many allies were angry. Athens was becoming an empire. But the money built something the world still visits.

On the , the rocky hill above the city, the Athenians raised a marble temple to their goddess Athena. The took about fifteen years to build, from 447 to 432 BCE. Its columns lean slightly inward and its floor curves slightly upward, tricks that make it look perfectly straight to the eye. Inside stood a statue of Athena forty feet tall, covered in gold and ivory. Sculptors carved a band of figures around the building showing the people of Athens in procession.

Down the hill, in an open-air theater cut into the slope, Athenians invented . Plays began as songs to the god Dionysus and grew into stories acted by masked performers with a singing chorus. Tragedies by Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides showed kings and heroes destroyed by pride or fate. Comedies by Aristophanes made fun of politicians, philosophers and the war, sometimes with the targets sitting in the audience.

Theater was not a small hobby. Thousands of citizens attended festivals that lasted days, and the city paid for poor citizens’ tickets. Watching a play meant thinking together about justice, power and grief. Many of these plays survive, and theaters still perform them. Our words theater, drama, tragedy, comedy and chorus are all Greek.

23.7

Socrates, Plato and Aristotle

Main ideaThree Athenian thinkers taught people to question, reason and observe, and their methods are still used today.

means love of wisdom. Greek philosophers tried to understand the world by reasoning rather than by retelling myths. The most famous of them, , wrote nothing at all. He walked the streets of Athens in the late fifth century BCE asking people what they meant by words like courage, justice and goodness. When they answered, he asked another question, and another, until they saw that they did not really know. Teachers still call this the Socratic method.

Many Athenians found him annoying, and some found him dangerous. In 399 BCE, after Athens had lost a long war with Sparta, a jury of citizens convicted Socrates of not respecting the city’s gods and corrupting the young. He refused to beg or to flee. He drank the poison hemlock as the law required. His death showed both the freedom of Athens, which had let him question everything for decades, and its limits.

His student wrote down conversations, called dialogues, in which Socrates is the main speaker. Plato founded a school in Athens called the Academy. He argued that the things we see are imperfect copies of perfect ideas, and that the best state would be run by wise rulers, not by the crowd that had killed his teacher. Plato did not trust democracy.

Plato’s student took a different path. He wanted to look at the actual world. He collected and described animals, studied the constitutions of more than a hundred Greek cities, wrote the first books on logic and gave rules for good plays. Aristotle’s habit of careful observation and sorting things into categories became a model for science. For a while he was also tutor to a prince in Macedon named Alexander.

23.8

History and Medicine

Main ideaGreek writers began to explain wars and diseases by evidence and natural causes instead of by the will of the gods.

Before the Greeks, people recorded kings’ victories, but no one had asked, in a careful way, why events happened. , born in the 480s BCE, did. He traveled through Egypt, Persia and Greece, listened to priests, soldiers and merchants, and compared their stories. Sometimes he reported tales he doubted and said so. Later writers named him the father of history, and the word history comes from his Greek word for inquiry.

Thucydides, an Athenian general, went further. He wrote the story of the long war between Athens and Sparta, from 431 to 404 BCE, which he had lived through. He left out the gods, checked witnesses against each other and looked for causes in fear, ambition and self-interest. He said he wrote not to entertain but to be useful to anyone who wanted to understand how people behave.

Doctors were making the same turn. On the island of Kos, a physician named and his followers taught that disease had natural causes, such as diet, climate and the body’s balance, not angry gods. They watched patients closely and wrote down what they saw, day by day. A famous Hippocratic text on epilepsy, then called the sacred disease, argued that it was no more sacred than any other illness.

The Hippocratic writers were often wrong about the body. They had not dissected humans and believed health depended on four fluids they called humors. But their method, observe, record, look for natural causes, was the right one. Doctors today still speak of the Hippocratic Oath, a promise to help patients and do no harm, which grew from that school.

Alexander and the Greek Legacy

23.9

Alexander the Great

Main ideaIn just over a decade, Alexander of Macedon conquered the Persian Empire and spread Greek culture from Egypt to India.

The long war between Athens and Sparta left the Greek city-states weak. To their north lay , a kingdom the Greeks considered half-barbarian. Its king, Philip II, built a professional army with long pikes and clever tactics. In 338 BCE he defeated the Greek cities and forced them into a league under his control. Two years later he was murdered, and his son Alexander became king at age twenty.

had been tutored by Aristotle and had grown up on the poems of Homer. In 334 BCE he led about forty thousand men across into Asia to attack the Persian Empire. He won battle after battle, at the river Granicus, at Issus and at Gaugamela in 331 BCE, where he broke the army of King Darius III. He took Egypt, where he was welcomed as pharaoh, and the great cities of Babylon and Persepolis.

He kept marching east, through what is now Afghanistan and into the Indus valley of India. His soldiers, exhausted after eight years of fighting, finally refused to go farther. Alexander turned back. In 323 BCE, in Babylon, he died of a fever at age thirty-two. He had never lost a battle.

Alexander founded cities along his route, many named Alexandria, and settled Greek soldiers in them. He married a Persian princess and encouraged his officers to do the same. Was he a visionary uniting peoples or a conqueror who burned Persepolis and killed those who crossed him? The sources say both. How you judge him depends on whether you stand with the Greeks who followed him or the peoples he conquered.

23.10

The Hellenistic World

Main ideaAfter Alexander, Greek language and learning blended with Egyptian, Persian and other cultures across a wide Hellenistic world.

Alexander left no clear heir, and his generals fought over his empire for decades. It broke into several kingdoms: Egypt under the family of Ptolemy, much of Asia under the Seleucids, and Macedon itself. Historians call the next three centuries the age, from Hellas, the Greek name for Greece. It means Greek-like, because Greek culture spread far beyond Greece and mixed with local ways.

The greatest Hellenistic city was in Egypt. Its harbor was marked by a lighthouse so tall it was counted among the seven wonders of the world. Its kings built the Museum, a research center, and the famous Library, which aimed to collect every book in the world. Greeks, Egyptians and Jews lived side by side, and the Hebrew scriptures were translated into Greek there.

Scholars in Alexandria and other cities made remarkable discoveries. organized geometry into a system of proofs that students studied for two thousand years. Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth by comparing shadows in two cities, and came close to the real number. of Syracuse worked out the law of the lever and the principle of floating bodies. Doctors in Alexandria dissected human bodies and described the nerves.

The Hellenistic world was not simply Greece made bigger. A Greek-speaking merchant in Egypt might pray to a god who was half Greek Zeus and half Egyptian Osiris. Buddhist statues in what is now Pakistan were carved in a Greek style. Greek became the shared language of trade and learning across the eastern Mediterranean, which is why, centuries later, the New Testament was written in Greek.

The story

The Morning the Mountain Woke

In the year 79, a Roman seaside town went about an ordinary August day, with no idea that the green mountain nearby was a volcano.

Pompeii sat by the bay south of Rome, a busy town of maybe twelve thousand people, with paved streets, bakeries, a big open forum and public baths. On this morning a baker slid round loaves into his oven, stamped with his mark. Boys carried water from a street fountain. A tavern owner chalked prices on the wall. Election slogans, painted in red, urged citizens to vote for a friend as town official. Above it all rose Vesuvius, a broad mountain covered in vineyards. No Roman living remembered it ever erupting.

Around midday the mountain exploded. A column of ash and stone shot miles into the sky, then spread like an umbrella. Within hours, small pumice stones began to fall on Pompeii like hail. Roofs sagged. People tied pillows on their heads and ran for the gates. Others stayed, hoping it would stop. Some were enslaved and had been told to guard the house.

Across the bay, a seventeen-year-old named Pliny watched with his uncle, a naval commander and famous author. The uncle took ships to rescue people and died on the shore, overcome by fumes. Years later the nephew described the day in two letters to the historian Tacitus. He wrote of the cloud shaped like a pine tree, of darkness in daytime and of people crying out for their families. His letters are our eyewitness account.

Before dawn the next day, a wave of superheated gas and ash rushed down the mountain and killed everyone still in Pompeii. Ash buried the town under many feet of debris. It was forgotten for almost seventeen hundred years. When diggers found it in the 1700s, they uncovered a Roman town frozen in a single day: bread still in the oven, dogs on chains, a dining room set for lunch.

Archaeologists found hollows in the hardened ash where bodies had decayed. They poured plaster into the hollows and got casts of the people of Pompeii in their last moments, some huddled together, one covering his face. Pompeii tells us what no book does: how ordinary Romans, rich, poor, free and enslaved, actually lived.

Main ideaThe Roman Republic balanced power among consuls, the Senate and citizen assemblies, and the plebeians fought to win written laws and a share of power.

A is a state without a king, where citizens choose their leaders. In Rome, two were elected each year to run the government and lead the army. Each could block the other, and after one year they had to step down. This kept any one man from becoming a king. The was a council of about three hundred older men, mostly from wealthy families, who served for life. It did not make laws in a strict sense, but its advice was almost always followed. Roman citizens were divided into two classes. The were a small number of old, powerful families who held all the offices at first. The were everyone else: farmers, shopkeepers, soldiers. The Fall and What Survived

24.9

Why the Western Empire Fell

Main ideaNo single cause ended Rome in the west; weak government, money troubles, plague, and pressure from migrating peoples combined over two centuries.

After 180 CE the empire ran into trouble. In one fifty-year stretch of the 200s, more than twenty men claimed the title of emperor, most of them generals who were killed by their own troops or by rivals. Wars on the borders needed more soldiers, and paying them needed more money. Emperors put less silver in each coin, so prices soared. Plague swept through the empire more than once and cut the population.

The emperor Diocletian, around 300 CE, tried to fix things by splitting the empire into eastern and western halves with separate rulers. Constantine built a new capital, Constantinople, in the richer, safer east. The west, poorer and with a longer frontier along the Rhine and Danube rivers, was left with fewer resources.

Beyond those rivers lived Germanic peoples such as the Goths, Vandals and Franks. For centuries they had traded with Rome, served in its armies and sometimes settled inside the empire. In the late 300s, pushed by the Huns from central Asia, whole peoples crossed the border seeking safety and land. Rome could not stop them or fully absorb them. In 410 the Goths sacked Rome itself, the first time in eight hundred years an enemy had taken the city. In 455 the Vandals did it again.

In 476 a Germanic general named Odoacer removed the last western emperor, a boy named Romulus Augustulus, and did not bother to name another. Historians use that date for the fall of the western empire. But nothing dramatic happened that day. Roman roads, laws, towns and the Latin language went on under new kings. The eastern half, which we call the Byzantine Empire, lasted another thousand years, until 1453.

The story

Silk Out, Glass Home

Around the year 100, a caravan leaves the Han capital for the west, carrying cloth that the Romans will pay for in gold.

Before dawn, the western gate of Chang'an is already loud. Chang'an is the capital of Han China, one of the largest cities in the world, laid out in a grid of walled wards. A merchant named Lu checks the loads on forty camels. Each carries bolts of silk, packed in oiled cloth against the rain. Silk is made from the cocoons of moths fed on mulberry leaves, and how to make it is a secret the Chinese have kept for centuries. Lu also carries bronze mirrors and lacquered boxes.

The caravan will not go to Rome. No single trader ever did. Lu will travel for months along the Gansu corridor, a strip of grassland between mountains and desert, past the last Chinese fort. There, in an oasis town, he will sell his silk to Sogdian merchants from central Asia. They will carry it over mountains to Persia. Persians will sell it to Greeks and Syrians, who will ship it across the Mediterranean. By the time a Roman woman wears it, the silk has changed hands a dozen times and its price has multiplied.

The road is dangerous. The Taklamakan Desert is so dry that caravans hug its edges from oasis to oasis. Bandits wait in the passes. Camels can go days without water and can smell it from far off, which is why Lu trusts them more than horses. At night the drivers sleep in walled rest stops, where Lu hears languages he does not know and sees priests of religions he has never met.

At the oasis market, Lu trades. He takes back things China cannot make or grow: glass bowls from Roman Syria, so clear they seem like frozen water, wool carpets, grapes and walnuts, a horse from the Ferghana valley that his emperor's army prizes. He also takes back stories. A Buddhist monk from India asks to travel with the caravan east. Lu agrees. The monk carries scrolls of teaching that will change China more than any glass bowl.

Months later Lu rides back through the western gate of Chang'an. He has never left the lands between China and Persia, yet his silk is now in Rome and Rome's glass is on his camel. Historians call this web of routes the Silk Roads. It carried cloth, spices, paper, religions, inventions and, sometimes, deadly disease, across the whole of Eurasia.

Main ideaHan China invented paper and began choosing officials by examination, two ideas that spread around the world.

Main ideaThe Silk Roads were a web of land and sea routes across Eurasia along which goods passed through many hands between China, India, Persia and Rome.

There was never one silk road. The name was invented by a German geographer in the 1800s for a network of routes that connected China with the Mediterranean. The main land routes ran west from Chang’an through the Gansu corridor, split around the Taklamakan Desert, crossed the Pamir Mountains and continued through Persia to Syria. Sea routes joined southern China, Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and Egypt with the monsoon winds.

The Han opened the western routes on purpose. Around 138 BCE the emperor Wudi sent an envoy named Zhang Qian west to find allies against the Xiongnu. He was captured and held for years, escaped and came home with reports of rich kingdoms and fine horses in the Ferghana valley. Han armies followed, set up forts and made the roads safer. Trade grew.

Goods flowed both ways. From China came , lacquerware, bronze mirrors and later paper and porcelain. From India came cotton cloth, pepper and other spices, ivory and gems. From Persia and Rome came glass, wool, horses, wine, silver coins and gold. Rome’s writers grumbled that the empire was pouring gold into Asia to pay for luxuries. Along the way, oasis cities such as Samarkand and Kashgar grew rich as middlemen.

Almost no merchant traveled the whole distance. Goods passed from Chinese to Sogdian to Persian to Greek traders, each adding a markup. Main ideaThe same routes that spread goods and ideas also carried diseases, and by the 200s CE plague struck both Rome and Han China as both empires weakened.

Trade routes connect people, and people carry germs. A disease that one population has lived with for generations can be deadly to another that has never met it. As caravans and ships tied Eurasia together, sicknesses moved along with them. Historians cannot always name the exact disease from ancient descriptions, but they can trace the outbreaks.

In 165 CE, Roman soldiers returning from a war in the east brought home a that spread through the empire for fifteen years. The doctor Galen described its fever and rash; many scholars think it was smallpox. Millions may have died, and some estimates put the loss at a tenth of the empire’s population. A century later another wave hit. China’s records show major epidemics in the same period, and the Han population fell sharply between the census of 2 CE and the counts of the late 100s.

Disease was not the only cause, but both great empires ran into deep trouble at about the same time. Han China collapsed in 220 CE into three kingdoms and then centuries of division. Rome survived its crisis of the 200s but never fully recovered in the west. Nomads pressed on both frontiers. Some historians argue that the very connections that made the empires rich also made them fragile.

The lesson is not that connection is bad. The Silk Roads gave the world paper, zero, Buddhism in East Asia and a thousand smaller gifts. It is that a connected world shares everything, good and bad, and needs ways to manage that. Illinois today ships corn to Asia and receives electronics, and O’Hare Airport links Chicago to every continent. The questions traders faced on the Silk Roads, about trust, fair exchange and risk, are still ours.

Fifteen questions across the unit

1. Why did ancient Greece develop as many small city-states?

Why: Rugged geography cut valleys and islands off from one another, so each became its own polis.

2. Which group in Athens could vote?

Why: Only free adult male citizens voted; women, foreigners and the enslaved were excluded.

3. What method did Socrates use to teach?

Why: Socrates questioned people until they saw the gaps in what they thought they knew.

4. What happened to Greek culture after Alexander's death?

Why: The Hellenistic age blended Greek language and learning with Egyptian, Persian and Indian traditions.

5. Why did Rome have two consuls who served one year?

Why: Shared, limited power was the Republic's guard against one-man rule.

6. What made the plebeians' demand for the Twelve Tables important?

Why: Public written law meant priests and nobles could no longer bend secret rules.

7. What most weakened the Roman Republic before Caesar?

Why: Landless soldiers followed whoever paid them, letting generals march on Rome.

8. How did Augustus keep power without being called king?

Why: As first citizen he let the Senate meet but held the real power.

9. Which Roman legal idea is still used in American courts?

Why: The presumption of innocence and the right to face one's accuser come through Roman law.

10. What was true of the fall of Rome in 476?

Why: Odoacer removed the last western emperor, but Roman institutions carried on, and the east lasted until 1453.

11. How do we know Ashoka's own thoughts?

Why: More than thirty of Ashoka's edicts survive in his own words.

12. Which is an achievement of Gupta India?

Why: Indian mathematicians developed place-value numbers with zero during the Gupta age.

13. What did the Han dynasty take from Confucius?

Why: The Han made Confucian learning official and trained officials in it.

14. Why did silk cost far more in Rome than in China?

Why: No merchant went the whole way; goods changed hands many times, each with a markup.

15. Which statement best sums up the Silk Roads?

Why: The Silk Roads were many land and sea routes that spread trade, religions, inventions and plague.
