Germs, Vaccines and First Aid

Staying Well

Every day, your body meets germs too small to see. Most of the time, you never notice. Your skin, your immune system and simple habits like handwashing keep you well. Sometimes a germ gets through, and you catch a cold or a fever.

People have learned a lot about germs over hundreds of years. A country doctor named Edward Jenner made the first vaccine. Scientists found antibiotics. Health workers around the world worked together to wipe out smallpox.

In this unit, you will learn how germs spread and how your body fights back. You will also learn first aid for small hurts, when to call 911, and how to stay calm and get a trusted adult. You can be a real helper.

How do our bodies, science and our own actions keep us and the people around us safe and well?

The story

The Milkmaid's Clear Skin

An old country story helped a doctor find a way to stop one of the worst sicknesses in history.

Long ago in the English countryside, a sickness called smallpox scared almost everyone. It gave people a high fever and bumpy spots all over the skin. Many people died from it. Many who got well had scars on their faces for life.

Edward Jenner was a country doctor in the village of Berkeley, England. Farm families there told an old story. They said milkmaids who caught a mild sickness from cows almost never got smallpox. That cow sickness was called cowpox. It gave a milkmaid a few sores on her hands, and then she got well.

One well-known telling says a young milkmaid spoke to Jenner herself. She said she would never catch smallpox, because she had already had cowpox. Nobody knows her exact words. But Jenner kept thinking about the idea. Could a mild cow sickness protect a person from a deadly one?

In 1796 Jenner decided to test it. He took a little matter from a cowpox sore on the hand of a milkmaid named Sarah Nelmes. He put it into a small cut on the arm of an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps. James got a little sick and then got better.

Weeks later, Jenner exposed James to smallpox. James did not get sick. Jenner tested more people and wrote down what he saw. His work led to the first vaccine. Doctors today would never test on a child that way, but his idea changed the world.

Main ideaViruses are even smaller than bacteria, and they can only make more of themselves inside a living cell.

When you catch a cold, a is usually the cause. A virus is a germ even smaller than bacteria. The flu and chickenpox are also caused by viruses.

Bacteria and viruses are both germs, but they are different. Bacteria are living cells. Viruses are much smaller and are not cells at all. This difference matters when doctors choose a medicine.

9.3

How Germs Travel

Main ideaGerms spread through the air, by touch, and in food and water, so washing hands and covering coughs help.

Maya sneezes into the air. Tiny drops fly out. If she has a cold, the drops carry the virus. A friend nearby can breathe them in. That is one way germs .

Germs also spread by touch. Someone coughs into a hand, then touches a door handle. The next person touches it and then rubs an eye. Germs can also ride in food that was not cooked well, or in dirty water.

Simple habits block these paths. Wash hands with soap and water for about 20 seconds. Cough or sneeze into your elbow. Keep food clean and cooked. Drink clean water. These habits cut down the chances of getting sick. This is called good .

How a Vaccine Works

Main ideaA vaccine teaches the immune system to know a germ, so the body is ready before the real germ arrives.

A fire drill helps you practice before there is a real fire. A is like a drill for your immune system. It lets the body practice fighting a germ without getting very sick.

A vaccine holds a weak, dead or harmless piece of a germ, or instructions for making one. It is often given as a shot. The immune system sees it and makes antibodies. It also makes memory cells.

Later, the real germ may come. Now the body knows it. It can fight fast, often before you feel sick. This protection is called . Your doctor or nurse can tell you which vaccines you need and when.

9.8

The End of Smallpox

Main ideaVaccines used all over the world wiped out smallpox, and in 1980 it was declared gone.

After Jenner, many countries began to vaccinate people against smallpox. In 1967 the World Health Organization started a big push to end it everywhere. Health workers traveled to villages all over the world.

The workers found each new case. Then they vaccinated everyone around that sick person. This stopped the virus from finding new people. The last natural case was found in Somalia, in Africa, in 1977.

In 1980 the World Health Organization said smallpox was . That means it was wiped out in the wild. It is the first human disease ever wiped out. It took teamwork from many .

9.10

Why Antibiotics Skip Viruses

Main ideaAntibiotics work on bacteria but not on viruses, so they do not help a cold or the flu.

Sam has a runny nose and a cough. He asks his doctor for an antibiotic. The doctor kindly says no. Sam’s cold is caused by a virus, and antibiotics do not work on viruses.

Antibiotics attack parts that bacteria have, like their cell walls. Viruses do not have those parts. So the medicine has nothing to hit. For a cold, rest, fluids and time usually help most.

Using antibiotics when they are not needed can cause a problem. Some bacteria learn to survive them. This is called antibiotic . Using antibiotics only when a doctor says so helps keep them for everyone.

The story

Rosa Makes the Call

When her grandfather fell, a ten-year-old girl remembered what to do.

Rosa was doing homework at the kitchen table. Her grandfather was in the next room, reaching for a box on a high shelf. Then she heard a loud thump. She ran in. Grandpa was on the floor. He was awake, but he could not get up, and his arm looked hurt.

Rosa picked up the phone and dialed 911. A calm voice asked, "What is your emergency?" Rosa said her grandfather fell and could not get up. She gave her home address, street and number, just the way she had practiced. She answered each question and did not hang up.

The helper on the phone told Rosa to unlock the front door and stay with Grandpa. Soon an ambulance arrived. The paramedics checked Grandpa and took him to the hospital to look at his arm.

Later, Grandpa squeezed Rosa's hand. "You stayed calm," he said. "You knew what to do." Rosa smiled. Knowing her address and practicing the call had made a real difference.

Epinephrine Pens

Main ideaAn epinephrine pen is medicine for a severe allergic reaction, used by adults, trained helpers or the person it belongs to.

Some people with serious allergies carry an pen. One well-known brand is the EpiPen. Epinephrine is a medicine that can quickly help with anaphylaxis.

The pen gives a shot, often into the outer thigh. Adults and trained helpers, like school nurses, use it. Some older kids learn to use their own pen with their doctor’s help. After it is used, call 911, because the person still needs a doctor.

Your job as a friend is to know the signs and get an adult fast. Know where your friend’s pen is kept. Never play with someone’s pen. It is real .

10.9

Staying Calm Helps

Main ideaTaking slow breaths helps you think clearly so you can remember the steps and get help.

In an emergency, your heart may race and your hands may shake. That is normal. Your body is getting ready to act. Rosa felt this too when Grandpa fell.

Slow breathing helps. Breathe in through your nose while you count to four. Breathe out slowly. One or two slow breaths help your brain . Then you can remember what to do.

Staying also helps the hurt person. A kind, steady voice helps them feel safe. You can say, "Help is coming. I am here." You do not have to fix everything. Your job is to get help.

Think it through

Breathe in for four counts. Hold for four counts. Breathe out for four counts. Hold for four counts. Picture tracing the four sides of a square as you go.

Try it twice. How does your body feel afterward? When else could this help you?

Main ideaA first-aid kit holds supplies for small hurts, and knowing where it is helps you act fast.

Main ideaWhen a friend is hurt, one of the best ways to help is to get a trusted adult quickly.
