What the Hindu Texts Are

The Library

Hinduism does not have one single holy book. It has a whole library. Some texts are hymns. Some are long stories. Some are talks between a teacher and a student.

In this unit you will learn how Hindus sort these texts. You will learn about shruti, "what is heard," and smriti, "what is remembered." You will also learn how the texts traveled for thousands of years, first by memory and later on palm leaves, in comics and on screens.

By the end, you will be able to name the main Hindu texts. You will be able to say which group each belongs to. You will be able to explain how people kept them alive.

The big question

How have Hindus kept a great library of sacred texts alive for thousands of years?

The story

Two Kinds of Words

A grandmother tells her grandson that some words were heard and some were remembered.

Rohan sits beside his grandmother in their apartment in Naperville. A shelf by the window holds many books. Some are thick. Some are thin. Some have gold letters on the cover.

"Which one is the Hindu Bible?" Rohan asks. He heard a friend at school ask that. Grandma laughs softly. "There is no single book," she says. "There is a whole library."

She picks up a thin book of hymns. "Hindus say some words were heard," she says. "Wise seers heard them long, long ago. We call these shruti." Grandma turns the pages gently, as if they were very old.

Then she picks up a big, bright Ramayana. "And some words were remembered," she says. "People remembered them and told them again. These are called smriti."

Rohan looks at the shelf again. Now he sees two groups. Heard and remembered. He wants to know what is in each one. He asks his grandma to tell him more, one group at a time.

Shruti: What Is Heard

7.4

Seers Who Heard

Main ideaHindus hold that the shruti texts were heard by ancient seers called rishis.

Shruti means "what is heard." Hindus hold that these texts were not made up by people. They believe ancient seers heard them. These seers are called .

Many Hindus believe the rishis heard these words in deep meditation. Then they taught them to students. The students taught their own students. Hindus call this long chain of teachers a living tradition.

For this reason, many Hindus treat shruti as the most sacred group of texts. Scholars date the oldest shruti text, the Rig Veda, to about 1500 to 1200 BCE.

Smriti: What Is Remembered

7.7

Texts People Remember and Retell

Main ideaSmriti texts were remembered and passed on by people, and many Hindus know them best.

Smriti means "what is remembered." Hindus hold that people made these texts. People remembered them and passed them on. They still count as sacred. Many Hindus treat them with great respect all the same.

Smriti includes the two great epics. It includes the Puranas, books of old stories. It includes law books too. Many of the stories children love come from these books.

Shruti is ranked highest. But many Hindus know smriti best. Children grow up hearing the epics. Families act them out at festivals. A child may know the Ramayana long before hearing a Veda.

Inside the Mahabharata is a short text. It is called the . The name means "song of the Lord." It has 18 chapters and 700 verses. Most Hindus simply call it the Gita.

In the Gita, the warrior Arjuna does not want to fight. Krishna, his charioteer, teaches him. Many Hindus honor Krishna as God. Their talk takes place on a battlefield, just before a war.

Sanskrit, Gurus and Memory

How the Texts Traveled
Big questionHow did the Hindu texts travel across thousands of years?
The story

Forward and Backward

A boy repeats a line forward and backward until he knows every sound by heart.

It is early morning in a school in South India. Ten boys sit cross-legged on a stone floor. Their teacher sits in front of them. He chants a line from the Vedas.

The boys chant it back. Then the teacher chants the line again. This time he chants the words in pairs. One and two. Two and three. Three and four. The room fills with the sound of steady, careful voices.

Next he chants the words backward and forward in a pattern. The boys follow. One boy, Hari, frowns. The pattern is hard. He tries again. He listens closely and moves his lips without a sound.

The teacher waits. Hari tries a third time. This time every sound is right. The teacher nods. Hari smiles. The other boys clap their hands softly for him. Then they all begin the next line together.

Hari's teacher learned the same way from his own teacher. Hindus say this chain goes back thousands of years. Every sound was passed on by memory. Hari hopes to teach it himself one day.

The Language of the Texts

8.1

Sanskrit

Main ideaMost of the old Hindu texts are in Sanskrit, an ancient language of India.

Most of the old Hindu texts are in . Sanskrit is a very old language of India. The Vedas use an early form of it. Sanskrit has its own careful rules of grammar.

The name Sanskrit means "well made" or "polished." For centuries, scholars and priests used it. Few people speak it at home today. But priests still chant it every day.

Sanskrit is related to many other languages. It is a cousin of Latin, Greek and even English. That is why some Sanskrit words look a little like English words.

Passed Down by Memory

8.4

Before Writing

Main ideaThe Vedas were passed down by memory for many centuries before they were written.

The Vedas are very old. For many centuries, nobody wrote them down. Teachers taught them by heart. Students learned every word by listening. They repeated the lines many times each day.

Many Hindus treat the sound of the Vedas as sacred. The exact sounds matter. So it was important not to change even one syllable. A small slip could change the meaning or the sound.

Scholars think the Vedas were written down much later, perhaps in the first thousand years CE. Even then, many teachers kept teaching them by memory. Some families still pass on the chanting this way today.

8.5

Chanting Patterns

Main ideaSpecial chanting patterns helped keep every sound the same.

Vedic teachers made clever methods. Students chanted the words straight through. Then they chanted them one at a time. Each way of chanting made them listen very closely.

Next they chanted in pairs: one-two, two-three, three-four. Harder patterns went forward and backward. These patterns have names like krama and ghana.

If a student made a mistake, the patterns showed it. This worked like a checking system. Many scholars say it kept the Rig Veda very close to its old form.

8.7

Guru and Student

Main ideaHindu texts were passed on from guru to student.

A is a teacher. In old India, students often lived with their guru. They helped with daily work. They learned texts by heart. Learning could take many years.

The Upanishads often show a guru and a student talking. The student asks a question. The guru answers with a story or an example. This way of learning is still used in many Hindu schools.

Many Hindus still honor their gurus. They see learning as a chain. Each teacher passes on what an earlier teacher gave. Some families invite a guru to bless a child’s first lessons.

8.8

Palm-Leaf Books

Main ideaMany texts were later written on dried palm leaves.

Later, people wrote texts down. In much of India, they wrote on dried palm leaves. Each leaf was long and thin. Leaves were easy to find in warm, green parts of India.

Scribes scratched letters into the leaf with a pointed tool. Then they rubbed ink or soot on it. The letters turned dark. This made the writing easy to read and hard to wipe away.

The leaves were tied together with a string. Palm leaves wear out over time. So scribes copied texts again and again. Wooden boards on the top and bottom kept the leaves flat.

8.9

Retold in Many Languages

Main ideaThe stories were retold in Tamil, Hindi and many other languages.

Most people in India did not speak Sanskrit. So poets retold the stories in their own languages. They spoke Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, Marathi and many other languages.

The poet Kamban retold the Ramayana in Tamil, perhaps in the 1100s. The poet Tulsidas retold it in Hindi in the 1500s. His Ramcharitmanas is loved across North India.

The stories spread to many other languages too. Each retelling is a little different. Each helped more people know the stories. Some tell parts of the story that Valmiki’s poem does not.

Comics, TV and Apps

Main ideaToday the texts travel through comic books, TV and apps too.

The stories keep traveling. In 1967, Amar Chitra Katha began making comic books of the stories. Many children grew up reading them. The comics used bright pictures and simple words.

In 1987, a TV series of the Ramayana aired in India. Many people stopped what they were doing to watch each Sunday. Streets in many towns became quiet while the show was on.

Today there are cartoons, podcasts and apps. Families in Illinois can read the Gita on a phone. The ways change. The stories keep being told. Temples in Illinois hold classes on the texts as well.
