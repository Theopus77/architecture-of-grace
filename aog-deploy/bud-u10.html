Mahayana Sutras, Zen and Tibet

Genres II

Most Buddhists in the world today read texts that are not in the Pali Canon. In East Asia, people chant the Heart Sutra, honor the Lotus Sutra and call the name of Amitabha Buddha. In Tibet, monks study huge collections of translated texts and sing the songs of great yogis. In this unit, you will explore Mahayana sutras, Zen writings and Tibetan texts.

You will read the parable of the burning house, meet the bodhisattva of compassion, and learn what Mahayana teachers mean by emptiness. Then you will look at Zen koans and the Platform Sutra, Dogen's writings, Milarepa's life, the so-called Tibetan Book of the Dead and Shantideva's poem.

By the end, you will be able to explain key ideas like skillful means, emptiness and the bodhisattva path, tell how different schools choose their central texts, and show how a text's meaning can change when it reaches new readers.

How do Mahayana, Zen and Tibetan Buddhists use their texts, and how have outsiders read them?

Mahayana Sutras
Big questionHow do Mahayana sutras use parables, short chants and new figures to teach, and why do Mahayana Buddhists treasure them?
The story

Carts Full of Toys

A father must get his children out of a burning house, but they are too busy playing to listen.

Chapter 3 of the Lotus Sutra tells a parable. A rich old man owns a large, crumbling house with only one door. One day, fire breaks out. Inside, his children are playing games. They do not know what fire is, and they do not want to stop playing.

The father calls to them to come out, but they ignore him. He thinks quickly. He knows what they love. So he shouts that outside the door are carts they have always wanted: carts pulled by goats, by deer and by oxen.

The children rush out, pushing each other through the door, and they are safe. Then the father gives each child not the small carts he promised, but one great, splendid cart pulled by a white ox, better than anything they imagined.

In the sutra, the Buddha then asks: did the father lie? His disciple Sariputra answers no, because he saved his children's lives. Mahayana Buddhists read the story as a picture of how the Buddha teaches: using many methods to lead beings out of suffering toward one great goal.

Talk about itWas the father right to promise something different from what he gave? Why or why not?

A New Kind of Sutra

19.1

Where Mahayana Sutras Came From

Main ideaScholars date Mahayana sutras from about the 1st century BCE onward; Mahayana Buddhists see them as the Buddha's word.

Around the 1st century BCE and after, new Buddhist sutras began to circulate in India. They were written in Sanskrit and related languages. They called themselves the words of the Buddha and opened with ’Thus have I heard,’ like the older suttas.

Modern scholars date these by their language, ideas and first translations into Chinese. Many were written down over several centuries. Scholars see them as a new wave of Buddhist writing.

Mahayana Buddhists understand them differently. Some traditions teach that the Buddha taught these sutras to those ready to hear them, and that they were kept hidden until the time was right. Others stress that they express the Buddha’s true meaning. For them the sutras carry full .

19.2

The Lotus Sutra

Main ideaThe Lotus Sutra teaches that all beings can become buddhas and is central in East Asian Buddhism.

The (Saddharma Pundarika Sutra, ’Sutra of the Lotus of the True Dharma’) is one of the most important Mahayana texts. Its best-known Chinese translation was made by Kumarajiva in 406 CE.

The sutra teaches that the different paths the Buddha taught are really one path, the ’one vehicle’, leading to buddhahood. It says that all beings, even those who seem far from it, can become buddhas.

In East Asia, the Lotus Sutra shaped the Tiantai school in China and the Tendai school in Japan. The Japanese teacher Nichiren (1222–1282) taught that chanting its title is the heart of practice. Nichiren Buddhists today, including Soka Gakkai members, continue this practice. The lotus itself is a .

19.3

Skillful Means

Main ideaThe Lotus Sutra's idea of skillful means says the Buddha adapts his teaching to each listener.

Chapter 2 of the Lotus Sutra is titled ’Skillful Means’ (Sanskrit upaya). It says the Buddha, as the sutra presents him, adjusts his teaching to what each listener can understand, like a good teacher or doctor.

The burning house parable in Chapter 3 shows this idea. The father’s promise of three carts is a skillful means to get the children out; the one great cart stands for the one vehicle.

Mahayana readers have used skillful means to explain why the Buddha seems to teach different things in different texts. Critics within and outside Buddhism have asked where skillful means ends and misleading begins. The sutra’s own answer is that the father’s was to save his children.

Emptiness and the Heart Sutra

19.4

The Heart Sutra

Main ideaThe Heart Sutra is a very short Mahayana text on emptiness, chanted daily in much of East Asia.

The is one of the shortest Buddhist sutras. The common Chinese version has about 260 characters. It belongs to the Perfection of Wisdom (prajnaparamita) family of sutras.

In it, the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara speaks to Shariputra about (Sanskrit shunyata). Its most famous lines say that form is emptiness and emptiness is form. It ends with a short mantra.

Monks, nuns and laypeople in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam chant the Heart Sutra daily. It is often copied by hand as a practice. Many Zen, Chan and Pure Land temples include it in services.

Heart Sutra (Prajnaparamita Hrdaya), Chinese version attributed to Xuanzang (649 CE); see Red Pine, The Heart Sutra (2004) (paraphrased)

19.5

What Emptiness Means

Main ideaMahayana teachers explain emptiness as the idea that things depend on causes and conditions.

Emptiness is easy to misunderstand. Mahayana teachers stress that it does not mean nothing exists. Rather, they teach that nothing has a fixed, separate self. Everything depends on other things.

The Indian philosopher Nagarjuna (c. 2nd century CE) developed this idea in his , or ’middle way’, school. He linked emptiness to the older teaching of dependent arising, found in the Pali suttas.

Teachers often use everyday examples. A table depends on wood, a carpenter, rain that grew the tree and much more. Mahayana Buddhists say seeing this clearly weakens grasping and brings freedom.

19.8

Amitabha and the Pure Land

Main ideaPure Land sutras describe Amitabha Buddha's land of bliss, and devotees call his name to be reborn there.

The Pure Land sutras describe Buddha (’Infinite Light’). In the Larger Pure Land Sutra, a monk named Dharmakara makes vows, including a vow that all who sincerely call his name will be reborn in his land. He becomes Amitabha.

His land, (’Land of Bliss’), is described with jeweled trees, lotus ponds and birds that sing the teaching. Pure Land Buddhists believe that being born there makes awakening easy.

Pure Land Buddhism is one of the largest forms of Buddhism in East Asia. In China and Vietnam, devotees chant ’Namo Amituofo’. In Japan, Honen and Shinran taught the nembutsu, ’Namu Amida Butsu’. Shinran’s Jodo Shinshu is the tradition of the Buddhist Temple of Chicago.

Zen Stories and Tibetan Texts

Later Traditions
Big questionHow do Zen and Tibetan Buddhists use texts, and what is lost when their words are borrowed by popular culture?
The story

A Cup That Overflows

A teacher pours tea into a full cup until it spills across the table.

A modern collection of Zen stories, Zen Flesh, Zen Bones (1957), retells a short anecdote. A university professor comes to visit a Japanese Zen master named Nan-in. He wants to learn about Zen. Nan-in lived in Japan in the Meiji era.

Nan-in serves tea. He pours into the professor's cup until it is full, and then he keeps pouring. Tea spills over the rim, across the saucer and onto the table. The professor watches, then cannot stay quiet. "It is full!" he says. "No more will go in!"

"Like this cup," Nan-in says, as the story tells it, "you are full of your own opinions. How can I show you Zen unless you first empty your cup?" The story ends there, with no further explanation. The professor is left to think.

The story is short, sharp and a little surprising, like many Zen tales. It was collected and retold in English in the 20th century. Readers use it to think about how hard it is to learn something new when we already think we know.

Chan and Zen

20.1

From Chan to Zen

Main ideaChan Buddhism grew in China, spread to Korea, Vietnam and Japan, and stresses meditation and direct insight.

Buddhism took shape in China from about the 6th to 8th centuries CE. The word chan comes from the Sanskrit dhyana, meaning meditation. In Japan it is called Zen, in Korea Seon and in Vietnam Thien.

Chan tradition traces itself to Bodhidharma, an Indian monk who, the tradition says, came to China around 500 CE. Later sources give Chan a famous saying: ’a special transmission outside the scriptures, not depending on words’.

Yet Chan and Zen produced huge numbers of texts: sermons, records of teachers and collections of encounters. Scholars note this : a tradition that says it does not depend on words has created a rich literature. Its heart is from teacher to student.

The Platform Sutra

Main ideaThe Platform Sutra tells the story of Huineng, honored in Chan as the Sixth Patriarch.

The is the only Chinese text given the title ’sutra’ that centers on a Chinese teacher rather than the Buddha. It tells the story and sermons of (638–713), whom Chan honors as the Sixth Patriarch.

In the story, Huineng is a poor woodcutter who cannot read. He hears the Diamond Sutra recited and goes to a monastery. There he writes (through a helper) a short verse that shows, the text says, deeper insight than the head monk’s. The master secretly passes the robe to him.

Scholars have found that the text was edited over time and that parts of the story reflect later disputes between Chan groups. Chan and Zen Buddhists still read it as a key text on sudden .

20.3

Koans and the Gateless Gate

Main ideaA koan is a puzzling story or question that Zen teachers use in training; the Gateless Gate (1228) collects 48 of them.

A is a short story, question or saying from a Chan master that a student works on in meditation. Teachers say a koan cannot be solved by logic alone. The student may work on one koan for months or years.

The (Chinese Wumenguan, Japanese Mumonkan), compiled by Wumen Huikai in 1228, collects 48 koans with comments and verses. Its first case asks whether a dog has Buddha-nature. The master Zhaozhou answers, ’Wu!’ (Japanese: Mu), ’No’ or ’Not’.

The famous question ’What is the sound of one hand?’ is not in the Gateless Gate. It comes from the Japanese Zen master Hakuin (1686–1769). Rinzai Zen teachers still use koans with students today.

Wumen Huikai, Gateless Gate (Wumenguan, 1228), case 1; see Robert Aitken, The Gateless Barrier (1990) (paraphrased)

Dogen and Just Sitting

Main ideaDogen founded Soto Zen in Japan and wrote the Shobogenzo, stressing seated meditation.

(1200–1253) was a Japanese monk who traveled to China to study Chan. He returned and founded what became the Soto school of Zen in Japan. He was a monk of the Tendai school before he turned to Zen.

20.5

Zen and Popular 'Zen'

Main ideaZen is a Buddhist tradition with monks, rules and texts; the popular word 'zen' often means only 'calm'.

In English, ’zen’ is often used as an ad word. A spa, a phone app or a tidy room may be called ’zen’. In this use, the word usually just means calm or simple. The word has drifted far from its Buddhist home.

Zen as a Buddhist tradition is much more. It has temples, training, strict schedules, ordination, chanting, koans, sutras, precepts and teachers linked in lineages that trace back to the Buddha, as the tradition tells it.

Scholars and Zen teachers point out that borrowing the word without its setting can make the tradition hard to see. This is part of a wider discussion about : using parts of another culture without understanding or respect.

Tibetan Texts

20.6

Kangyur and Tengyur

Main ideaThe Tibetan canon has two parts: the Kangyur, words of the Buddha, and the Tengyur, commentaries.

Tibetan scholars translated a vast number of Indian Buddhist texts from Sanskrit into Tibetan, mostly between the 8th and 14th centuries. The collection was organized into two great parts by the scholar Buton (1290–1364) and others.

The (’translated words’) holds texts Tibetans count as the Buddha’s words, including sutras, Vinaya and tantras. Editions have about 100 to 108 volumes. The (’translated treatises’) holds commentaries by Indian masters, in about 220 volumes or more.

Because many Sanskrit originals were lost, the Tibetan translations are often the only surviving versions. Scholars around the world use them. Tibetan monasteries honor the volumes as sacred and carry them in on special days.

20.7

Milarepa's Songs

Main ideaMilarepa, a Tibetan yogi, is loved for his life story and his songs of realization.

(c. 1040–1123, dates vary) is one of Tibet’s most beloved figures. His biography, written in the 15th century by Tsangnyon Heruka, tells that as a young man he used black magic to take revenge on relatives who had cheated his family, causing deaths.

Filled with regret, he sought the teacher Marpa. Marpa put him through years of hard labor, building and tearing down towers, before teaching him. Milarepa then meditated for years in mountain caves, the story says, living on nettles until his skin turned green.

His , collected in the Hundred Thousand Songs of Milarepa, express his insights in simple, vivid poems. Tibetan Buddhists see his life as proof that anyone, even someone who did great harm, can change. His teaching line is the Kagyu school.

Think it through
From Harm to Healing

Picture Milarepa carrying stones up a hill to build a tower. Marpa orders him to tear it down and build it again, several times. Milarepa's back is raw. Tibetan tellers say Marpa was clearing away the karma of Milarepa's past harm.

The 'Tibetan Book of the Dead'

Main ideaThe Bardo Thodol is read to guide the dying and the dead, and its English title shaped how the West saw it.

The , ’Liberation Through Hearing in the Intermediate State’, is part of a group of texts Tibetan tradition says was hidden by Padmasambhava and found in the 14th century by Karma Lingpa. A is an in-between state, such as the time between death and rebirth.

In Tibet, a lama or relative reads the text aloud near a dying or recently dead person. Tibetan Buddhists believe it guides the person’s mind through the bardo toward liberation or a good rebirth.

In 1927, W.Y. Evans-Wentz published an English version titled The Tibetan Book of the Dead. The title echoed the Egyptian Book of the Dead and made the text famous in the West. Some scholars note that it was only one of many funeral texts in Tibet and was read through Western ideas.

Shantideva's Guide

Main ideaShantideva's Bodhicaryavatara is a poem on the bodhisattva path, deeply loved in Tibetan Buddhism.

was an Indian monk of the 8th century CE at Nalanda. His poem, the (’Entering the Path of Awakening’), describes the path of the bodhisattva in ten chapters.

The poem covers generosity, patience, effort, meditation and wisdom. Its chapter on patience is famous for looking at anger closely and giving reasons to let it go. It also includes verses dedicating all merit to the good of others.

Tibetan teachers, including the current Dalai Lama, often teach from this poem. The Dalai Lama has often quoted a dedication verse from its last chapter as a personal favorite. It shows how Indian texts live on through Tibetan .
