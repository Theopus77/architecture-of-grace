Traditions of India and East Asia

Traditions

Across India and East Asia, people light lamps, ring bells, share free meals and bow to their grandparents. These actions come from some of the oldest traditions in the world. Millions of people follow them today, including many families in Chicago.

In this unit you will meet Hinduism and Buddhism, then Sikhism, Jainism, and the teachings of Confucius and the Dao. You will learn their stories, words, holidays and practices. You will see what adherents believe and why their practices matter to them.

By the end, you will be able to explain key words, match practices to traditions and compare how each tradition teaches people to live well. You will describe each one fairly, in its own words.

The big question

How do traditions from India and East Asia teach people to live a good life?

The story

A Lamp and a Bell

Two Chicago kids, two traditions, and one autumn week full of light and sound.

It is a cool night in October. Arjun kneels by the front window of his family's apartment. He holds a small clay lamp called a diya. His grandmother pours in a little oil and helps him light the wick. Soon a row of tiny flames glows along the sill.

"Why do we light so many?" Arjun asks. His grandmother smiles. She says many Hindus light lamps at Diwali to remember that light wins over darkness. She tells him the story of Prince Rama coming home. The people lit lamps to welcome him back.

A few miles away, on the North Side of Chicago, Mai walks into a Buddhist temple with her mother. The room is quiet. It smells of incense. A calm statue of the Buddha sits at the front, with flowers and fruit placed before it.

Mai takes a soft mallet and taps a large bowl-shaped bell. A low hum fills the room and slowly fades. Her mother tells her the sound can help people stop, breathe and pay attention. Then they sit together in silence for a few minutes.

Arjun and Mai follow different traditions. Both traditions began long ago in India. Both have stories, teachers and practices. In this chapter you will learn how Hindus and Buddhists describe the world and how they try to live well in it.

Many Paths in Hinduism

11.1

One Tradition, Many Ways

Main ideaHinduism is a very old tradition from India with many ways to worship and many sacred texts.

Hinduism began in India more than 3,000 years ago. It has no single founder. It grew over many centuries from many teachers, songs and customs. Today about 1.2 billion people are Hindu. Most live in India and Nepal. Many also live in the United States, including Chicago.

Hindus have many sacred texts. The oldest are the , collections of hymns and teachings. Later texts include the Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita. Great story poems, like the Ramayana, are loved by families. Children often hear these stories from parents and grandparents.

Hindus do not all worship the same way. Some honor Vishnu most. Some honor Shiva. Some honor a goddess, called Devi. Many Hindus call their way of life , which means "the eternal way." Scholars say Hinduism is really a family of related paths.

11.2

Many Gods and One Spirit

Main ideaMany Hindus describe many gods and goddesses as faces of one great spirit, called Brahman.

Hindu temples hold many images. There is Ganesha, who many Hindus say removes obstacles. There is Lakshmi, linked to good fortune. There is Shiva, the dancer. Each image is called a . Hindus believe the deity can be present in the murti during worship.

Many Hindus also speak of . In the Upanishads, Brahman is the one great spirit behind everything. Many Hindus teach that the many gods are different faces of this one reality. It is a little like one person being a parent, a teacher and a friend.

Not every Hindu explains this the same way. Some Hindus focus on one god as supreme. Others focus on Brahman beyond any image. Hindu teachers have discussed these ideas for centuries. Students can describe these views without deciding which one is right.

11.3

Worship at Home and Temple

Main ideaPuja is Hindu worship with offerings, light and prayer, done at home and in temples.

Many Hindu homes have a small shrine. Each morning, a family member may do . Puja means worship. The person offers flowers, fruit, water or incense to the murti. They may ring a bell and wave a lamp in circles. They often say or sing a prayer.

Hindus also worship in a , or temple. A priest leads puja there. People bring offerings and receive a blessing. Many Hindus take off their shoes before going in. There are Hindu temples in the Chicago area, such as in Lemont and Aurora.

Water is sacred in Hindu tradition. The Ganges River in India is especially holy to Hindus. They call it Ganga Ma, or Mother Ganga. Millions of pilgrims bathe in it. Many Hindus believe its water washes away wrongdoing.

11.5

Karma: Actions Matter

Main ideaHindus teach karma, the idea that every action brings results.

Imagine planting a seed. A sunflower seed grows a sunflower, not a tomato. Many Hindus use this picture for . Karma means action. Hindus teach that good actions bring good results and harmful actions bring harmful results.

Hindus also speak of . Dharma is duty, the right way to live. A student’s dharma may be to study and respect teachers. A parent’s dharma may be to care for the family. Many Hindus believe that doing your dharma well creates good karma.

The Bhagavad Gita is a famous Hindu text. In it, the god Krishna teaches the warrior Arjuna. Krishna tells him to do his duty without worrying about rewards. Many Hindus read the Gita for advice about how to act well.

The Buddha and His Teaching

11.7

Prince Siddhartha

Main ideaBuddhists tell how Prince Siddhartha left his palace to find the answer to suffering.

Buddhists tell the story of Siddhartha Gautama. He was born a prince in South Asia, about 2,500 years ago. Scholars date his life to around 500 BCE. His father kept him inside a beautiful palace. The prince had everything he wanted.

One day Siddhartha rode outside the palace. The story says he saw an old man, a sick man and a dead body. He learned that everyone grows old, gets sick and dies. Then he saw a calm monk. Siddhartha decided to leave home and search for peace.

He studied with teachers and went without food for a long time. It did not bring him the answer. Then he sat under a tree to meditate. Buddhists believe he reached there. From then on, he was called the , which means "the awakened one."

11.8

The Four Noble Truths

Main ideaThe Buddha taught that craving causes suffering and that a path can end it.

After his enlightenment, the Buddha began to teach. His first teaching was about the . Buddhists often compare him to a doctor. A doctor names the illness, finds the cause, says it can be cured and gives medicine.

The first truth is that life includes suffering. The second is that suffering comes from craving, or wanting things too much. The third is that suffering can end. Buddhists call this ending . The fourth truth is that there is a path to follow.

Think of wanting a new toy so much that you feel upset. Buddhists say craving works like that, but bigger. It can make people unhappy even when they have a lot. The Buddha taught a middle way, not too much and not too little.

11.9

The Eightfold Path

Main ideaBuddhists follow the Eightfold Path of wise seeing, kind acting and careful minds.

The fourth Noble Truth is a path. Buddhists call it the . It has eight parts. It is often drawn as a wheel with eight spokes. The wheel reminds Buddhists that all eight parts work together.

Some parts are about wisdom, like seeing clearly. Some are about acting well. These include right speech, right action and right livelihood, or honest work. Other parts train the mind. These include effort, mindfulness and concentration.

Many Buddhists also keep five basic precepts, or promises. They promise not to kill, not to steal, not to lie, not to misuse the senses and not to take intoxicants. Monks and nuns keep many more rules.

Main ideaVesak honors the Buddha, and Hindus and Buddhists share some words but explain them differently.

is a major Buddhist holiday. It often falls in May, at a full moon. Many Buddhists remember the Buddha’s birth, enlightenment and death on this day. They visit temples, light lanterns and give gifts to the poor. In some places, people free caged birds.

Hindus and Buddhists both come from ancient India. They share some words, like karma, dharma and rebirth. But they use them in different ways. For example, many Hindus teach there is a lasting soul, the atman. The Buddha taught that there is no unchanging self.

Both traditions offer practices to live well. Hindus may do puja and follow their dharma. Buddhists may meditate and follow the Eightfold Path. Scholars compare these carefully. They describe each tradition in its own words.

The Sikh Tradition

12.1

Guru Nanak's Message

Main ideaSikhs follow the teachings of Guru Nanak, who taught one God and the equality of all people.

Sikhism began in the Punjab region of South Asia about 500 years ago. Its first teacher was , born in 1469. Sikhs tell that as a young man he went into a river and was gone for three days. When he came back, he said, "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim."

Sikhs understand this to mean that God sees all people as one family. Guru Nanak taught that there is one God. He taught that all people are equal, whatever their religion, caste or gender. A is a teacher. Sikhs honor ten human Gurus, from Guru Nanak to Guru Gobind Singh.

The word Sikh means learner or student. Sikhs try to remember God, work honestly and share with others. Today there are about 25 to 30 million Sikhs. Most live in India. Many live in Canada, Britain and the United States.

The Jain Tradition

12.4

Mahavira and Ahimsa

Main ideaJains follow Mahavira's teaching of ahimsa, doing no harm to any living thing.

Jainism is an ancient tradition from India. Jains honor 24 great teachers called . The most recent was . He lived in India about 2,500 years ago, around the same time as the Buddha. He left his home to live a very simple life.

The heart of Jain teaching is . Ahimsa means non-violence, doing no harm. Jains believe every living thing has a soul, even tiny insects. So they try hard not to hurt any being. They see ahimsa in actions, words and even thoughts.

There are about 4 to 5 million Jains today, by common estimates. Most live in India. Some live in the Chicago area. The Jain Society of Metropolitan Chicago has a temple in Bartlett.

Acaranga Sutra 1.2.3, a Jain scripture, in our own words (paraphrased)

12.5

Living Gently

Main ideaJains practice ahimsa in daily life through careful eating, speaking and working.

Jains show ahimsa in many small ways. Most Jains are vegetarian. Many also avoid root vegetables, like onions and potatoes. Pulling up roots can kill tiny living things in the soil, Jains explain. Many do not eat after sunset.

Jain monks and nuns are extra careful. Some carry a soft broom to gently sweep insects from their path. Some wear a cloth over their mouth. This keeps them from breathing in tiny insects. They walk from place to place instead of riding.

Jains keep other vows too. They try to tell the truth, not steal, and not own too much. Not owning too much is called aparigraha. Jains teach that holding things lightly helps the soul become free.

12.6

Compare: Ahimsa and Karma

Main ideaHindus, Buddhists and Jains all speak of karma and non-harm, but in their own ways.

Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism all began in India. All three use the word karma. Jains explain karma in a special way. They describe it as fine matter that sticks to the soul. Harmful acts add more. Kind acts and self-control help clear it away.

All three traditions honor ahimsa too. Many Hindus and Buddhists are vegetarian for this reason. Jains usually make ahimsa the most central rule of all. Mahatma Gandhi, a Hindu, learned from Jain ideas. He used non-violence to work for India’s freedom.

Scholars compare traditions by asking the same question of each. For example: What happens because of what we do? Each tradition answers with its own words and stories. Comparing does not mean ranking.

Think it through
Three Answers

Question: What happens because of what we do? A Hindu might say: our karma shapes our next life. A Buddhist might say: craving and actions keep the cycle of suffering going. A Jain might say: harmful actions bind karma to the soul.

How are these three answers alike? How is the Jain answer different?

12.7

Confucius and Respect

Main ideaConfucius taught respect for parents and teachers and kindness as the base of a good society.

lived in China about 2,500 years ago. His Chinese name is Kongzi, which means Master Kong. He was a teacher. He believed a peaceful society starts with good families and good leaders. After he died, his students wrote down his sayings in the .

Confucius taught . That means respect and care for parents and grandparents. He also taught respect for teachers and elders. He said leaders should be good examples, not bullies. Good manners and rituals help people live well together, he taught.

Confucius’s most important value is often called ren. Ren means kindness or humaneness toward others. Many families in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam still honor his ideas. Some call Confucianism a religion; others call it a way of life.

The Dao: The Way of Nature

Main ideaDaoists try to live in harmony with the Dao, the natural way of all things.

Daoism is an ancient tradition from China. Its best-known book is the . Tradition says a wise man named Laozi wrote it. Scholars are not sure who wrote it or when. It is short and full of poems about the Dao.

means the way. Daoists describe the Dao as the natural flow behind all things. They say it cannot be fully put into words. Daoists try to live simply and not force things. They learn from water, which is soft but can wear away rock.

The yin-yang symbol comes from Chinese thought. It is a circle of dark and light that fit together. and are opposites that need each other, like night and day. Many Daoists believe health and peace come from keeping them in balance.

12.9

Lunar New Year and Blended Ways

Main ideaLunar New Year and ancestor honoring show how many people in East Asia blend several traditions.

falls in late January or February. It follows the moon. Families clean the house to sweep out bad luck. They share big meals and give red envelopes with money to children. Each year is named for one of twelve animals, like the dragon or the rabbit.

During the new year, many families honor their . Ancestors are family members who lived long ago. People may set out food or burn incense before family pictures. This practice is linked to Confucian respect for family.

In China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam, many people follow more than one tradition. A family might honor ancestors in a Confucian way, visit a Buddhist temple and use Daoist ideas about balance. Many people there do not see this as a problem. Chicago’s Chinatown celebrates the new year with a parade.
