Capstone: Science and Community

Capstone

This capstone puts the whole course to work in two ways. First, you become the scientist. With a tape measure, a phone camera, a bubble level and a luggage scale, you measure your own speed, the slope of a path, the friction on a waxed and an unwaxed ledge, the hang time of an ollie and the energy you keep on a ramp. Each test uses the physics from earlier units: speed as distance over time, friction as μ times weight, h = g × t² ÷ 8 for a jump and v = √(2gh) for a drop.

Second, you become an advocate. Most public skateparks exist because a group of ordinary people asked for one, counted who would use it, answered their neighbors' worries and stood up at a city council meeting. You will follow that path from a first sketch to a written proposal, using history from Burnside, the Southbank undercroft and Skateistan, and design ideas that make room for beginners, girls and women, and wheelchair riders.

By the end you will be able to run a simple experiment safely, show every step of the arithmetic, report a result with its uncertainty, and write a proposal that backs each claim with evidence. The two halves meet in the middle: the slope you measure and the sound level you record become facts a council can trust.

The big question

How can careful measurement and steady community work turn skating into knowledge, and a wish into a public place?

Measure Your Own Skating

Science
Big questionHow can a phone, a tape measure and a notebook turn your own skating into numbers you can trust?
The story

A Parking Lot Becomes a Lab

On a quiet Sunday, a community class trades guesses for measurements.

The church parking lot is empty by two o'clock on Sunday, and the gate to the street is shut. Renee, who teaches eighth-grade science during the week, unloads a canvas bag from her trunk: a 30-meter tape measure, four orange cones, a roll of blue painter's tape, a bubble level and a spiral notebook. Five adults from her Saturday skate class arrive with boards, helmets and wrist guards.

Marcus, the youngest at 31, asks what they are measuring. Renee hands him the tape. 'You,' she says. 'Your speed, your ollie, your board on a waxed ledge. Today we stop saying fast and high, and start saying how fast and how high.' She has him stretch the tape across the lot and set two cones exactly ten meters apart.

Dolores sets her phone on a backpack well off to the side of the path and switches the camera to 60 frames per second. Marcus rolls through the cones at an easy pace, five times in a row. Between runs, nobody talks about style. They talk about frame numbers, and who wrote down run three.

By four o'clock the notebook holds a column of times, a slope measured with the bubble level and a borrowed one-meter board, the pull on a luggage scale dragging a weighted deck along a portable ledge, and the hang time of Marcus's best flat-ground ollie. Renee does the arithmetic out loud on the hood of her car, step by step, so everyone can check it.

The numbers mostly match the physics they predicted. Where they do not, the group argues happily about why: a crack in the asphalt, a slow thumb on a stopwatch, a wax job that wore off. Renee closes the notebook and says the best part of science is that next Sunday, they can test their arguments.

Talk about itWhat is one thing about your own skating, or a friend's, that you could measure with a tape and a phone, and what would you expect to find?
Section 1

Measuring Speed

35.1

Speed from Distance and Time

Main ideaAverage speed is the distance you cover divided by the time it takes, so two marked cones and a clock are enough to measure it.

On an empty, fenced parking lot, Marcus sets two cones exactly 10.0 meters apart with a tape measure. A friend films from the side while he rolls past both cones at an easy pace. The video shows that he took 2.5 seconds to go from the first cone to the second. Those two numbers are all you need to find his .

Average speed is distance divided by time. Here it is 10.0 m ÷ 2.5 s = 4.0 , written m/s. To compare with a car’s speedometer, multiply by 3.6 to get kilometers per hour: 4.0 × 3.6 = 14.4 km/h. In miles per hour, 4.0 m/s is about 9 mph, a brisk cruising pace on a skateboard.

The word average matters. Marcus may have sped up a little after a push or slowed near the second cone. The calculation gives one number that stands for the whole stretch. A shorter , the measured gap between two marks, gives a number closer to the speed at a single moment, but it is harder to time well.

Choose a test site with no moving cars, a smooth surface and plenty of room to stop past the second cone. Wear a helmet and wrist guards even for slow test runs, because a pebble can stop a wheel without warning. The person filming stands well to the side of the path, never in front of the rider.

35.2

Counting Video Frames

Main ideaCounting video frames gives a more exact time than a hand stopwatch, because each frame is a fixed slice of a second.

A stopwatch started by hand is often off by around a fifth of a second, because of , the short delay between seeing something and moving your thumb. Video avoids that delay. Most phones record at 30 or 60 , meaning 30 or 60 still pictures every second. Many free video apps let you step forward one frame at a time.

Here is the method. Step through the clip until the front axle lines up with the first cone, and write down that frame number, for example frame 112. Step on until the front axle reaches the second cone: frame 187. The board took 187 − 112 = 75 frames. At 30 frames per second, the time is 75 ÷ 30 = 2.5 seconds.

Each frame at 30 frames per second lasts 1 ÷ 30, or about 0.033 s. That is the of your clock, the smallest step it can show. Pick a frame one too early or one too late, and the 2.5 s run could read 2.47 s or 2.53 s. The speed would then shift from 4.0 m/s to about 4.05 or 3.95 m/s. Filming at 60 frames per second halves that step to about 0.017 s.

Use the same part of the board at both cones, such as the front axle, so the distance you measured matches the distance you timed. Check the camera settings before you film, and write the frame rate in your notebook next to the clip’s name. A frame rate you guessed wrong would make every time in the test wrong by the same factor.

35.3

Repeat Runs and Honest Error

Main ideaRepeating a test, reporting the mean and the range, and filming from the right spot make a measurement trustworthy.

Where you film from changes what you see. If the camera looks at a cone from an angle, the board can seem to reach the cone before it really has. This is called . To shrink it, draw a chalk line straight across the path at each cone and film from far back, so both lines are in view. The farther back the camera, the smaller the angle and the smaller the error.

One run is a story; five runs are data. Marcus rolls through the gate five times and gets 2.5, 2.4, 2.6, 2.5 and 2.5 seconds. The , or average, is the sum divided by the count: (2.5 + 2.4 + 2.6 + 2.5 + 2.5) ÷ 5 = 12.5 ÷ 5 = 2.5 s. The mean is a better estimate than any single run, because small timing errors in either direction tend to cancel.

The tells how spread out the runs are. His fastest run was 2.4 s and his slowest was 2.6 s, a spread of 0.2 s. A scientist reports both numbers: about 2.5 s, give or take 0.1 s. That gives a speed of 4.0 m/s, somewhere between about 3.8 and 4.2 m/s.

If one run is far off, such as 3.4 s because a wheel hit a crack, write it down and note what happened instead of quietly deleting it. Decide before the test what rule you will use for leaving out a run, and state that rule in your notes. Choosing the rule after you see the numbers makes it too easy to keep only the runs you like.

Section 2

Slopes and Friction

35.4

Finding the Slope of a Path

Main ideaA slope's grade is its rise divided by its run, and on a gentle slope gravity pulls along it with about g times the grade.

Before trusting a speed test on a gentle slope, measure the slope. Lay a straight board exactly 1.00 meter long on the path, lift its low end until a on top reads level, and measure the gap under the lifted end. If the gap is 0.05 m, the path climbs 0.05 m for every 1.00 m across. That is its .

Rise over run is the slope’s . Here 0.05 ÷ 1.00 = 0.05, or a 5% grade. A phone’s level app gives the angle instead: a 5% grade is about 2.9 degrees. Road signs in the United States often post steep grades as a percent, so the number matches what drivers already see.

Gravity pulls straight down, but only part of that pull acts along a slope. On a gentle slope that part is about g times the grade: 9.8 m/s² × 0.05 ≈ 0.49 m/s². With no friction, a rider starting from rest would gain about half a meter per second of speed every second. Friction and air take some of that back, which the next test measures.

Do slope tests only on paths closed to cars, such as a park path or an empty lot with a slight tilt. Pick a grade you can already stop on with a foot brake. A gentle grade gives plenty of data, while a steep hill adds speed faster than most riders can control. A street with traffic is never a test site.

35.5

The Coast-Down Test

Main ideaTiming a board's speed and measuring how far it coasts on level ground reveals how strongly rolling losses slow it.

On level ground, a rolling board slowly loses speed even when nobody brakes. The wheels squash a little where they meet the ground, the bearings rub, and air pushes back on the rider. The wheel and ground part of this loss is called . A measures all of these losses together.

Here is the method. Set up the 10 m gate so that its far cone is the start line, on smooth, level pavement. Push to a steady speed, stop pushing before the gate, and stand still on the board as it rolls through. Let it coast until it stops. Measure from the start line to the front wheels with the tape, and use the video to get your speed through the gate.

Suppose the board crosses the line at 4.0 m/s and coasts 40 m. If the slowing is about steady, the is a = v² ÷ (2d) = (4.0 × 4.0) ÷ (2 × 40) = 16 ÷ 80 = 0.20 m/s². Divide by g to compare surfaces: 0.20 ÷ 9.8 ≈ 0.02. That is an example; your own board, wheels and pavement will give their own number.

Repeat the test with different wheels or on rougher pavement, and compare. Soft wheels, around 78A on the durometer scale, roll over pebbles and cracks but squash more. Harder wheels, near 99A to 101A, usually coast farther on smooth concrete but feel every bump. Change only one thing between tests, so you know which change made the difference.

35.6

Waxed and Unwaxed Ledge

Main ideaPulling a loaded deck along a ledge with a luggage scale measures the coefficient of friction before and after waxing.

Skaters rub wax on a ledge’s edge so boards slide and grind more easily. You can test whether that works with a , the hand-held kind that hooks to a suitcase and reads its load. Use your own portable ledge or one at a park that allows wax. Wax on public benches and planters makes them slippery for other people and is often against local rules.

Lay an old deck on top of the ledge with its grip tape facing up, so the bottom of the deck touches the ledge as in a boardslide. Add a backpack of books until the deck and load have a mass of 5.0 kg. Hook the scale to a truck and pull slowly along the ledge. Read the scale while the deck slides at a steady pace, not at the first jerk.

Weight is mass times gravity: 5.0 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 49 N. Most luggage scales read in kilograms, so multiply the reading by 9.8 to get newtons. If the scale reads 2.5 kg on bare concrete, the pull is 2.5 × 9.8 = 24.5 N. The is μ = pull ÷ weight = 24.5 ÷ 49 = 0.50. After waxing, a reading of 1.2 kg gives 1.2 × 9.8 ≈ 11.8 N, and μ = 11.8 ÷ 49 ≈ 0.24. These are example readings.

The extra tug at the very start is , the grip between surfaces that are not yet moving. It is usually larger than the of surfaces already sliding. That is why a slide can feel sticky as it begins and smoother once it moves. Repeat each test three times, report the mean, and test again after twenty slides to see how quickly the wax wears off.

35.7

Predicting a Slide

Main ideaOn level ground a slide lasts a distance of v² ÷ (2μg), so lowering friction or adding speed makes it longer.

Once you know μ, you can predict how far a board slides before friction stops it. On level ground, kinetic friction slows a sliding board by μ times g. The is d = v² ÷ (2μg), where v is the speed when the slide begins. Notice that speed is : double the speed and the slide gets four times longer.

Take a slide that starts at 3.0 m/s on the bare ledge, with μ = 0.50. Then d = (3.0 × 3.0) ÷ (2 × 0.50 × 9.8) = 9.0 ÷ 9.8 ≈ 0.92 m. On fresh wax, with μ = 0.24, d = 9.0 ÷ (2 × 0.24 × 9.8) = 9.0 ÷ 4.70 ≈ 1.9 m. Cutting μ about in half roughly doubled the slide.

Test the prediction without riding. Use the loaded deck from the luggage-scale test, give it a firm shove along the ledge, and film it beside a tape measure. Count frames over its first 0.3 m to estimate its starting speed, then measure where it stops. Compare that length with d = v² ÷ (2μg).

Real slides rarely match perfectly. The deck may bounce, tilt or hit a rough patch, and μ changes as wax spreads and wears. A is a simple rule that stands in for the real thing. When your measured slide lands close to the prediction, the model is working; when it misses by a lot, something in the setup differs from what you assumed, and that is worth finding.

Hang Time and Height

35.8

Hang Time from Frames

Main ideaThe time a board spends in the air sets how high it rose, by the rule h = g × t² ÷ 8.

is how long the board is off the ground during an ollie. Film the trick from the side at 30 or 60 frames per second. Find the last frame where the tail still touches the ground, the moment of . Then find the first frame where any wheel lands. If take-off is frame 210 and landing is frame 225, that is 15 frames, or 15 ÷ 30 = 0.50 s.

Gravity alone sets that time. Once the board leaves the ground it is in : it rises, slows, stops at the top and falls back. When take-off and landing are at the same height, rising and falling take equal time. So the climb took half the hang time, 0.25 s. An object falling from rest drops a distance of ½ × g × t² in a time t.

Now the height: h = ½ × 9.8 × 0.25 × 0.25 = 4.9 × 0.0625 ≈ 0.31 m. A shortcut does both steps at once with the full hang time: h = g × t² ÷ 8 = 9.8 × 0.50 × 0.50 ÷ 8 = 2.45 ÷ 8 ≈ 0.31 m. A half-second ollie rises about 31 centimeters, close to the length of a school ruler held upright.

Film ollies on flat ground you know well, with your helmet buckled, before measuring anything else. The goal is a clean measurement, not a record. A comfortable ollie you land every time gives better data than a stretch you land once. Ask the person filming to stand to the side, square to the ollie spot, with the whole board in the frame from take-off to landing.

35.9

Checking the Height

Main ideaComparing the predicted height with marks on a wall tests the model, as long as you measure the same thing and know your frame error.

A prediction is only useful if you test it. Stick strips of painter’s tape on a wall or post behind the ollie spot, one every 10 centimeters from the ground up. In the clip, pause on the highest frame and read where the middle of the deck sits against the marks.

Measure the right thing. The formula gives how far the board’s middle rose, not how high the lowest wheel cleared. A board at rest already sits about 10 cm off the ground at its middle, so subtract that from the peak reading. If the middle of the deck reaches the 40 cm mark, the is about 40 − 10 = 30 cm, close to the 31 cm predicted from a 0.50 s hang time.

A single frame matters. At 30 frames per second, counting 14 frames instead of 15 gives 0.467 s and a height of about 0.27 m. Counting 16 gives 0.533 s and about 0.35 m. One frame either way shifts the answer by about 4 centimeters, and that spread is the of the method. Filming at 60 frames per second makes each frame half as long and cuts the uncertainty roughly in half.

When the two numbers disagree by more than the uncertainty, look for a cause. Landing on a raised surface, or with the board tilted so one truck touches early, changes the time between take-off and landing. Write the cause beside the result instead of forcing the numbers to agree.

Energy and the Write-Up

35.10

Energy on a Ramp

Main ideaHeight becomes speed on a ramp, and comparing the predicted speed with the measured one shows how much energy friction and air took.

A ramp trades height for speed. At the top a rider has , stored by height, equal to mgh. At the bottom that energy has become , the energy of motion, equal to ½mv². Set the two equal and the mass cancels, leaving v = √(2gh). A heavier rider and a lighter rider get the same predicted speed.

Take a rider who already drops in comfortably, starting from the of a mini ramp 1.2 m tall. (Riders still learning can roll down a small bank instead.) The predicted speed at the bottom is v = √(2 × 9.8 × 1.2) = √23.52 ≈ 4.85 m/s. To measure it, tape marks 1.0 m apart on the flat bottom and count frames. If the board crosses 1.0 m in 7 frames at 30 frames per second, that is 1.0 ÷ 0.233 ≈ 4.3 m/s.

The measured speed is lower than the prediction, as it nearly always is. To compare energies in , take a 70 kg rider. Potential energy at the top is 70 × 9.8 × 1.2 ≈ 823 J. Kinetic energy at the bottom is ½ × 70 × 4.3 × 4.3 = 35 × 18.49 ≈ 647 J. So 647 ÷ 823 ≈ 0.79, or about 79%, of the starting energy showed up as motion.

The missing 176 joules went into rolling resistance, bearing friction, air drag, the sound of the wheels, and the rider’s legs, which bend and soak up some energy. The rider’s body also does not drop the full 1.2 m, because the center of mass starts and ends above the board. Measuring the gap between prediction and result is how you learn which losses matter most.

Writing It Up Like a Scientist

Main ideaA clear report states the question, the hypothesis, the method, the results and a conclusion, so someone else can repeat the test.

A lab report lets someone else repeat your test and check your answer. Start with a clear question, such as: does wax lower the friction on my ledge? Then write a , a testable guess with a reason: wax will cut μ about in half, because it leaves a slick layer between the deck and the concrete. A hypothesis is useful even when it turns out wrong, because the test still teaches you something.

The lists every step and every measurement so a stranger could repeat it: the 5.0 kg load, the luggage scale, three pulls per surface, the ledge’s location and the date. Include a sketch with distances marked. Note anything that might matter, such as the temperature or whether the concrete was dusty.

Results come next: a table of every reading, the mean for each condition and the range. A simple bar chart helps a reader see the difference at a glance. Then the answers the question in one or two sentences and says how sure you are. For example: fresh wax lowered μ from about 0.50 to about 0.24, and the effect faded after twenty slides.

End with what you would change next time and the new question the test raised. Share the report with your crew or class, and invite them to repeat the test with their own boards. When other people get similar numbers, everyone can trust the result more.

Section 4

Making the Case

36.11

Parks Skaters Built Themselves

Main ideaBurnside, the Southbank undercroft and Skateistan show how skaters who build and care for a space can change how a city sees them.

In 1990, skaters in Portland, Oregon, began pouring concrete under the Burnside Bridge without permission. It was a park, short for do it yourself. They shaped banks and walls by hand, paid for supplies themselves and kept building. Over time the city accepted the park instead of tearing it out, and Burnside became one of the best-known skateparks in the world.

In London, skaters have ridden the beneath the Southbank Centre since the 1970s. When a redevelopment plan threatened to move them, the Long Live Southbank gathered wide public support, and an agreement in 2014 kept the space for skating. The campaign treated a skate spot as part of the city’s culture, worth saving like a theater or a gallery.

In 2007, Oliver Percovich founded Skateistan in Kabul, Afghanistan, starting with a few boards and children who gathered to ride. It grew into a skate school that paired skateboarding with classroom lessons, and it welcomed girls, who had few chances to play sport in public. It shows that a skate space can also be a place to learn.

These stories show a pattern useful to any proposal: skaters who show up, build trust and care for a space can change how a city sees them. They also show the limits. Building without permission carries real risks, so most towns now invite skaters into the official process, where the work is legal, inspected and built to last.

36.12

Writing the Proposal

Main ideaA strong proposal answers the council's questions with evidence: need, site, design, cost, safety and support.

A is the written case for the park. A strong one fits on a few pages and answers the questions council members will ask. Why is a park needed? Where will it go, and what will it look like? What will it cost, and who will pay? How will it be kept safe and clean?

makes the case. Count how many people skate in town, with a survey at schools and a sign-in sheet at a skate-a-thon. List where skaters ride now and the problems that causes, such as tickets or close calls with cars in parking lots. Your own measurements help too: the slope of a proposed site from a 1-meter level test, or a sound reading at the nearest home.

Use examples from towns like yours. Find a nearby park of similar size, then ask its parks department how many people use it, what upkeep costs each year and what they would change. Facts from a real neighbor carry more weight than general claims. Attach the design drawings, the budget table and each from parents, teachers, police or local businesses.

Write plainly. Lead with a one-paragraph summary, use headings and put numbers in tables. Thank the people who helped. When the proposal goes to the council, bring skaters of different ages to speak briefly, so members can see the whole community the park would serve.
