Rich and Poor, Then and Now

Class

Long ago, in many parts of the world, the family you were born into set your place in life. A king's child grew up to rule. A farmer's child grew up to farm. In this unit you will visit castles in England, samurai in Japan, scholars in China, and rulers of the Aztec and Inca.

Then you will come to today. You will learn words like income, wealth and opportunity. You will see why some people and countries have more than others. You will also meet the neighbors, libraries and food banks that help.

By the end, you will be able to explain how ranks by birth worked and how they changed. You will be able to name reasons people have more or less. And you will be able to share your own idea about what makes a chance fair, using facts.

The big question

How did being rich or poor work long ago, how does it work now, and what makes a fair chance?

The story

Agnes Carries the Water

It is a cold morning in England in the year 1300. Agnes has a job to do.

The sun is just coming up. Agnes is ten years old. She lives in a small village in England. The year is 1300. Her house has one room and a roof made of straw. Her family shares it with a pig and some hens.

Agnes picks up two wooden buckets. She walks down the muddy path to the stream. She fills the buckets. They are heavy. Water splashes on her feet. She starts the long walk home.

On the hill stands the lord's castle. It has thick stone walls and a tall tower. Flags wave from the top. Agnes has never been inside. The lord owns the land her family farms. Her father works his fields three days each week.

A rider in fine clothes trots past. It is the lord's son. He does not look at Agnes. She steps off the path and waits. One day he will own the castle. One day Agnes will carry water for her own children.

Agnes thinks about this as she walks. Who decided that he lives in a castle? Who decided that she carries water? No one asked either of them. They were both born into their places.

Talk about itAgnes and the lord's son were born into different lives. What do you think each of them would want to change?

Born Into a Place

9.1

What Is a Rank?

Main ideaLong ago, in many places, the family you were born into set your rank in life.

Think about Agnes and the lord’s son. They lived in the same village. But their lives were very different. He rode a horse and lived in a castle. She carried water and lived in a hut. The difference came from their families.

A is a person’s place in a group, from high to low. When people are sorted into ranks like steps on a ladder, we call it a . The top step had the most power and wealth. The bottom step had the least.

In many places long ago, you stayed on the step where you were born. A farmer’s child usually became a farmer. A noble’s child became a noble. This was true in Europe, Asia, Africa and the Americas. It began to change only slowly.

Kings and Queens

Main ideaA king or queen ruled a kingdom, and the crown usually passed to their child.

In 1272, a man named Edward became King of England. He did not win an election. His father had been king before him. When Edward died, his son became king next. That is how the crown moved in many kingdoms.

A is a king or queen who rules a country. A monarch often ruled for life. When the crown passes down to a child, we say the rule is . You get it from your family, the way you might get your grandmother’s ring.

Monarchs made laws, led armies and collected taxes. Many said God had given them the right to rule. But a monarch could not rule alone. They needed nobles, soldiers and farmers. That need gave other people some power too.

Ranks in Japan and China

9.5

Emperor, Shogun and Samurai

Main ideaIn Japan, samurai warriors served powerful lords, and people were sorted into ranks by birth.

Japan had an emperor. People honored him. But for hundreds of years, a military ruler called the held the real power. Under the shogun were great lords. Each lord had warriors who served him.

These warriors were the . Samurai trained with swords and bows. They were taught to be loyal, brave and calm. Many also studied poetry and writing. Being a samurai passed from father to son.

From about 1600 to the 1860s, Japan’s rulers kept samurai apart as the top rank. Official teaching listed farmers next, then craft workers, then merchants who bought and sold. Merchants were ranked low, yet some became very rich. Wealth and rank did not always match.

9.8

The Inca and Shared Work

Main ideaThe Inca ruler was at the top, and families paid their share by working for the state.

High in the Andes Mountains of South America, the Inca built a large empire. About 500 years ago, it ran along the mountains for thousands of miles. Its ruler was the . People treated him as holy.

The Inca did not use coins. Instead, families paid by working. This duty was called mit&#x27;a. Men took turns building roads, bridges and walls. Some served as soldiers. Some worked in mines.

In return, the state kept food in big storehouses. It could feed people in a bad year. Nobles and officials watched over the work. Most families farmed their own land too. Rank came mostly from birth, as in Europe and Japan.

Peasants Push Back

Main ideaAfter a terrible plague, workers were scarce, and English peasants demanded more freedom.

In 1348, a terrible sickness reached England. People called it the . It was a plague, a disease that spread fast. Fleas helped spread it. Across Europe, it killed about one third of the people, or maybe more.

After the plague, there were far fewer workers. Lords needed help in their fields. Some peasants asked for higher pay. Some moved to find better work. Lords and the king tried to stop them with new laws.

In 1381, thousands of English peasants marched to London. This was the Peasants&#x27; Revolt. They wanted an end to serfdom. The young king met them and made promises. Then he broke them. Still, over the next hundred years, serfdom in England slowly faded away.

Old Ranks Begin to Fade

Main ideaOver hundreds of years, many countries ended ranks set by birth and gave more people rights.

In 1215, English nobles made King John agree to a paper called . It said even the king had to follow the law. At first it helped mostly nobles. Later, people used its ideas to claim rights for everyone.

Change came in many places, often very slowly. Russia freed its serfs in 1861. Japan ended the samurai as a ruling rank in the 1870s. China stopped its old exams in 1905. Each change had its own story and its own struggles.

Today, most countries say people are under the law. Birth still affects people’s lives in many ways. But a farmer’s child can become a doctor, a teacher or a leader. People worked and argued for a long time to make that possible.

10.1

Income and Wealth

Main ideaIncome is money you get over time; wealth is everything you own, minus what you owe.

Ms. Park is a bus driver. Every two weeks she gets a paycheck. That paycheck is her . Income is money that comes in over time. It can come from a job, a business or other places.

Ms. Park also owns things. She has a small house, a car and some money in the bank. All that she owns, minus what she owes, is her . If she still owes money on her house, that part is not really hers yet.

Income and wealth are not the same. A person can earn a good income but own little. Another person can own a farm but have a small income this year. Both words help us understand who has more, and why.

Rich and Poor Countries

Main ideaCountries differ a lot in wealth, and the share of people in extreme poverty has fallen over time.

In some countries, most homes have running water and electricity. In others, many families carry water from a well. Marisol’s town in Honduras has a clinic open two days a week. Luis’s city has many hospitals.

Some people live in . The World Bank uses that term for living on only a couple of dollars a day. In 1990, more than one in three people in the world lived this way. By 2019, it was about one in ten.

Why did this happen? Many countries grew more food, built factories and sent more children to school. Trade between countries grew too. People still debate which causes mattered most. Hundreds of millions of people still live in extreme poverty.

10.10

Different Ideas About Helping

Main ideaPeople disagree about who should help most: families, charities, businesses or the government.

A town has a problem. Some families cannot afford food. The town meets to talk. One neighbor says the church and food pantry should help. Another says the government should run a program. A store owner offers to hire more workers.

People have different ideas about who should help. Some think programs, paid for by taxes, work best for big problems. They say help should reach everyone. Others think families, charities and businesses help best. They say local people know their neighbors’ needs.

Each view has supporters and critics. Most countries use a mix. In the United States, food pantries, churches, businesses and government programs all help. Adults debate the right mix at every election.
