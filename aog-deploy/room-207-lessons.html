How to Use the Closed-Loop Routing Tables

A one-page “Score → Intervention Crosswalk” opens every unit of Book 5 · 11–12.

1 · Screen. Run the Grace Compass check-in. It scores three domains — A (Regulation & Well-Being), B (Self-Compassion & Growth), C (Social Competency & Repair).

2 · Route to the lowest domain. A student’s lowest-scoring domain is where support is needed most. Find that domain on the unit’s crosswalk; the rows beneath it are this unit’s targeted lessons, novel scenes, and anchor charts.

3 · Run the Teacher Move. The last column gives a one-line, low-prep way to deliver the row — usually a scaffold so students fill content, not structure.

4 · Mind the red flag. A red ★ (elevated) or ★★ (highest-sensitivity) badge means safety steps apply: counselor co-facilitation, posted crisis resources, and — for ★★ — pre/post consultation. Never route a flagged row without them.

Doing well? If all three domains score 75+, use the ◆ enrichment row — an integration capstone — instead of a support row.

Important: deliver lessons in order. The crosswalk tells you where a student’s need is met within the unit — it is not a license to reorder the curriculum. The same routing logic runs the Grace Compass digital screener, so print and screen always agree.

UNIT 1 · LESSON 1Lesson 1

The Exit Interview

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
50–55 min · August Week 1 · First lesson of Book 5
OBJECTIVE

Students conduct a structured self-examination of who they have been in high school — what they have performed, what they have actually built, and what they are choosing to carry forward — and write the first line of who they are choosing to become.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness (primary) · Responsible Decision-Making (supporting)

DURATION

50–55 min · August Week 1 · First lesson of Book 5

KEY VOCABULARY

exit interview · transition identity · curated self · authentic self · carry-forward · leaving and arriving · threshold

OPENING REVIEW

None — first lesson of Book 5. Use the Year-Start Introduction Protocol for Book 5 before beginning.

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 11–12 · LATE HIGH SCHOOL

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1B.5a Implement a plan to build on a strength, meet a need, or address a challenge.

1A.5a Evaluate how expressing one’s emotions in different situations affects others.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

ANCHOR CHART

THE THRESHOLD — LEAVING AND ARRIVING THE THRESHOLD: The liminal space between who you have been and who you are becoming. You are standing in it right now — this year, this room, this version of yourself. WHAT YOU ARE LEAVING: The identity context in which you have built yourself. The people who know your history. The roles that have defined you. The version of yourself that was built in response to this particular environment. WHAT YOU ARE ARRIVING INTO: An environment where none of your prior relational architecture exists. Where you will introduce yourself to people who have no idea who you were. Where you get to choose, from scratch, what version of yourself shows up. THE ESSENTIAL QUESTION: Of everything you have been and built in high school — what do you choose to carry through the threshold? What do you choose to leave behind? POST: Before class. KEEP: All of Unit 1.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
OPENING RITUAL

90 seconds · Year-Start Version 'Both feet on the floor. One breath slower than usual.' [Pause 15 seconds.] [For students with prior AoG experience]: 'You have done this before. Notice how it feels different when you know where you are in the year — and where you are in your life.' [For students without prior AoG experience]: 'This is how this class begins. Every time. It is a choice to be present — not to a screen, not to the period after this one, but to what is actually happening right now. In your life. Which is significant.' 'This is the beginning of your last year of high school. Whatever that means to you — relief, grief, excitement, terror, some combination of all of them — it is real. This class will not ask you to manage those feelings. It will ask you to think honestly about who you are choosing to become.' [Post: 'Of everything you have been and built in high school — what do you choose to carry forward?']

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · follow the steps · read aloud or in your own words

01THE EXIT INTERVIEW — PART 1: WHO HAVE YOU BEEN?10 MIN

'An exit interview is a structured conversation held when someone is leaving an organization. The purpose: to understand honestly what their experience was, what they contributed, and what they are taking with them. Today you are conducting an exit interview with yourself — about your high school experience.' [Pause.] 'Not the version for the college essay. Not the version your parents would read. The honest version.' Students open journals — 8 minutes of private writing: 'Three roles I have played in high school — not jobs or positions, but identity roles. Who I have been to different groups of people. Two things I am genuinely proud of having built here — not accomplishments, but qualities or capacities. One thing about the identity I have carried in high school that I am not taking with me.'

02THE EXIT INTERVIEW — PART 2: WHO ARE YOU ARRIVING AS?10 MIN

'In the environment you are moving toward — college, work, a new city, a new community — none of the people you meet will know your history. They will not know who you were in 9th grade. They will not know the story of how you became who you are. They will only know who shows up.' [Pause.] 'That is not a loss. It is an opportunity — and a responsibility. You get to choose what version of yourself arrives.' Students write privately: 'The version of me that I am choosing to send into the next chapter — what are their three most essential qualities? Not the ones I perform. The ones that are actually mine.' Then: 'The first line of who I am choosing to become: ___.' Private. Never shared.

03THE CONTINUITY QUESTION — WHAT TRAVELS THROUGH THE THRESHOLD?10 MIN

'Here is the question nobody asks in the college application or the graduation ceremony: of everything you have built in yourself — the values, the relationships, the understanding, the capacity for grace — what travels through the threshold with you? And how does it travel when the context that held it is gone?' Write on board: CONTINUITY QUESTION: 'What are you carrying through the threshold — and what holds it in place when the context changes?' 'Your integrity anchor from Book 4 does not disappear when you leave this school. But the environment that kept it visible — the community that knew you as someone who lived by it — is gone. What holds it in place when nobody is watching and nobody knows your history?' Students write: 'The thing about who I am that is most at risk of being lost in the transition is ___. What holds it in place is ___.' Private.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During the journal writing in Step 1, a student who writes intensely and at length about a role they have been playing that they clearly did not choose — a role assigned by their family, their community, or their circumstances.

MAY SIGNAL This student has been inhabiting an identity shaped more by others' expectations than by genuine self-authorship. The threshold of departure may be simultaneously terrifying and liberating for them — they are losing the context that defined them, but also the context that constrained them.

ACTION Do not approach during the lesson. After class: 'I noticed today felt significant. The roles we've been playing — sometimes we don't get to choose them. I'd like to talk more about what you're thinking about carrying forward — and what you're ready to leave behind.'

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During Step 3, a student who writes that nothing about who they are is at risk of being lost — who seems entirely confident that the transition poses no identity challenge.

MAY SIGNAL Total confidence about identity continuity at the threshold of departure is unusual at 11–12. This student may have done deep prior work on identity stability — or may be protecting themselves from the vulnerability of naming what is actually precarious.

ACTION Do not challenge publicly. After class, if the student seems disengaged from the depth of the material: 'The transition question — does it feel like a non-issue for you? I'm curious what makes it feel settled.' Ask with genuine curiosity, not skepticism.

GRADE-BAND DIFFERENTIATION
GRADES 9 · 10

GRADES 11 · 12

In B4-U1, students examined the gap between their curated and authentic selves. The threshold question was about individual moments and relationships. The stakes were interpersonal.

The threshold question is existential. The transition is from a defined identity context to an undefined one. The stakes are not interpersonal — they are about who you fundamentally are when the environment that shaped you is no longer present. This is a qualitatively different kind of question.

EXIT TICKET

"Three roles I have played in high school are ___, ___, and ___. The version of me I am choosing to send into the next chapter has these three qualities: ___, ___, and ___. The thing about who I am that is most at risk of being lost in the transition is ___. What holds it in place is ___." Journal — private. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

First lesson of Book 5 — the tone you set here defines the register for the entire Capstone year. These students are near-adults facing one of the most significant transitions of their lives. The Exit Interview must feel like a conversation between people who are taking the question seriously — not like a worksheet. Hold the room with genuine weight and genuine warmth simultaneously. The Continuity Question (Step 3) is the most important conceptual move of the year's opening — plant it here, return to it throughout Book 5. Students who cannot identify anything at risk in the transition are worth noting — genuine identity stability is rare at 11–12, and what presents as confidence is often either deep prior work or defended vulnerability. Know which.

FAMILY CONNECTION · CONVERSATION STARTER

Today students began Book 5: The Capstone — the final year of Architecture of Grace. The first lesson asked them to conduct an Exit Interview with themselves: Who have I been in high school? Who am I choosing to become? Of everything I have built — what do I choose to carry through the transition ahead? This is the essential question of senior year — and it is one of the most important questions any person asks at any threshold in their life. Conversation-starter: 'Is there a version of you in high school that you're ready to leave behind? And is there something you've built here that you want to make sure travels with you?'

SCENARIO CARDS

Print and laminate before unit. Two cards per lesson.

B5-1A THE EXIT INTERVIEW

THE SITUATION

Sofia is a semester away from graduation. Her college essay identified herself primarily by her academic achievements and extracurricular leadership. Now, sitting with the Exit Interview, she is being asked: 'Who were you becoming here — beneath the record?' She stares at the question. She has a very good answer about her record. She does not know how to answer about herself.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What is the Exit Interview asking that Sofia's application did not ask — and why is that harder to answer? Sofia has a clear answer about her record and almost no answer about herself. What has she been building — and what has she been neglecting? The Exit Interview asks you to name what you are releasing, carrying forward, and choosing to build. What is the purpose of each of those three things? Begin your own Exit Interview: what identity are you releasing as you leave this building — and why?

THE EXIT INTERVIEW The Exit Interview is not a performance review. It is an invitation to name what actually grew in you during high school — beneath the record, beneath the title, beneath the accomplishment. Many students can articulate their transcript. Fewer can articulate their character development. The Exit Interview asks for the second thing.

B5-1B THE EXIT INTERVIEW

THE SITUATION

When Darius fills out his Exit Interview, the hardest section is 'what I am releasing.' He has been the class clown since sixth grade. It protected him from taking things seriously enough to be disappointed by them. He has used it to manage anxiety, deflect from vulnerability, and stay likable across social groups. 'I don't think that's who I want to be anymore,' he writes. 'But I don't know who I am without it.'

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What function has the 'class clown' identity served for Darius — and what has it cost him? Darius says 'I don't know who I am without it.' What does that feel like — and what does it make possible? Releasing an identity is not the same as erasing it. What would it mean for Darius to carry the humor forward without being defined by it? What identity have you been performing in high school that you want to release or revise as you move forward?

RELEASING AN IDENTITY Some identities we adopt in high school serve a genuine protective function — they help us survive environments that feel unsafe, manage anxiety, or maintain social standing. The work of the Exit Interview is not to condemn those identities. It is to ask: does this still serve me? Is this who I want to carry forward? And if not — who is underneath it?

Mapping Your Values

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
50–55 min · August Week 2
OBJECTIVE

Students distinguish values they have examined and genuinely claimed from values they have inherited and absorbed without examination, and produce a values map that is honest rather than aspirational.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness (primary) · Responsible Decision-Making (primary)

DURATION

50–55 min · August Week 2

KEY VOCABULARY

examined values · absorbed values · values map · values hierarchy · conflict between values · values under pressure · genuine vs. inherited

OPENING REVIEW

1–2 min: 'Last lesson: the Exit Interview — who are you choosing to become? Today: what are you actually choosing to become — and where did those choices come from?'

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 11–12 · LATE HIGH SCHOOL

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1B.5a Implement a plan to build on a strength, meet a need, or address a challenge.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3A.5a Apply ethical reasoning to evaluate societal practices.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

ANCHOR CHART

EXAMINED vs. ABSORBED VALUES ABSORBED VALUES: Values you hold because you were raised to hold them. They were never tested. They arrived before you had a choice about them. Not necessarily wrong — but not yet genuinely yours. EXAMINED VALUES: Values you have held up to the light. You have asked: do I actually believe this? Does it hold when it costs me something? Does it survive contact with evidence, with experience, with competing values? These are genuinely yours — not because you chose them from scratch, but because you chose to keep them after examination. THE VALUES HIERARCHY: When two values conflict — which wins? That hierarchy reveals what you actually value, regardless of what you say you value. THE EXAMINATION QUESTION: 'Have I actually tested this — or have I just never been asked to?' POST: Before class. KEEP: Unit 1.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
OPENING RITUAL

60–90 seconds 'Both feet on the floor. One breath slower than usual.' [Pause 10 seconds.] 'Last lesson: who are you choosing to become? Today: where did the person you are choosing to become actually get their values? Were they chosen — or did they arrive? Today we look at that distinction. It is one of the most important questions of this year.' [Post: 'Do you actually believe what you say you believe — or have you just never been asked to?']

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · follow the steps · read aloud or in your own words

01EXAMINED vs. ABSORBED — THE DISTINCTION8 MIN

'Most of the values you hold right now arrived before you had any real choice about them. Your family, your community, your culture, your religious tradition, your peer group — all of them deposited values into you before you could evaluate them.' [Pause.] 'That is not a problem. It is how humans work. The question is not whether your values were absorbed — all values are initially absorbed. The question is whether you have examined them.' Write on board: ABSORBED = arrived before you could evaluate them. EXAMINED = you have held them up and decided to keep them, or not. 'The value that has been absorbed but never examined is the one most likely to collapse under real pressure — because you have never consciously decided to hold it. The value that has been examined is the one that holds when it costs you something specific.'

02THE VALUES MAPPING EXERCISE10 MIN

'Open your journal. You are going to produce a values map — not the impressive version, the honest one.' Walk through the mapping structure: 'First: list 10 values you hold or believe you hold. Do not curate this list — write quickly, don't evaluate yet. Second: for each value, mark it E (examined) or A (absorbed). Be honest — if you have never really tested it, it is A. Third: look at your hierarchy. When two values conflict, which wins? That hierarchy is your actual values map — not the stated one, the revealed one. Fourth: identify one absorbed value that you are ready to examine — one you have been carrying without ever choosing it.' Students work privately for 8 minutes. No sharing. No performance.

03THE EXAMINATION QUESTION — IN PRACTICE10 MIN

'The Examination Question is this: Have I actually tested this — or have I just never been asked to?' Write it on the board. 'A value that has never been tested is not yet genuinely yours. It might become yours when tested — but until then, it is inherited furniture.' [Pause.] 'Here is the specific adult relevance of this: in the next year, you will enter environments that hold different values from the ones you grew up with. Different religious orientations, different political communities, different cultural norms, different definitions of success and goodness and what a life should look like. Some of your absorbed values will survive that encounter. Some will not. The ones that survive examination are the ones worth building your adult life around.' Pairs: 'Share one absorbed value you named — and the examination question you would need to apply to it. What would it mean to genuinely test this value?' No sharing with the class — pairs only.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During the values mapping in Step 2, a student who marks every single value as Examined — who believes all their values have been fully tested and genuinely claimed.

MAY SIGNAL At 17–18, full examination of all held values is almost always an overestimate of prior interior work, or a performance of sophistication rather than genuine reflection. The student who has done the most examination is usually the one most willing to acknowledge how much remains unexamined.

ACTION Do not challenge publicly. In pairs (Step 3), push the examination question more directly: 'What would it actually mean to test this — not just think about it, but put it in a situation where holding it cost you something specific?'

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During Step 3, a student who identifies a core religious or community value as an absorbed value they have never examined — and appears troubled by naming it.

MAY SIGNAL This student may be in the early stages of a genuine values examination that has significant stakes within their family or community. The Capstone register asks for honest examination — and for some students, that examination carries real relational risk.

ACTION After class: 'The value you named today — I noticed that felt significant. Examining inherited values is one of the most important things you can do in this year. It is also one of the hardest, especially when the stakes are real. I'm available to think through it with you.' Do not press. Open the door.

GRADE-BAND DIFFERENTIATION
GRADES 9 · 10

GRADES 11 · 12

In B4-U1-L3, students identified integrity anchors — non-negotiable values that hold under pressure. The focus was identifying and naming what they already held. The examination question was present but not primary.

The Capstone goes further — not just naming what you hold, but asking where it came from and whether you have consciously chosen it. The adult threshold demands this: in the next environment, you will need to know which values are genuinely yours and which were borrowed. You cannot build an adult life on borrowed furniture.

EXIT TICKET

"One value I have genuinely examined and consciously claimed is ___. One value I have been absorbing without examination is ___. The Examination Question I need to apply to it is ___. What it would cost me to genuinely test this value is ___." Journal — private. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

The values mapping exercise is deceptively simple and genuinely difficult. The most important facilitation move: resist affirming the aspirational map. When a student says 'I value integrity' — ask what it cost them last week. The values hierarchy under pressure is the honest one. Students examining inherited religious or community values may be doing so for the first time in a structured context — the emotional stakes can be real. Hold the examination question without requiring a particular answer. The examination is valuable regardless of its outcome — a value that survives examination is more genuinely held; a value that doesn't survive was never really held. Both outcomes are honest.

The Examination Question they are working with this unit: 'Have I actually tested this — or have I just never been asked to?' You can support this at home by sharing your own values examination: 'I was raised to believe ___, and I've come to think ___ about that now.' Conversation-starter: 'Is there something you were raised to believe that you're still working out whether you actually believe it yourself?'

B5-2A RADICAL AUTHENTICITY

THE SITUATION

When Layla got into college, she posted a carefully worded, emotionally measured celebration on social media. Then she went to her room and cried with relief for twenty minutes. The post was real — she was grateful. But it was also curated — she didn't show the tears, the fear that had preceded the acceptance, or the complicated feelings about leaving. 'I showed them the best version of my real reaction,' she said. 'Is that still authentic?'

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

Layla showed a real reaction — just not all of it. Where is the line between appropriate curation and inauthenticity? Layla's question — 'is that still authentic?' — is genuinely complicated. What criteria would you use to answer it? What would radical authenticity have looked like for Layla in this moment — and would it have been better? As you approach your own significant transitions, what is the difference between sharing genuinely and sharing everything?

THE AUTHENTICITY SPECTRUM Radical authenticity is not the requirement to share everything with everyone. It is the commitment to showing up honestly in ways that are consistent with who you actually are. The spectrum runs from total mask to radical transparency. The question is not 'did I share enough' but 'was what I shared real?' Layla's post was real. Whether it was adequate to the moment is a separate question — one she is right to ask.

B5-2B RADICAL AUTHENTICITY

THE SITUATION

Theo is discovering that he performs differently for each of the audiences in his life: more earnest with his parents, more ironic with his friends, more serious in academic settings, more relaxed with his girlfriend. Each version feels like him. None of them is all of him. 'I'm not being fake with anyone,' he says. 'But I'm not being fully real with anyone either.'

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

Is Theo's experience of performing differently across contexts inauthenticity — or sophistication? Theo says 'I'm not being fake but I'm not being fully real.' What is the difference between those two? Audience collapse — the collapse of all these separate performances into one unified self — is described as both terrifying and liberating. Why both? What would it mean for Theo to find one person with whom he is his full self — and what would that require?

Values Under Fire

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
50–55 min · September Week 1

★ PPRA — COUNSELOR AWARENESS

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 11–12 · LATE HIGH SCHOOL

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3B.5b Evaluate how responsible decision making affects interpersonal and work group relationships.

3A.5a Apply ethical reasoning to evaluate societal practices.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

OBJECTIVE

Students examine a situation in which their stated values and their actual choices diverged — applying the Examination Question to their own behavior rather than their beliefs — and articulate what the divergence revealed about their genuine values hierarchy.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness (primary) · Responsible Decision-Making (primary) · Self-Management (supporting)

DURATION

50–55 min · September Week 1

KEY VOCABULARY

values-behavior gap · divergence · values under pressure · the honest map · integrity · rationalization · the real test

OPENING REVIEW

'Last lesson: examined vs. absorbed values — what is the Examination Question?' [Answer.] 'Today: what happens when values meet real pressure — when what you said you believed and what you actually chose diverged? Today: the honest map.'

★ PPRA — COUNSELOR AWARENESS REQUIRED

★ PPRA-designated. Students examine situations where their stated values and actual behavior diverged — potential for disclosure of personal ethical failures, moral conflicts, and significant shame material. Standard opt-out protocols apply. Brief counselor before delivery. Counselor on standby and reachable same day. This lesson carries the highest personal disclosure risk of Unit 1. All journal work is private — never ask students to share their specific divergence situation with the class.

ANCHOR CHART

THE VALUES-BEHAVIOR GAP STATED VALUES: What you say you believe. What appears on the values map. ACTUAL VALUES: What your behavior reveals when the situation is real and the cost is immediate. THE VALUES-BEHAVIOR GAP: The distance between them. Everyone has one. The question is not whether the gap exists — it does. The question is: what does your gap reveal about what you actually value? RATIONALIZATION: The story you tell yourself about why the gap was justified. 'I would have lived by my value but the circumstances were exceptional.' Rationalization is not always dishonest — sometimes the circumstances genuinely were exceptional. The test: would the same circumstances justify the gap next time? THE HONEST MAP: The values map produced by examining your behavior, not your beliefs. The most important self-knowledge available at this age. POST: Before class. KEEP: Unit 1.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
OPENING RITUAL

90 seconds 'Both feet on the floor. One breath slower than usual.' [Pause 15 seconds.] 'Last lesson: we mapped our values — the examined ones, the absorbed ones. Today we look at something harder. Not what you say you value — what you actually chose when the situation was real and the cost was immediate. Those two things are not always the same.' [Post: 'What did your actual choices reveal about what you really value?']

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · follow the steps · read aloud or in your own words

01THE VALUES-BEHAVIOR GAP — NAMING IT HONESTLY8 MIN

01THE DIFFERENCE FROM THE INTEGRITY ANCHOR8 MIN

'In Book 4, you named your integrity anchors — the specific values that hold under social pressure. The Moral North Star is different. It is not a value you hold — it is the principle that makes your values coherent. The WHY behind the WHAT.' Write on board: INTEGRITY ANCHOR = what you hold. MORAL NORTH STAR = why it matters / what orients everything else. 'For some people, the north star is something like: human dignity is non-negotiable. Everything else — honesty, justice, compassion, accountability — flows from that. For others, it is a religious conviction, a philosophical commitment, a relational principle that has been tested and held under genuine pressure.' [Pause.] 'The north star is not the same as a belief system. You can share a belief system with many people and have a different north star from each of them — because the principle that does the orienting is genuinely personal.'

02FINDING YOUR NORTH STAR — THE EXAMINATION10 MIN

'Here is how you find it: look at every significant moral choice you have made under real pressure — the ones you are proud of, and the ones you are not. What is the common thread? What is the principle that was present in the choices you are proud of — and absent or overridden in the choices you are not?' [Pause.] Students write privately for 8 minutes: 'Looking at my moral history — the choices I am proud of and the choices I am not — the common thread in the choices I am proud of is ___. The principle that seems to be present when I am most genuinely myself is ___. The principle that gets overridden when I fail is ___. Based on this honest examination, my Moral North Star — the guiding principle that stays when everything else is uncertain — is ___.' Private. Journal only. This is significant.

03WHAT IT COSTS AND WHAT IT GIVES10 MIN

'The north star has a cost. Orienting by it — actually allowing it to determine your direction — produces friction with people and institutions whose north star is different.' Write on board: NORTH STAR COST = the friction produced by genuine orientation. 'That cost is not evidence that you have the wrong north star. It is evidence that it is genuinely yours — a principle you have examined and chosen, rather than one that produces no friction because it makes no real demands.' [Pause.] 'But the north star also gives something: stability. When you are lost — and in the next year, you will be lost, in ways you cannot predict — you can find north. Not because you have a map, but because you know which direction to face.' Students write: 'The cost of my Moral North Star in the environments I am entering is ___. What it gives me — the stability it produces — is ___.' Private.

B5-6A THE MORAL NORTH STAR

THE SITUATION

In college, Fatima is offered an internship at a company whose practices she has serious concerns about — the hiring process, the environmental record, the way junior employees are treated. The salary is three times what any comparable internship offers. Her advisor says: 'This is a significant opportunity.' Her Integrity Anchor says something different. She turns it down. She gets a worse internship and a better year.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What is Fatima's Moral North Star telling her — and what is the financial pressure telling her? Fatima's advisor is not wrong about the opportunity. How do you hold that alongside the clarity of the North Star? She turns it down and 'gets a better year.' What does that mean — and how do you measure a 'better year'? Design your own Moral North Star statement: the value you will never trade for success, approval, or belonging.

THE MORAL NORTH STAR A Moral North Star is a non-negotiable — a value so central that trading it produces a person you do not want to be, regardless of what the trade provides. Identifying it clearly in advance is what makes it possible to hold the line when the pressure is real. Fatima's clarity made the decision possible. The 'better year' she describes is the evidence that the North Star pointed somewhere real.

B5-6B THE MORAL NORTH STAR

THE SITUATION

Theo has always been able to identify what he believes — and has almost never been able to hold it under social pressure. He knows his North Star: he will not participate in public cruelty, even when it is directed at someone he doesn't particularly like. He knows it. He has failed it three times this year. Each time he participated in the pile-on, he felt it cost him something he had to work to get back.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What does it mean to know your North Star and still fail to hold it? Theo says each failure 'cost him something he had to work to get back.' What did it cost — and what did the recovery require? Knowing a North Star is not the same as holding it. What builds the capacity to hold it under pressure? What is one situation in the coming year where Theo's North Star is likely to be tested — and what can he build now to hold it then?

HOLDING THE LINE Identifying a Moral North Star is the first step. Holding it when the cost is real and immediate is the second — and much harder — one. The capacity to hold the line builds over time: through the small decisions that align with the North Star even when they are inconvenient, and through the recovery work after the moments when they don't. The North Star does not make you immune to failure. It makes failure legible — and recovery possible.
