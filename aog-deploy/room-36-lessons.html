BEFORE YOU TEACH

How to Use the Closed-Loop Routing Tables

A one-page “Score → Intervention Crosswalk” opens every unit of Book 3 · 6–8.

1 · Screen. Run the Grace Compass check-in. It scores three domains — A (Regulation & Well-Being), B (Self-Compassion & Growth), C (Social Competency & Repair).

2 · Route to the lowest domain. A student’s lowest-scoring domain is where support is needed most. Find that domain on the unit’s crosswalk; the rows beneath it are this unit’s targeted lessons, novel scenes, and anchor charts.

3 · Run the Teacher Move. The last column gives a one-line, low-prep way to deliver the row — usually a scaffold so students fill content, not structure.

4 · Mind the red flag. A red ★ (elevated) or ★★ (highest-sensitivity) badge means safety steps apply: counselor co-facilitation, posted crisis resources, and — for ★★ — pre/post consultation. Never route a flagged row without them.

Doing well? If all three domains score 75+, use the ◆ enrichment row — an integration capstone — instead of a support row.

Important: deliver lessons in order. The crosswalk tells you where a student’s need is met within the unit — it is not a license to reorder the curriculum. The same routing logic runs the Grace Compass digital screener, so print and screen always agree.

UNIT 1 · LESSON 1Lesson 1

The Mask vs. The Mirror

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →This lesson’s worksheet is interactive — open it: type in it, turn it in, Print my copy / Print it blank →
35–40 min · September Week 1
OBJECTIVE

Students distinguish between the persona they project socially (Mask) and their authentic self (Mirror), and identify at least two gaps using the Identity Audit.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness · accurate self-perception and identity formation

SCENARIO CARDS

Card 1·A (Amara — New School, New Mask) · Card 1·B (Jordan — The Class Clown)

QUICK REVIEW

None — this is Lesson 1. Begin with the opening ritual: "Put both feet on the floor. Take one breath that is slower than usual." Ninety seconds. You will use this ritual all year.

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1B.3a Analyze how personal qualities influence choices and successes.

1A.3a Analyze factors that create stress or that motivate successful performance.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

SAFETY CHECK

Before class: Confirm crisis resource numbers (988 Lifeline · Crisis Text Line: HOME to 741741) are posted visibly. Know your school counselor's name and same-day contact method. You will use these throughout the year.

This lesson is not high-disclosure-risk — but the Identity Audit occasionally surfaces something significant for students carrying heavy self-perception burdens.

ANCHOR CHART · MASK VS. MIRROR — TWO-COLUMN DISPLAY

Post this before class. Keep visible all year.

LEFT COLUMN — THE MASK: What I show others. What I perform. What I hide. What I emphasize.

RIGHT COLUMN — THE MIRROR: What is actually there when the performance stops. My real opinions. My actual struggles. What I value.

Grade 6: Label each column with a simple icon — a theatrical mask and a reflective surface. Grades 7–8: Add below the columns: "The gap between these two is where the cost lives."

OPENING · 3–4 MINUTES
"Think about the last time you walked into a social situation and consciously thought about how you were coming across. What were you managing? What were you hiding? That process of management is what we are going to examine today."

Pause 10 seconds. Let the question land. Students write in journals for 60 seconds before any class discussion.

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 4 · read aloud or in your own words

01Introduce the Mask5 MIN

"Everyone wears a Mask — a social performance. The Mask is not always dishonest. Often it is sensible. The problem is not the Mask. The problem is when the Mask becomes so habitual that we lose contact with what is underneath."

Point to the Mask column. Ask: "What kinds of things go in this column?" Take 2–3 answers.

02Introduce the Mirror5 MIN

"The Mirror is what is actually there when the performance stops — your real opinions, your actual struggles, the things you find funny when you are not worried about whether it is the right thing to find funny. The Mirror is not more virtuous than the Mask. It is just more real."

Point to the Mirror column. Ask: "What kinds of things live in the Mirror column?" Take 2–3 answers.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE Students who go very still during the Mirror introduction, or who look away from the anchor chart.

MAY SIGNAL A student who cannot tolerate even hearing the Mirror column described may have very high identity fusion with a curated self — or significant fear of what they would find underneath.

ACTION Note privately. Do not address publicly. Monitor through Unit 2.

WORKSHEET

Identity Audit WS1 · U1·L1 · Primary Activity — Private

Two-column private reflection. Left: Who I am when performing. Right: Who I am when not performing. Students work independently and silently. Never collected. Never shared unless student chooses.

03Identity Audit · Private10 MIN

"Left column: who I am when I am performing — what I show, what I hide, what I emphasize. Right column: who I am when I am not performing — what I actually think, feel, value, and struggle with. Work in complete silence. This is private."

Set a visible 10-minute timer. Circulate — do not read over shoulders.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student who stares at the blank worksheet for more than 2 minutes, or who writes a single word and stops.

MAY SIGNAL May indicate significant difficulty accessing the Mirror column — either because it feels unsafe or because the gap has been suppressed for a long time.

ACTION Move quietly toward them after 2–3 minutes. Say low: "You can label the columns with one word each if that helps." Do not linger. Note for Unit 2 watch list.

04Discussion — The Gap8 MIN

"Without revealing the contents of your columns — where is the gap between your two sides biggest?" Take 3–4 responses. Redirect to the anchor chart as each lands.

"What does maintaining that gap cost you — in energy, in authenticity, in connection?" Take 2–3 responses.

"What would it take to reduce the gap by even 10%? Not radical transparency — just one small step toward being slightly more real in a safe context." Let this sit without requiring answers aloud.

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD 1·A Amara — New School, New Mask

Transfers schools, constructs a careful Mask. "I don't know which one is me anymore." Use if time allows — pairs only. Four discussion questions.

CARD 1·B Jordan — The Class Clown

Funniest person in every room, reads philosophy books in private. For Grades 7–8 or faster classes. Introduces identity-foreclosure concept. Use optional — do not cut Step 3 or Step 4 to accommodate.

TEACHING TIP

Grade 6: Scaffold the Identity Audit with concrete examples before students begin — sports tryouts, lunch table choices, what you post vs. what you feel.

Grades 7–8: Introduce "identity foreclosure" — committing to a persona so early that you stop questioning it. Ask: "When did you decide who you were going to be, and was that decision really yours?"

EXIT TICKET

"The version of me that almost no one sees is ___. Why does that version stay hidden?"

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Which students seemed most activated — with relief (as if they have been waiting for permission to name the gap) or with resistance (dismissing the concept)? Students who heavily resist may have very high identity fusion with their curated self. Note for Unit 2.

Which students completed both columns quickly and easily? Those students may have unusually high self-awareness — or may have been performing the reflection rather than doing it.

The Anatomy of a Mistake

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
35–40 min · September Week 2
PPRA — COUNSELOR AWARENESS
OBJECTIVE

Students distinguish shame ("I am a bad person") from guilt ("I made a choice that did not fit my integrity"), and explain why guilt is more useful than shame for growth.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness: shame vs. guilt distinction · Self-Management: reframing self-critical thoughts

SCENARIO CARDS

Card 1·C (Maya — "I am stupid") · Card 1·D (Darius — Keeps Paying for It)

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "Last lesson we talked about the Mask and the Mirror. Give me one word for the Mask column and one for the Mirror column." Call on 2–3 students. "Today we look at what happens when the person under the Mask makes a mistake."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1A.3a Analyze factors that create stress or that motivate successful performance.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3A.3a Evaluate how honesty, respect, fairness, and compassion enable one to take the needs of others into account when making decisions.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

LEGAL · PPRA NOTE

PPRA Designation — All standard opt-out protocols apply. This lesson is PPRA-designated. All standard opt-out protocols apply. Brief your school counselor before delivery — awareness only, no co-facilitation required. Students who show visceral recognition of chronic shame should be noted for counselor follow-up.

ANCHOR CHART · SHAME VS. GUILT — T-CHART

Draw this T-chart on the board before class. Keep visible throughout this lesson and revisit in Unit 2.

SHAME COLUMN: "I AM bad." → Produces hiding, withdrawal, defensiveness, aggression. Focuses on self. Almost never produces growth or repair.

GUILT COLUMN: "I DID something that did not fit who I want to be." → Produces discomfort directed toward repair. Functional and forward-moving.

Add student descriptors to each column as the lesson progresses.

OPENING · 2–3 MINUTES
"Has anyone ever done something they regret — and then spent more time feeling terrible about themselves than actually fixing the problem? That experience — where the feeling of badness becomes bigger than the behavior itself — is what we examine today."

Pause. Let students recognize the experience without sharing yet.

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 5 · read aloud or in your own words

01Define Shame6 MIN

"Shame is a global, identity-level response — I AM bad." Write "I AM bad" on the Shame side. "It produces hiding, withdrawal, defensiveness, or aggression. It focuses on the self, not the situation. Shame almost never produces growth or repair."

Ask: "What does shame feel like in your body?" Take 2–3 responses. Add to the T-chart.

02Define Guilt5 MIN

"Guilt is a specific, behavior-level response — I DID something that did not fit who I want to be." Write "I DID something bad" on the Guilt side. "It produces discomfort directed outward toward the repair. Guilt is functional; shame is not."

Ask: "What is different about how guilt feels compared to shame?" Take 2–3 responses. Add to chart.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During the shame definition, a student who nods emphatically, exhales, or immediately names very specific shame phrases without hesitation.

MAY SIGNAL This student likely lives with chronic shame as a central feature of their inner life — not a passing experience. They are at elevated risk for Unit 2 L6 material.

ACTION Note name privately. Brief counselor before Unit 2 L1. Do not address in the moment.

03T-Chart Scenario Analysis12 MIN

Work through Cards 1·C and 1·D. For each: "What is the emotional response? Is that shame or guilt — how do you know? What would the alternative response look like? What does each lead to?"

Model Card 1·C as a whole class. Pairs for Card 1·D. Prioritize 1·C (Maya: "I am stupid") — it illustrates the core distinction cleanly.

04The Digital Dimension5 MIN

"In middle school, mistakes often have a digital component — screenshots, group chats, posts that spread. The digital permanence of mistakes can intensify shame dramatically."

Ask: "How does the public nature of a mistake change how it feels?" Take 2–3 responses. Note on chart: "Digital permanence → shame amplifier."

05The Integrity Framework5 MIN

"Guilt tells us a choice did not fit our integrity." Students write privately: "A choice that would NOT fit my integrity is ___."

Do not share. Keep private. This plants the seed for Lesson 4 (Peer Pressure).

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During partner discussion of Card 1·C, a student who deflects or jokes every time the conversation lands near a personal connection.

MAY SIGNAL Humor is a developmentally normal defense, but consistent deflection during shame content may indicate this topic is hitting very close to home.

ACTION Do not name or interrupt the behavior. After class, note the pattern privately. Monitor in Unit 2.

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD 1·C Maya — "I am stupid"

Failed math test → avoidance → "I am just stupid." Whole-class first. Prioritize.

CARD 1·D Darius — Keeps Paying for It

Apologized, was forgiven — still self-punishing 6 months later. For Grades 7–8 after 1·C debrief.

TEACHING TIP

Grades 7–8 Extension: Explore the neuroscience — shame activates the same threat response as physical danger (fight/flight/freeze). Guilt activates a social bonding and repair circuit. "That is why shame makes you want to hide, and guilt makes you want to fix things."

Grade 6: Keep concrete. The T-chart is the anchor. Avoid going into shame neuroscience at Grade 6 — the distinction itself is the learning.

EXIT TICKET

"The difference between shame and guilt, in my own words, is ___."

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Students who show the most visceral recognition of shame — as if it is a central feature of their daily inner life — are at elevated risk. Note names privately for counselor awareness.

Chronic shame is a significant risk factor for the disclosure material in Unit 2 and 3. Brief your counselor before Unit 2 Lesson 1 on any students this lesson brought to your attention.

40 min · September Week 3
EQUITY ALERT
OBJECTIVE

Students examine their digital footprint as an extension of identity and identify one way their digital self diverges from their authentic self.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness: digital identity · Social Awareness: permanence and impact of digital choices

SCENARIO CARDS

Card 1·E (Kezia — Perfect Digital Life) · Card 1·F (Marcus — Deleted Post)

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "Last lesson: shame vs. guilt. Quick check — finish this sentence: Shame says 'I AM ___'; guilt says 'I DID ___'. Today we look at a new layer of identity: the digital version of yourself."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1B.3a Analyze how personal qualities influence choices and successes.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3B.3b Evaluate strategies for resisting pressures to engage in unsafe or unethical activities.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

Equity Alert — Deliver explicitly. Not optional.

Deliver this before Step 1 — not at the end, not as a footnote: "Some people and families have very good reasons to be careful online. Limited digital presence is often wisdom — not absence. That is also worth naming. We are looking at patterns today, not judging the size of your digital footprint." Students from families navigating immigration, safety concerns, or privacy needs may have deliberately minimal digital presence and real anxiety around digital permanence. For students without significant digital access: "Apply the same questions to your in-person social presence — how you present at lunch, in class, in the hallways."

ANCHOR CHART · THE DIGITAL BACKPACK

Draw this image on the board before class — a backpack filling up. Label what goes in: posts, comments, likes, shares, screenshots, reactions, messages.

Key message: "Unlike a physical backpack, this one is mostly permanent, potentially public, and carries things longer than you intended."

Add as the lesson progresses: "→ Once it's in, it stays."

OPENING · 3 MINUTES
"If someone who had never met you could only learn about you through your digital presence — your posts, your comments, your likes, your shares — what would they know about you? Would that picture be accurate? Would you want it to be?"

Students write 60 seconds in journals before any sharing.

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 4 · read aloud or in your own words

01The Digital Backpack Metaphor7 MIN

"Everything you post, share, react to, and comment on is added to your Digital Backpack." Point to the visual. "Unlike a physical backpack, this one is mostly permanent, potentially public, and often carries things longer than you intended."

Ask: "What kinds of things go into a digital backpack without you realizing?" Take 3–4 responses. Add to the anchor chart visual.

WORKSHEET

Digital Backpack Audit U1·L3 · Private Activity

Patterns audit — NOT specific posts. What do you typically post about? What tone do you use? What do you react to? Does the digital picture match your Mirror column from Lesson 1? Never collected.

02Digital Backpack Audit · Private10 MIN

"Work privately. This is about patterns — not specific posts. What does your digital presence tend to emphasize? What does it hide? Does your digital self match the Mirror column from Lesson 1?"

Set a 10-minute timer. For students without digital access: "Apply the same questions to your in-person social presence."

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During the audit, a student who writes very quickly and then turns the paper over or closes their journal hard.

MAY SIGNAL May indicate the audit has landed on something specific and uncomfortable — possibly a post or pattern they have been carrying with shame.

ACTION Do not retrieve or probe. Note privately. If the student approaches you voluntarily after class, follow your disclosure protocol.

03The Permanence Reality7 MIN

"Even deleted content can be screenshot, cached, or remembered." Use Card 1·F (Marcus forwarded a screenshot of somebody else's texts in June, deleted it within minutes — a copy resurfaced fourteen months later). "The digital environment operates on different rules than in-person interaction."

Ask: "Marcus deleted the screenshot within minutes — why is it still appearing more than a year later?" Take answers. Confirm: screenshots, shares, memory. Add to the backpack visual: "→ Once it's in, it stays."

04One Thing to Change6 MIN

"Identify one specific aspect of your digital behavior you want to be more intentional about — not necessarily remove, but do with more awareness."

Students write in journals: "One way my digital self diverges from my authentic self is ___. One thing I want to be more intentional about is ___." No sharing required.

Then teach the three rules for the digital backpack, starting tonight: (1) Phone charges OUTSIDE the bedroom. (2) 24-hour rule before any emotional post — if you still want to post it tomorrow, post it. (3) One trusted adult sees your accounts — not as surveillance, as witness. "Not punishments — nervous-system protections." Post the three rules beside the backpack visual.

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD 1·E Kezia — Perfect Digital Life, Hidden Pain

"If I posted what I actually feel, I'd lose everything." Illustrates gap between curated digital self and authentic self.

CARD 1·F Marcus — Deleted Post, Still Circulating

The forwarded screenshot of somebody else's texts resurfaced 14 months later. Anchors the permanence reality discussion. Use 1·E for the gap discussion, 1·F for the permanence step. Do NOT ask students to share real examples of posts they regret.

TEACHING TIP

Grade 6: Focus on the gap concept and permanence. Keep the audit very broad — general patterns only. Do not probe specific posts.

Grades 7–8: Introduce "audience collapse" — posting for one audience when many others may be watching. "How does knowing multiple audiences might see your content change what you feel comfortable expressing?"

EXIT TICKET

"One way my digital self diverges from my authentic self is ___. One thing I want to be more intentional about is ___."

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Equity follow-up: Did the equity alert land meaningfully — did students with limited digital access seem included rather than othered?

Pacing note: The permanence reality discussion often runs long at Grades 7–8. Monitor time at the 20-minute mark.

Peer Pressure & The Volume of Integrity

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →This lesson’s worksheet is interactive — open it: type in it, turn it in, Print my copy / Print it blank →
40 min · September Week 4
OBJECTIVE

Students identify at least two mechanisms by which peer pressure operates and articulate one strategy for maintaining integrity when social pressure is high.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Management: maintaining values under social pressure · Social Awareness: understanding group dynamics

SCENARIO CARDS

Card 1·G (Sofia — Didn't Stop the Mockery) · Card 1·H (The Lunch Table Vote)

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "Three lessons in. Quick check: what is the Mask? What is the Mirror? What is the difference between shame and guilt?" Take 3 quick answers (one per question). "Today we look at the biggest external force that pushes you toward the Mask: peer pressure."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2D.3b Define unhealthy peer pressure and evaluate strategies for resisting it.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3B.3b Evaluate strategies for resisting pressures to engage in unsafe or unethical activities.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

ANCHOR CHART · SIGNAL VS. NOISE — THE VOLUME OF INTEGRITY

Draw this on the board before class. Keep posted through the end of Unit 1.

SIGNAL: Integrity — the consistent, quiet voice of your values.

NOISE: Peer pressure — loud, surrounding, hard to hear past.

Add the three mechanisms as you teach them: SOCIAL PROOF · FEAR OF EXCLUSION · IDENTITY MERGER. Central question: "What makes the noise louder?" Students contribute.

OPENING · 2–3 MINUTES
"Have you ever been in a situation where you knew what the right thing to do was — and then the pressure of the people around you made it feel impossible to do it? What happened?"

Students write 60 seconds in journals. No sharing yet.

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 4 · read aloud or in your own words

01The Volume Metaphor7 MIN

"Integrity is a signal — the consistent, quiet voice of your values. Peer pressure is noise — loud, surrounding, and hard to hear past." Point to the anchor chart. "The challenge of adolescence is not that the signal goes away. It is that the volume of the noise makes the signal almost impossible to hear."

Ask: "What makes the noise louder?" Take 3–4 answers. Add to chart.

02Three Mechanisms of Peer Pressure8 MIN

Write each on the board as you explain: (1) SOCIAL PROOF — "Everyone is doing it, so it must be right." (2) FEAR OF EXCLUSION — "If I don't go along, I lose my seat at the table." (3) IDENTITY MERGER — "My group's opinion is my opinion."

Then: "These are not moral failures. They are features of human social cognition. Your brain is wired for these responses. Knowing that doesn't make them go away — but it gives you a fighting chance against them."

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE During the Fear of Exclusion mechanism, a student who goes very still, breaks eye contact, or looks down in a sustained way.

MAY SIGNAL This student may currently be experiencing active social exclusion — not theoretical. The mechanism may not be abstract for them.

ACTION Do not single out or address publicly. Approach quietly after class: "I noticed today landed for you. I'm available to talk." Note for counselor.

03Scenario Card Analysis10 MIN

"Small groups. For each scenario: What is the peer pressure mechanism? What is the integrity response? What would it cost? What would it give?"

Run Card 1·G as a whole class first — walk through all four questions together. Then groups on Card 1·H.

04The One-Ally Strategy5 MIN

"Research shows that having ONE person who shares your position dramatically reduces conformity. Before a social pressure moment — find one person who might agree with you. You don't have to announce it. Just know they're there."

Students write a name or descriptor privately — not to share. Also distribute and introduce the Values Under Pressure worksheet as a private journal entry.

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD 1·G Sofia — Didn't Stop the Mockery

Didn't join in, laughed once, felt sick. Targets bystander behavior. Work through all four questions as a whole class first.

CARD 1·H The Lunch Table Vote

"Vote" to exclude someone. Introduces vote as social coercion mechanism. Use for faster classes after 1·G debrief.

UNIT 4 · LESSON 1Lesson 1

What Is Grace?

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
40 min · April Week 1
OBJECTIVE

Students define grace in the curriculum's secular sense and identify three acts of grace in different registers of difficulty.

CASEL FOCUS

Social Awareness: understanding grace as a chosen practice · Responsible Decision-Making: the generative shift from Units 1–3

SCENARIO CARDS

Grace Cards A (The Unexpected Kindness) & B (The Second Chance)

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "We have spent three units looking inward and at difficult relationships. Today Unit 4 begins — and the direction shifts. The question stops being 'how do I deal with what happened' and starts being 'what kind of person am I choosing to become?' That is the Unit 4 question."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3A.3a Evaluate how honesty, respect, fairness, and compassion enable one to take the needs of others into account when making decisions.

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2A.3a Predict others’ feelings and perspectives in a variety of situations.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

Unit 4 Teaching Posture.

Shift from "holding the room" to "celebrating growth." Unit 4 is generative — your job in these lessons is to open space for students to practice grace, not to excavate difficult material. The disclosure risk in Unit 4 is significantly lower than in Units 2–3. Name the shift explicitly in Lesson 1: "This unit feels different. That's by design."

ANCHOR CHART · WHAT IS GRACE? — DEFINITION ANCHOR CHART · ♾ PERMANENT THROUGH L7

Post prominently — stays up through L7.

The definition: Grace is the deliberate choice to extend something generous to someone who has not necessarily earned it.

Below: "Grace is not sentimentality. It is chosen strength."

Three registers of difficulty:

1. Grace that is easy — extending generosity toward someone you like or who has been kind to you.

2. Grace that costs something — extending generosity toward someone you don't know or feel neutral toward.

3. Grace that is hard — extending generosity toward someone who has not been kind to you.

OPENING · 3 MINUTES
Use the opening ritual — the same one from the first lesson. "Put both feet on the floor. Take one breath that is slower than usual." After 90 seconds: "That is the same ritual we have used all year. Notice how it feels different now than it did in September. That difference is some of what this year has built."
▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 3 · read aloud or in your own words

01Define Grace — Address Religious Connotation Directly8 MIN

"Before we go any further, I need to address something: the word grace. In many religious traditions, grace is a theological concept — divine, unearned favor. In this curriculum, we use the word in its psychological and relational sense. Grace here is a human practice — the deliberate choice to extend something generous to someone who has not necessarily earned it."

Post the definition on the anchor chart. Address questions about the secular framing now.

02Three Registers of Difficulty8 MIN

"Grace comes in different registers of difficulty." Point to the three on the anchor chart.

"The question Unit 4 asks is: can you practice the harder registers? Can you choose something generous for someone who hasn't necessarily earned it — not because you are required to, but because of who you are choosing to become?"

03Sticky Note Gallery · Primary Activity12 MIN

"On one sticky note: name one act of grace you have SEEN in this school in the last month. On a second sticky note: name one act of grace you are choosing to OFFER this week. Post both on the anchor chart wall."

Students post anonymously. Read 5–6 aloud. "Look at what grace already looks like in this building. We are not starting from nothing."

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student who cannot identify a single act of grace they have seen in the school environment.

MAY SIGNAL This student may be in a significantly grace-depleted environment — socially isolated, or in a context where grace is genuinely uncommon for them. This is important data about their social experience.

ACTION Do not single out. Make a note privately. For Unit 4 L6 (Grace Audit), this student's observation scope may need scaffolding — they may need to start with a smaller context than the whole school.

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD A The Unexpected Kindness

Classmate sits next to excluded Nadia without being asked. The "cost" element — grace that costs something — is the key concept.

CARD B The Second Chance

Drama teacher quietly reinvites Marcus with one condition. Grace-with-accountability. "The condition is not punishment — it is dignifying expectation."

TEACHING TIP

Cultural Connection: Grace and generosity exist in every cultural tradition under different names — hospitality, community care, elder respect. Invite connection: "What does this practice look like in your family's tradition?" Honor student answers without requiring disclosure.

Grade 6: Keep the register focus concrete — easy grace, medium-cost grace, hard grace. Students can name examples from their own experience without personal disclosure.

Grades 7–8: "Is grace something you choose once — or something you maintain? What would it mean to practice the harder register consistently over time?"

EXIT TICKET

"One act of grace I have seen is ___. What made it grace was ___."

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

First lesson of Unit 4. The opening ritual callback — "notice how this feels different than September" — is one of the most powerful moments of the year. Allow it to land.

Address the religious connotation of "grace" directly and early. Students from diverse faith traditions will bring different associations. The sticky note gallery tells you a great deal about the grace climate in your classroom. What does it reveal?

FAMILY CONNECTION

A Note Home

We began our final unit — Grace and Generosity — today. Your child has spent this year learning hard things about themselves and others. Now we are asking: given everything you have learned, what kind of person do you choose to be? Ask them: "What does grace look like in your world right now?"

What Does Mercy Look Like?

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
40 min · April Week 2
EQUITY ALERT
OBJECTIVE

Students define mercy as the choice to restrain power, understand the mercy-growth connection, and sit with the mercy-justice tension as genuine ethical territory.

CASEL FOCUS

Social Awareness: mercy as a relational practice · Responsible Decision-Making: mercy-justice tension

SCENARIO CARDS

Mercy Cards A (The Overdue Apology), B (The Repeated Mistake), C (The Power Difference)

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "Last lesson: what is our definition of grace?" (student answers) "Today we look at grace's close relative: mercy. Grace extends something generous. Mercy restrains something harsh. Different motion — same spirit."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3A.3a Evaluate how honesty, respect, fairness, and compassion enable one to take the needs of others into account when making decisions.

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2A.3a Predict others’ feelings and perspectives in a variety of situations.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

Equity Alert — Deliver explicitly. Not optional.

Deliver before scenario work: "Mercy that benefits those in power at the expense of accountability is not the mercy we are studying. If a system that causes harm asks its victims to extend mercy, that is not mercy — that is silencing." "What we are talking about today is the choice individuals make with the power they personally hold — choosing kindness when harshness would be justified."

OPENING · 2 MINUTES
"When is restraint an act of strength, not weakness? Today we look at mercy — the choice to hold back a response you could legitimately make, because something more important than being right is at stake."
▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 2 · read aloud or in your own words

01Define Mercy8 MIN

"Mercy is the choice to restrain power — withholding a negative response that would be justified. Not because the consequence is not deserved, but because something more important than being right is at stake: the person's capacity to grow."

Write on board: "Mercy = restraining power you could legitimately use."

Ask: "When is restraint an act of strength, not weakness?" Take 3–4 responses.

02The Mercy-Justice Tension10 MIN

"Here is the genuine ethical tension: Mercy and justice are sometimes in conflict. If mercy means letting someone off the hook who has caused real harm, is that mercy — or is it injustice to the person who was harmed? That tension is REAL. This lesson does not resolve it. It asks you to sit with it."

Run Card A as a class model — does mercy apply? What does justice require? Can you have both?

03Scenario Cards B & C15 MIN

Pairs on Card B (The Repeated Mistake). Card C (The Power Difference) as a whole class — deliver the equity alert first.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student who insists that justice and mercy are always compatible — who cannot sit with the tension.

MAY SIGNAL This student may be uncomfortable with genuine ethical complexity. The inability to hold the tension may indicate a rigid moral framework that will come under pressure in more complex real-world situations.

ACTION Do not resolve the tension for them. "I notice you want to find the answer that makes both work. What happens when they genuinely don't?" Let the discomfort be the lesson.

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD A The Overdue Apology

Priya embarrassed Jamie. Late apology was genuine. Jamie has every social right to exclude Priya. Whole-class model. Sit with the tension — no clean answer.

CARD B The Repeated Mistake

Theo keeps being late. Two chances already given. Mercy vs. enabling. "Grace with a clear condition."

CARD C The Power Difference

Teacher quietly accepts late assignment. Equity question — is mercy equally available to all students?

TEACHING TIP

The mercy-justice tension is the destination — do not resolve it artificially. Let students sit with the discomfort of there being no clean answer.

Card C raises the equity question directly: mercy is not equally distributed. Do not shy away from this.

Grades 7–8: "Is there a difference between mercy from someone who was harmed and mercy from a third-party observer? Does the source of the mercy change what it means?"

EXIT TICKET

"Mercy, in my own words, is ___. A time I received mercy was ___. A time I could have shown mercy and didn't is ___."

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Do not resolve the mercy-justice tension. The lesson is the tension itself. Card C is the most important card for equity — do not skip it if time allows.

Students who have been in situations where they were asked to extend mercy to someone in power may have strong reactions. Hold space for that.

FAMILY CONNECTION

A Note Home

Today students studied mercy — the deliberate choice to restrain power. At home, look for a mercy moment this week with your child — and name it explicitly: "I am choosing to extend some mercy here."

UNIT 4 · LESSON 3Lesson 3

Giving the Benefit of the Doubt

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
40 min · April Week 3
OBJECTIVE

Students practice charitable interpretation as a daily skill and learn to use the Pause as a concrete, learnable four-step tool.

CASEL FOCUS

Social Awareness: charitable interpretation · Responsible Decision-Making: the Pause as a skill

SCENARIO CARDS

Charitable Cards A (The Ignored Message) & B (The Short Answer)

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "Last lesson: mercy. Quick — what is mercy?" (student answers) "Mercy restrains a response you could make. Today: giving the benefit of the doubt is an even earlier intervention — choosing a generous interpretation BEFORE you respond."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2A.3a Predict others’ feelings and perspectives in a variety of situations.

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1A.3b Apply strategies to manage stress and to motivate successful performance.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

ANCHOR CHART · THE PAUSE — FOUR-STEP ACTION SEQUENCE (ADD TO FLAME VS. PAUSE POSTER)

Add these four steps directly to the existing Flame vs. Pause poster from U3L4.

1. Stop before sending / posting / responding.

2. Ask: What do I actually want to happen here?

3. Consider: What will this action cause?

4. Choose: minimum effective response — or disengage entirely.

New addition: "Charitable Interpretation" as Step 0 — before the Pause.

OPENING · 2 MINUTES
"When someone you don't know well does something ambiguous, what is your first interpretation? Today we look at where that default comes from — and how to change it."
▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 2 · read aloud or in your own words

01The Automatic Negative Interpretation7 MIN

"When someone we don't know well does something ambiguous, our brain's default is often the worst interpretation. 'They ignored me on purpose.' 'They said that to be mean.' Ask: Where does that automatic interpretation come from?"

Take 3–4 responses. "It comes from in-group/out-group bias — our brains give people in our 'group' the benefit of the doubt automatically. People outside our group do not get that same default. Grace says: extend it anyway."

02Scenario Card Practice15 MIN

"Pre-printed scenarios with TWO interpretation options per scenario — choose the charitable one and practice the four-step Pause. 'Which interpretation is more charitable? Does choosing the charitable interpretation mean pretending the other one is impossible? No — it means choosing not to act on the worst interpretation until you have more information.'"

Equity Alert — Deliver explicitly. Not optional.

"Many ELL and marginalized students know what it feels like to NOT receive charitable interpretation because of how they look or sound." "We are talking about what WE choose to do — not claiming that the world already treats everyone with this grace. It doesn't. That is exactly why it matters."

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student who consistently selects the negative interpretation in every scenario card — who cannot access or articulate the charitable one.

MAY SIGNAL This student may have developed a defensive negative-default as a protective response to a history of real social harm. The negative interpretation may have been accurate often enough that it is now their baseline.

ACTION Do not correct or push. After class: "I noticed today felt different for you. I'm available." Equity consideration: for marginalized students, negative interpretation is sometimes the statistically accurate one.

03Debrief & Step 0 on the poster10 MIN

Debrief Card A, then Card B. On Card B, teach the Three-Strike Rule: (1) FIRST time — charitable interpretation. Maybe they didn't mean it. (2) SECOND time — notice the pattern, name it privately. Do not say it aloud yet. (3) THIRD time — the pattern is real. Distance is wisdom. "You are not being unforgiving. You are a person who has learned this person hurts you regularly. Trust the pattern."

Then add Step 0 — Charitable Interpretation — to the Flame vs. Pause poster, above Step 1 (see the build specification below).

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD A The Ignored Message

Layla's friend was unresponsive for two days. Grandmother hospitalized. Apply the Pause. "Charitable interpretation is not naivety — it is a choice that protects the relationship until you have real information."

CARD B The Short Answer

Cameron's flat response during group work. Nadia interpreted it as dismissal. Cameron was anxious. "Final question is important: How does this change if Cameron often gives flat answers to Nadia specifically?" — limits of charitable interpretation. Debrief with the Three-Strike Rule: first time, charitable interpretation; second time, notice the pattern and name it privately; third time, the pattern is real and distance is wisdom.

TEACHING TIP

The Pause four-step sequence is now added to the existing Flame vs. Pause poster from U3L4 — this is a visual reinforcement of the year-long skill arc.

Grade 6: Focus on the two-interpretation format as a concrete choice. "Which interpretation keeps the relationship open while you get more information?"

Grades 7–8: "Is charitable interpretation naive — or is it strategic? What does it give you even when you're wrong?"

EXIT TICKET

"One person I could give more benefit of the doubt to is ___. One charitable interpretation I could try is ___."

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Charitable interpretation has limits — Card B surfaces this appropriately. Do not teach charitable interpretation as infinite. "When a pattern is clearly directed at one person, the charitable interpretation is no longer the most accurate one."

The equity alert is important — acknowledge that charitable interpretation is not the default experience for all students.

FAMILY CONNECTION

A Note Home

Today students practiced charitable interpretation. At home, model the pause: "That could mean a few different things. What is another explanation besides the obvious one?"

ANCHOR CHART · THE PAUSE — ADD CHARITABLE INTERPRETATION (STEP 0) TO EXISTING CHART

Build Specification

Add to the existing Flame vs. Pause chart from U3L4 — above Step 1:

Step 0: Charitable Interpretation — "Is there a more generous explanation available? Choose it — until you have evidence it is wrong."

"We automatically give charitable interpretation to people we like. That's where the real practice begins."

The addition reads as a prerequisite — a step that happens before the Pause sequence begins.

UNIT 4 · LESSON 4Lesson 4

Small Acts, Big Difference — The Grace Challenge

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
40 min · May Week 1
OBJECTIVE

Students commit to three specific, daily micro-moments of grace and understand the compounding effect of consistent small acts.

CASEL FOCUS

Relationship Skills: prosocial behavior · Responsible Decision-Making: Grace Challenge commitments

SCENARIO CARDS

None — the Grace Challenge Tracker is the primary artifact

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "Last lesson: the benefit of the doubt — an internal choice about interpretation. Today's lesson is external and daily: what does grace look like when you do it consistently, specifically, every single day? That is the Grace Challenge."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2C.3a Analyze ways to establish positive relationships with others.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3C.3a Evaluate one’s participation in efforts to address an identified school need.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

ANCHOR CHART · MICRO-MOMENT MENU — EXAMPLES OF SPECIFIC GRACE ACTS

Post as an anchor chart — students choose from this menu or generate their own.

· Say the name of the person you sit next to when you greet them.

· Acknowledge someone's contribution in a group before adding your own.

· Make eye contact and nod when someone is speaking.

· Notice when someone is left out and move toward them.

· Let someone go first in a situation where you could have gone first.

Specificity is the key requirement. "Being nicer" is not a micro-moment.

OPENING · 2 MINUTES
"Research on prosocial behavior shows that consistent small acts of kindness produce measurable wellbeing benefits for both giver and receiver. The effect is real — and it compounds. One micro-moment of grace every day for a week is seven moments. Across a month, thirty. Across a year, three hundred and sixty-five. Character is built one choice at a time — but those choices accumulate."
▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 2 · read aloud or in your own words

01The Compounding Effect7 MIN

Expand the research context: "Sonja Lyubomirsky's research shows that consistent acts of kindness produce measurable wellbeing benefits for both giver and receiver — not just the recipient."

Draw the compounding effect on the board: 1 per day × 365 = 365 micro-moments. "Grace is not a feeling. It is a practice. A practice that compounds."

WORKSHEET

WS9: Grace Challenge Tracker U4·L4 · Primary Artifact · Used through L5

Three SPECIFIC, OBSERVABLE, DAILY micro-moments of grace. Specificity requirement is non-negotiable. Daily completion log + weekly reflection. Issued today. Followed up at L5.

02Grace Challenge Commitments12 MIN

"Write three specific, observable, daily micro-moments. Use the menu on the board or generate your own. Test each one: Can I do this in less than 30 seconds? Does it cost me something — even a small amount of social risk or attention? Is it something I can observe — did I actually do it or not?"

Teacher circulates and checks specificity. If too vague: "Can you make it more specific? What does that look like in 30 seconds?"

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student whose three commitments are all passive or internal — "think kindly about someone," "try to be more patient."

MAY SIGNAL These are not observable micro-moments — they are intentions. The Grace Challenge requires external, observable action. The student may be defaulting to the internal work they have been doing all year.

ACTION Approach quietly: "These are good intentions — can you find the version of each that someone else could see? What would it look like from the outside?"

03Pair check & first commitment10 MIN

Pairs swap trackers and test each other's three: under 30 seconds? costs something? observable? Revise anything vague. Then each student picks the one they will do first — tomorrow, before lunch — and writes it at the top of the tracker.

TEACHING TIP

Follow-up is essential. Say explicitly today: "At Lesson 5, I am going to ask: did you do your three things? What did you notice?" The commitment works better when students know a follow-up is coming.

Honor students whose cultural traditions already include deeply embedded community generosity practices — name these as already counting.

Grade 6: The micro-moment menu is essential — students at Grade 6 benefit from the concrete examples. Let them choose from the menu before generating their own.

EXIT TICKET

"My three Grace Challenge commitments are: (1) ___, (2) ___, (3) ___. What I expect to notice after a week of these: ___."

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Specificity check is the most important facilitation move in this lesson. Circulate and ask: "Can I do this in under 30 seconds? Can I observe whether I did it?"

Announce explicitly: "At Lesson 5, I will ask: did you do your three things?" The follow-up is what makes the commitment real. WS9 is used through L5 — students carry it with them between lessons.

A Note Home

Your child made a Grace Challenge commitment this week. Ask them tonight: "What are your three?" Follow up during the week: "Did you do one of your Grace Challenge things today? What happened?"

UNIT 4 · LESSON 5Lesson 5

Grace With Hard People

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
40 min · May Week 2
OBJECTIVE

Students distinguish between difficult people and unsafe people, and practice maintaining a grace posture toward the former while protecting themselves from the latter.

CASEL FOCUS

Relationship Skills: grace with difficult people · Self-Management: distinguishing difficult from unsafe

SCENARIO CARDS

Hard People Cards A (The Persistent Critic) & B (The Complicated History)

QUICK REVIEW

1–2 min: "Grace Challenge check-in — who did all three of their commitments every day this week?" (Optional show of hands.) "What did you notice — even a small ripple?" Take 3–4 observations. "Today: what happens when the person you are extending grace toward is genuinely difficult?"

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2D.3a Evaluate strategies for preventing and resolving interpersonal problems.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3A.3a Evaluate how honesty, respect, fairness, and compassion enable one to take the needs of others into account when making decisions.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

ANCHOR CHART · DIFFICULT VS. UNSAFE — VISUAL SPECTRUM

Draw this spectrum before class.

LEFT END — EASY: Someone you like, someone who has been kind to you.

MIDDLE — DIFFICULT: Irritating, challenging, hard to be around. Grace is appropriate and possible.

RIGHT END — UNSAFE: Active harm, ongoing danger. Safety is the priority. Grace is not the goal here.

Bold dividing line between DIFFICULT and UNSAFE. Key message: "The distinction between these two changes everything about what the right response is."

OPENING · 2 MINUTES
"Grace with hard people is one of the most important — and most misunderstood — lessons of the year. It is NOT asking you to be gracious toward unsafe people. It IS asking you to notice: is this person difficult or unsafe? That distinction changes everything."
▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 2 · read aloud or in your own words

01Difficult vs. Unsafe — The Core Distinction8 MIN

"A difficult person is irritating, challenging, hard. An unsafe person is one with whom your safety is at risk. Grace with difficult people is one of the hardest practices of the year. But grace with unsafe people is not what this curriculum teaches — ever. That distinction changes everything about what the right response is."

Walk through the spectrum on the board with 2–3 class-generated examples.

02Scenario Card Practice20 MIN

"Pairs work through two cards. For each: Where does this person fall on the spectrum? Is grace the goal here, or is safety? What does grace look like if appropriate? How is that different from acceptance of mistreatment?"

Work one card as a whole-class model first.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student who consistently places everyone they describe at the "unsafe" end of the spectrum — every difficult person is also dangerous.

MAY SIGNAL This student may have a history where difficult people have been unsafe — the distinction was not available to them because in their experience, difficulty and danger have been linked.

ACTION Do not challenge the placement directly. "What specifically makes that person unsafe vs. difficult?" Keep it curious, not corrective. Note for counselor — this pattern is clinically significant.

SAFETY CHECK

If a student describes their "hard person" in ways suggesting ongoing unsafe conditions — not just discomfort — do NOT offer the grace-with-difficult-people framework. Safety, not grace, is the appropriate response for unsafe situations.

ELL note: For students with difficult relationships connected to immigration stress or family separation, the internal posture concept (choosing an internal orientation toward someone you cannot physically avoid) is the safest takeaway.

SCENARIO CARDS

CARD A The Persistent Critic

Classmate eye rolls every time Priya contributes. Whole-class model. Final question is critical: "What is grace NOT asking Priya to accept?"

CARD B The Complicated History

Two students with history — one processed the hurt. Now notices the other struggling. "Grace-at-a-distance." Connects to U3L3.

TEACHING TIP

Grade 6: Keep the difficult/unsafe distinction very concrete. School-based examples only. The "grace is not acceptance of mistreatment" principle needs to be stated explicitly.

Grades 7–8: Card B is Grade 7–8 appropriate. The grace-at-a-distance concept — "I want good things for you without wanting to be close to you again" — is sophisticated and resonant for older students.

EXIT TICKET

"The difference between a difficult person and an unsafe person is ___. One difficult person toward whom I can practice grace is ___. One thing that makes that hard is ___."

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

TEACHER REFLECTION

The non-negotiable teaching point: grace is not required toward unsafe people. State it early, state it clearly, return to it in the debrief.

Card B (Complicated History) deliberately builds on U3L3 forgiveness/reconciliation — if students remember that distinction, the connection enriches the lesson.

Post as reference during observation. Post alongside the What Is Grace? chart from U4L1.

Scripted teaching sequence · Steps 1 — 3 · read aloud or in your own words

01The Grace Audit Assignment10 MIN

"Your final project for Unit 4 is a Grace Audit. Observe the school environment this week — the hallways, the cafeteria, the classroom, the places where students interact — and find one specific Grace Gap. Then design and execute one micro-moment response to that gap."

WORKSHEET

Grace Audit Checklist U4·L6 · Primary Artifact · Final Project

Pre-structured observation checklist: (1) The Grace Gap identified. (2) Who it affects and how. (3) Connection to curriculum. (4) Micro-moment design. (5) Micro-moment execution (completed after L6). Debrief at L7.

02In-Class Observation Practice15 MIN

"Before you take this audit out into the school: practice observing right here. Spend 5 minutes looking around this classroom with 'grace gap' eyes. What do you notice? Where is grace noticeably absent in this room right now?"

Students observe and note for 5 minutes. Take 3–4 observations. "That is what your eyes will be looking for this week."

03Micro-Moment Design10 MIN

"Design your micro-moment response. It must: (1) Be specific. (2) Be executable in 30 seconds or less. (3) Cost you something — even a small amount of social courage. (4) Address the Grace Gap you identified."

Students draft on the checklist. Teacher circulates and checks specificity: "Is this something you can actually do, in a specific moment, this week?"

Equity Alert — Deliver explicitly. Not optional.

Students from marginalized communities may identify systemic Grace Gaps — racial exclusion, language-based exclusion, social hierarchy gaps. Honor these as legitimate and important observations. Do NOT redirect toward 'safer' or smaller observations. These are the most significant Grace Audit findings in the class. A micro-moment addressing a systemic gap is still a meaningful act.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student whose Grace Gap observation is about themselves — "the Grace Gap is that no one includes me."

MAY SIGNAL This student's observation is true and important — but the Grace Audit is designed as an outward-facing observation. This student may not be able to access the outward perspective while still in acute social pain.

ACTION Validate: "That is a real gap — and it matters. Can we also find one gap somewhere else in the building that you can address with a micro-moment?" If the student cannot shift, note for counselor. This student may need additional support before L7.

Journal entry — student writes privately. Never collected. Never graded.

The Grace Audit reflection (Step 5 of the checklist) is completed after students execute their micro-moment — this carries into L7.

The in-class observation practice (Step 2) is essential — students who skip it tend to identify vague or generic Grace Gaps for the fieldwork.

Equity note: the most significant observations in your class will come from students who have personally experienced grace-depleted environments. Honor them.

Community Charter & Year-End Celebration

40–50 min · June Week 2
★ ELEVATED
OBJECTIVE

Students synthesize the full year's learning through the Community Charter co-creation, acknowledge individual growth, and close the year with a ritual of collective commitment.

CASEL FOCUS

All five CASEL domains — this is the year-end synthesis lesson

SCENARIO CARDS

None — this lesson is a celebration, not a scenario exercise

QUICK REVIEW

No Quick Review. Open with the individual acknowledgment preparation context: "Today is the end of the year. Before we begin: I have something to give each one of you."

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 6–8 · MIDDLE / JUNIOR HIGH

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3C.3a Evaluate one’s participation in efforts to address an identified school need.

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2C.3b Demonstrate cooperation and teamwork to promote group effectiveness.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

PRE-LESSON CHECKLIST

All must be met before class begins.

☐ Post-assessment printed and ready to distribute (administer BEFORE the celebration)

☐ Future-Self Letters from U1L7 retrieved from secure storage — sealed envelopes with student names

☐ Charter paper and signing materials prepared

☐ You have prepared ONE specific, evidence-based growth observation for EACH student to share during the individual acknowledgment

☐ No student will be overlooked — review your class list before class begins

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · Post-Assessment → Letter Return → Charter → Signing → Acknowledgment → Closing · read aloud or in your own words

01Post-Assessment (Before Celebration)10 MIN

"Before we begin our year-end celebration, I want you to complete the same reflection tool you filled out at the start of the year."

Distribute the post-assessment alongside the pre-assessment (students compare). Students complete silently. Collect both documents. "You will get these back today — so you can see where you started and where you are now."

02Return Future-Self Letters8 MIN

"In September, you wrote a letter to yourself. Today I am returning it."

Distribute sealed envelopes — students open privately, read silently for 4–5 minutes. "How does the person who wrote that letter compare to where you are now? Write one sentence in your journal."

No sharing required. The private comparison is the learning.

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE A student who opens their letter and immediately tears up or goes very still.

MAY SIGNAL The person who wrote the letter in September and the person reading it in June may feel very different. This is the curriculum working.

ACTION Hold the room carefully. Do not rush. "Take your time." Allow the emotion. Do not explain or interpret it. This is one of the most important moments of the year.

03Community Charter Co-Creation12 MIN

"The Community Charter is a collective written commitment — created by THIS class — about how you choose to treat each other. Each of you contributes one commitment. One sentence. Specific. Yours."

Students write on slips of paper. Teacher compiles and reads aloud. Students add to the charter paper collectively. "This is co-created — no one writes it for you."

04The Signing Ritual5 MIN

"Every member of this class signs the Community Charter. Sign it as a commitment to what it says — not as a performance for me, but as an act of choosing who you are becoming."

Pass the charter and have every student sign. Display in the classroom. Photograph for the district adoption file.

05Individual Acknowledgment — Non-Negotiable8 MIN

"I am going to name each of you — every single person in this room — and share one specific thing I observed about your growth this year. Not your grade. Not your performance. Your growth."

Work through each student. One specific, evidence-based, true growth observation per student. Prepare these in writing before class — do not improvise. No student is overlooked.

TEACHING TIP

The individual teacher acknowledgment — each student named specifically with something true about their growth — may be one of the most significant moments of the year for some students.

For ELL students, this may be one of the most significant English-language experiences of the year. Deliver every affirmation with full sincerity, specificity, and eye contact.

Prepare these in writing before class. Do not improvise. Improvised acknowledgments often come out generic — and students know the difference.

06Closing Script — Deliver Verbatim4 MIN

"What was built here was not an accident. It was an assignment — one that you showed up for, lesson by lesson, across an entire year. You are not the same person who walked into this room in September. The Architecture of Grace you have been building is yours now. Every brick is yours. Every lesson is yours. Carry it."

Brief silence. Then: "This class is dismissed."

Submit anonymous aggregate data to the Architecture of Grace, LLC if your district has opted in. Store pre/post assessments privately and securely.
