Famous Stories from the Talmud

Aggadah

The Talmud is full of laws. It is also full of stories. The rabbis called these stories aggadah. In this unit you will hear some of the most famous ones. A tree jumps. A stream runs backward. A voice speaks from heaven. And the rabbis still decide for themselves.

You will also meet the people behind the pages. Rabbi Akiva was a poor shepherd who began to learn late in life. His wife Rachel helped him do it. Beruriah was a learned woman whose sharp reading of a psalm is still told today. Many rabbis worked with their hands, as shoemakers and woodcutters.

By the end you will be able to retell these stories and name where they are found, like Bava Metzia 59b. You will be able to explain what each story teaches about arguing well. And you will see how the rabbis tried to be kind, even when they disagreed.

The big question

What do the Talmud's stories teach about arguing well and treating people kindly?

The Oven of Akhnai: "It Is Not in Heaven"

Argument
Big questionWhen wise people disagree, how did the rabbis decide who was right?
The story

The Tree That Jumped

A carob tree jumps, a stream runs backward, and the rabbis still say no.

Picture a study house in the town of Yavneh, near the sea, about 1,900 years ago. Rabbis sit on benches. They are arguing about an oven. The oven is made of clay rings. Sand is packed between the rings. One rabbi says the oven is clean and can be used. All the other rabbis say it is not.

The one rabbi is Rabbi Eliezer. He is old and very wise. He gives reason after reason. The others listen. Then they shake their heads. They are not convinced. So Rabbi Eliezer tries something new. He says, "If I am right, let this carob tree prove it!" The story says the tree pulled itself out of the ground and jumped far away.

The rabbis look at the tree. Then they say, "You cannot prove a law with a tree." Rabbi Eliezer points to a stream. The story says the water ran backward, uphill. The rabbis say, "You cannot prove a law with a stream." The walls of the study house begin to lean in. Rabbi Joshua stands and scolds the walls. They stop, half fallen.

Then a voice comes from heaven. It says Rabbi Eliezer is right. The room is silent. Rabbi Joshua stands up again. He says words from the Torah: "It is not in heaven." He means the Torah was already given to people. People must decide the law now, by the vote of the majority. The rabbis keep their ruling. The oven stays as they said.

This story is in the Talmud, in the tractate called Bava Metzia, on page 59b. Jewish readers have loved it and argued about it for more than a thousand years. In this chapter you will read it slowly. You will see how the rabbis argued, what they decided, and what happened after. You will also see why the story ends with a warning about hurting people's feelings.

Talk about itRabbi Eliezer had wonders on his side. The rabbis still said no. Was that fair? What would you have done?
Section 1

An Oven and an Argument

11.1

What the Oven Was

Main ideaThe argument in this story began over a real oven and a rule about what could make it unclean.

The story starts with an oven. Long ago, ovens were made of clay. Some were made in rings, one stacked on another. Sand was packed between the rings. This oven was built that way. The question was simple. Could this oven become ?

The is the oldest part of the Talmud. It is a book of rules written down about the year 200 CE. In the tractate Kelim, chapter 5, mishnah 10, it records this oven. Rabbi Eliezer said the oven could not become unclean. The other rabbis said it could.

"Unclean" here does not mean dirty. It is a word for a special state in Jewish law. Some things, like an oven, could be made unclean and then could not be used for holy food. The rabbis cared about it because people in their towns asked them these questions every day.

From the source
The Mishnah Records the Oven
The Mishnah describes an oven cut into rings with sand packed between each ring. Rabbi Eliezer says it cannot become unclean. The other sages say it can. This is the oven that the Talmud calls the oven of Akhnai.
Mishnah Kelim 5:10, paraphrased from the Hebrew; see also Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 59b (paraphrased)

11.3

Why the Oven Has a Name

Main ideaThe Talmud says the oven was called Akhnai because the rabbis wrapped it in arguments like a snake.

The Talmud calls this the oven of Akhnai. Akhnai is close to a Hebrew word for a snake. Why give an oven a snake’s name? The Gemara asks that very question. The is the part of the Talmud that talks about the Mishnah.

One answer is that Akhnai was the name of a person who owned the oven. The Gemara gives a second answer. The rabbis surrounded the oven with words. Their arguments circled it like a snake coils around a thing. So it became the oven of Akhnai.

This tells you something about the Talmud. It loves questions, even small ones. Why this name? Why that word? Each answer opens a door. Readers today still enjoy the picture of a snake made of wrapping around a clay oven.

Think it through

Section 2

Signs and a Voice

11.4

The Tree and the Stream

Main ideaRabbi Eliezer called on a carob tree and a stream, but the rabbis said wonders cannot prove a law.

Rabbi Eliezer had given every reason he could think of. The sages still said no. So the Talmud says he called on the world around him. "If the law is as I say, let this tree prove it." A carob is a tree with long brown pods that can be eaten.

The tree pulled itself out of the ground. It moved a long way. Some say a hundred cubits, some say four hundred. A is an old measure, about the length of a person’s forearm. The sages answered calmly. "No proof can be brought from a carob tree."

Rabbi Eliezer tried again. "Let the stream of water prove it." The water turned and ran backward. The sages said, "No proof can be brought from a stream." The Talmud is teaching something here. A law about ovens is decided by reasons and votes, not by wonders.

From the source
The Sages Answer the Tree
Rabbi Eliezer said: if the law is as I say, let this carob tree prove it. The carob tree was uprooted and moved a great distance. The sages said to him: no proof can be brought from a carob tree.
Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 59b, paraphrased (not public-domain wording) (paraphrased)

11.5

The Walls Lean In

Main ideaWhen the walls of the study house began to fall, Rabbi Joshua scolded them, and they stayed half leaning.

Rabbi Eliezer tried a third time. "If the law is as I say, let the walls of the study house prove it." The walls began to lean inward. The students must have felt afraid. A is a room or building where people learn Torah together.

Rabbi Joshua stood up. He spoke to the walls. "When scholars argue about the law, what business is it of yours?" The Talmud says the walls did not fall, out of respect for Rabbi Joshua. But they did not stand straight, out of respect for Rabbi Eliezer.

The story adds a sweet detail. The walls are still leaning. They never chose a side. Readers see a little joke here. Even a wall could not settle this argument. It also shows . The walls honored both rabbis, and so does the story.

Think it through
Walls That Would Not Choose

The walls stopped halfway. They did not fall on the sages, because Rabbi Joshua was there. They did not stand up straight, because Rabbi Eliezer was there. The Talmud says they are still leaning to this day.

11.6

It Is Not in Heaven

Main ideaRabbi Joshua answered a voice from heaven with a Torah verse: the Torah was already given, so people must decide.

At last a voice came from heaven. The Talmud calls it a , which means "daughter of a voice." The voice said, "Why do you argue with Rabbi Eliezer? The law is as he says in every place." Now what could the sages say?

Rabbi Joshua rose to his feet. He quoted the Torah, from the Book of Deuteronomy, chapter 30, verse 12. "It is not in heaven." In the Torah, these words say the teaching is close to people, not far away. Rabbi Joshua gave the words a new use.

A later rabbi, Rabbi Jeremiah, explains what he meant. The Torah was given at Mount Sinai long ago. Since then, rabbinic tradition holds, the rabbis do not listen to a voice from heaven for the law. The Torah itself says to follow the majority. So the sages kept their ruling.

From the source
The Verse Rabbi Joshua Used
It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say: 'Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, and make us to hear it, that we may do it?'
Deuteronomy 30:12, Jewish Publication Society translation, 1917

In the Torah the verse says the teaching is near. How did Rabbi Joshua use it in the argument?

11.7

My Children Have Won

Main ideaThe story says God laughed and said, "My children have defeated me"; the Talmud reads this as joy, not anger.

The story has one more scene. Rabbi Nathan met the prophet . In Jewish tradition, Elijah did not die and sometimes visits the rabbis. Rabbi Nathan asked him, "What did God do at that moment?" Elijah answered, "He laughed and said, ’My children have defeated me, my children have defeated me.’"

Readers have thought about this line for many centuries. Rabbinic tradition reads the laugh as joy. The sages had used the Torah’s own rule. That rule comes from Exodus, chapter 23, verse 2, which the rabbis read as "follow the majority." So, in the story, God was glad.

Not every reader agrees on what the story means. Some Jewish readers say it gives the rabbis the right to decide the law. Some say it warns against pride. Academic readers ask when the story was written and by whom. Each reading looks at the same page. The page holds them all.

From the source

Rabbi Nathan met Elijah and asked what God did in that hour. Elijah said: He laughed and said, My children have defeated me, my children have defeated me.
Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 59b, paraphrased (not public-domain wording) (paraphrased)

Section 3

After the Argument

11.8

A Sad Ending, Told Gently

Main ideaThe sages put Rabbi Eliezer outside the group, and the Talmud shows the pain that this caused on every side.

The story does not end with the laugh. The sages decided that Rabbi Eliezer had gone too far. They put him under a . That meant he had to stay apart from the other rabbis for a time. They also burned things he had declared clean. It was a hard day.

Someone had to tell him. The sages worried that the wrong messenger would hurt him too much. So Rabbi Akiva went. He was Rabbi Eliezer’s own student. He wore black clothes. He sat far away. He said softly, "Teacher, it seems your friends are keeping apart from you." Rabbi Eliezer tore his clothes and wept.

The Talmud says the world felt his pain. Crops failed. Bread spoiled in the ovens. Rabban Gamliel, the head of the court, was on a ship. A great wave rose. He said, "I did not act for my own honor. I acted so the people would not be split." The sea grew calm. The story is asking a question. Was the ban right?

Think it through
Choosing a Messenger

The sages asked: who will tell Rabbi Eliezer? Rabbi Akiva said: I will go, so that no one goes who might hurt him more. He dressed in black, sat far off, and spoke gently. Still, his teacher wept.

What did Rabbi Akiva do to soften hard news? Think of a time you had to tell someone something hard.

11.9

The Gate of Hurt Feelings

Main ideaThe sugya ends with a warning: words that hurt a person's feelings are always heard.

The end of the story is quiet and sad. Rabbi Eliezer’s wife, Imma Shalom, was Rabban Gamliel’s sister. She feared her husband’s pain would harm her brother. Each day she stopped Rabbi Eliezer from praying the special prayer of the hurt. One day she was busy, and he prayed. That day her brother died.

Imma Shalom explained it with a teaching from her family. "All the gates are locked, except the gate of hurt feelings." The Hebrew word is ona&#x27;ah. It means wronging a person, including with words. Rabbinic tradition holds that a cry from hurt feelings is always heard.

This is why the story sits where it does. It comes right after a Mishnah about hurting people with words. Bava Metzia, chapter 4, mishnah 10, says not to remind a person of their past mistakes. The oven story is the Gemara’s example. Even great sages can wound with words. The Talmud wants its readers to see the cost.

From the source
Do Not Wound with Words
The Mishnah teaches that just as there is wronging in buying and selling, there is wronging with words. It gives examples: do not ask a seller the price of a thing you do not mean to buy, and do not say to a person who has changed, remember what you used to do.
Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:10, paraphrased from the Hebrew (paraphrased)

11.10

Disagreeing with Respect

Main ideaReaders take different lessons from the story, but all of them see a model for arguing with care.

Jewish readers have read this story in many ways. Orthodox readers often see it as the base for rabbinic authority. Once the Torah was given, the rabbis decide. Conservative and Reform readers often stress the human side. Law grows through people’s debate. Academic readers study how the story was shaped in Babylonia over centuries.

No reading erases another. The Talmud itself keeps Rabbi Eliezer’s opinion on the page. It never says he was foolish. It calls him a great teacher. It also shows that the winners paid a price. That is how the Talmud tends to argue. Every side gets a voice, and the is not forgotten.

Think about a class in Illinois today. Students disagree about a rule for the room. They give reasons. They vote. The ones who lose still matter. Nobody mocks them. That is the shape of this story. The rabbis argued hard. Then the Talmud asked them to be kind. The two go together.

The story

A Better Way to Read a Verse

A woman corrects her husband's prayer with a better reading of a verse.

Picture a small house in the land of Israel about 1,850 years ago. A teacher named Rabbi Meir lives here with his wife, Beruriah. Some rough men live nearby. They bother Rabbi Meir all the time. They make his life hard. One day he has had enough. He begins to pray that they will die.

Beruriah hears him. She does not shout. She asks a question. "Why do you pray for that?" Rabbi Meir points to a verse in the Book of Psalms. It says, "Let sinners cease from the earth." To him that means the bad men should be gone.

Beruriah shakes her head. She knows the Hebrew very well. She says the word does not have to mean sinners. It can mean sins. Pray that the sins will end, she tells him. Then look at the end of the verse. It says the wicked will be no more. If the sins end, there will be no more wicked people. They will have changed.

Rabbi Meir listens to her. He changes his prayer. He asks that the men will turn from their bad ways. The Talmud says they did. This story is in the tractate Berakhot, on page 10a. It is only a few lines long. But readers have remembered it for many centuries.

In this chapter you will meet three people. Rabbi Akiva was a poor shepherd who could not read. Rachel was the woman who believed he could learn. Beruriah was a scholar quoted by the rabbis. You will also meet rabbis who fixed shoes, cut wood and healed the sick. The Talmud was made by real people with real lives.

Akiva the Shepherd

12.1

Water Wears Away Stone

Main ideaRabbinic tradition tells that Akiva began learning to read at forty, after seeing what dripping water did to a rock.

Rabbi Akiva became one of the greatest rabbis in the Talmud. But he did not start that way. Tradition says he was a poor . He worked for a rich man named Kalba Savua. Akiva was about forty years old. He had never learned to read.

A story in Avot de-Rabbi Natan, chapter 6, tells how he began. Akiva stood by a well. He saw a stone with a hole worn in it. What made the hole? Dripping water, one drop at a time. He thought, "If soft water can cut hard stone, then words of Torah can enter my heart."

He went to a teacher with his young son. They learned the letters together. He kept going, day after day. This story is . Aggadah means the stories and sayings in rabbinic books, not the laws. Readers tell it to show that a person can start late and still learn.

12.3

Twenty-Four Years

Main ideaAkiva studied for twenty-four years, and when he came home he said all his learning belonged to Rachel.

Akiva went away to study for twelve years. When he came back, he had many students with him. Near his house, he heard a neighbor speak to Rachel. "Your husband left you so long!" Rachel answered, "If he asked me, I would let him study twelve more years." Akiva heard this. He turned around and went back to study.

Twelve more years passed. That makes twenty-four in all. This time the Talmud says he came home with 24,000 students. The whole town came out to meet him. Rachel came too, in worn clothes. She pushed through the crowd to reach him. The students tried to push her back.

Rabbi Akiva stopped them. He said, "Leave her. What is mine and what is yours is hers." He meant that all his learning, and all his students’ learning, came from her. Her father also came. He took back his vow. The Talmud tells this in Ketubot 63a. The number twenty-four is a ; historians cannot check it.

Section 2

Akiva the Teacher

12.4

Thousands of Students

Main ideaThe Talmud says Rabbi Akiva's thousands of students were lost because they did not honor one another, and he began again with five.

The Talmud gives Rabbi Akiva 24,000 students. That is a huge number. It is a , the kind a story uses to mean "very many." Tractate Yevamot, page 62b, tells what happened to them. It is sad. They all died in one season, between the spring holidays.

The Gemara gives a reason. It says they did not treat each other with . They were learned, but they did not respect one another. Many Jewish readers see a warning here. Knowing a lot is not enough. How you treat the person next to you matters just as much.

Rabbi Akiva did not give up. The Talmud says he went south and taught five new students. One of them was Rabbi Meir, whom you will meet again. Another was Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai. From these five, rabbinic tradition holds, much of the Mishnah’s teaching came.

12.5

A Great Principle

Main ideaRabbi Akiva called "love your neighbor as yourself" a great principle of the Torah.

The Torah says, in Leviticus, chapter 19, verse 18, "Love thy neighbour as thyself." Rabbi Akiva taught something about that verse. He said it is a great of the Torah. A principle is a big rule that many small rules come from.

This saying is found in the , an early rabbinic book that goes through Leviticus line by line. It is also in the Jerusalem Talmud, Nedarim 9:4. There, another sage, Ben Azzai, gives a different answer. He points to Genesis 5:1, which says all people are made in God’s image. Ben Azzai says that verse is even greater.

Why? Readers explain it this way. "Love your neighbor" asks you to care for others as you care for yourself. Ben Azzai worried: what if a person does not care for himself? So he chose a verse about every person’s worth. The Talmud keeps both answers. Both are still taught today.

12.6

Akiva's Courage

Main ideaRabbi Akiva kept teaching Torah when Rome forbade it, and the Talmud remembers his brave death.

Rabbi Akiva lived under the rule of . After a Jewish revolt, historians date it to about 132 to 135 CE, Rome made hard laws. Teaching Torah in public was forbidden. Rabbi Akiva kept teaching. The Talmud, in Berakhot 61b, tells that he was arrested for it.

The Talmud tells his death gently but clearly. He was put to death by the Romans. It was the hour for saying the , the prayer that begins "Hear, O Israel." He said it. His students asked how he could pray at such a time. He said he had waited all his life to love God with all his soul.

Jewish tradition remembers him as one of ten sages killed by Rome. Some prayers on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, recall them. Historians say the details of the story were shaped over time. But they agree that Rabbi Akiva died in this period. Readers see courage and calm in the way the Talmud tells it.

Think it through
Teaching When It Was Forbidden

A friend asks Rabbi Akiva why he keeps teaching when it is against the law. The Talmud says he answered with a story about fish. A fox told the fish to come up on dry land to be safe. The fish said: if we are afraid in the water, where we live, how much more afraid would we be out of it? Torah, he meant, was his water.

What did Rabbi Akiva mean by comparing Torah to water for a fish?

Section 3

Beruriah the Scholar

12.7

A Learned Woman

Main ideaBeruriah is a woman scholar whose words the Talmud quotes and whose opinions the rabbis respected.

Beruriah lived in the land of Israel around 150 CE. She was the daughter of a rabbi, Hananiah ben Teradyon. She married Rabbi Meir, a student of Rabbi Akiva. The Talmud names her and quotes her. That is rare. Most of the voices on the page are men.

Tractate Pesachim, page 62b, tells how quickly she learned. It says she studied three hundred from three hundred teachers in one day. Readers know this is a round number. It is the Talmud’s way of saying she was very sharp.

In tractate Tosefta Kelim, an early rabbinic book, her opinion on a question of law is recorded. A sage says she spoke well. Jewish readers today, from many branches of Judaism, point to Beruriah. She shows that a woman’s learning had a place in the rabbis’ world, even if that place was small.

12.8

Sins, Not Sinners

Main ideaBeruriah read a Hebrew word in Psalm 104 as "sins" rather than "sinners," and Rabbi Meir changed his prayer.

Some rough men were bothering Rabbi Meir. He prayed that they would die. Beruriah asked him why. He pointed to Psalm 104, verse 35. It says, "Let sinners cease from the earth." Beruriah looked at the Hebrew word. It is chata&#x27;im. It can mean sinners. It can also mean sins.

She said: read it as sins. Pray for sins to end, not for people to end. Then she pointed to the rest of the verse. "And let the wicked be no more." If the sins end, she said, the wicked will be no more. They will have become good. The verse fits her reading better.

Rabbi Meir prayed for the men to change. The Talmud, in Berakhot 10a, says they did. This is a lesson in . A single word can hold two meanings. Beruriah chose the kinder one, and she showed from the verse itself why it was right.

Psalm 104:35 (compare the Jewish Publication Society translation, 1917) with Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 10a, paraphrased (paraphrased)

12.9

Beruriah's Sharp Words

Main ideaIn Eruvin 53b Beruriah corrects a rabbi and a student, showing she knew the rabbis' rules well.

Beruriah could be sharp. Tractate , page 53b, has two short stories about her. In one, a sage named Rabbi Yose the Galilean was traveling. He met her on the road. He asked, "By which road do we go to the town of Lod?"

Beruriah answered him with a tease. The sages had said not to talk too much with women. So why, she asked, did he use so many words? He could have said, "Which to Lod?" Readers smile at this. She used the rabbis’ own rule to correct a rabbi, and she used it with wit.

In the other story, she saw a student learning quietly. She scolded him. She quoted a verse from Second Samuel about a thing being "ordered in all things and sure." If learning is set in all your body, she said, by saying it aloud, it will stay. Reading aloud is still a habit today.

Think it through
Which to Lod?

Rabbi Yose asks: By which road do we go to Lod? Beruriah answers: You should have said, Which to Lod? Two words instead of eight. She is teasing him with a rule the sages themselves taught about speaking briefly.

Was Beruriah being rude, funny, or teaching a lesson? What in the story makes you think so?

Section 4

Rabbis with Jobs

12.10

Sandals, Wood and Bricks

Main ideaMany sages worked with their hands; the Talmud remembers a sandal-maker, a laborer and a builder.

The rabbis of the Talmud were not paid to be rabbis. Most had jobs. One sage is called Rabbi Yochanan the . His saying is in Pirkei Avot, chapter 4, mishnah 11. He said that a group that gathers for the sake of heaven will last. His name tells us what he did all day.

Hillel, a sage of about 30 BCE, was poor. Tractate Yoma, page 35b, says he worked each day for a small coin. He paid half of it to enter the study house. One winter day he had no coin. He climbed on the roof to listen through the window. Snow covered him. Tradition remembers him as a woodcutter.

Hillel’s partner in debate was Shammai. Tractate Shabbat, page 31a, shows Shammai holding a builder&#x27;s cubit, a measuring stick. So readers say Shammai was a builder. These jobs were not shameful. The Talmud praises work, and it shows the rabbis doing it.

From the source

Rabbi Yochanan the Sandal-maker taught: every gathering that is for the sake of heaven will in the end endure, and one that is not for the sake of heaven will not endure.
Mishnah Avot 4:11, paraphrased (compare Charles Taylor, Sayings of the Jewish Fathers, 1877) (paraphrased)

12.11

Doctors and Charcoal

Main ideaThe Talmud shows sages healing the sick and working at dirty jobs, and it honors them for it.

Rabbi Joshua, whom you met in the oven story, had a job too. Tractate Berakhot, page 28a, says the walls of his house were black. Another rabbi guessed why. "You must be a -maker." Charcoal burns for cooking and heat. Making it is dirty, smoky work.

Some sages were healers. Shmuel, a sage of Babylonia around 220 CE, was a . Tractate Bava Metzia, page 85b, tells that he treated the eyes of Rabbi Judah the Prince, the head of the rabbis. Shmuel also studied the stars. He said the paths of the sky were as clear to him as the streets of his town.

Tractate Taanit, page 21b, tells of Abba the bloodletter. Bloodletting was a common treatment then. Abba treated men and women with care for their dignity. He would not take money from the poor. The Talmud says he was greeted from heaven every day, more often than a great sage. Kindness in a job counted as much as learning.

Think it through
Jobs on the Page

Rabbi Joshua made charcoal. Rabbi Yochanan made sandals. Shmuel treated eyes. Abba let blood and would not charge the poor. Hillel worked for a coin a day. All of them are honored in the Talmud for their learning, and their jobs are told as part of who they were.

Why might the Talmud want readers to know what jobs its sages had?
