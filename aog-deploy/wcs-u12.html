The Political Spectrum in Depth

Ideologies

People often sort political ideas along a line from left to right. That line is a helpful start, but it hides a lot. Two people who use the same label may want very different things. Two people with different labels may agree on more than they expect. In this unit you will look closely at the main ideas that shape politics in Europe, the Americas and beyond.

You will meet the thinkers behind these ideas, such as John Locke, Adam Smith, John Stuart Mill, Edmund Burke and Karl Marx. You will see how liberalism, conservatism, socialism, social democracy, libertarianism and nationalism grew from real problems in real places. For each one, you will hear what supporters say and what critics say.

By the end, you will be able to explain what each idea stands for, compare two ideas fairly, and notice when a word means different things in different countries. You will also be able to use evidence to build and defend your own argument while treating other views with respect.

The big question

How do different political ideas answer the question of how people should live together, and what do their supporters and critics say?

Mainstream Ideas
Big questionHow can the same political word mean different things, and how do mainstream ideas balance freedom, tradition and fairness?
The story

Two Liberals at One Table

A student from London and a student from Chicago both say they are liberal, and then they start to argue.

Priya grew up in London. Marcus grew up in Chicago. They met at a summer program for high school students interested in government. On the first night, a leader asked everyone to name one word that described their politics. Both of them said the same word: liberal. They smiled at each other across the table. They expected to agree about almost everything.

The next day the group discussed a question about taxes and health care. Marcus said the government should do more to help people pay for doctors and college. Priya frowned. She said that in Britain, calling yourself liberal often meant you wanted government to stay small and let people and markets make choices. Marcus looked surprised. To him, that sounded like what Americans usually called conservative.

Then they found a place where they did agree. Both cared deeply about free speech, fair trials and the right to vote. Both believed people should be free to practice any religion. They realized that their shared word had deep roots in the same old ideas. Over time, the word had grown in different directions on each side of the Atlantic Ocean.

By the end of the week, they had made a chart on the whiteboard. One column showed what liberal meant in Britain and much of Europe. The other showed what it meant in the United States. A third column listed the ideas they both still shared. Their teacher said the chart was a good start. To understand politics, she said, you have to know where the words came from.

Classical Liberalism

23.1

Locke and Natural Rights

Main ideaJohn Locke argued that people are born with rights to life, liberty and property, and that governments exist to protect those rights.

In 1690, an English thinker named John Locke published a book called the Second Treatise of Government. England had just lived through a civil war and the overthrow of a king. Locke asked a basic question: why should anyone obey a government at all? His answer shaped politics in Britain, America and far beyond.

Locke said every person is born with . These are rights no ruler gives you, so no ruler can rightly take them away. He named three: life, liberty and property. People agree to form a government to protect these rights better than they could alone. This agreement is often called a .

If a government breaks that agreement and attacks people’s rights, Locke said, the people may replace it. This idea is sometimes called the right of revolution. Americans used Locke’s ideas in the Declaration of Independence in 1776. Locke’s thinking is the first root of , a set of ideas that puts individual rights and limited government first.

23.2

Adam Smith and Free Markets

Main ideaAdam Smith argued that free trade and competition, not government control, usually make a nation richer.

Picture a baker in Scotland in the 1770s. She wakes before dawn to make bread. She does not do it mainly out of kindness. She does it to earn a living. Yet her work feeds the whole street. The Scottish thinker Adam Smith used scenes like this in his 1776 book, The Wealth of Nations.

Smith argued that when people are free to buy, sell and compete, they often serve one another while serving themselves. He called this a system of natural liberty. Today we call it a . Prices rise and fall with supply and demand, which guide people toward what others need. Smith used the phrase to describe this effect.

Smith did not say government should do nothing. He believed it should provide defense, courts and some public works like roads. He also warned that business owners sometimes join together to raise prices. His ideas became the second root of classical liberalism. Supporters praise markets for creating wealth. Critics say markets alone can leave some people behind, which is a debate that continues today.

Mill and the Harm Principle

Main ideaJohn Stuart Mill argued that society may limit a person's freedom only to prevent harm to others.

Imagine someone in 1850s London giving a speech that almost everyone in the crowd hates. Should the police stop the speaker? The English thinker John Stuart Mill said no, as long as the speaker was not causing harm to others. He explained why in his 1859 book On Liberty.

Mill’s rule is called the . You may do what you like with your own life, even if others think it is foolish. Society can step in only when your actions harm someone else. Mill also defended . He argued that even wrong ideas are useful, because testing them helps people understand the truth better.

Mill warned about a danger he called the . That means a crowd or popular opinion can crush people who think differently, even without a cruel king. Supporters say Mill protects every minority voice. Critics ask where harm begins and ends, and whether some speech or habits harm society in quieter ways. Mill is the third major root of classical liberalism.

23.4

The Welfare State

Main ideaModern liberals argue that government should help people through programs like pensions and health care so they can use their freedom.

In the early 1900s, factory workers in Britain and America often had no pay if they got sick, lost a job or grew old. Some thinkers asked a hard question. Is a person truly free if poverty keeps them from ever making real choices? Their answer changed the meaning of liberalism for many people.

These thinkers developed . It keeps the older love of rights and elections. But it adds the belief that government should act to give people a fair chance. In Britain, a 1942 report by William Beveridge helped lead to the National Health Service in 1948. In the United States, President Franklin Roosevelt’s New Deal created Social Security in 1935.

A government that provides pensions, health care, unemployment help and similar programs is called a . Supporters say it protects people from falling into deep poverty through no fault of their own. Critics say it costs a great deal in , can grow inefficient and may reduce people’s drive to work. Countries still debate how large these programs should be.

23.5

Burke and Gradual Change

Main ideaEdmund Burke argued that societies should change slowly and keep the wisdom stored in long-standing traditions and institutions.

In 1789, crowds in Paris stormed the Bastille prison, and the French Revolution began. Many people in Britain cheered. Edmund Burke, an Irish-born member of the British Parliament, did not. In 1790 he published Reflections on the Revolution in France. He predicted that tearing down every old institution at once would lead to disorder and violence.

Burke became a founder of modern . Conservatives value , meaning customs and beliefs handed down over generations. Burke said traditions hold the tested wisdom of many people over many years. No single group of clever planners, he argued, could replace that wisdom quickly. He favored , like repairing a house one room at a time.

Burke was not against all change. He had supported the American colonists’ complaints and criticized abuses in British rule of India. He believed that institutions such as families, churches, local communities and courts protect freedom. The French Revolution did turn violent in the Reign of Terror of 1793 to 1794. Burke’s supporters see that as proof of his warning. His critics say his caution protected unfair privileges.

23.6

Conservatism and Its Critics

Main ideaConservatives today value tradition, family, faith and order, while critics argue that some traditions protect unfairness.

Think of a small town where the same family has run the hardware store for three generations. Neighbors know one another at church, at school games and at the fire station. Many conservatives point to scenes like this. They say strong families and local groups do much of the work that holds a society together.

Conservatives in many countries share a few common themes. They often stress , meaning safety and respect for law. They tend to value family, religious faith and national history. Many favor lower taxes and prefer local groups and , such as churches, clubs and charities, over large national programs. Conservatives disagree among themselves about trade, the size of government and foreign policy.

Supporters say conservatism protects what works and prevents costly mistakes. Critics say some traditions kept unfair rules in place for too long. They point out that past customs excluded many people from voting or owning property. Conservatives often answer that lasting reforms, like expanding the vote, were strongest when built on a nation’s own traditions of law and rights.

Libertarianism

Main ideaLibertarians want government kept very small so individuals have the widest possible freedom in both personal and economic life.

Imagine a person who wants low taxes and few business rules. The same person also wants government to stay out of private personal choices. In the usual left-right picture, that person seems to belong on both sides at once. Many such people call themselves .

Libertarianism takes classical liberalism to its fullest form. Libertarians value above almost everything else. Many argue that government should mainly protect people from force and fraud, run courts and defend the country. A government limited to those jobs is sometimes called a . Thinkers linked to these ideas include the economist Friedrich Hayek and the philosopher Robert Nozick.

Supporters say libertarianism respects each adult as able to run his or her own life. They argue that free markets produce more wealth and fewer abuses of power. Critics say a minimal state would leave poor, sick or unlucky people without enough help. They also argue that some problems, like pollution, need shared action. Libertarians reply that charities, families and markets can often do more than people expect.

The Center

Main ideaCentrists borrow ideas from both left and right and often seek compromise, though critics say the middle can lack clear direction.

Picture a city council split over a new bus line. One side wants it fully paid by taxes. The other wants a private company to run it. A third group suggests a public-private partnership with a modest fare. That third group, looking for a middle path, is acting like centrists.

One Word, Two Meanings

23.10

Liberal in America and Europe

Main ideaIn the United States 'liberal' usually means favoring more government help, while in much of Europe it often means favoring markets and limited government.

Remember Priya and Marcus from the start of this chapter. Both used the word liberal, but they meant different things. Their confusion is common. Historians can explain it by tracing how the word traveled and changed over two hundred years. The story of one word can teach a lot about politics.

In Britain and much of Europe, liberal parties grew from classical liberalism. The Liberal Democrats in Britain and the Free Democratic Party in Germany are examples. Such parties often stress civil rights, free markets and lower taxes, though each has its own mix. In some European countries, the word is used, often by critics, for strong support of free markets and less regulation.

In the United States, the word shifted in the 1930s. Supporters of the New Deal began calling themselves liberals. Since then, an American usually means someone who favors more government programs and regulation. Many Americans who hold classical liberal views on markets now call themselves conservative or libertarian. This is why it helps to ask what a means before assuming you agree or disagree.

Reading Labels Fairly

Main ideaPolitical labels are shortcuts, so a fair thinker asks what a person actually believes before judging.

Imagine two classmates arguing online. One calls the other a socialist. The other replies with a different label. Neither has asked what the other actually believes about taxes, schools or rights. Scenes like this happen every day. Labels can end a conversation before it really starts.

A political is a shortcut. It groups many beliefs under one word. Shortcuts help, but they also hide differences. In the United States, the two major parties each include people with a wide range of views. Surveys often find that many voters hold some views linked to the left and some linked to the right. Political scientists call this being .

A fair thinker uses a few habits. First, ask what the other person means by a word. Second, state the other view in words its supporters would accept. This is sometimes called , the opposite of attacking a weak version. Third, look for shared goals, as Priya and Marcus did. These habits do not require anyone to give up their beliefs. They make real debate possible.

A Nurse Explains Her Taxes

A nurse from Copenhagen and her cousin from Ohio compare paychecks and are both surprised.

Mette is a nurse at a hospital in Copenhagen, the capital of Denmark. One summer her American cousin Jake visited. Jake had just started his first job at a hardware store in Ohio. Then Mette was surprised. Jake told her he could open a business in a few days, and that many of his friends had started their own shops. She asked how people afford medical care in America. They talked until midnight. Neither changed the other's mind completely. But both left the table understanding that their countries had made different choices about what to share and what to keep.

What Socialism Means

Main ideaSocialism is the idea that major industries and wealth should be owned or controlled by the public or the community rather than by private owners.

In the 1820s, a Welsh factory owner named Robert Owen ran a large cotton mill in New Lanark, Scotland. He shortened work hours, opened a school for workers’ children and improved housing. He believed that workers and owners should share more fairly in what they produced. Owen is often called one of the early socialists.

Socialists disagree with one another about how far and how fast to go. Some want the government to own nearly everything. Others want workers to own their own companies together, which is called a cooperative. Supporters say socialism reduces poverty and unfairness. Critics argue that without private ownership and profit, people have weaker reasons to work hard, invent new things and use resources carefully.

Marx and Class Struggle

Main ideaKarl Marx argued that history is driven by conflict between classes and predicted that workers would overthrow capitalism.

In 1840s England, factory workers, including children, often worked twelve hours or more a day. A German writer named Karl Marx and his partner Friedrich Engels studied these conditions closely. Engels had seen them firsthand in Manchester. In 1848 the two men published The Communist Manifesto.

Marx argued that history is a story of . In every age, he said, one class owns the means of production and another does the work. In his own time, the owners of factories and capital were the . The workers who sold their labor for wages were the . Marx believed owners kept part of the value workers created as profit.

Critics point out that the revolutions he predicted did not happen in the rich industrial countries he expected. 24.3

Communist States and Their Record

Main ideaTwentieth-century communist states claimed Marx's ideas, ruled as one-party dictatorships and caused mass suffering, while some people in them also gained schooling and jobs.

In 1917, Vladimir Lenin and the Bolsheviks seized power in Russia. They founded what became the Soviet Union. Later, Mao Zedong’s Communist Party took control of China in 1949. These governments said they were building the society Marx described. In practice, they became where no rival party or free press was allowed.

The human cost was enormous. Under Joseph Stalin, millions of Soviet people were sent to the , a network of forced labor camps. In China, Mao’s Great Leap Forward from 1958 to 1962 caused a famine in which tens of millions died, by most historians’ estimates. In Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge regime killed or starved about a fifth or more of the population in the late 1970s.

Defenders of these states pointed to rising literacy, mass schooling and rapid industry. Many historians agree those gains were real in some places. But most judge that they came at a terrible price in lives and freedom. Many socialists in democracies also rejected these regimes. They argued that a run by a dictatorship was not what they meant by socialism at all.

Social Democracy and the Nordic Model

24.4

Democratic Socialism and Social Democracy

Main ideaDemocratic socialists and social democrats both work through elections, but social democrats keep a market economy while democratic socialists want to move further from capitalism.

In the early 1900s, workers’ parties across Europe faced a choice. Should they seek revolution, as Marx predicted, or win power through elections? In Germany, a writer named Eduard Bernstein argued for the peaceful path. He said workers could improve their lives step by step through votes, unions and laws.

Two related ideas grew from this choice. seeks public or worker ownership of major industries, but only through elections and with full freedoms. goes less far. It accepts a market economy and private business, but uses taxes, and welfare programs to reduce poverty and inequality. Over time, most large European workers’ parties became social democratic.

The two terms are often mixed up, even by politicians. A useful test is to ask about ownership. A social democrat usually keeps most businesses private. A democratic socialist wants much more of the economy in public or shared hands. Supporters of both say they make democracy fairer. Critics say high taxes and strong unions can slow growth and make it harder for new businesses to start.

The Nordic model has three main parts. First, a market economy with open trade and private business. Second, high taxes that pay for a large . That is why most scholars call these countries social democracies, not socialist states. Supporters say the model shows markets and fairness can go together. Weighing the Nordic Model

Main ideaPeople disagree about whether the Nordic model could work elsewhere and whether its benefits are worth its costs.

Jake asked Mette a fair question: would Denmark’s system work in the United States? Economists and political scientists have argued about this for decades. The debate helps show how hard it is to copy one country’s choices into another place with a different history and size.

Supporters of the Nordic model note that these countries have long, healthy lives, high school completion and lower than many other rich nations. They argue that a strong safety net lets people take risks, such as changing jobs or starting a business. Some point out that Denmark makes it relatively easy to hire and let go of workers, a mix called .

Critics raise several points. Nordic countries are small and, until recent decades, fairly similar in background, which may build . High taxes leave less money in each paycheck and may push some talented people or firms abroad. Sweden also cut some taxes and opened some public services to private providers starting in the 1990s. That suggests the model itself keeps changing and remains debated.

24.7

Nations Uniting

Main ideaIn the 1800s nationalism helped unite Italians and Germans, each into a single country.

In 1850, there was no country called Italy. The Italian peninsula was split into several kingdoms and states, some ruled by foreign powers. Germany was divided into dozens of states. People spoke related languages and shared histories, but they lived under many different governments.

In Italy, Giuseppe Garibaldi led volunteer soldiers, and the statesman Camillo di Cavour used diplomacy. Italy became a kingdom in 1861.

In Germany, Otto von Bismarck, chief minister of Prussia, used wars and diplomacy to unite the German states. The German Empire was declared in 1871. This process is called . Independence Movements

Main ideaIn the 1900s nationalism powered independence movements in India and across Africa that ended colonial rule.

In 1930, Mohandas Gandhi set out on a march of about 240 miles to the sea, and thousands of Indians joined along the way. There they made salt from seawater, breaking a British law that taxed salt. The Salt March showed the world that Indians wanted to rule themselves. India had been under British for generations.

Indian nationalists, led by Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru, used , such as marches, boycotts and refusing to obey unjust laws. India became independent in 1947. That same year, British India was divided into India and Pakistan. The partition caused mass migration and deadly violence, a painful reminder that independence came with great costs.

In Africa, the Gold Coast won independence from Britain in 1957 and took the name Ghana. Its leader, Kwame Nkrumah, inspired movements across the continent. By the late 1960s, most African countries had gained . Supporters see these movements as nationalism at its best: people claiming the right to govern themselves. Many new states then faced hard struggles with borders drawn by colonial powers.

Main ideaExtreme nationalism, as in Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany, treated other peoples as enemies and led to war and genocide.

In 1922, Benito Mussolini and his Fascist followers took power in Italy. In 1933, Adolf Hitler became chancellor of Germany. Both leaders used nationalism in its most extreme form. They claimed their nation was superior, demanded total loyalty and crushed anyone who disagreed. This kind of rule is called .

Fascist and Nazi governments banned rival parties, controlled the press and used violence against opponents. Nazi Germany built its ideas on , the false belief that some races are better than others. The Nazis murdered about six million Jews in the , along with millions of others, including Roma, disabled people and political prisoners. Their wars of conquest led to World War II.

Historians often separate everyday patriotism from extreme nationalism. Patriotism is love of one’s country and its people. Populism

24.10

Populism Left and Right

Main ideaScholars describe populism as dividing society into a pure people and a corrupt elite, and it can appear on the left or the right.

In the 1890s, American farmers in states like Kansas and Nebraska were angry. Crop prices were falling, and they felt railroads and banks treated them unfairly. They formed the People’s Party, also called the Populists. They demanded government action to help ordinary farmers against powerful companies.

Today many political scientists use a definition from the scholar Cas Mudde. He describes as a view that splits society into two groups: the people, seen as pure and good, and the , seen as corrupt and selfish. Populists say politics should express the will of the people. Populism is a style or frame that attaches to other ideas, so it can lean left or right.

Supporters say populism gives a voice to people whom leaders have ignored. Critics warn that if a leader claims to speak for the only true people, rivals and courts may be treated as enemies of the nation.

24.11

Weighing Populism

Main ideaScholars disagree about whether populism mainly strengthens democracy by voicing ignored people or weakens it by attacking checks on power.

Picture a town where a factory closed and many families lost their jobs. At a rally, a candidate says the experts and insiders forgot this town. The crowd cheers. Many people there feel heard for the first time in years. Scenes like this help explain why populist messages can be powerful in many countries.

Some scholars see populism as a useful . It can push parties to notice problems they ignored, such as lost jobs or distant leaders. They argue that democracy should listen when many voters feel shut out. Populist movements have raised issues that mainstream parties later took up, like the direct election of senators. The Populists backed it in their 1892 platform, Progressives kept pushing for it, and it became law in 1913.

Other scholars worry about , the rules that stop any one leader from holding too much power. If a leader says only he or she speaks for the people, then courts, a free press and the opposition may be called enemies. Many scholars say the key test is whether a movement respects , meaning the acceptance that a nation holds many groups with real disagreements.
