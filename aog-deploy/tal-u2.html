Rabbis Who Told Stories

Stories

The Talmud is full of stories about rabbis. A rabbi is a Jewish teacher. In this unit you will meet three of them: Hillel, Honi, and Akiva. They lived about 2,000 years ago. Jewish tradition still tells their stories today.

Each story has a picture in it. A man hops on one foot. An old man plants a tree. Water drips on a stone. You will read the stories and talk about them. Then you will find the big idea inside each one.

By the end, you will be able to tell each story in your own words. You will know what Hillel said on one foot. You will know why the old man planted his tree. And you will know how Akiva learned to read.

The big question

What do the rabbis' stories teach about being kind, giving, and learning?

Big questionWhat did Hillel teach a man who could only stand on one foot?
The story

The Man Who Hopped

A man hops on one foot and asks for the whole Torah before he falls over.

Long ago, about 2,000 years ago, a man came to a teacher named Shammai. The man had a strange idea. He said, "Teach me the whole Torah. Teach it while I stand on one foot." Then he lifted one foot and began to hop.

Shammai was a builder. He held a builder's stick in his hand. The Talmud says Shammai got upset. The Torah is very long! Nobody can learn it that fast. Shammai pushed the man away with his stick.

The man did not give up. He hopped to another teacher named Hillel. He asked the same thing. "Teach me the whole Torah while I stand on one foot." Hillel did not get upset. He thought for a moment.

Then Hillel said something short. "What is hateful to you, do not do to your friend. That is the whole Torah. The rest explains it. Go and learn." The man put his foot down. He had his answer. Now he wanted to learn more.

Talk about itWhy do you think Hillel gave a short answer instead of a long one?
Section 1

Two Teachers

3.1

Who Was Hillel?

Main ideaHillel was a rabbi who lived about 2,000 years ago and was known for being patient.

A is a Jewish teacher. Hillel was a rabbi. He lived about 2,000 years ago. He lived in the land of Israel. Jewish tradition says he came from Babylonia. That is far to the east.

The Talmud tells many stories about Hillel. In the stories, Hillel stays calm. He is kind to people who ask questions. He is kind even when they are rude.

Hillel had many students. Jewish tradition calls them the House of Hillel. They studied his ideas for a long time after he died.

Think it through
Picture a Patient Teacher

Look at a teacher sitting on a low bench. A child runs up and asks a question. Then another child asks. Then another. The teacher does not sigh. The teacher smiles and answers each one. The Talmud says Hillel was like this.

How can you tell when a teacher is being patient?

was a rabbi too. He lived at the same time as Hillel. The Talmud says he was a builder. He was strict. He liked rules to be clear and firm.

Main ideaA man asked to learn the whole Torah while standing on one foot, and Shammai sent him away.

The is the first five books of the Bible. It is very long. Jewish people read it a little each week. It takes a whole year to read it all.

3.4

Hillel's Answer

Main ideaHillel said: what is hateful to you, do not do to another; the rest is explanation, go and learn.

The man hopped to Hillel. He asked the same thing. Hillel did not send him away. Hillel gave him one short rule.

The rule was this. What is to you, do not do to your friend. Hateful means something you would hate. If you hate being teased, do not tease.

Then Hillel said more. That is the whole Torah, he said. The rest explains it. Go and learn. The Talmud says the man went and learned.

From the source
Hillel's Short Rule
The Talmud says Hillel told the man: What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow. That is the whole Torah. The rest is its explanation. Go and learn it.
Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a, retold in simple words (compare Rodkinson's translation, 1896–1903) (paraphrased)

Patience and Kindness

3.5

Hillel Stays Calm

Main ideaThe Talmud shows Hillel staying patient even when people try to make him angry.

The Talmud tells another story about Hillel. Two men made a bet. One said he could make Hillel angry. He went to Hillel’s house on a busy day.

The man knocked and asked silly questions. Why do some people have round heads? Why do some people have weak eyes? Hillel came out each time. He answered kindly.

The man could not make Hillel angry. He lost his bet. Being means staying calm when it is hard. The Talmud holds up Hillel as patient.

From the source
The Bet
The Talmud tells that a man bet a large sum that he could make Hillel angry. He kept calling Hillel out of his house with silly questions. Each time Hillel came, wrapped himself in his cloak, and said, My son, what do you want? At the end the man said, You made me lose my bet. Hillel answered that it was better to lose the money than for Hillel to lose his temper.
Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a, retold in simple words (compare Rodkinson's translation, 1896–1903) (paraphrased)

What did Hillel say was worth more than money?

The story

A Tree for Someone Else

An old man plants a tree he will never eat from, and a passerby cannot understand why.

One day a man named Honi was walking down a road. He saw an old man digging a hole. The old man put a small tree in the hole. It was a carob tree. Honi stopped to watch.

Honi asked a question. "How long until this tree gives fruit?" The old man said, "Seventy years." Honi was surprised. Seventy years is a very long time. The old man would not live that long.

"Then why plant it?" Honi asked. "You will never eat from it." The old man smiled. He said, "When I was born, there were carob trees. My grandparents planted them for me. So I plant this tree for my grandchildren."

Honi sat down to think about that. The Talmud says he fell asleep. And the story gets even stranger. But that part is for the next lesson.

Talk about itHave you ever done something nice for a person you will never meet? What was it?

Main ideaThe Talmud tells how Honi met an old man planting a carob tree for his grandchildren.

was a Jewish teacher. He lived about 2,000 years ago in the land of Israel. Jewish tradition remembers him for praying for rain. The Talmud tells stories about him.

4.2

Seventy Years

Main ideaIn the Talmud's story, Honi sleeps seventy years and wakes to see the tree giving fruit.

The story goes on. Honi sat down to eat. He fell asleep. Rocks hid him from sight. The Talmud says he slept for years!

When Honi woke up, he saw a man picking carobs. It was the same tree. Honi asked, "Did you plant this?" The man said, "No. My grandfather did."

The old man’s plan had worked. His grandchild was eating the fruit. Jewish tradition tells this story to teach about giving to the future.

Akiva Learns to Read

4.3

The Shepherd

Main ideaRabbi Akiva started as a shepherd who could not read, and he began to learn as a grown-up.

Rabbi became one of the most famous rabbis in the Talmud. But he did not start as a rabbi. He started as a . A shepherd takes care of sheep.

Akiva was poor. He could not read. He worked for a rich man. The Talmud says the rich man’s daughter saw that Akiva was good and humble. She married him.

She told Akiva to go and study. Jewish tradition says he was about forty years old. He had to learn the alphabet, like a small child. He did it anyway.

Think it through

Look at a grown man sitting on a small bench. Around him are little children. They are all learning the alphabet. The man is much older than everyone else. He holds his slate and says the letters out loud, one by one. He does not look ashamed. He looks like he wants to learn.

How might Akiva have felt learning with little children? What might have helped him keep going?

4.4

Water and Stone

Main ideaAkiva saw water wearing a hole in a stone and decided learning could shape him too.

Jewish tradition tells how Akiva got the courage to learn. One day he stood by a well. He saw a stone with a hole in it. Water dripped on the stone.

Akiva asked, "Who made this hole?" People said the water did. It fell drip by drip, day after day. Slowly, it wore a hole in the hard stone.

Akiva thought about that. Water is soft. Stone is hard. But water won. He said: if water can shape stone, then learning can shape me. He began to .

From the source
The Drip That Won
An old rabbinic book tells that Akiva, at forty years old, had not yet learned anything. He stood by a well and saw a stone worn away. He asked who had hollowed it out, and was told the water had done it, drop by drop. He said to himself: if soft water can carve hard stone, then words of Torah, which are hard, can surely carve my heart, which is flesh.
Avot de-Rabbi Natan, chapter 6, retold in simple words (paraphrased)

What the Stories Teach

4.5

Planting for Others

Main ideaThe carob tree story teaches about giving to people who will come after us.

Think about the old man again. He worked hard. He dug a hole and planted a tree. He knew he would never taste the fruit. He did it anyway.

Jewish tradition calls this kind of giving a gift to the . The future is the time that has not come yet. You can help people you will never meet.

Kids can plant for the future too. You can plant a real tree. You can leave a room clean for the next class. You can teach a younger kid a game.

Think it through
A Gift You Will Not See

Look at a class planting little trees on the edge of a playground in Chicago. The trees are only as tall as the kids. In many years, they will be tall and shady. The kids who planted them will be grown up and gone. New kids will sit in the shade.

What is one thing you could do this week for someone who comes after you?

Words to know
future
the time that has not come yet; tomorrow, next year, and long after

Akiva began learning at about forty. The Talmud says he studied for many years. He became a great rabbi. Many students came to learn from him.

The Talmud tells stories of the rabbis to teach. Akiva’s story teaches two things. It is to learn. And little steps add up, like drips of water.

The Talmud is full of rabbis like Hillel, Honi, and Akiva. Their stories are still told today. In this course, you will meet many more of them.
