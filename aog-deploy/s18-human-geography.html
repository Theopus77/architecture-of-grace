Density is people divided by area

Two countries, each with the same number of people. From the total alone they look identical.

Density is people DIVIDED BY area — never the total population. A country can have a huge population and be nearly empty, or a small one and be packed. And nothing on the map is random: every pattern has a reason you can find.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

A country with a huge population must be densely populated.

Density is a ratio. Canada and Russia have large populations spread over enormous land, so their density is among the lowest on earth — while Bangladesh, far smaller in area, is among the highest. Always divide.

Divide. Total population is not density.

⚠️ People often think…

In the demographic transition, birth rates fall first.

DEATH rates fall first, in Stage 2 — clean water, food supply, basic medicine. Births stay high for a generation or more, and that gap between the two curves is exactly where the population explosion happens.

Deaths fall in Stage 2, births in Stage 3.

⚠️ People often think…

An immigrant and an emigrant are two different kinds of people.

They are the same person described from two countries. Leaving Poland you are an emigrant; arriving in Chicago you are an immigrant. Immigrant = IN, emigrant = EXIT.

Same person, two viewpoints.

⚠️ People often think…

A population pyramid with a wide base means a country is doing well.

It means young and growing — a statement about structure, not health. A wide base predicts enormous demand for schools and jobs in fifteen years. A narrow base predicts a shrinking workforce supporting more retirees. Each shape is a different problem, not a grade.

The shape predicts needs, not success.

⚠️ People often think…

A city is where it is because of good land.

That is only SITE. SITUATION — what it sits between and which routes reach it — usually matters more. Chicago’s ground is flat and swampy; it grew because it sits where the lake, the rivers and the rail lines meet.

Site is the ground. Situation is the connections.

⚠️ People often think…

Naming one reason someone moved is a complete migration answer.

Migration questions want a PUSH and a PULL — what drove them out and what drew them in. “There were no jobs” is half; “no jobs there, and family plus factory work here” is the whole thing.

Name one push and one pull.

Worth knowing cold

The words that get swapped

Almost every lost point in this unit comes from one of these four pairs. Learn each pair as a pair.

  1. Immigrant / emigrant Immigrant = IN to a country. Emigrant = EXIT from one. The same person is both, seen from two sides.
  2. Refugee / displaced A refugee has crossed an international border. An internally displaced person has not — and gets far less legal protection because of it.
  3. Site / situation Site is the ground itself: the hill, the harbor, the river. Situation is the connections: what it sits between and what routes reach it.
  4. Push / pull Push factors drive people out of where they are. Pull factors draw them to somewhere else. A full answer names one of each.

Where, why, change, move, effect

1 Watch one

A country’s population pyramid has a very wide base. What will it need in twenty years?

  1. Read the shape: a wide base means most people are children right now.
  2. Move the whole shape up twenty years. Those children become young adults.
  3. Ask what young adults need in enormous numbers: jobs, housing, and soon after, schools for their own children.
  4. So: jobs and housing above all. The pyramid is a forecast you can read directly. ✓

Helpful Hints

🧭 The five questions

Where are the people? → Why there? → How is the population changing? → Where are they moving, and why? → What does that do to the place?

Where, why, change, move, effect.

🤝 The one idea

Nothing in human geography is random. Every pattern on the map — a city, a border, an empty region — has a reason you can find.

⚠️ Traps the test loves

  • Density is people ÷ area. Not total population.
  • Immigrant = In. Emigrant = Exit.
  • In Stage 2 the DEATH rate falls first. Births fall later, in Stage 3.
  • Wide base = young and growing. Narrow base = aging.
  • Site is the ground. Situation is the connections.
  • A refugee has crossed a border. An internally displaced person has not.

🎯 How the test will ask

  • A population pyramid — “what will this country need in twenty years?”
  • A short story of a family — “name the push and the pull.”
  • A density calculation — divide, and keep the units.
  • A map — “what does it show, and what does it hide?”
  • A city — “explain its location with site and situation.”

  • Describe all the stages of the demographic transition?
  • Read a population pyramid and predict what comes next?
  • Sort push factors from pull factors?
  • Explain a city’s growth with site and situation?
  • Say what a map shows and what it hides?
