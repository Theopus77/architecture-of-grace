Buddhism: The Four Noble Truths

Traditions

A monk in Thailand walks barefoot at dawn with an alms bowl. A student in Chicago sits in a meditation hall and watches her breath. A family in Kyoto listens to a temple bell ring 108 times on New Year's Eve. A Tibetan monk in exile chants a mantra in India. All four call themselves Buddhists, and all four trace their practice to one teacher who lived in northern India about 2,500 years ago.

This unit studies that teacher and the traditions that grew from him. The first chapter reads the Buddha's life as the tradition tells it, then studies the core teachings that nearly all Buddhists share: the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, the Three Marks of Existence and the Three Jewels, using the Dhammapada in Max Müller's 1881 translation. The second chapter follows the sangha, the community of monks, nuns and laypeople, as it carried the teaching across Asia and into the United States, dividing into Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana along the way.

By the end of the unit you will be able to explain the Four Noble Truths in your own words, read a verse of the Dhammapada closely, compare the Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana branches without ranking them, and describe how Buddhism changed as it met new cultures, from China to Chicago. As in every unit, the goal is to understand what Buddhists believe and practice, attributed to them and described fairly, not to decide whether it is true.

The story

Four Sights Beyond the Wall

As Buddhist tradition tells it, a sheltered prince rode out of his palace four times and came back a different man.

The story that Buddhists have told for more than two thousand years begins with a father who wanted to protect his son. According to tradition, a prince named Siddhartha Gautama was born into the Shakya clan in the foothills of the Himalayas, in what is now southern Nepal. A seer is said to have told his father that the boy would become either a great king or a great religious teacher. The father wanted a king. So he built a life for Siddhartha inside high walls, with gardens, music, fine food and no sight of anything sad.

The tradition says the plan worked until Siddhartha was twenty-nine. Then he asked to ride out through the city. On the first ride he saw a man bent with age, his skin loose and his hands shaking. Siddhartha had never seen an old person. His charioteer, Channa, explained that this happens to everyone who lives long enough. On the second ride the prince saw a man burning with fever, too weak to stand. On the third he saw a corpse being carried to the cremation ground, and mourners weeping behind it.

Each time, the story says, the prince asked the same question: does this happen to me too? Each time the answer was yes. Age, sickness and death were not accidents that struck other people. They were the shape of every human life, including his own, including the lives of his wife and his newborn son. The gardens and music now seemed to him like a curtain drawn over a window.

On the fourth ride he saw something different: a wandering holy man with a shaved head and a single robe, carrying a bowl and walking calmly. The man owned nothing, yet he looked at peace. Siddhartha asked who he was. Channa told him this was a renouncer, someone who had left home to search for an end to suffering. That night, the tradition says, the prince slipped out of the palace, cut his hair, exchanged his silk for a plain robe and began the search himself.

Historians cannot confirm the details of this story, and Buddhists themselves read it in different ways. Some take it as history, others as a teaching told in the form of a life. But nearly every Buddhist school agrees on what the four sights mean. Before anyone can look for a cure, they have to admit that something is wrong. The four sights are the moment the diagnosis begins.

37.1

Siddhartha in History and Tradition

Main ideaBuddhists trace their tradition to Siddhartha Gautama, a teacher in northern India around the fifth century BCE, whose life is known mostly through later Buddhist texts.

In the town of Lumbini, in southern Nepal, a stone pillar stands in a park. It was raised around 250 BCE by the Indian emperor Ashoka, and its inscription says that the Buddha was born here. It is the oldest physical marker of the Buddha’s life that we have. Buddhists from Thailand, Japan, Tibet, Sri Lanka and the United States travel to Lumbini to walk where they believe their teacher’s story began.

The man they honor is . Buddhist tradition says he was born a prince of the clan, which is why he is also called Shakyamuni, the sage of the Shakyas. Scholars agree that he was a real teacher who lived in the Ganges valley of northern India. They do not agree on his dates. Older scholarly estimates place his death around 483 BCE, and Sri Lankan tradition earlier still; many historians today prefer a date closer to 400 BCE. This course follows common practice and says he lived in about the fifth century BCE.

Almost everything else we know comes from Buddhist texts written down centuries after his death, first in the language and later in Sanskrit and Chinese. These texts were preserved as teaching, not as newspaper reports. That does not make them worthless as history, but it means a student should read them the way this course reads any sacred text: asking who wrote them, for whom, and why. Buddhists themselves hold a range of views, from taking every detail as fact to treating the life story as a pattern that every seeker can follow.

The title is not a name. It comes from a word meaning awake, so Buddha means the awakened one. In Buddhist teaching, Siddhartha became the Buddha at a specific moment, which this section will reach. Until then he was a prince, then a wandering seeker like thousands of others in an India full of new religious movements, including the Jain teacher Mahavira, who lived at about the same time.

37.2

Renunciation and the Middle Way

Main ideaAfter years of extreme self-denial nearly killed him, Siddhartha chose a Middle Way between luxury and self-torture, and Buddhists see that choice as the foundation of the path.

A famous statue from Gandhara, in present-day Pakistan, shows a seated man so starved that every rib and vein stands out and his eyes have sunk into his skull. It is a portrait of Siddhartha during the years after he left home. According to tradition, he first studied with two meditation teachers, mastered what they taught, and found it did not end suffering. Then he joined five ascetics in the forest and tried to conquer the body by force.

An is someone who practices severe self-denial for a religious goal, and in ancient India there were many. The texts say Siddhartha ate a single grain of rice a day, held his breath until his head roared, and sat unmoving in the heat and cold. Tradition says that he grew so thin he could feel his spine through his stomach. After about six years he collapsed by a river. A village girl named Sujata offered him a bowl of rice cooked in milk, and he accepted it. His five companions, disgusted, left him.

Buddhists remember that bowl of rice as a turning point. Siddhartha had lived in a palace of pleasure and found it empty. He had lived in a forest of pain and found it useless. The body, he concluded, is the instrument of the search, and a broken instrument cannot play. In his first sermon he would name two extremes to avoid: devotion to sense pleasure and devotion to self-torment. Between them lies what he called the .

The Middle Way is not a rule about eating. It is a general principle that later Buddhist thinkers applied to almost everything: to how hard to practice, to how to hold opinions, and to how to think about the self. A monk in Thailand today eats one or two meals before noon and none after, which is more than the starving ascetic ate and less than the prince did. Buddhists point to that schedule as the Middle Way made into a daily habit.

37.3

Awakening Under the Bodhi Tree

Main ideaBuddhists believe Siddhartha reached full awakening while meditating at Bodh Gaya, and that he then taught for forty-five years before his death.

At Bodh Gaya, in the Indian state of Bihar, a large fig tree grows beside a stone temple. Pilgrims sit beneath it in silence, and some tie prayer flags to its branches. Buddhists hold that this tree descends from the one under which Siddhartha sat down one evening and vowed not to rise until he understood the cause of suffering. The species is now called the tree, from the word for awakening.

The texts describe that night as a struggle. A figure named , whose name means death, tried to break Siddhartha’s concentration with armies, storms, temptations and finally doubt. Mara asked who could witness that Siddhartha deserved awakening. In a gesture that appears in countless statues, Siddhartha touched the ground with his right hand and called the earth itself as witness. Buddhists read Mara in different ways: some as a real being, many as a picture of fear and craving inside the mind.

Toward dawn, the tradition says, Siddhartha saw his past lives, saw how beings rise and fall according to their actions, and saw the chain of causes that produces suffering and the way to break it. At that moment he became the Buddha. He is said to have hesitated to teach, thinking no one would understand, and then to have walked to the Deer Park at , near Varanasi, where he found the five ascetics who had abandoned him. His first sermon to them is called setting the wheel of the teaching in motion.

For the next forty-five years, according to the texts, the Buddha walked the roads of the Ganges valley, teaching kings, farmers, merchants and outcasts, and gathering a community of monks and nuns. He died at about eighty near the town of Kushinagar, after eating a meal that made him ill. His last recorded words, in the Pali account, urge his followers to work out their liberation with diligence because all conditioned things pass away. Buddhists call his passing the , the final release.

The Four Noble Truths

37.4

The First Truth: Dukkha

Main ideaThe Buddha's first truth is that ordinary life is marked by dukkha, a word that covers pain, stress and the unreliability of everything we lean on.

A doctor in an emergency room does not begin with the cure. She begins by asking what hurts. Buddhists often describe the Buddha’s first sermon the same way: as a physician’s four steps, which name the illness, its cause, whether it can be cured, and the treatment. These are the , the core of Buddhist teaching in every school. The first is the diagnosis: there is .

Dukkha is a Pali word usually translated as suffering, but that English word is too narrow. Dukkha includes obvious pain, such as sickness, grief and death. It also includes the frustration of not getting what we want and the anxiety of losing what we have. At its deepest, it means that nothing we grasp can be relied on, because everything changes. A good meal ends. A friendship shifts. A body ages. Even happiness carries dukkha, Buddhists say, because it cannot last.

The first sermon lists the forms: birth is dukkha, aging is dukkha, sickness is dukkha, death is dukkha; being joined to what one dislikes, being parted from what one loves, and not getting what one wants are dukkha. Notice that the list is not a claim that life is only misery. Buddhists insist the first truth is not pessimism but honesty, in the way that a diagnosis is not pessimism. Its purpose is to make a cure possible.

Compare this starting point with others in this course. The Hebrew Bible opens with a good creation and asks how it went wrong. The Buddha opens with an observation about experience and asks what drives it. Neither tradition denies pain; they place it differently. A student comparing them should describe each on its own terms rather than deciding which is gloomier or more hopeful.

37.5

Craving and Its Ending

Main ideaThe second and third truths teach that suffering arises from craving and that when craving ceases, suffering ceases, a state Buddhists call nirvana.

In the Pali texts the Buddha compares the mind to a fire that keeps burning as long as fuel is added. The second Noble Truth names the fuel. Suffering arises from , a word that literally means thirst and is usually translated as craving. It is the pull toward pleasant things, the push away from unpleasant ones, and the wish for existence itself to go on as we like. Buddhists say craving is not the same as needing food or wanting a friend to be well. It is the grasping that turns a wish into a demand.

The teaching connects craving to a wider view of cause and effect. Buddhists hold that actions driven by craving, hatred and ignorance produce results, and that beings are reborn again and again in a cycle called , carrying the fruits of past action, or . The word karma means action; in Buddhist usage it points to intentional action and its consequences. Rebirth in Buddhism differs from the Hindu idea in one key way, which a later lesson explains: Buddhists deny a permanent soul that passes from life to life.

The third truth is the good news of the diagnosis. Because suffering has a cause, removing the cause removes the suffering. When craving is extinguished completely, what remains is . The word means blowing out, like a flame. Buddhist teachers are careful to say nirvana is not nothingness and not a heaven; it is the end of greed, hatred and delusion, and with them the end of rebirth. The Buddha is said to have declined to describe it in ordinary terms, and in one parable he compares his teaching to a raft that a traveler leaves behind once he reaches the far shore.

Different Buddhists describe nirvana in different ways. Theravada texts emphasize the peace of the person who has fully let go, called an arhat. Some Mahayana texts speak of nirvana as not separate from this world, once the world is seen rightly. What all agree on is the logic of the truths: if the fire needs fuel, then stop adding fuel and the fire goes out.

37.6

The Eightfold Path

Main ideaThe fourth truth is the Eightfold Path, eight linked practices that Buddhists group under wisdom, ethical conduct and mental discipline.

Above the doors of many Buddhist temples, and on their gates and roofs, you will see a wheel with eight spokes. It is the wheel, and in Buddhist art its eight spokes stand for the fourth Noble Truth: the . The word dharma has many meanings in India. In Buddhism it means above all the Buddha’s teaching, and also the way things truly are. The path is the treatment the physician prescribes.

The eight parts each begin with a word usually translated right, in the sense of complete or skillful rather than correct on a test. They are right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration. Buddhists do not treat these as eight steps climbed one after another. They are eight strands of one rope, practiced together, each supporting the others.

Teachers commonly gather the eight into three groups. Right view and right intention are wisdom: understanding the Four Noble Truths and intending kindness and letting go. Right speech, right action and right livelihood are ethical conduct, or : not lying or speaking harshly, not killing or stealing, and earning a living that does not harm others, such as avoiding trade in weapons or living beings. Right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration are mental discipline, or : training the mind through meditation.

A concrete example: a Buddhist shop owner in Chicago who refuses to sell counterfeit goods is practicing right livelihood and right action. When she notices irritation with a rude customer and lets it pass without a sharp reply, she is practicing right mindfulness and right speech. The path, Buddhists say, is not reserved for monks. Its parts fit an ordinary Tuesday.

Marks of Existence and the Three Jewels

37.7

Impermanence, Suffering, Non-Self

Main ideaBuddhists teach that all conditioned things share three marks: they are impermanent, unsatisfactory and without a fixed self.

In the Milindapanha, a Pali text compiled around the turn of the common era, a monk named Nagasena is questioned by a Greek king named Menander. Nagasena asks the king to point to his chariot. Is it the wheels? The axle? The pole? The king admits it is none of these parts, yet there is no chariot apart from the parts. Chariot, Nagasena says, is a useful name for the parts working together. And so, he argues, is the word I.

This story illustrates the third of what Buddhists call the . The first mark is , impermanence: everything that arises from causes will change and pass away. The second is dukkha, already studied: what is impermanent cannot give lasting satisfaction. The third is , non-self. Buddhists teach that what we call a self is a flow of five changing bundles, the : physical form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness. No unchanging owner stands behind them.

Here Buddhism parts from the Hindu teaching studied in the previous unit. The Upanishads teach that the true self, atman, is eternal and one with Brahman. The Buddha, according to the Pali texts, declined to affirm any such self. Rebirth still happens, Buddhists say, the way one candle lights another: a process continues, but no unchanging thing passes across. Buddhist schools have debated for centuries how to explain this, and a student should present it as a teaching they hold, not as a puzzle they failed to notice.

The Three Marks are meant to be seen, not merely believed. Buddhist meditators watch a sensation arise and fade, and notice that the wish for it to stay is itself a source of distress. Seeing impermanence directly, they say, loosens grasping. That is why the Dhammapada connects knowing the three marks with the way to purity.

37.8

Buddha, Dharma, Sangha

Main ideaBuddhists express commitment by taking refuge in the Three Jewels, the Buddha, the dharma and the sangha, and lay Buddhists undertake five precepts.

In a temple in Sri Lanka, in a meditation hall in Colorado, or in a family shrine room in Vietnam, a Buddhist ceremony often begins with the same short chant, in Pali or in translation: I go to the Buddha for refuge; I go to the dharma for refuge; I go to the sangha for refuge. Repeated three times, this is called . For many Buddhists it is the act that marks a person as a Buddhist, the way the shahada does for a Muslim.

The three refuges are called the . The Buddha is the teacher and the proof that awakening is possible for a human being. The dharma is the teaching itself and the truth it describes. The is the community: in its strict sense the order of monks and nuns, and in a broader sense everyone who follows the path. Refuge does not mean asking the Buddha to rescue anyone. Buddhists compare it to trusting a doctor, a medicine and the nurses who help you take it.

Along with refuge, lay Buddhists commonly undertake the : to refrain from killing living beings, from taking what is not given, from sexual misconduct, from false speech, and from intoxicants that cloud the mind. These are worded as training commitments a person takes on, not as commands from a god. Monks and nuns take many more rules. On holy days some laypeople keep eight precepts, adding, for example, not eating after noon.

Comparing traditions helps here. The Ten Commandments in Exodus are framed as words spoken by God to a people in covenant. The five precepts are framed as steps a person undertakes to reduce harm and clear the mind. Both forbid killing, stealing and lying. A student writing about them should attribute each to its tradition and describe the different frames rather than asking which list is better.

Main ideaThe Dhammapada, a collection of 423 Pali verses, is among the most widely read Buddhist texts, and Müller's 1881 translation made it available in English.

Two Bells, One Tradition

On a winter night in Kyoto and a Sunday morning in Chicago, a bell calls Buddhists together, and the two sounds are linked by more than two thousand years of travel.

It is almost midnight on New Year's Eve in Kyoto, Japan. At the temple of Chion-in, a crowd stands in the cold beneath a bronze bell taller than a person. A team of monks in black robes grips ropes tied to a heavy wooden beam. One monk calls out, the team leans back, and the beam swings forward into the bell. A deep sound rolls out over the city and hangs in the air for a long time before it fades.

The monks will strike the bell 108 times, some before midnight and some after. In Japanese Buddhist tradition, the number 108 stands for the many worldly desires and troubles that bind a person to suffering. Each stroke is meant to let one of them go, so that the new year can begin with a clearer mind. Temples across Japan do the same thing on the same night, and millions of people listen in person or on television.

More than 6,000 miles away, on Chicago's North Side, a smaller bell sounds on a Sunday morning. It calls members of a Japanese American Buddhist temple to their weekly service. Many of the families have belonged for generations. Their grandparents and great-grandparents came to Chicago in the 1940s, after the United States government released Japanese Americans from the camps where it had held them during World War II. The service includes chanting and a short talk, and the congregation repeats a phrase in Japanese: Namu Amida Butsu.

Both bells belong to the same family of Buddhism, called Pure Land, which centers on a Buddha named Amida. Yet neither temple looks much like the Deer Park where, as the last chapter told, the Buddha first taught. There were no bronze bells there, no Japanese words and no Sunday services. The teaching had to travel across mountains, deserts and oceans, and it changed with every language and culture it entered.

This chapter follows that journey. It begins with the community the Buddha left behind, then follows an emperor who sent the teaching abroad, and then traces the three great branches that grew from it. Along the way it asks a question scholars and Buddhists both ask: when a tradition changes this much, what holds it together?

Life in the Monastery

Main ideaThe sangha of monks and nuns lives by the monastic rules of the Vinaya and depends on laypeople, who give food and support in return for teaching.

Just after sunrise in a town in northern Thailand, a line of monks in orange robes walks barefoot down the street. Each carries a covered bowl. Along the way, laypeople kneel and place rice, fruit and curry in the bowls, and the monks chant a short blessing in return. This is the , and it has taken place in Buddhist countries for more than two thousand years. The monks do not ask for anything. They simply walk, and the community decides whether to give.

The alms round shows how the works. In the strict sense the sangha is the order of fully ordained monks, called bhikkhus, and nuns, called bhikkhunis. Buddhist tradition says the Buddha founded the order of monks early in his teaching. It also says that his aunt and foster mother, Mahapajapati, asked to be ordained and became the first nun. Monks and nuns own almost nothing, do not marry, and devote their lives to study, meditation and teaching.

Their life is governed by the , the collection of monastic rules. In the Theravada version, fully ordained monks keep 227 rules and nuns keep more than 300. The rules cover large matters, such as celibacy and not killing, and small ones, such as how to wear a robe and when to eat. Each year, during the rainy season, monks traditionally stay in one monastery for about three months of study and practice. This retreat is called vassa.

The relationship between monks and laypeople is an exchange. Laypeople give , meaning generosity: food, robes, medicine and support for the monastery. Monks give the teaching, blessings and an example of the path. Buddhists believe giving produces good karma, so the gift benefits the giver as well. In some Theravada countries the line of fully ordained nuns died out centuries ago. Since the 1990s some women have been ordained again, and Buddhists continue to debate whether that ordination is valid.

Main ideaBuddhism traveled out of India by sea to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia and by the Silk Road to China, and from there to Korea, Japan and Tibet.

A bodhisattva is a being who has vowed to become a buddha, not for personal release alone but in order to free all living beings from suffering. Some say it once; some say it thousands of times a day. For some of the largest schools of Japanese Buddhism, this simple act of calling a Buddha’s name lies at the center of religious life.

, called Amida in Japanese, is a buddha described in Mahayana sutras that were popular in China from early on. According to these texts, long ago as a bodhisattva he made a series of vows. One promised that anyone who sincerely called on him would be reborn in his , a realm of beauty and peace in the west. There, free of the distractions of ordinary life, beings could reach awakening easily. Pure Land practice spread widely in China, Korea, Vietnam and Japan.

In Japan, the monk Honen (1133–1212) taught that in a troubled age most people could not master the difficult practices of monks. They could, however, call on Amida with trust. His follower Shinran (1173–1263) went further. He taught that awakening comes entirely through Amida’s power, called other-power, not through one’s own effort. For Shinran the nembutsu is an expression of gratitude. He also married, and priests of the school he inspired, Jodo Shinshu, may marry and raise families.

Pure Land shows how varied Buddhism is. A Theravada teacher stresses that each person must walk the path alone; a Shinran follower stresses trust in the power of another. Both call themselves followers of the Buddha, and each can explain from its own texts why its approach fits. A careful student describes both without deciding which is the real Buddhism. Pure Land temples are among the oldest Buddhist congregations in the United States.

Zen in Japan

Main ideaZen, which grew from Chinese Chan, emphasizes seated meditation and direct insight, and it shaped Japanese arts from gardens to tea.

At the temple of Ryoanji in Kyoto, visitors sit on a wooden porch facing a rectangle of raked white gravel with fifteen stones set in it. There are no plants except moss, no water and no explanation. The garden is usually dated to the late fifteenth century, and no one knows for certain what it means. Many visitors simply sit and look. The garden is a product of , a school of Buddhism that values direct seeing over words.

Zen began in China, where it was called Chan. Both words come from the Sanskrit dhyana, meaning meditation. Chan tradition traces its lineage to Bodhidharma, an Indian monk said to have come to China around the fifth or sixth century; historians treat much of his story as legend. Chan teachers stressed that awakening is not learned from books but seen directly, and that every person already has the nature of a buddha. Chan grew in dialogue with Daoist ideas of naturalness and simplicity.

Zen reached Japan in the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The monk Eisai (1141–1215) brought the Rinzai school, which uses the , a puzzling question or story that a teacher gives a student, such as the question of what one’s face was before one’s parents were born. The koan cannot be solved by logic, and working on it is meant to break through ordinary thinking. The monk Dogen (1200–1253) founded the Soto school, which centers on , simply sitting in meditation, which Dogen treated as awakening itself.

Zen shaped Japanese culture well beyond the temple. The tea ceremony, ink painting, calligraphy, rock gardens and some martial arts all developed under its influence. In the twentieth century, writers such as D. T. Suzuki introduced Zen to American readers, and Zen centers opened in many American cities. Some Zen teachers caution that the art and style are only the surface of Zen. At its core, they say, is the daily discipline of sitting.

Vajrayana and the Dalai Lama

Main ideaVajrayana Buddhism, rooted in Tibet, adds mantras, mandalas and teacher lineages to Mahayana, and its best-known figure is the Dalai Lama.

In a school gym or museum hall, four Tibetan monks lean over a wooden platform for days. Using thin metal funnels, they tap out colored sand grain by grain into a detailed circular design of palaces, gates and symbols. When the design is finished, they chant, then sweep it all into a pile and pour the sand into a river. This sand has been made in many American cities. The monks say its destruction teaches impermanence.

The mandala belongs to , the diamond or thunderbolt vehicle, the form of Buddhism that grew in India and was carried to Tibet from the seventh and eighth centuries CE. Vajrayana accepts the Mahayana bodhisattva ideal and adds practices its followers regard as especially powerful. These include the mandala, a sacred diagram of an awakened world; the , a sacred phrase such as Om mani padme hum, linked with the bodhisattva of compassion; and visualization of buddhas in meditation.

Vajrayana stresses the bond between student and teacher. A Tibetan teacher is called a . Tibetan Buddhists hold that some lamas are reborn again and again to continue their work, and that each rebirth, called a tulku, can be recognized as a child. The most famous line is the , the most prominent teacher of the Gelug school. Tibetan Buddhists regard him as a human form of Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion.

The fourteenth Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso, was born in 1935. After China took control of Tibet in the 1950s, he fled to India in 1959, and he has led a Tibetan community in exile from Dharamsala. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1989. Tibetan Buddhism is also practiced in Mongolia, Bhutan, Nepal and parts of India and Russia. Questions about Tibet’s government and about who will choose the next Dalai Lama remain political as well as religious.

Buddhism Meets Confucius and Laozi

Main ideaIn China, Buddhism met Confucian family values and Daoist ideas, and over centuries the three were described as three teachings that could live side by side.

Every summer, in the seventh lunar month, Chinese families in China, Taiwan, Singapore and overseas communities hold the Ghost Festival. They offer food to ancestors and to wandering spirits, and temples hold services for the dead. The festival is linked to a Buddhist story about a monk named Mulian. He discovers that his mother is suffering in a hell and, on the Buddha’s advice, rescues her by making offerings to the sangha.

The story answers a real problem. When Buddhism arrived in China, it met a culture shaped by Confucian teaching, which places , loyalty and care owed to one’s parents and ancestors, at the center of a good life. Critics asked how a son could be filial if he shaved his head, left his family and had no children to continue the ancestral rites. The Mulian story shows a monk whose religious life saves his mother. That message made Buddhism fit Chinese family values.

Buddhism also met , the Chinese tradition centered on the Dao, the Way. Early translators sometimes borrowed Daoist words to explain Buddhist ideas; the word dao itself was used for the path. Great translators such as Kumarajiva, around 400 CE, produced Chinese versions of many sutras, and the monk Xuanzang traveled to India and back in the seventh century to collect texts. His journey later inspired the famous Chinese novel Journey to the West.

The meeting was not always peaceful. Some emperors supported Buddhism generously, while others restricted it; in 845 CE the Tang emperor Wuzong closed thousands of monasteries and forced many monks and nuns back to lay life. Over time, however, many Chinese came to speak of the , Confucianism, Daoism and Buddhism, as complementary. A family might honor ancestors in Confucian ways, visit a Daoist temple and hold Buddhist funeral rites. The next unit studies Confucianism and Daoism in depth.

Buddhism in America and Chicago

Main ideaBuddhism came to America through Asian immigrants and Western converts, was introduced at Chicago's 1893 Parliament, and today lives in temples across the Chicago area.

In September 1893, at the World’s Parliament of Religions in Chicago, a young man from Sri Lanka in a white robe rose to speak. He was , a Buddhist reformer. Also on the program was Soyen Shaku, a Japanese Zen abbot. For many in the audience it was their first chance to hear Buddhists describe their tradition in their own words. Soyen Shaku’s student and translator, D. T. Suzuki, later spent years in America and became one of Zen’s best-known writers in English.

By then Buddhism was already in America through immigrants. Chinese workers built temples in California in the 1850s, and Japanese immigrants founded Pure Land temples in Hawaii and on the West Coast; their national organization became the . During World War II the U.S. government forced Japanese Americans on the West Coast into camps. After release, thousands resettled in Chicago, and they founded or strengthened Japanese Buddhist temples on the city’s North Side.

A second wave came after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened the country to more Asian immigrants. Thai, Vietnamese, Cambodian, Lao, Korean, Chinese, Tibetan and Sri Lankan Buddhists built temples across the country. The Chicago area now holds dozens of them, from Japanese and Thai temples to Vietnamese, Cambodian and Tibetan centers. Some serve mainly one immigrant community and use its language, while others draw many converts.

Scholars often describe two broad groups among American Buddhists. come from families and cultures where Buddhism has long been practiced. Convert Buddhists come from other backgrounds, and many first met Buddhism through meditation. The two groups sometimes practice quite differently, and people debate how they should relate. Surveys suggest that about 1 percent of American adults are Buddhist, while many more practice meditation without calling themselves Buddhist.
