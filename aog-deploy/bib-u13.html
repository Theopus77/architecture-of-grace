The New Testament in Its World

The New Testament

The New Testament is short. Its 27 books fit in the back quarter of most Bibles. Yet they were written across a huge world, from Jerusalem to Rome, in a time of Roman rule, Jewish debate and long sea voyages. This unit reads those books inside that world. It asks who ruled, who argued, who traveled and who wrote.

You will read the four Gospels as four portraits of one teacher, alike and different. You will follow the book of Acts from a Jerusalem festival to a Roman prison. You will read letters as letters, with senders, receivers and reasons. You will open Revelation and learn how an apocalypse works. And you will trace how these books, and not others, became a canon.

Throughout, the course describes and attributes. It says what the text says and what Christians, Jews, Muslims and scholars have made of it. It does not say who is right. By the end you will be able to name each kind of writing, place it in its time, and explain how two readers can read the same page differently.

Gospels: Four Portraits, One Roman World

The Gospels
Big questionHow do four different Gospels, written in a Roman world, tell the story of one teacher from Galilee?
The story

A Coin and a Trap

In the Temple courts, a small silver coin becomes the center of a question meant to catch a teacher out.

Matthew 22 tells of a day in Jerusalem, in the courts of the Temple. Jesus is teaching. A group walks up with a question. Some are Pharisees, teachers of the law. Others, the text says, are supporters of Herod, the family Rome let rule. They do not agree on much. But today they have one plan.

Their question sounds polite. Is it right, they ask, to pay the tax to Caesar, or not? The trap is in the two answers. Say yes, and the crowd hears a teacher taking Rome's side. Say no, and the Romans hear a rebel. Either way, the questioners win.

Jesus asks to see the coin used for the tax. Someone hands him a denarius. On it is the face of the emperor and his title. Whose picture is this, he asks, and whose name? Caesar's, they answer. Then give Caesar what is Caesar's, he says, and give God what is God's.

The Gospel says the questioners were amazed, and they walked away. Readers have argued for two thousand years about what the answer means. But the scene shows something else too. It shows a world. There is a Temple, a Roman tax, a coin with an emperor's face, and groups who disagree about how to live under Rome.

This chapter reads the four Gospels inside that world. We will meet the rulers, the groups and the places. We will ask when each Gospel was written, and why they are alike and different. And we will read the stories closely, as literature and history, the way a class in a public school can.

Section 1

Judea and Galilee Under Rome

25.1

Rome, Herod and Pilate

Main ideaThe Gospels are set in a land ruled by Rome through local kings and Roman governors.

The Gospels tell their stories in a small corner of the Roman Empire. Rome took control of the region in 63 BCE. It ruled through local kings at first. The best known is the Great, a king who rebuilt the Temple in Jerusalem on a grand scale. Matthew 2 sets the birth of Jesus in his reign. Herod died c. 4 BCE, and Rome split his kingdom among his sons.

Galilee, in the north, went to Herod Antipas. Luke 3 names him as ruler when John the Baptist begins to preach. Judea, in the south, soon came under a Roman governor instead. The governor in the Gospels is Pontius . He held office c. 26 to 36 CE. All four Gospels say Pilate ordered the death of Jesus. Rome, not local rulers, held the power to execute.

Roman rule meant taxes, soldiers and coins with the emperor’s face. It also meant a , a count of people for taxing. Luke 2 opens the birth story with a census. Historians debate the date and details of that census. What is clear is that the Gospels expect readers to know this world. A tax collector, a centurion and a governor all appear as ordinary parts of the scene.

In 1961 archaeologists at Caesarea found a stone with Pilate’s name and title on it. It is the first object outside written texts to name him. Finds like this do not prove or disprove what the Gospels say about him. They show that the setting is a real place and time that can be studied.

25.2

The Temple and the Groups

Main ideaJewish life in the Gospels centered on the Temple, and several groups disagreed about how to keep the Torah under Rome.

The Temple in Jerusalem was the center of Jewish worship in the time of the Gospels. Priests offered sacrifices there. Pilgrims came for festivals like Passover. Herod’s rebuilding made it one of the largest sacred sites in the Roman world. The Gospels return to it again and again. Jesus teaches in its courts and, in a famous scene, overturns the tables of the money changers.

Within Jewish life, groups differed about how to live faithfully. The were teachers who applied the Torah to daily life. They believed in resurrection and in oral traditions passed down from teacher to student. Many scholars see them as the ancestors of later rabbinic Judaism. The Gospels show Jesus often arguing with Pharisees, but also eating with them and sharing many of their views.

The were linked to the priests and the Temple. The Gospels say they did not believe in resurrection. The historian Josephus describes them as a small group with influence among the wealthy. A third group, the , lived apart, some in a community by the Dead Sea. The Gospels never name them. A fourth kind of people, whom Josephus and the Gospels call , wanted to resist Rome by force.

Reading the Gospels well means keeping these groups straight. It also means care. Christian readers have sometimes turned "Pharisee" into an insult. Jewish readers point out that the Pharisees were careful, serious teachers. A fair reading names each group by what it taught, not by a verdict.

25.3

The Dead Sea Scrolls

Main ideaScrolls found near the Dead Sea in 1947 give a rare window into Jewish life and scripture in the time of the Gospels.

In 1947 a shepherd near the Dead Sea threw a stone into a cave and heard pottery break. Inside were jars holding old scrolls. Over the next years, searchers found more caves near a site called . Together the finds are called the Dead Sea . They hold about 900 manuscripts, most in pieces, written mainly in Hebrew and Aramaic.

The scrolls date from about 250 BCE to 70 CE. That is the world just before and during the time of the Gospels. About a quarter of them are copies of books of the Hebrew Bible. Every book except Esther is present. Before 1947, the oldest complete Hebrew Bible manuscripts were from around 1000 CE. The scrolls pushed the evidence back a thousand years.

Other scrolls belong to the community itself. They include rules for shared life, hymns, and commentaries on the prophets. Many scholars think the community was Essene, though not all agree. The scrolls never mention Jesus or the early Christians. Their value for Gospel study is the setting. They show how some Jews of that time read scripture, waited for God to act, and shared meals and property.

Studying the scrolls is a good example of careful reading. Scholars compare a scroll of Isaiah with later copies and note the differences. They date the ink and the handwriting. They ask what the text says before asking what it means. That is the same method this course uses for the Gospels.

Section 2

Four Books and Their Dates

25.4

What a Gospel Is

Main ideaA gospel is an ancient kind of life-story written to persuade, and the New Testament has four of them.

The word comes from an Old English word meaning "good news." It translates the Greek word euangelion. Mark 1:1 opens with it: the beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ. At first the word meant the message itself. Later it became the name for the four books that tell the story of Jesus: Matthew, Mark, Luke and John.

A gospel is a genre, a kind of writing. It looks most like an ancient , a life of a famous person. Greek and Roman writers produced many of these. But the Gospels have a purpose beyond information. John 20:31 says its stories were written so that readers may believe. The Gospels tell a life to persuade, and they say so.

That shapes how a careful reader works. A gospel is not a modern news report. It chooses scenes, orders them, and repeats themes. Each writer, whom tradition names an , has a point of view. Reading a gospel well means asking what each writer includes, leaves out and emphasizes. Christians read the Gospels as scripture. Scholars also read them as ancient texts with authors and audiences. A public-school class can do the second while respecting the first.

25.5

When the Gospels Were Written

Main ideaMost scholars date the Gospels to c. 65–100 CE, with Mark first and John last.

None of the Gospels names its author inside the text. The names Matthew, Mark, Luke and John come from early Christian tradition, attached by the second century. Scholars date the books by clues inside them and by when other writers first quote them. Most place all four between about 65 and 100 CE. That is one to three generations after the events they describe.

Most scholars think came first, around 65 to 75 CE. Mark 13 seems to know the war with Rome that ended with the Temple’s destruction in 70 CE. Matthew and Luke are usually dated c. 80 to 90 CE. Both appear to use Mark as a source. John is usually placed c. 90 to 100 CE. Its style and stories differ from the other three.

Some scholars argue for earlier dates, and some for later. The dates are estimates, so this course writes "c." before them. What matters for a reader is the order and the gap. The Gospels were written in Greek, decades after Jesus, for communities across the Roman world. Before that, stories about Jesus were passed on by word of mouth, what scholars call .

Christians hold different views about what this means. Some stress that the writers relied on eyewitnesses. Others stress the freedom each writer took. A class does not have to settle that. It can note the evidence, hold the dates loosely and read each book on its own terms.

25.6

The Synoptics and John

Main ideaMatthew, Mark and Luke share so much that they are called the Synoptics; John tells the story its own way.

Lay Matthew, Mark and Luke side by side and a pattern appears. They share many of the same stories, often in the same order and with the same words. Because they can be "seen together," they are called the Gospels. Most scholars explain the likeness by saying Matthew and Luke both used Mark. Many also think they shared a second source of sayings, which scholars call . Not everyone agrees on Q.

John is different. It has no parables of the familiar kind and no birth story. It has long speeches and a few great signs, like turning water into wine. Jesus in John speaks openly of who he is, using phrases like "I am the bread of life." John also tells of several trips to Jerusalem, where the Synoptics tell of one. Most scholars think John was written last and knew the Synoptic tradition, but wrote independently.

Christians have read the four together for a long time. In the second century some tried to merge them into one story. The church kept four instead. Readers have compared them to four portraits of one person, each from a different angle. A careful reader in a public school does the same thing. Notice what each Gospel shares, and notice where each stands alone.

John 1 does not describe the baptism at all; John the Baptist reports what he saw.

Section 3

Reading the Teaching

25.7

Parables Close Up

Main ideaA parable is a short story that makes the listener think, and the Gospels record dozens of them.

A is a short story drawn from daily life that carries a point about something bigger. Jesus in the Synoptics teaches in parables about seeds, sheep, coins, workers and feasts. Mark 4 says he taught the crowds this way. Jewish teachers of the same era also used short stories, called meshalim in Hebrew, so parables would have been a familiar form.

Take the sower in Mark 4. A farmer scatters seed. Some falls on the path, some on rock, some among thorns, some on good soil. Only the last grows. The story ends, and the listener is left to work out what the seed and the soils mean. Mark then adds an explanation given to the disciples in private. Many scholars think that layering shows how the early church used the story.

Parables often surprise. In Luke 10 a man is robbed and left on the road. A priest and a Levite pass by. The one who stops is a , a member of a group that Jews of Judea and Galilee often distrusted. The story turns the listener’s expectation over. Jesus ends by asking which of the three was a neighbor. The lawyer who asked cannot even say the word Samaritan. He says, the one who showed mercy.

Readers have taken parables many ways. Some early Christians read every detail as a symbol. Many modern scholars read each parable for its one sharp point. Either way, the form invites the reader to do the work. That is why a class can discuss a parable without being told what to believe.

25.8

The Sermon on the Mount

Main ideaMatthew 5–7 gathers Jesus's teaching into one long sermon that opens with blessings and closes with a rule for living.

Matthew 5 to 7 is the longest block of teaching in the Gospels. Matthew sets it on a mountain, so readers call it the Sermon on the . Luke 6 has a shorter, similar sermon set on a plain. Many scholars think Matthew gathered sayings from different times into one speech. Christians have long read it as a summary of Jesus’s teaching.

It opens with the , a set of blessings. Blessed are the poor in spirit, the mourners, the meek, those who hunger for what is right, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers. The pattern echoes the Psalms, which also begin with "blessed." Each line names a group that the world overlooks and says the kingdom belongs to them.

The middle of the sermon is a series of contrasts. "Ye have heard that it was said... but I say unto you." Jesus quotes a commandment and then presses it deeper. Do not kill becomes do not stay angry. Love your neighbor becomes love your enemies. Christians and scholars disagree about whether this replaces the Torah or intensifies it. Matthew 5:17 says Jesus came not to destroy the law but to fulfill it.

The sermon includes the Lord’s Prayer and ends with the : treat others as you would want to be treated. The Jewish teacher Hillel, a generation earlier, is remembered in the Talmud as teaching a similar rule in a negative form. Comparing the two is a good way to see Jesus inside the Jewish teaching of his time.

25.9

Miracle Stories

Main ideaThe Gospels tell of healings, feedings and stilled storms, and readers interpret these stories in different ways.

A large share of Mark is made of stories. Jesus heals a man with a withered hand, feeds a crowd with five loaves and two fish, and calms a storm on the Sea of Galilee. The Gospels use several words for these events. Mark and Matthew tend to say "works of power." John calls them and gives seven of them, each pointing to who Jesus is.

The stories share a shape. There is a need, an act or a word, and a response from the crowd. Mark 2 tells of a paralyzed man lowered through a roof by his friends. Jesus first says the man’s sins are forgiven. Teachers of the law object. Then Jesus tells the man to rise and walk. The story is as much about the argument as about the healing.

Readers handle these stories in different ways. Many Christians believe the events happened as told. Some Christians read them as signs with a meaning beyond the event. Historians usually say they cannot judge whether a miracle occurred, but they can study what the stories meant to the people who told them. Jewish and Muslim readers have their own views; the Qur’an, for example, describes Jesus healing the sick by God’s permission.

A public-school class does not decide whether a miracle happened. It can ask what each story says, what the people in it do and say, and how the story fits the Gospel it belongs to. That is reading, not believing or disbelieving.

Think it through

One reader says, this shows Jesus's power over nature. A second says, the point is the question about fear. A third says, a historian cannot test the weather, but can see that the story made the disciples look weak and Jesus strong.

Which reader is reading the text as scripture, which as literature, and which as history? Can one person do all three?

Section 4

The Passion and the Resurrection

25.10

The Passion Narrative

Main ideaAll four Gospels end with a long account of Jesus's last week, arrest, trial and death, called the Passion.

The word comes from a Latin word for suffering. It names the part of each Gospel that tells Jesus’s last days in Jerusalem. All four Gospels give it more space than any other stretch of time. In Mark, the last week fills about a third of the book. Some scholars have called Mark a Passion story with a long introduction.

The outline is shared. Jesus enters Jerusalem before Passover. He shares a last meal with his disciples. He prays in a garden called Gethsemane. He is arrested at night, questioned by the high priest, and handed to Pilate. Pilate sentences him to , a Roman punishment for rebels and slaves. He dies and is buried in a tomb.

The details differ. Mark and Matthew give one last saying from the cross, a line from Psalm 22. Luke gives three others. John gives three more. The Synoptics place the last supper as a Passover meal. John places the death on the day the Passover lambs were killed. Readers have worked hard to fit the accounts together. Many scholars prefer to read each on its own.

The Passion has a painful history. Some Christians over the centuries used it to blame Jewish people as a whole for Jesus’s death. That reading fed violence against Jews. In 1965 the Catholic Church formally rejected it, and many Protestant churches have as well. A careful reader notes that the Gospels describe a Roman execution, ordered by a Roman governor, in a city of many opinions.

Think it through
Two Timelines of One Week

Mark 14 says the last supper was the Passover meal, eaten the night before Jesus died. John 13 and 19 say Jesus died on the day of preparation, before the Passover meal was eaten, at the hour the lambs were killed. Both cannot be a simple calendar record. Some readers say John moved the date to make a point: Jesus dies as the Passover lamb. Others say Mark's timeline is the one with the symbolic shape.

What might each Gospel gain by placing the death where it does? Which kind of question is this, history or literature, or both?

25.11

The Resurrection Accounts

Main ideaEach Gospel ends with an empty tomb and, as Christians believe, a risen Jesus, and each tells it differently.

Every Gospel ends at a tomb. Women come early on the first day of the week. The stone is rolled away. The body is gone. From there the accounts differ. Mark’s oldest manuscripts end at 16:8 with the women fleeing in fear, saying nothing. Matthew adds guards, an earthquake and a meeting in Galilee. Luke tells of two travelers on the road to Emmaus. John tells of Mary Magdalene in the garden and of Thomas, who doubts and then believes.

Christians believe that Jesus rose from the dead. This is the center of the Christian faith. Paul, writing before the Gospels, says in 1 Corinthians 15 that if Christ did not rise, faith is empty. Easter, the yearly Christian celebration of the resurrection, is the oldest Christian festival. Christians differ about how to picture it, but the belief unites them.

Readers outside the faith read the stories differently. Jewish tradition does not accept the resurrection. Muslims honor Jesus as a prophet, and most read the Qur’an as saying he was not killed on the cross. Historians note that the earliest followers plainly believed Jesus had appeared to them. Whether that belief was true is a question of faith, not history, and this course does not answer it.

What a class can do is read closely. Who comes to the tomb in each Gospel? Who speaks? Where do the followers meet Jesus, in Galilee or Jerusalem? Noting the differences is not an attack on the stories. Christian scholars have done it for centuries, and many see the differences as a sign that no one edited the accounts to match.

The story

A Letter About a Brother

A prisoner writes to a man he knows well, about a slave who ran away and came back with a new name for himself.

Somewhere in the Roman Empire, in the 50s or 60s CE, a man sits in custody. He cannot travel, so he writes. His name is Paul, and he has a friend beside him named Onesimus. Onesimus is a slave. He has run away from his owner, a Christian named Philemon, who hosts a church in his house.

Under Roman law a runaway slave could be punished harshly. Paul knows this. He also knows Philemon. So he writes the shortest letter that carries his name, only 25 verses. He does not order Philemon to do anything. He says he could, but he would rather ask.

The letter is careful and warm. Paul calls Onesimus his child. He says he is sending him back, and that Philemon should receive him no longer as a slave, but as a beloved brother. He adds that if Onesimus owes anything, Paul will pay it. Then he asks Philemon to prepare a guest room.

The letter never says what Philemon did. Readers have argued about it ever since. Some say Paul was quietly undoing slavery from inside. Others say he left the system in place and only asked for kindness. In the 1800s, Americans on both sides of the slavery debate quoted this letter. It is one page long, and it has never stopped being read.

This chapter follows the story from Jerusalem to Rome. It reads Acts, the book that tells how the movement spread. It reads the letters of Paul and others as letters, with senders, receivers and reasons. It opens Revelation, the strange last book. And it asks how these writings, and not others, became the New Testament.

A Sequel to Luke

Main ideaActs continues the Gospel of Luke and tells how Jesus's followers spread from Jerusalem toward Rome.

The Acts of the Apostles opens by looking back. Its first line speaks of a "former treatise," an earlier book, written to a man named Theophilus. The Gospel of Luke opens with the same name. The word means one who is sent. Acts uses it for the twelve closest followers of Jesus and, in places, for Paul. 26.4

The Roads and the Sea

Main ideaActs traces Paul's travels across the eastern Mediterranean, using the roads and shipping lanes of the Roman Empire.

Acts describes several long journeys. Paul’s base is in Syria, a large city where Acts 11 says followers were first called Christians. From there he travels with companions like Barnabas, Silas and Timothy. Maps of the New Testament usually draw three missionary journeys and a final voyage to Rome. The numbering is a reader’s tool; Acts itself does not count them.

The Roman Empire made such travel possible. Paved roads ran between major cities. Ships crossed the in the sailing season. Greek was spoken from Syria to Italy. Roman peace, the Pax Romana, kept most roads safe. Paul, a Roman citizen, could appeal to that system, and Acts shows him doing so more than once.

The journeys were still hard. In 2 Corinthians 11, Paul lists shipwrecks, beatings, hunger and nights without sleep. Acts 27 tells of a storm and a wreck on the island of Malta. Travel by land was slow, perhaps twenty miles a day on foot. A journey from Jerusalem to Rome could take months.

The pattern in each city repeats. Paul goes first to the synagogue, where he can argue from the scriptures. Some Jews and some Gentiles who worshiped with them join him. Others object. He moves on, leaving a small community behind. His letters, written later, go back to those communities.

Letters as a Genre

26.6

How an Ancient Letter Works

Main ideaPaul's letters follow the shape of ordinary Greek letters, with a greeting, a thanksgiving, a body and a farewell.

Thousands of ordinary letters from the Roman world survive on papyrus. They open with the sender’s name, then the receiver’s, then a greeting. Then comes the business, then a farewell. Paul’s letters use the same shape and stretch it. His greetings are long and his thanksgivings can run a whole chapter. Scholars call this form the .

Look at the opening of Romans. Paul names himself first, in a sentence that runs six verses. He names the receivers: all in Rome who are loved by God. Then comes his greeting. It replaces the usual Greek word for "hello" with two words that matter to him: and peace. Nearly every letter of his begins this way.

After the greeting comes a thanksgiving for the readers, then the body. The body is where Paul teaches, argues and answers questions. Near the end comes a section of practical advice. Then a farewell with greetings to named people. Romans 16 greets more than two dozen by name, including women like Phoebe, Priscilla and Junia.

Knowing the form helps a reader. A letter is one side of a conversation. Paul writes to a real community with a real problem. When he says "you," he means them, not every reader since. Understanding the , the situation that caused the letter, is the first step in reading one.

26.7

Thirteen New Testament letters open with Paul’s name. In the Bible they run from Romans to Philemon. That order is not the order they were written. It is roughly by length, longest first, with letters to churches before letters to persons. So Romans, the longest, comes first, and Philemon, the shortest, comes last.

Scholars ask which letters Paul wrote himself. Most agree on seven: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon. These are called the letters. About the other six, scholars disagree. Some think followers wrote in Paul’s name after his death, a common practice in the ancient world. Others think Paul wrote them all. Christians hold both views.

In the order of writing, 1 Thessalonians is usually put first, around 50 CE. That makes it the oldest book in the New Testament, older than any Gospel. Galatians and the Corinthian letters follow in the 50s. Romans, often called his most complete statement, is dated about 57 CE. Philippians and Philemon were written from prison, though scholars debate which prison.

Each letter has a subject. Galatians argues that Gentiles need not keep the whole Torah. 1 Corinthians answers a list of questions and includes the famous chapter on love. Romans lays out Paul’s whole way of thinking about , law and God’s promises to Israel. Reading a letter as a whole, from greeting to farewell, keeps each idea in its place.

26.9

Revelation: Visions Under Rome

Main ideaRevelation is an apocalypse, a book of visions and symbols written to encourage churches under Roman pressure.

The last book of the New Testament opens with a man named John on , a small island off the coast of Asia Minor. He says he is there because of the word of God. He writes to seven churches in nearby cities, including Ephesus. Then he describes visions: a throne, a scroll with seven seals, a dragon, beasts, a woman clothed with the sun, and at last a new heaven and a new earth.

Revelation belongs to a genre called , from a Greek word meaning unveiling. Daniel in the Hebrew Bible is the same kind of book. An apocalypse uses symbols, numbers and visions to speak to people in a hard time. Seven means completeness. A beast stands for an empire. Babylon, the city that destroyed the first Temple, stands in Revelation for Rome.

Most scholars date the book to about 95 CE, in the last years of the emperor . A second-century writer, Irenaeus, gives that date. Some argue for an earlier date under Nero. Either way, the churches faced pressure to honor the emperor as divine, and some had suffered. The book tells them to hold on, and it promises that Rome will fall and God will make all things new.

Christians have read Revelation in very different ways. Some read it as a map of the future. Others read it as a message for its own time that still speaks. Others read it as poetry of hope. Eastern Orthodox churches long left it out of their public readings. It is the only New Testament book that some early church lists were unsure about.

26.10

How 27 Books Became One

Main ideaThe New Testament canon formed over three centuries, and Athanasius's list of 367 CE is the first to match today's 27 books.

No one sat down in the first century and wrote the New Testament. Gospels and letters circulated one by one. Communities copied the ones they valued and read them aloud. Over time some writings were accepted almost everywhere: the four Gospels, Acts and Paul’s letters. Others were debated: Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude and Revelation. Still others, like the Shepherd of Hermas, were read for a while and then set aside.

The list of accepted books is called the , from a Greek word for a measuring rod. Several forces shaped it. Around 140 CE a teacher named Marcion proposed a short canon with one Gospel and ten letters of Paul, and rejected the Hebrew Bible. Most churches rejected his list, and in doing so began to state their own. A fragment from around 200 CE, called the Muratorian fragment, gives an early list close to today’s.

The first surviving list with exactly the 27 books of today’s New Testament comes from , bishop of Alexandria, in an Easter letter of 367 CE. Councils in North Africa in the 390s agreed. The order and the number have been stable since, though churches still differ on the Old Testament. The word "Bible" itself means "the books," plural.

Christians explain the canon in different ways. Some say the church recognized the books God had inspired. Others describe a human process guided by use, tradition and argument. Historians describe the process without ruling on the first claim. A class can trace how the list took shape and leave the question of why to each reader.

Why: Luke and Acts are both addressed to Theophilus, and Acts calls Luke its "former treatise."

2. What happens at Pentecost in Acts 2?

Why: Acts 2 tells of wind, fire and many languages at the festival of Pentecost, followed by Peter's speech.

3. Which of Paul's letters is thought to be the oldest book in the New Testament?

Why: 1 Thessalonians is usually dated c. 50 CE, before any Gospel.

4. What does Paul ask in his letter to Philemon?

Why: Philemon 16 asks that the returning Onesimus be received no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother.

5. Why are James, Peter, John and Jude called "catholic" letters?

Why: Catholic here means general or universal; most of these letters are not sent to one city.

6. What kind of book is Revelation, and which Hebrew Bible book is most like it?

Why: Both Revelation and Daniel use visions, symbols and numbers to speak hope in a time of pressure.

7. Which inscription helps date Paul's stay in Corinth?

Why: Acts 18 names Gallio as proconsul while Paul was in Corinth; the Delphi inscription dates his term to c. 51–52 CE.

8. What is the significance of Athanasius's letter of 367 CE?

Why: Athanasius's Easter letter gives the first list matching today's New Testament; Marcion rejected the Hebrew Bible, and Jerome made the Latin Bible.

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Which Roman official do all four Gospels say sentenced Jesus?

Why: Pilate, governor of Judea c. 26–36 CE, gives the sentence in every Gospel.

2. Which group in the Gospels was linked to the Temple priests?

Why: The Sadducees were the priestly party; the Essenes lived apart and the Zealots resisted Rome.

3. Where were the Dead Sea Scrolls found?

Why: The scrolls came from caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea, starting in 1947.

4. Which Gospel do most scholars think was written first?

Why: Mark is dated c. 65–75 CE, and Matthew and Luke appear to use it as a source.

5. Which Gospel has long "I am" speeches and seven signs?

Why: John stands apart from the Synoptics with discourses, "I am" sayings and seven signs.

6. In the parable of the Good Samaritan, who passes by the injured man?

Why: Luke 10 says a priest and a Levite pass by before the Samaritan stops.

7. Which set of blessings opens the Sermon on the Mount?

Why: Matthew 5 opens with the Beatitudes, each beginning "Blessed are."

8. What does this course say about whether the resurrection happened?

Why: The course attributes beliefs, notes other traditions' views, and does not rule on the claim.

9. Which book continues the Gospel of Luke?

Why: Acts opens by recalling its "former treatise" to Theophilus, the same person Luke addresses.

10. Where was Paul's base for his journeys?

Why: Acts 13 sends Paul out from Antioch in Syria, and he returns there between journeys.

11. How are Paul's letters arranged in the New Testament?

Why: Romans, the longest, is first and Philemon, the shortest, is last; churches come before persons.

12. What does Paul ask Philemon to do?

Why: Philemon 16 asks that Onesimus be received no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother; it does not say more.

13. Which Hebrew Bible book is most like Revelation in kind?

Why: Daniel and Revelation are both apocalypses, using visions and symbols in a time of pressure.

14. What does "Babylon" stand for in Revelation, most readers agree?

Why: Revelation borrows the name of the city that destroyed the first Temple for the empire of its own day, Rome.

15. Whose 367 CE letter first lists exactly the 27 books of the New Testament?

Why: Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, gave the first surviving list matching today's New Testament.

1. (Unit 12) Which psalm type is the largest group?

Why: Laments, cries for help that often end in trust, outnumber every other type.

Why: Nathan's promise about David's house is read by Jews as hope for a messiah from David's line and by Christians as fulfilled in Jesus.

Why: Exodus 2:5-10 says Pharaoh's daughter found the basket, took pity, and named the child Moses.

Why: Prophets like Amos delivered messages about what people were doing now and what would follow.

Why: The Hebrew le-David allows more than one reading, and the two kinds of readers differ.

How does a New Testament writer use the shape of a genre to make a point? Choose either a Gospel scene (for example Matthew 22:15–22 or Luke 10:25–37), a letter (Philemon 1–25 or 1 Corinthians 13), or a vision (Revelation 21:1–4). Cite two or three passages by book, chapter and verse. Explain what the text says, how its genre works, and how at least two kinds of readers have read it.

  • Name the book, chapter and verse every time you cite the text.
  • Attribute every belief: "Christians believe," "Jewish tradition reads," "many scholars think."
  • Compare readings; do not judge which is true.
  • Use the unit's terms: gospel, Synoptic, parable, Passion, epistle, apocalypse, canon.
  • Say what the text leaves unsaid as well as what it says.
