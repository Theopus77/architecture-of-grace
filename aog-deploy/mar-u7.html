Circle and Ring

Origins

Two arts in this unit meet in a shape. Capoeira is played in a roda, a circle of people with music. Boxing happens in a ring, a square with ropes. Both shapes have long histories, and both were once linked to trouble with the law.

Capoeira grew among Africans and their children in Brazil. A law of 1890 made it a crime. Teachers in Salvador later opened schools, and in 2014 UNESCO honored the capoeira circle. Boxing in England moved from bare-knuckle prize fights to the gloved Queensberry Rules of 1867.

In this unit, you will read old pictures, laws and rule books. You will explain how capoeira moved from the street to the academy. You will compare who runs boxing in schools, colleges, clubs and town gyms.

The big question

How can an art that the law once pushed out become a sport with schools, rule books and honors?

A Bow, a Song, a Circle

Capoeira
Big questionHow did capoeira go from a crime in Brazil's law books to a treasure honored by Brazil and the world?
The story

The Instrument on the Wall

In a school in Salvador, a berimbau hangs where a law once said the game was a crime.

Salvador is a port city in the state of Bahia, in northeast Brazil. In its old center, called the Pelourinho, there are capoeira schools up narrow stairs. On one wall hangs a berimbau, a wooden bow with one wire string and a gourd.

Beside the berimbau hang old black-and-white photographs. They show teachers who played capoeira in the early 1900s. Some of them played it when the law of Brazil called capoeira a crime. The police could arrest a person for it.

Today the same city has signs for tourists that point to capoeira schools. In 2014, UNESCO, a world body for education and culture, honored the capoeira circle. It added the circle to its list of treasures that people keep alive by doing them.

How does an art go from a police record to a world list? This chapter follows capoeira through old paintings, Brazilian laws, two famous teachers in Salvador and the schools they opened.

Before There Were Schools

13.1

Africans in Colonial Brazil

Main ideaCapoeira grew among enslaved Africans and their children in Brazil, which was a colony of Portugal.

Portugal ruled Brazil as a from the 1500s until 1822. Portuguese owners forced millions of Africans across the Atlantic Ocean. They made them work on sugar farms, in gold mines and in city houses. Brazil took in more enslaved Africans than any other country in the Americas.

Many of these people came from Central Africa, from lands around today’s Angola and Congo. Others came from West Africa. They brought their languages, songs, drums and games. In Brazil, they and their children made new traditions from them.

13.2

A Painter's Record

Main ideaA picture made by the painter Johann Moritz Rugendas, printed in 1835, is one of the oldest images of capoeira.

Johann Moritz Rugendas was a painter from Germany. He traveled in Brazil in the 1820s. He drew cities, forests, farms and the people he met. Later, his pictures were printed in a book about his trip, published in 1835.

One picture has the title Jogar Capoëra, which means "to play capoeira" in Portuguese. It shows two Black men facing each other in the middle of a group. People stand around them and watch. One man plays a drum. Rugendas also gave the picture a French title that called it a war dance.

The picture is a , a record made at the time. It shows that people played capoeira in a circle with music by the 1820s. But it was made by an outsider. Historians read it carefully, because a visitor may not have known what he was seeing.

13.3

Capoeira in Police Records

Main ideaIn the 1800s, police in Rio de Janeiro arrested people for capoeira, and their records are now sources for history.

Rio de Janeiro was Brazil’s capital for most of the 1800s. It was a busy port full of enslaved and free Black workers. City leaders feared groups of capoeira players on the streets. Police arrested people for playing capoeira, even before a national law named it.

Police wrote down each arrest in record books. The books list names, dates and the reason for the arrest. Historians have searched these books to learn who played capoeira. Many of those arrested were enslaved men. Others were free Black workers, and some were poor immigrants.

The records also name street groups called . Two large groups in Rio were the Nagoas and the Guaiamuns. They controlled different parts of the city. Police records tell us about them, but they tell the story from the side of the police.

A Law Against the Circle

13.4

The Golden Law of 1888

Main ideaBrazil ended slavery on May 13, 1888, the last country in the Americas to do so.

For more than 300 years, slavery was legal in Brazil. Enslaved people fought it in many ways. Some ran away and built free towns called quilombos. The most famous, Palmares, lasted for most of the 1600s.

On May 13, 1888, Princess Isabel signed the . In Portuguese it is called the Lei Áurea. It was very short. It said that slavery in Brazil was ended. Brazil was the last country in the Americas to end slavery.

The law did not give land, money or schooling to freed people. Many former enslaved people stayed poor. They moved to cities looking for work. Many capoeira players were among them, and city leaders soon aimed new laws at them.

13.5

The Penal Code of 1890

Main ideaIn 1890, Brazil's new Penal Code made playing capoeira in the streets a crime.

In November 1889, Brazil stopped having an emperor. It became a , a country led by elected leaders. The new government wrote new laws quickly. In 1890 it published a , the book of laws about crimes and punishments.

One chapter of the 1890 Penal Code was about people it called idle, and about capoeira players. Article 402 said that doing capoeira in streets and squares was a crime. The punishment was time in prison. Leaders of groups could be punished more.

The law was written only two years after slavery ended. It aimed at an art played mostly by Black Brazilians. Police used it to arrest players for decades. Many historians see it as one way the new republic tried to control Black city life.

It is a crime to do, in streets and public squares, the exercises of skill and body movement known as capoeira. The punishment is prison.

13.6

Keeping the Game Alive

Main ideaPlayers kept capoeira alive in yards and on docks until the law changed in 1940.

The 1890 law did not stop capoeira. In Salvador, in the state of Bahia, players met in backyards, at docks and at street festivals. Many worked as dock workers, market sellers or builders. Older players taught younger ones by playing with them.

Many capoeira histories say players used in those years. A nickname made a player harder to find in police records. Today many capoeira groups still give students a nickname. Groups describe it as a link to that past.

In 1940, Brazil wrote a new penal code. The new code did not name capoeira as a crime. After 50 years, playing capoeira in the street was no longer against the national law.

From Backyard to Academy

13.7

Mestre Bimba's Academy

Main ideaIn Salvador in 1932, Mestre Bimba opened one of the first capoeira academies, with set lessons and rules.

Manuel dos Reis Machado was born in Salvador in 1899. People called him Bimba. Mestre means master teacher in Portuguese. He said he learned capoeira as a boy from an African man who worked on ships.

In 1932, Bimba opened an , a school with a fixed room and set lessons. He called his style the Regional Fighting of Bahia. It later became known as Capoeira Regional. In 1937, the government of Bahia gave his school an official license.

Bimba’s academy had rules for students. It kept a list of members and held graduations. Many of his first students were university students and middle-class men. They brought capoeira into rooms that had once kept it out. In 1953, Bimba and his students showed capoeira to President Getúlio Vargas.

13.8

Mestre Pastinha and Angola

Main ideaIn 1941, Mestre Pastinha opened a center in Salvador to keep the older style, called Capoeira Angola.

Vicente Ferreira Pastinha was born in Salvador in 1889. People called him Mestre Pastinha. He said he learned capoeira as a boy from an African man named Benedito. He also worked as a painter and a shoemaker.

In 1941, Pastinha opened the Sports Center of Capoeira Angola. It later moved to the Pelourinho, in Salvador’s old center. is the name for the older style. The name honors Angola, in Central Africa, where many enslaved people had been taken from.

Pastinha wanted to keep the old songs, rituals and slow games. In 1964, he published a book called Capoeira Angola. In 1966, he led a group to Dakar, in Senegal, for the First World Festival of Negro Arts. He died in Salvador in 1981.

13.9

Brazil Names a Treasure

Main ideaBrazil named the capoeira circle a national treasure in 2008, and UNESCO added it to a world list in 2014.

Brazil has a government office that protects its history and culture. It is called IPHAN. In 2008, IPHAN listed two parts of capoeira as Brazilian . One was the , the circle where capoeira is played. The other was the work of the mestres who teach it.

In 2014, UNESCO added the capoeira circle to its world list of intangible cultural heritage. Intangible means it is not an object you can hold. It lives in songs, games and teaching. UNESCO is part of the United Nations.

These honors came 118 years and 124 years after the 1890 law. The same circle that police once broke up is now protected. Capoeira groups say that mestres and players, not laws, kept it alive in between.

13.10

Capoeira Travels the World

Main ideaStarting in the 1970s, Brazilian mestres moved abroad and opened capoeira schools in many countries.

In the 1970s, some Brazilian capoeira teachers moved to the United States and Europe. They taught in dance studios, colleges and community centers. By the 1990s, capoeira schools were open in many big cities around the world.

One teacher who moved was João Oliveira dos Santos, called Mestre João Grande. He had been a student of Mestre Pastinha in Salvador. He opened a Capoeira Angola school in New York City in the 1990s. In 2001, the National Endowment for the Arts gave him a National Heritage Fellowship.

Capoeira groups abroad often keep the Portuguese songs. Students learn Portuguese words to sing them. Many groups visit Salvador to meet the mestres. The circle that began in Brazil now meets in many languages, but the songs stay in Portuguese.

The story

Two Books on Two Desks

One desk holds a boxing rule book from 1867; another holds a school sports handbook.

On a library desk lies a small printed page from London. It lists twelve short rules for boxing. It is dated 1867. It asks for gloves, rounds of three minutes and a rest of one minute between them.

On a desk in an Illinois high school office lies a thick handbook. It is from the Illinois High School Association, the IHSA, which began in 1900. It lists the sports that schools may play for state titles. Wrestling is on the list. Boxing is not.

Across town, a boxing gym has a ring, heavy bags and a sign-up sheet. Young people train there after school. The gym follows rules too, but they come from a different group, not from the school.

This chapter asks how the rule book on the first desk came to be. Then it asks why boxing lives in clubs, newspaper tournaments and town gyms more than in school gyms.

Boxing Before Gloves

14.1

A Bronze Boxer in Rome

Main ideaAncient Greek and Roman boxers wrapped their hands in leather straps, and a bronze statue shows one at rest.

Greek writers said boxing joined the ancient Olympic Games in 688 BCE. Greek boxers did not wear soft gloves. They wrapped their hands and wrists in long leather . There were no rounds. A bout went on until one boxer gave up or could not go on.

In 1885, workers digging in Rome found a bronze statue of a seated boxer. Today it is called the Boxer at Rest. It was made more than 2,000 years ago. It is in the National Museum of Rome.

The statue shows leather straps wound around the boxer’s hands. It also shows a broken nose, cuts and swollen ears. Artists made the injuries on purpose. The statue is evidence that ancient boxing hurt the people who did it.

14.2

Broughton's Rules of 1743

Main ideaIn 1743, the London boxer Jack Broughton wrote seven rules, the first written rules for English boxing.

In the 1700s, boxing matches in England were called . Two men fought with bare fists for money. Crowds bet on the winner. Many towns saw the fights as disorder, and they were often against the law.

Jack Broughton was a champion boxer in London. He ran a building where fights were held, called an amphitheater. In 1743 he wrote seven rules for fights there. One rule gave a fallen boxer 30 seconds to come back to the middle. Another said no one could hit a man who was down.

Broughton also taught boxing to rich young men. For practice, he used padded gloves called . Real prize fights stayed bare-knuckle. Broughton’s rules guided English prize fights for nearly 100 years.

14.3

The Last Bare-Knuckle Title

Main ideaUnder the London Prize Ring Rules, fights had no set length; the last bare-knuckle heavyweight title fight in 1889 went 75 rounds.

In 1838, English boxing adopted new rules called the . They replaced Broughton’s rules. A round ended whenever a boxer went down. There was no limit on the number of rounds. Fights could last for hours.

Prize fights were illegal in many American states. Fans rode special trains to secret places so police could not stop the fight. On July 8, 1889, John L. Sullivan fought Jake Kilrain near Richburg, Mississippi. They fought with bare fists in the summer heat.

Sullivan won after 75 rounds. It was the last heavyweight fought with bare knuckles under London rules. Mississippi later charged Sullivan for breaking its law against prize fighting. After this, top boxers moved to gloves and the Queensberry Rules.

A Rule Book with Gloves

14.4

Who Wrote Queensberry

Main ideaJohn Graham Chambers wrote the Queensberry Rules; the Marquess of Queensberry lent them his name in 1867.

The rules carry the name of John Sholto Douglas, the ninth of Queensberry. A marquess is a high-ranking noble in Britain. He was a young sports fan who backed the new rules and gave cups as prizes.

The man who wrote the rules was John Graham Chambers. He was a Welsh sportsman who had rowed at Cambridge University. In 1866 he helped start the Athletic Club in London. It held contests for people who did not compete for money.

The rules were published in 1867. They were meant for amateur contests with gloves. Over the next 25 years, professional boxers took them up too. People still call boxing’s basic rules the Queensberry Rules.

14.5

Twelve Short Rules

Main ideaThe Queensberry Rules set three-minute rounds, a one-minute rest, a ten-second count and gloves.

The Queensberry Rules had just twelve short rules. They set each at three minutes. They gave one minute of rest between rounds. They banned wrestling and hugging in the ring.

A fallen boxer now had ten seconds to get up, not 30. If he could not, the other boxer won. Rule 10 asked for fair-sized gloves of the best quality. Under older rules, a round could last a few seconds or many minutes.

The clock changed boxing. Rounds of equal length made bouts easier to follow and to judge. Gloves made the bare-knuckle prize fight look old. Most boxing rules today still use three-minute rounds for men.

Each round lasts three minutes, and there is one minute of rest between rounds.

Gloves Win the Title

Main ideaIn 1892 in New Orleans, James J. Corbett beat John L. Sullivan in a heavyweight title fight with gloves under Queensberry Rules.

On September 7, 1892, John L. Sullivan fought James J. Corbett at the Olympic Club in New Orleans, Louisiana. Both men wore gloves. They fought under the Queensberry Rules, with three-minute rounds.

Sullivan was the famous bare-knuckle champion. Corbett was a former bank clerk from San Francisco. Newspapers called him Gentleman Jim. Corbett won in the 21st round and became the heavyweight champion.

The fight was held indoors, under electric lights, before a paying crowd. It was legal in New Orleans. The fight showed that a big title could be won with gloves, rounds and a under Queensberry Rules.

Think it through
Two Title Fights

1889: outdoors near Richburg, Mississippi, in the summer sun. Bare fists. No limit on rounds. Against state law. 1892: indoors in New Orleans, under electric lights. Gloves. Three-minute rounds. A legal event with tickets.

Amateur Boxing and the Olympics

Main ideaAmateur boxing got its own governing bodies and joined the modern Olympics in 1904; women's boxing joined in 2012.

In 1880, the Amateur Boxing Association was founded in England. It was a , a group that writes rules and runs championships. It held national amateur championships with gloves and short bouts.

Boxing joined the modern Olympic Games in 1904, in St. Louis, Missouri. All the boxers that year were from the United States. Olympic boxers were amateurs. Over the years, Olympic boxing added weight classes and new rules for safety.

Women boxed in exhibitions long before they could win Olympic medals. Women’s boxing first joined the Olympics in 2012, in London. Nicola Adams of Great Britain won the first women’s Olympic boxing gold medal, in the flyweight class.

Golden Gloves in Chicago

Main ideaNewspapers started Golden Gloves tournaments for amateur boxers in the late 1920s, and Chicago's became one of the best known.

In 1927, the New York Daily News started an amateur boxing tournament called the . Winners got a small golden glove charm. The next year, the Chicago Tribune ran its own Golden Gloves in Chicago.

Arch Ward, the Tribune’s sports editor, helped run the Chicago tournament. Boxers came from Chicago neighborhoods, from towns in Illinois and from nearby states. Champions from Chicago and New York then met in an intercity match.

The Golden Gloves did not belong to schools. A newspaper ran it, and clubs and gyms trained the boxers. It gave young amateurs a place to compete close to home. Many later professional champions started in Golden Gloves bouts.

14.9

College Boxing Ends

Main ideaThe NCAA held college boxing championships from 1932 to 1960 and stopped after a Wisconsin boxer died.

The , the National Collegiate Athletic Association, runs college sports championships. It held its first national boxing championships in 1932. For almost 30 years, college boxing drew big crowds. The University of Wisconsin had one of the strongest teams.

In April 1960, the NCAA boxing championships were held in Madison, Wisconsin. A Wisconsin boxer named Charlie Mohr was hurt in his final bout. He died several days later. His death shocked his school and the nation.

The University of Wisconsin ended its boxing team. The NCAA voted to stop its boxing championships after 1960. Today some colleges have boxing clubs run by students. They are clubs, not NCAA teams.

Think it through
A Team or a Club?

Before 1960, a college boxer wore the school's colors at an NCAA championship. Today, a college boxer may join a club that students run. The club finds its own coach and its own bouts, outside the NCAA.

What changed for college boxers after 1960? Why might a college choose a club instead of a team?
