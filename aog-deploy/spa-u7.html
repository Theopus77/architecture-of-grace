Who, Where, When, Why

Questions

Every conversation runs on questions. Who is the new student? Where is the bathroom? When is the party? How much does the empanada cost? In this unit you learn the small Spanish words that start those questions: quién, qué, dónde, cuándo, por qué, cómo and cuánto.

Each question word asks for one kind of answer. Quién wants a person. Dónde wants a place. Cuándo wants a time. You will learn to spot the accent mark that every question word carries, and to tell por qué (why) from porque (because).

Then you put the words to work. You will interview a classmate, answer in full sentences and use polite words like por favor, gracias, de nada and perdón. By the end, you can ask and answer the questions people use when they first meet.

The big question

How do questions help us get to know people and find out what we need?

The story

The Case of the Last Empanada

One empanada is missing, and Lupe asks questions until she finds out who took it.

Lupe's grandmother made a big plate of empanadas for the family party. An empanada is a folded pastry with a filling inside. At six o'clock, one empanada was left on the plate. At seven o'clock, the plate was empty. Lupe put her hands on her hips. "¿Quién se comió la última empanada?" she asked. (Who ate the last empanada?)

Her little brother Tomás shook his head. "¡Yo no!" (Not me!) Lupe tried a new question. "¿Dónde estabas a las siete?" (Where were you at seven?) Tomás pointed to the yard. "Afuera, con el perro." (Outside, with the dog.) Lupe looked at the dog. The dog looked at the floor.

Lupe asked her cousin Rafa. "¿Cuándo viste la empanada?" (When did you see the empanada?) Rafa thought hard. "A las seis. Estaba en la mesa." (At six. It was on the table.) Then Lupe asked, "¿Por qué hay migas aquí?" (Why are there crumbs here?) There was a trail of crumbs from the table to the back door.

Lupe followed the crumbs outside. There sat the dog, Canela, with crumbs on her nose. Everyone laughed. Grandma said, "¿Cómo lo sabías?" (How did you know?) Lupe smiled. "¡Con preguntas!" (With questions!) Each question word had pointed her to one new clue: who, where, when, why and how.

Section 1

Asking About People and Things

13.1

¿Quién? Asks Who

Main idea¿Quién? means who, and the answer is always a person.

Picture a knock at your door. You want to know who it is. In Spanish you ask (who?). ¿Quién es? means Who is it? The answer names a person: Es mi tía. (It is my aunt.) A question word is a word that starts a question and asks for one kind of answer.

Look at more examples. ¿Quién es tu maestra? (Who is your teacher?) ¿Quién canta? (Who is singing?) If you ask about more than one person, use (who, for a group). ¿Quiénes son ellos? (Who are they?) The -es ending shows that the answer is more than one person.

Spanish questions start with an upside-down mark, ¿, and end with ?. English speakers often forget the first one. Also look at the accent mark on the é in quién. Every question word in this chapter carries a written accent. Without it, the word is spelled wrong.

Think it through
Who Is at the Door?

Mamá: ¿Quién es? (Who is it?) Voz: Soy yo, Lupe. (It is me, Lupe.) Mamá: ¿Quiénes están contigo? (Who is with you?) Lupe: Tomás y Rafa. (Tomás and Rafa.)

Point to a classmate and ask a partner ¿Quién es? Your partner answers with Es and the name.

¿quiénes?
who? for a group — ¿Quiénes son ellos? (Who are they?)

13.2

¿Qué? Asks What

Main idea¿Qué? means what, and it asks about a thing or an action.

Picture a friend holding a gift box. You want to know what is inside. You ask (what?). ¿Qué es? means What is it? Your friend answers: Es un libro. (It is a book.) The answer names a thing, not a person.

¿Qué? can also ask about an action. ¿Qué haces? means What are you doing? The answer is a verb: Leo. (I am reading.) Try more. ¿Qué comes? (What are you eating?) Como una manzana. (I am eating an apple.) Look at the question word first. It tells you what kind of answer to give.

Watch the accent. Qué with an accent asks a question. Que without an accent means that, as in Creo que sí (I think so). English speakers often mix up ¿qué? and ¿quién? Remember: qué is for things, quién is for people.

Think it through

Ana: ¿Qué es? (What is it?) Beto: Es un regalo. (It is a gift.) Ana: ¿Qué hay adentro? (What is inside?) Beto: ¡Un libro de dinosaurios! (A dinosaur book!)

Hold up something from your desk. Ask a partner ¿Qué es? and have your partner answer Es un… or Es una…

13.3

¿Cómo? Asks How

Main idea¿Cómo? means how, and it asks what something is like or how someone is.

Picture a friend coming back from being sick. You want to know how she feels. You ask (how?). ¿Cómo estás? means How are you? She answers: Estoy bien. (I am fine.) You already know this question from greetings.

¿Cómo? also asks what something is like. ¿Cómo es tu perro? (What is your dog like?) Es pequeño y negro. (It is small and black.) Notice that English says what here, but Spanish says cómo. That surprises many English speakers. Think of it as How is your dog, in looks?

Watch the accent again. Cómo with an accent asks how. Como without an accent can mean I eat. Como tacos means I eat tacos. The accent changes a question word into a different word. So write cómo with an accent every time you ask.

Think it through
What Is It Like?

Step 1: Pick a thing: tu perro (your dog). Step 2: Put ¿Cómo es…? in front: ¿Cómo es tu perro? Step 3: Answer with es and describing words: Es grande y café. (It is big and brown.)

Ask a partner ¿Cómo es tu casa? or ¿Cómo es tu mascota? Answer with Es and two describing words.

Words to know

como

Section 2

Place, Time and Amount

13.4

¿Dónde? Asks Where

Main idea¿Dónde? means where, and the answer is a place.

Picture yourself looking for your backpack. You ask your brother (where?). ¿Dónde está mi mochila? means Where is my backpack? He answers: Está en la cocina. (It is in the kitchen.) The answer is a place.

Look at the verb in the question. To ask where something is, Spanish uses está, from estar. ¿Dónde está el baño? (Where is the bathroom?) ¿Dónde están mis zapatos? (Where are my shoes?) Use están when you ask about more than one thing.

A common mistake is ¿Dónde es el baño? Use estar, not ser, for where something is. Another mistake is dropping the accent on the o. Write dónde with the accent. The word (in, at) often starts the answer: en la escuela, en casa.

Think it through
The Lost Backpack

Lupe: ¿Dónde está mi mochila? (Where is my backpack?) Tomás: ¿En tu cuarto? (In your room?) Lupe: No. (No.) Tomás: ¡Está en la cocina, en la silla! (It is in the kitchen, on the chair!)

13.5

¿Cuándo? Asks When

Main idea¿Cuándo? means when, and the answer is a time, a day or a date.

Picture your friend talking about a party. You want to know when it is. You ask (when?). ¿Cuándo es la fiesta? means When is the party? She answers: Es el sábado. (It is on Saturday.) The answer is a time or a day.

Here ser is correct, because an event is happening. ¿Cuándo es tu cumpleaños? (When is your birthday?) Es el 5 de mayo. (It is May 5.) Notice the order: the day comes first, then de, then the month. Spanish writes months with small letters.

Holidays make good practice. ¿Cuándo es el Día de la Independencia de México? It is el 16 de septiembre. ¿Cuándo es el Día de los Muertos? It is el 1 y el 2 de noviembre. Remember the accent: cuándo, with the mark on the a.

13.6

¿Cuánto? Asks How Much

Main idea¿Cuánto? asks how much, and ¿cuántos? or ¿cuántas? asks how many.

Picture a food stand with empanadas. You want to know the price. You ask (how much?). ¿Cuánto cuesta? means How much does it cost? The seller answers: Dos dólares. (Two dollars.) The answer is a number.

To ask how many, the word matches the thing you count. For a masculine word, use ¿cuántos? ¿Cuántos perros tienes? (How many dogs do you have?) For a feminine word, use ¿cuántas? ¿Cuántas empanadas hay? (How many empanadas are there?) The ending -os or -as matches the noun.

English speakers often say ¿Cuántos empanadas? That is a mistake, because empanada is feminine. Check the noun first, then pick -os or -as. And keep the accent on the a: cuánto, cuántos, cuántas.

Section 3

Why, Because and the Accent

13.7

¿Por qué? and Porque

Main idea¿Por qué? (two words, accent) asks why, and porque (one word, no accent) answers because.

Picture your little brother looking sad. You ask (why?). ¿Por qué estás triste? means Why are you sad? He answers: Porque no hay empanadas. (Because there are no empanadas.) The question and the answer sound almost the same.

Look at how they are written. The question is two words, por qué, with an accent on the é. The answer is one word, , with no accent. It means because. Say both aloud. In the question, your voice leans on qué. In the answer, it leans on por.

Many students write porque in the question. That is a mistake. Use this check. Does it have question marks around it? Then write ¿Por qué? Is it the start of an answer? Then write porque. This small difference shows up in many Spanish tests.

Think it through
Why? Because!

Rafa: ¿Por qué hay migas en el piso? (Why are there crumbs on the floor?) Lupe: Porque el perro comió la empanada. (Because the dog ate the empanada.) Rafa: ¿Por qué? (Why?) Lupe: ¡Porque tenía hambre! (Because it was hungry!)

Ask a partner ¿Por qué estás contento? or ¿Por qué estás cansado? Your partner answers with Porque…

Words to know
¿por qué?
why? — two words with an accent: ¿Por qué estás triste?
porque
because — one word, no accent: Porque estoy cansado.
Check yourself

1. Which is written correctly as a question?

Why: The question why is two words, por qué, with an accent on the é.

2. Which word starts the answer? ___ estoy cansado.

Why: Because is porque: one word, no accent.

3. What does Porque tengo hambre mean?

Why: Porque means because, and tengo hambre means I am hungry.
13.8

The Accent That Marks a Question

Main ideaEvery question word carries a written accent, and the accent helps you spot a question.

Look at this list: quién, qué, cómo, dónde, cuándo, cuánto, por qué. Every one has an acento (a written accent mark). The mark sits on one vowel. It shows the question word, and it shows which part to say louder.

Many of these words have a twin without the accent. Que means that. Como can mean I eat or like. Donde means where in a statement: la casa donde vivo (the house where I live). Porque means because. The twin words are not questions, so they have no accent.

Here is a simple rule for now. If the word asks a question, write the accent. A common mistake is to type fast and skip it. On a phone, hold down the letter to find é, ó or á. Check each question word before you send it.

Think it through
Find the Twins

Question: ¿Cómo estás? (How are you?) Not a question: Como pan. (I eat bread.) Question: ¿Dónde vives? (Where do you live?) Not a question: la casa donde vivo (the house where I live) Question: ¿Por qué? (Why?) Not a question: porque (because)

Write two sentences: one with ¿Qué…? and one with que. Circle the accent in the question.

Words to know
el acento
the written accent mark — the small line over a vowel, as in qué or dónde
la pregunta
the question — Tengo una pregunta. (I have a question.)
Check yourself

1. Which question word is spelled correctly?

Why: Dónde has one accent, on the first o.

2. Why does como in Como pan have no accent?

Why: Como without an accent is a form of comer; cómo with an accent asks how.

3. This is a statement, not a question. Which word fits? la casa ___ vivo (the house where I live)

Why: This is a statement, not a question, so donde has no accent.
13.9

Ask About Any Part

Main ideaYou can ask about any piece of a sentence by swapping that piece for a question word.

Start with one sentence: Tomás come una empanada en la cocina a las seis. (Tomás eats an empanada in the kitchen at six.) Each piece answers a different question. You can ask about any piece you do not know.

Swap the person for quién: ¿Quién come una empanada? Swap the food for qué: ¿Qué come Tomás? Swap the place for dónde: ¿Dónde come Tomás? Swap the time for cuándo: ¿Cuándo come Tomás? Notice the verb often moves before the person in a question: ¿Qué come Tomás?

English speakers often keep English order and say ¿Qué Tomás come? Spanish usually puts the verb right after the question word. Question word first, then the verb, then the person. This pattern works with every question word you learned.

Think it through
One Sentence, Four Questions

Sentence: Rafa juega en el parque el sábado. (Rafa plays in the park on Saturday.) Who? ¿Quién juega en el parque? Where? ¿Dónde juega Rafa? When? ¿Cuándo juega Rafa? What does he do? ¿Qué hace Rafa?

Take the sentence Lupe lee en la biblioteca. Make two questions from it with different question words.

Words to know
la palabra interrogativa
the question word — like quién, qué, dónde or cuándo
la respuesta
the answer — the part that fills in what the question asked
Check yourself

1. Sentence: Ana canta en la escuela. Which question asks about the place?

Why: Dónde asks for the place, and the verb comes right after the question word.

2. Which question has the usual Spanish word order?

Why: Spanish puts the verb right after the question word: ¿Qué come Tomás?

3. The answer is A las seis. Which question was asked?

The story

The School News Interview

Two student reporters get five minutes to interview the new principal in Spanish.

Marisol and Diego are reporters for the school news. Their school has a new principal, and she speaks Spanish and English. The reporters get five minutes with her. Diego holds the notebook. Marisol holds the list of questions. Her hands shake a little. "Buenos días," she says. (Good morning.)

The principal smiles. "Buenos días. Pasen, por favor." (Good morning. Come in, please.) Marisol reads the first question. "¿Cómo se llama usted?" (What is your name?) She uses usted, the polite you, because the principal is an adult at school. "Me llamo Señora Ortiz," the principal says. (My name is Mrs. Ortiz.)

Diego asks the next question. "¿De dónde es usted?" (Where are you from?) "Soy de Puerto Rico, pero vivo en Chicago." (I am from Puerto Rico, but I live in Chicago.) Diego writes fast. Then he drops his pencil. "¡Perdón!" he says. (Sorry!) Señora Ortiz laughs and hands it back. "De nada." (You are welcome.)

Marisol asks one last question. "¿Qué le gusta de nuestra escuela?" (What do you like about our school?) "¡Los estudiantes!" she answers. (The students!) The reporters stand up. "Muchas gracias," they say. (Thank you very much.) Their interview goes on the school news the next morning, with every answer in a full sentence.

The First Three Questions

14.1

¿Cómo te llamas?

Main idea¿Cómo te llamas? asks a friend's name, and you answer Me llamo and your name.

Picture a new student sitting next to you. You want to know her name. You ask (What is your name?). Word for word, it is How do you call yourself? She answers: Me llamo Sofía. (My name is Sofía.)

Look at the pattern. Te goes with tú (you, for a friend). Me goes with yo (I). ¿Cómo te llamas? Me llamo Sofía. You can also answer Soy Sofía (I am Sofía). Both answers are correct.

English speakers sometimes say Me llamo es Sofía. That is a mistake, because llamo is already the verb. Say Me llamo Sofía, with no es. With an adult, ask the polite way: ¿Cómo se llama usted? (What is your name?)

Think it through

Turn to a partner. Ask ¿Cómo te llamas? and answer with Me llamo. Then say Mucho gusto.

14.2

¿Cuántos años tienes?

Main ideaSpanish uses tener (to have) for age: Tengo nueve años means I am nine.

Picture a birthday party with candles on a cake. You want to know how old your cousin is. You ask (How old are you?). Word for word, it is How many years do you have? Your cousin answers: Tengo diez años. (I am ten.)

Look at the verb. Spanish uses tener (to have) for age, not ser. Tengo means I have. Tienes means you have. Tiene means he or she has. Mi hermano tiene seis años. (My brother is six.) Say the number, then años (years).

English speakers often say Soy nueve. That is a mistake. It sounds like I am the number nine. Always use tener for age: Tengo nueve años. Also keep cuántos with -os, because año is masculine.

14.3

¿Dónde vives?

Main idea¿Dónde vives? asks where you live, and you answer Vivo en and a place.

Picture a new friend at the park. You want to know where she lives. You ask (Where do you live?). She answers: Vivo en Chicago. (I live in Chicago.) Vives and vivo come from vivir (to live).

Look at the endings. Vives ends in -es and goes with tú. Vivo ends in -o and goes with yo. Her answer can be a city, a street or a neighborhood: Vivo en Pilsen. Vivo en la calle Main. (I live on Main Street.) The word en means in or on.

Do not mix this up with ¿De dónde eres? That asks where you are from. Soy de México. (I am from Mexico.) A person can be from one place and live in another. The reporters in the story heard both: Soy de Puerto Rico, pero vivo en Chicago.

Section 2

Listening and Answering

14.4

Listen for the Question Word

Main ideaThe question word, heard first, tells you what kind of answer to give.

Picture a fast question from a new friend. You may not catch every word. Listen for the question word first. It is usually at the start. If you hear dónde, answer with a place. If you hear cuándo, answer with a time.

Try it. ¿Cuántos hermanos tienes? You hear cuántos, so the answer is a number: Tengo dos hermanos. (I have two brothers or siblings.) ¿Quién es tu mejor amigo? You hear quién, so the answer is a person: Es Diego.

A common mistake is to answer the wrong kind of question. If someone asks ¿Dónde vives? and you say Tengo diez años, the talk stops. When you miss the question, it is fine to say (What? Pardon?) or Otra vez, por favor. (Again, please.)

Why: Cuántos asks how many, so the answer has a number.

14.5

Answer in a Full Sentence

Main ideaA full-sentence answer repeats the verb from the question with a new ending.

Picture your teacher asking, ¿Dónde vives? You could say just Chicago. A frase completa (full sentence) is better: Vivo en Chicago. It shows you know the verb. It also gives the listener more to work with.

Here is the trick. Find the verb in the question and change its ending. ¿Hablas español? Sí, hablo español. ¿Tienes un perro? Sí, tengo un perro. ¿Vives en Chicago? Sí, vivo en Chicago. The tú ending -as or -es becomes the yo ending -o.

Many students repeat the question’s ending by mistake: Sí, hablas español. That means Yes, you speak Spanish. Change the ending to -o for I. To say no, put no before the verb: No, no tengo un perro. The first no answers; the second makes the sentence negative.

Think it through
Turning the Verb Around

Question: ¿Hablas inglés? (Do you speak English?) Step 1: Find the verb: hablas (you speak). Step 2: Change -as to -o: hablo (I speak). Answer: Sí, hablo inglés. (Yes, I speak English.)

Answer in a full sentence: ¿Tienes una mascota? ¿Hablas español? Change each verb ending to -o.

Words to know
la frase completa
the full sentence — an answer with a verb, not just one word
sí
yes — with an accent; si without an accent means if
Check yourself

1. Which is the best full-sentence answer to ¿Hablas español?

Why: Change the tú ending -as to the yo ending -o: hablo.

2. How do you answer No to ¿Tienes un perro? in a full sentence?

Why: Put no before the verb, and use tengo for I.

3. Why is Sí, hablas inglés a wrong answer to ¿Hablas inglés?

Why: Hablas goes with tú; for I, the ending changes to -o.
14.6

Asking Back: ¿Y tú?

Main ideaAfter you answer, ask ¿Y tú? (And you?) to keep the conversation going.

Picture a talk that stops after one answer. It feels stiff. A conversation is a back-and-forth. After you answer, ask the same thing back with (And you?). ¿Cómo te llamas? Me llamo Diego. ¿Y tú? Me llamo Sofía.

With an adult, use the polite form, (And you?). Buenos días, ¿cómo está usted? Bien, gracias. ¿Y usted? Muy bien. The reporters in the story would use ¿Y usted? with the principal.

A common slip is to use ¿Y tú? with a teacher. It is not rude to a friend, but it is too casual for an adult you do not know well. Remember: tú for friends and family, usted for adults at school and people you should show respect.

Think it through
Keep It Going

Marisol: ¿Cuántos años tienes? (How old are you?) Diego: Tengo diez años. ¿Y tú? (I am ten. And you?) Marisol: Tengo nueve años. ¿Dónde vives? (I am nine. Where do you live?) Diego: Vivo en Chicago. ¿Y tú? (I live in Chicago. And you?)

Have a four-line talk with a partner. Each time you answer, add ¿Y tú?

Words to know
¿Y tú?
And you? — asks the same question back to a friend
¿Y usted?
And you? — the polite form, for an adult
Check yourself

1. A classmate says Me llamo Ana. ¿Y tú? What does ¿Y tú? mean?

Why: ¿Y tú? means And you? and asks the question back.

2. Your teacher asks how you are. You answer Bien, gracias. What do you add?

Why: With a teacher, use the polite form usted.

3. Why do speakers add ¿Y tú? after answering?

Why: Asking back turns one answer into a back-and-forth.
Section 3

Polite Words for a Good Interview

14.7

Por favor and Gracias

Main ideaPor favor means please and gracias means thank you; they make any request polite.

Picture yourself asking the principal for five minutes of her time. You add (please). Un momento, por favor. (One moment, please.) Por favor can go at the start or the end of a request.

When someone helps you, say (thank you). For extra thanks, say muchas gracias (thank you very much). Notice muchas ends in -as, to match gracias. Many learners say mucho gracias, but that is not correct.

The reporters used both words. They asked each question politely and ended with Muchas gracias. In many Spanish-speaking families, children learn to use these words with every adult. They are small words, but people notice them.

Think it through
Asking Politely

Diego: Una pregunta, por favor. (One question, please.) Señora Ortiz: Sí, claro. (Yes, of course.) Diego: ¿Qué le gusta de la escuela? (What do you like about the school?) Señora Ortiz: ¡Los estudiantes! Diego: Muchas gracias. (Thank you very much.)

Ask a partner for a pencil in Spanish with por favor. When you get it, say muchas gracias.

Words to know
por favor
please — Otra vez, por favor. (Again, please.)
gracias
thank you — muchas gracias (thank you very much)
Check yourself

1. Which is correct for thank you very much?

Why: Gracias is feminine and plural, so muchas matches it.

2. What does Otra vez, por favor mean?

Why: Otra vez means again, and por favor means please.

3. A friend hands you a book. What do you say?

Why: Gracias thanks someone for helping you.
14.8

De nada and Perdón

Main ideaDe nada answers gracias, and perdón says sorry or excuse me.

Picture yourself handing a friend a pencil. She says Gracias. You answer (you are welcome). Word for word, it means of nothing, as if to say it was no trouble. De nada always answers gracias.

Now picture yourself bumping into someone in the hall. You say (sorry, or excuse me). You can also use perdón to get attention: Perdón, ¿dónde está la biblioteca? (Excuse me, where is the library?) Keep the accent on the o.

A common mix-up is to answer gracias with gracias, or with por favor. The right reply is de nada. Diego in the story used both words. He dropped his pencil and said perdón. The principal handed it back, he said gracias, and she said de nada.

Think it through
In the Hallway

Lupe bumps into Rafa. Lupe: ¡Perdón! (Sorry!) Rafa: No pasa nada. (It is okay.) Lupe picks up his book. Rafa: Gracias. (Thank you.) Lupe: De nada. (You are welcome.)

Act out the hallway scene with a partner. Use perdón, gracias and de nada.

Words to know
de nada
you are welcome — the answer to gracias
perdón
sorry or excuse me — Perdón, ¿dónde está el baño?
Check yourself

Why: De nada (you are welcome) answers gracias.

2. You bump into a classmate. What do you say?

Why: Perdón means sorry or excuse me.

3. Which sentence politely asks a stranger for directions?

Why: Perdón gets attention politely, and dónde asks where.
14.9

Putting an Interview Together

Main ideaA good interview has a greeting, a few clear questions, full answers and a polite thank-you.

Picture your own interview with a classmate. It has four parts. First, a greeting: Hola or Buenos días. Second, three to five questions, each with a different question word. Third, listening and writing down each respuesta (answer).

Fourth, a polite ending: Muchas gracias. Here is a sample list of questions. ¿Cómo te llamas? ¿Cuántos años tienes? ¿Dónde vives? ¿Cuál es tu comida favorita? (What is your favorite food?) ¿Por qué te gusta?

Check your list before you start. Does each question have ¿ at the start and ? at the end? Does each question word have its accent? Did you use tú for a classmate and usted for an adult? Then take turns being the reportero or reportera (reporter).

Think it through
An Interview Plan

1. Greeting: ¡Hola! 2. ¿Cómo te llamas? 3. ¿Cuántos años tienes? 4. ¿Dónde vives? 5. ¿Qué te gusta hacer? (What do you like to do?) 6. Ending: Muchas gracias.

Use this plan to interview a partner. Write each answer as a full sentence about them, like Se llama Ana.

Words to know
la entrevista
the interview — a talk where one person asks questions and writes answers
el reportero / la reportera
the reporter — the person who asks the questions
la respuesta
the answer — Escribo cada respuesta. (I write each answer.)
Check yourself

1. What is the best way to end an interview?

Why: A thank-you is a polite way to end an interview.

2. You interview the school nurse, an adult. Which question fits?

Why: With an adult you use usted, which takes the vive form.

3. Your partner answered Me llamo Ana. How do you write it about her?

Why: To tell about her, use se llama, the he or she form.
