Cloth is not the same in every direction

Cloth is two sets of threads crossing. One set runs down the length, the other runs across. The firm band down the side was finished by the loom, not cut.

Those have names. The finished edge is the selvage. Threads running with it are the lengthwise grain; threads running across are the crosswise grain; 45 degrees to both is the bias. The arrow on a pattern piece is the grainline, and it has to run parallel to the selvage.

Four things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

Fabric is a flat sheet, so a pattern piece can go on any way it fits.

Fabric has a direction built into it. The lengthwise threads hardly give, the crosswise threads give a little, and the diagonal gives a lot. A piece laid the wrong way is cut from a different cloth than the piece beside it.

Measure both ends of the arrow to the selvage. Same number.

⚠️ People often think…

Washing the fabric first is a waste of time, because the finished garment gets washed anyway.

Cotton, rayon and wool shrink. If they shrink before you cut, the pieces stay the size you cut them. If they shrink after, the sleeves come up short and there is no cloth left to add.

Shrink the cloth while it is still a rectangle.

⚠️ People often think…

Rayon is made from wood, so it is a natural fiber.

The wood pulp is broken down and re-formed with chemicals in a factory. The tree is only the raw material. The factory made the fiber, so rayon is counted as manufactured.

Ask who made the fiber, not where the material grew.

⚠️ People often think…

You cut along the line, and then you sew along that same line.

There are two lines. The cutting line is the solid outer one. The seam line is the broken line inside it, on most patterns 5/8 of an inch in. Sew the outer line and the garment comes out more than an inch too wide.

Cut the outer line. Sew the inner one.

Helpful Hints

🧭 The unit in one line

A fiber is spun into yarn → yarn is woven or knit into fabric → the fabric has a selvage, a grain and a bias → you wash it, fold it, lay the pattern on it with the grainline parallel to the selvage → you pin, cut, mark and sew. Every step after the first one depends on getting the grain right.

Wash. Fold. Line up the arrow. Pin. Cut. Mark.

🔑 The one idea

Fabric is not a blank sheet. It has a direction built into it. The lengthwise grain runs the same way as the selvage and hardly gives. The crosswise grain gives a little. The bias, at 45 degrees, gives a lot. The grainline arrow on a pattern piece tells you which way the garment must line up with that direction — and if you ignore it, the cloth will win.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Do not buy a pattern by the size on your store clothes. Patterns are sized by body measurement — chest, waist, hip — and the number is often two or three sizes larger. Measure, then read the chart.
  • Do not cut before you wash. Cotton, rayon and wool can shrink. Wash and dry the fabric the way you will wash the finished garment, then cut.
  • Do not cut on the cutting line and then sew on the cutting line. The seam line sits inside it — on most patterns 5/8 of an inch in. Cut the outer line; sew the inner one.
  • Do not lift the fabric to cut it. Keep the bottom blade of the shears flat on the table and let the fabric lie still. Lifting shifts the layers and the two sides come out different.
  • Do not eyeball the grainline arrow. Measure from each end of the arrow to the selvage. The two numbers must match.
  • Do not put a hot iron on polyester, nylon or spandex. They melt. The dots inside the iron symbol on a care label are the heat setting: one dot low, two medium, three high.
  • Do not cut a “place on fold” edge. That edge is laid against the folded edge of the fabric and stays joined. Cut it and you get two halves instead of one whole piece.

🎯 How you will be asked

  • “Natural or manufactured?” for a list of fibers — and “where does each one come from?”
  • “Woven or knit?” with two tests: does it stretch across, and does the cut edge fray?
  • Label a folded layout: selvage, fold, raw edge, grainline, notch, cutting line, seam line.
  • A care label with symbols — “what is this garment telling you to do, and not to do?”
  • “The skirt twists on the body. The sewing is neat. What went wrong, and when?”

✅ Before the test, can you…

  • Name four natural fibers and four manufactured ones, and say where each comes from?
  • Give two tests that tell a knit from a woven?
  • Point to the selvage, the lengthwise grain, the crosswise grain and the bias on a piece of fabric?
  • Say why fabric is washed before it is cut?
  • Find your pattern size from a measuring tape and the envelope chart, and read the yardage for 45-inch fabric?
  • Name six markings on a pattern piece and say what each one is for?

📚 Go to the sources

These open another website and need the internet. Use the search box on each site with the words given — the pages move, the sites do not.

  • Federal Trade Commission — the U.S. agency that requires the care label and the fiber-content label in your clothes. Search “care labeling rule” and “textile fiber products”.
  • University of Minnesota Extension — free, plain-language guides from a public university on laundry, stains and caring for fabric. Search “laundry” or “stain removal”.
  • Smithsonian National Museum of American History — photographs of real sewing machines, patterns and clothing from the collection. Search “sewing machine” or “dress”.
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics — what tailors, dressmakers and custom sewers actually do all day, what they earn, and what training they need.
