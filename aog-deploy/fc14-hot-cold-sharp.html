A kitchen has three kinds of danger

An ordinary kitchen counter. A pan on the stove, a knife on the board, a carton left out. Nothing here looks like a problem.

Hot, sharp, and should-be-cold. Nearly everything that goes wrong in a kitchen is one of those three, and each one has a move that goes with it. Hot things stay hot long after they stop looking hot, so you keep your hands off and ask before you touch. Sharp things are safest when they are treated as sharp — carried point-down, put down flat, and never caught when they fall. And cold food is cold for a reason: warm is where germs grow, so it goes back in the fridge. You are not learning to be afraid of a kitchen. You are learning which of the three you are standing next to.

Two things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

If something is not glowing or steaming, it is safe to touch.

Hot does not look like anything. A pan that came off the stove ten minutes ago looks exactly like a cold one, and a glass dish out of the oven looks like a glass dish. Steam is worse still — it is invisible for the first few centimeters above a pot, and it burns more than the water it came from, because it dumps all its heat into your skin at once. So the test is never how it looks. The test is: has this been near heat recently, and do I know for certain that it has not?

Hot has no color. Ask before you touch.

⚠️ People often think…

A blunt knife is safer than a sharp one, because it cannot cut you as easily.

A blunt knife cuts people more often, not less. A sharp blade bites into the food where you put it and stays there. A blunt one slides off the skin of a tomato or the curve of a carrot — and now there is a blade moving fast in a direction nobody chose, with a hand somewhere near it. Every professional kitchen keeps its knives sharp for exactly this reason. What makes a knife dangerous is not the edge. It is the slip.

Sharp knives bite. Blunt knives slip. The slip is what cuts you.

Work out which danger you are next to

1 Watch one

Somebody has just taken a tray of biscuits out of the oven and put it on the counter.

  1. Ask which of the three this is. It came out of an oven, so it is HOT.
  2. Check whether it looks hot. It does not — a metal tray looks the same at any temperature.
  3. That is the trap, so ignore how it looks and use what you know: it was in an oven a minute ago.
  4. The move for HOT is hands off, and ask.
  5. “Is that tray still hot?” ✓ One question, and it costs you nothing to ask it.
2 Do one with me

Fill in the three boxes.

  1. A knife is sliding off the counter. What do you do?
  2. Milk has been out on the counter all morning. Where does it go?
  3. Which is safer to use — a sharp knife or a blunt one?

Helpful Hints

🧭 The unit in one line

A kitchen has three kinds of danger — hot, sharp, and should-be-cold → hot has no appearance, so a pan off the heat looks exactly like a cold one and you ask before you touch → sharp things travel point-down at your side and you step back rather than catch a falling one → and cold food goes straight back, because warm is where germs grow and about two hours out is too long.

Which of the three am I next to? Then the move is decided.

🔑 The one idea

Three is a short enough list to run through every single time you stand in a kitchen, and that is the point of it. You are not being asked to be frightened of a room — you are being asked one question: which of the three am I next to? Once you know that, the move is already decided and you do not have to work anything out under pressure. Two of the three are also invisible: hot has no color, and food that is about to make you ill smells fine. So for two of them, like handwashing, you trust a rule instead of a sense.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Hot has no color. A pan off the heat, a glass dish out of the oven and a mug of tea all look exactly like cold ones.
  • Steam is invisible near the pot and carries MORE heat than the water it came from. Lift lids away from your face.
  • A blunt knife cuts MORE people than a sharp one, because it slips instead of biting. The slip is the danger, not the edge.
  • Never catch a falling knife. Step back and let it hit the floor — the knife will be fine.
  • A WET cloth on a hot pan burns you almost instantly, because water carries the heat straight through.
  • Smelling food does not prove it is safe. Plenty of food that would make you ill smells and looks normal.
  • Water on burning oil makes it much worse. Heat off, cover the pan — or at your age, leave and get an adult.
