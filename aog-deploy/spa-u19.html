The Spanish-Speaking World and Register

Cultures

Three students sit down for lunch in Pilsen. One is from Buenos Aires, one from Madrid, one from Mexico City. They order the same juice with two different words, say gracias with two different sounds, and use three different pronouns for you. Nobody misunderstands anyone. That table is the Spanish-speaking world in miniature: twenty countries and Puerto Rico, hundreds of millions of speakers, one language with many voices. This unit is a map of that world and a guide to moving through it.

In the first chapter you will learn where Spanish is official, how it came to the United States and to Illinois, and how it varies: vos in Argentina and Central America, vosotros in Spain, the th sound of Madrid and the s of everywhere else, guagua and camión and colectivo for one bus. You will trace words back to Arabic (almohada, ojalá), Nahuatl (chocolate, tomate) and Taíno (huracán, canoa), and see that Spanish has always borrowed.

In the second chapter you will learn register: when to say tú and when to say usted, how to open and close a formal letter, how to soften a request with podría and quisiera, which words are false friends, and how to read the signs, menus and headlines you pass every day. By the end you will be able to write to a company for a summer job in Spanish that sounds serious, text your cousin in Spanish that sounds like you, and know the difference on purpose.

The story

Three Friends, One Lunch Order

Three exchange students share a table in Pilsen and discover that they speak the same language three different ways.

In July, a summer program at a Chicago university brings students from around the Spanish-speaking world to the city. Valentina is from Buenos Aires, Jorge is from Madrid, and Daniela is from Mexico City. They meet on the first day and decide to have lunch together on 18th Street in Pilsen, a neighborhood where Spanish is heard on every corner. Daniela feels at home. The other two are about to learn something.

The waiter brings menus. "¿Vos querés compartir algo?" Valentina asks Daniela. (Do you want to share something?) Daniela pauses. She has heard *vos* in Argentine shows, but no one in her family says it. "Sí, claro," she answers. (Yes, of course.) Then Jorge looks at the drinks and asks for "un zumo de naranja" (an orange juice). The waiter, from Michoacán, smiles and writes down *jugo*. Same drink, different word.

Jorge says "gracias" and the c sounds like the th in the English word think. Valentina says "calle" (street) and the ll sounds almost like the sh in shoe. Daniela says both words the way most of Chicago says them. Nobody is wrong. Each of them is speaking the standard Spanish of their own country, and each country has its own sounds.

The food arrives: tacos, guacamole, *elotes*. "¿Sabíais que aguacate viene del náhuatl?" Jorge asks. (Did you all know that avocado comes from Nahuatl?) Valentina laughs. "En Argentina decimos palta," she says. (In Argentina we say palta.) That word comes from Quechua, a language of the Andes. By the end of lunch the three friends have collected a list: guagua, camión, colectivo, autobús. Four words for one bus.

This chapter is about that table. Spanish is an official language in twenty countries and in Puerto Rico, and it is the second most common language in homes across the United States. It has words borrowed from Arabic, Nahuatl and Taíno, and it has more than one way to say you. Learning where it is spoken, and how it changes from place to place, is what lets you sit down at that table and understand everyone.

A Language Across the Map

37.1

Twenty Countries and an Island

Main ideaSpanish is an official language in twenty countries on three continents, plus Puerto Rico, and each place speaks its own standard form of the language.

Daniela’s family is from Mexico, the country with more Spanish speakers than any other. But Spanish is not one country’s language. It is an (official) language in twenty countries. In North America: Mexico. In Central America: Guatemala, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Panama. In the Caribbean: Cuba and the Dominican Republic. In South America: Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, Paraguay, Uruguay, Argentina and Chile. In Europe: Spain, where the language began. And in Africa: Equatorial Guinea, a small country on the west coast that was once a Spanish colony.

Puerto Rico is a territory of the United States, not a country, and Spanish and English are both official there. Most people on the island speak Spanish at home. So when people count "the Spanish-speaking world," they usually say twenty countries plus Puerto Rico. Each of these places calls its language español or castellano (Castilian). Both words mean the same thing. In Spain and much of South America you hear castellano more; in Mexico and Central America you hear español more.

Spanish is one of the most widely spoken languages in the world, with hundreds of millions of native speakers. Learning it connects you to a (Spanish-speaking) world that stretches from the Río Grande to the southern tip of Chile. And every one of those countries has its own standard: its own accent, its own favorite words, and sometimes its own pronoun for you. A Spanish speaker from Madrid and one from Buenos Aires understand each other with no trouble, the way a speaker from London and one from Chicago do.

A small note on the map: the (continent) of South America is where most of the Spanish-speaking countries sit, but Brazil, its largest country, speaks Portuguese. Belize, in Central America, speaks English. So the Spanish-speaking world is not "everything south of Texas." It is a specific list of places, and by the end of this chapter you will know it.

37.2

Spanish in the United States

Main ideaSpanish has been spoken in what is now the United States since the 1500s, and today tens of millions of Americans speak it at home.

When Valentina arrived in Chicago, she expected to speak English all summer. Instead she heard Spanish at the airport, on the train, and in the grocery store. She should not have been surprised. The United States has tens of millions of Spanish speakers, more than most of the twenty countries on the list. After English, Spanish is the language most spoken in American homes.

Spanish is not a new arrival. In 1565, Spanish settlers founded St. Augustine, in what is now Florida, decades before the English settlement at Jamestown. Spanish missions and towns grew across what is now Texas, New Mexico, Arizona and California. In 1848, after the war between Mexico and the United States, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo moved the (border) south, and the Spanish-speaking people who lived north of it became residents of the United States without moving at all. In 1898, Puerto Rico passed from Spain to the United States, and in 1917 Puerto Ricans became U.S. citizens.

Today most Spanish speakers in the United States, or their families, came from Mexico, Puerto Rico, Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Central America and South America. Many students in this course are hablantes de herencia (heritage speakers): people who grew up hearing Spanish at home, understand it well, and may speak it with family but never studied its grammar in school. If that is you, this course names things you already know how to do.

Being (bilingual) is a real skill, and it is common across the country. Spanish in the United States also has its own flavor. Words like troca (truck) and parquear (to park) are borrowed from English and are heard in many communities. They are not wrong; they are the marks of a language living next to another one. In formal writing, you would use el camión or la camioneta and , the same way you would choose a different word for an essay than for a text.

37.3

Spanish in Illinois and Chicago

Main ideaMore than two million Illinois residents are Hispanic or Latino, and Chicago neighborhoods like Pilsen, Little Village and Humboldt Park carry Spanish in their streets, murals and food.

The three friends chose Pilsen for lunch because Daniela had read about it. Pilsen, on the Lower West Side of Chicago, was first a Czech and Polish (neighborhood); its name comes from a city in the Czech Republic. In the second half of the 1900s it became largely Mexican American, and today 18th Street is lined with taquerías, bakeries and murales (murals) that tell the stories of Mexican and Mexican American families. The National Museum of Mexican Art sits in Harrison Park in the same neighborhood.

A little to the southwest is Little Village, called La Villita in Spanish. Its main street, 26th Street, is one of the busiest shopping streets in Chicago, and its welcome arch reads "Bienvenidos a Little Village." Every September, Mexican Independence Day is celebrated there with a parade. In Humboldt Park, on the Northwest Side, the (community) is largely Puerto Rican. Two huge steel Puerto Rican banderas (flags) arch over Division Street, a stretch known as Paseo Boricua.

These neighborhoods are not the whole story. Spanish is spoken in Cicero, Berwyn, Waukegan, Aurora, Elgin and Joliet, and in small towns across the state. According to the 2020 census, more than two million Illinois residents identified as Hispanic or Latino, most of them of Mexican origin, with large Puerto Rican, Guatemalan, Ecuadorian and Cuban communities as well. In Chicago itself, close to a third of residents are Hispanic or Latino.

What this means for you is simple. The Spanish in this course is not a language from far away. Your vecinos (neighbors) speak it. A student who learns to read a menu on 26th Street, greet a shop owner on Paseo Boricua, or talk with a classmate’s grandmother in Cicero is using Spanish as a real language of Illinois.

Vos: Another Way to Say You

Main ideaIn Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and much of Central America, vos replaces tú, and it has its own present-tense endings: hablás, comés, vivís, sos.

When Valentina asked "¿Vos querés compartir algo?" she used a pronoun Daniela had never studied. is a second way to say you, singular and informal, just like tú. It is the normal pronoun in Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay, and in most of Central America: Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua and Costa Rica. In those places, tú may sound stiff or foreign. In Mexico, the Caribbean, Spain and most of Colombia and Peru, tú is the everyday choice. Using vos is called .

Vos comes with its own present-tense endings, and they are easy: the stress moves to the last syllable, and the accent shows it. For -ar verbs: hablar → (you speak). For -er verbs: comer → (you eat). For -ir verbs: vivir → (you live), which looks exactly like the vosotros form. Compare tú: tú hablas, tú comes, tú vives. The difference is one accent mark and a shift in where the voice lands.

Here is the part that makes vos simpler than tú. Stem-changing verbs do not change their stem with vos, because the stress is on the ending, not the stem. Tú quieres, but . Tú puedes, but . Tú tienes, but . The one truly irregular form to memorize is ser: tú eres becomes (you are). "¿De dónde sos?" (Where are you from?) is one of the first things you hear in Buenos Aires.

Commands work the same way: drop the -r and add an accent. Hablá. Comé. Viví. Vení (come). Mirá (look). The object forms do not change: te and ti stay the same, so "te quiero" and "para vos" (for you) sit side by side. You do not need to use vos yourself unless you live where it is spoken. You do need to recognize it, because millions of speakers, and a great deal of music, film and television, use it every day.

37.5

Vosotros in Spain

Main ideaSpain uses vosotros and vosotras for an informal group of people you would each call tú; everywhere else, ustedes covers every group.

At lunch, Jorge asked "¿Sabíais que aguacate viene del náhuatl?" He was speaking to two friends, informally, so he used (you all). In Spain, vosotros (or vosotras for a group of women) is the plural of tú, and is the plural of usted. A teacher in Madrid might say "vosotros" to her students and "ustedes" to their parents. In Latin America, that distinction disappears: ustedes is the plural for everyone, friends and strangers alike.

Vosotros has its own set of endings. Present tense: hablar → , comer → coméis, vivir → vivís. Ser is , and tener is tenéis. "¿De dónde sois?" means where are you all from. The pattern in the present is a vowel plus -is, with an accent on that vowel for -ar and -er verbs. Commands drop the final -r and add -d: hablad, comed, vivid. "¡Venid aquí!" means come here, all of you.

The possessive for vosotros is , vuestra, , vuestras. "Vuestra casa es bonita" means your (all of you) house is pretty. The object pronoun is : "Os veo mañana" (I will see you all tomorrow). In Latin America, all of these become the ustedes forms: su casa, los veo mañana, hablan, comen, viven.

Because most Spanish speakers in the United States come from Latin America, most U.S. classrooms teach ustedes as the everyday plural, and that is what this course does. But you will meet vosotros in books from Spain, in films, in older texts like Don Quijote, and in any conversation with a Spaniard. Recognizing habláis, sois and vuestro is enough; you can answer with ustedes forms and be understood perfectly.

37.6

The Sounds of s, c and z

Main ideaIn most of Spain, c before e or i and z sound like the th in think; in Latin America, the Canary Islands and parts of Andalusia they sound like s, and neither way is wrong.

Jorge said "gracias" and Daniela heard something like gra-thias. That is , the sound system of most of Spain: the letter z, and the letter c before e or i, are pronounced like the th in the English word think. So (shoe), cinco (five) and cerveza (beer) all carry that th sound, while s stays s. In this system, casa (house) and caza (hunt) sound different.

Daniela and Valentina, like almost everyone in Latin America, use : z and soft c sound exactly like s. Casa and caza sound the same; so do cocer (to cook) and coser (to sew). The seseo is also the norm in the Canary Islands and in much of Andalusia in southern Spain. Because the great majority of Spanish speakers live in the Americas, the seseo is by far the more common system, and it is what most people in Chicago say. In a few parts of Andalusia there is a third system, el ceceo, where s itself is pronounced with the th sound.

There are other regional sounds worth knowing. In the Caribbean, in coastal areas and in much of Argentina and Chile, an s at the end of a syllable often softens to a breath: "estás" can sound like eh-tah. Around Buenos Aires and Montevideo, ll and y are pronounced like the sh in shoe or the s in measure, so calle sounds like ca-she. In Mexico, the s is clear and the vowels can shorten. In Spain, j and g before e or i are stronger and rougher than in the Americas.

None of these is a mistake. Each is the standard (pronunciation) of a region, the way a Boston r and a Texas vowel are both English. When you learn Spanish, pick the (accent) you hear most, usually the one in your family or community, and learn to understand the others. The spelling never changes: zapato is z-a-p-a-t-o in Madrid, Mexico City and Buenos Aires, whatever the first sound.

How Do You Say Bus?

Main ideaEveryday objects often have a different word in each country: guagua, camión, colectivo and autobús are all one bus, and none of them is wrong.

By the end of lunch the three friends had a list of words for bus. Jorge, from Spain, says . Daniela, from Mexico City, says , a word that in most other countries means a truck. Valentina, from Buenos Aires, says el colectivo. In Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic and the Canary Islands the bus is . In Chile it is la micro, and in Colombia you also hear el bus. Every one of these is correct where it is used.

The same happens with cars. is the everyday word in Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean and Colombia, and it is the word most students in Chicago hear at home. El coche is the word in Spain, and it is also common in Mexico. is what you hear in Argentina, Chile and Uruguay. If you say carro in Madrid, people will understand you; they might picture a cart, and they will know you learned Spanish in the Americas. That is all.

Why does this happen? Spanish reached the Americas in 1492 and spread over three centuries, and each region kept developing on its own. Some regions kept old words that Spain dropped; some borrowed from local languages; some invented new uses, like camión for a bus. Regional words are called regionalismos, and every language has them. English has soda, pop and coke for the same drink, depending on where in the United States you are.

The rule for a learner is this: choose one variety and use it steadily, usually the variety of your family or the community around you. Learn to recognize the others. Never mark a regional word as wrong just because it is not the one you know. And when you are unsure what a word means in a new place, ask: "¿Cómo se dice bus aquí?" (How do you say bus here?)

Main ideaWhen a word is new, use context, ask ¿Cómo se dice…?, and remember that speakers adjust toward a shared, neutral Spanish when they talk across borders.

Main ideaSpanish has more than one word for you; tú and vos are for people you are close to, and usted is for people you show respect or distance to.

Andrés’s first mistake was one word: me puedes llamar instead of me puede llamar. That word is you. English has one you for everyone. Spanish has , and, in some countries, vos, and choosing among them is the first act of register. Tú (and vos where it is used) is for friends, family, classmates, children and anyone your own age in a casual setting. is for adults you do not know, elders, teachers, bosses, customers, officials, and anyone you want to show respect.

Usted uses the same verb forms as él and ella. "¿Cómo está usted?" (How are you?) uses está, not estás. "¿Puede llamarme?" (Can you call me?) uses puede, not puedes. The possessive is su ("su tienda," your shop) and the object pronoun is le or lo/la. In writing, usted is often abbreviated Ud. or Vd. Because usted forms look like third-person forms, a sentence like "¿Tiene tiempo?" can mean Do you have time? or Does he have time? Context, and sometimes adding usted, makes it clear.

Being too formal is a small awkwardness; being too familiar can feel disrespectful.

Main ideaWhen someone says puedes tutearme, every usted form in your speech shifts to tú: está becomes estás, su becomes tu, le becomes te.

If an adult addresses you as tú but keeps a title and formal tone, that is normal; adults often say tú to young people while expecting usted back. That is not rude. It reflects age and role, and it is how Andrés’s grandmother’s friends speak to him. Answering them with usted is a sign that he was raised well, and in many families that matters a great deal.

The Shape of a Formal Letter

Main ideaA formal Spanish letter opens with Estimado or Estimada plus a title and a colon, states its purpose in the first sentence, and closes with Atentamente.

Rocío and Andrés’s second letter follows a shape that Spanish speakers use for job applications, complaints, requests to a school, and business email. At the top, the place and date: "Chicago, 14 de abril de 2026." Notice the pattern: city, comma, day, de, month in lowercase, de, year. Then the greeting: for a man, for a woman, followed by the title and last name and a colon. "Estimada señora Herrera:" If you do not know the name, "Estimados señores:" or "A quien corresponda:" (To whom it may concern:) both work.

The first sentence names the purpose. Spanish has several ready phrases. "Le escribo para solicitar el puesto de…" (I am writing to apply for the position of…). "Me dirijo a usted para…" (I am addressing you in order to…). "Por medio de la presente…" (By means of this letter…) is older and very formal, common in Mexico. The verb means to apply for or request; the noun la solicitud is an application. is a position or job opening.

The body gives two or three short paragraphs: who you are, what you offer, when you are available. Then the closing sentences, which are nearly fixed: "Adjunto mi currículum" (I attach my résumé). Ordering is register in action. The waiter, using usted, asks "¿Qué desea?" or "¿Qué le traigo?" (What would you like? What can I bring you?). You answer with the polite forms you learned: "Quisiera los tacos al pastor, por favor," "Para mí, el pollo," or "¿Me trae un agua de horchata?" Asking for the check: "La cuenta, por favor" is fine; "¿Me podría traer la cuenta?" is softer. Two things to look for in small print: propina incluida (tip included) and IVA incluido (sales tax included). In much of Latin America and Spain the tax is already in the price, and tipping customs vary; in the United States, tip as you normally would.

Spanish menus often carry the vocabulary of the last chapter. A Mexican menu lists elotes, frijoles and aguacate; an Argentine one lists choclo, porotos and palta; a Puerto Rican one lists habichuelas and, for fried plantains, tostones. A Spanish menu lists patatas and zumo. Reading a menu from another country is a small test of everything you know about regional words, and context, as always, helps: a word under postres is a dessert whether or not you have seen it before.

One more courtesy: when you finish and others are still eating, or when you pass a table where people are eating, Spanish speakers say buen provecho (enjoy your meal). It is expected in Mexico and Central America, common in Spain, and a small sign that you know how to be at a table.

38.12

Reading News Headlines

Main ideaSpanish headlines drop articles, use the present tense for past events, and pack a whole story into a few formal words.

The last authentic text is the hardest to read and the most useful: the (headline) of a (newspaper). Spanish-language papers are everywhere: El País in Madrid, Clarín in Buenos Aires, El Universal in Mexico City, El Nuevo Día in San Juan, and in Chicago the weekly La Raza and Spanish sections of larger outlets. Their headlines follow rules that make them look strange until you know the pattern.

First, headlines drop articles. "Gobierno anuncia nuevo plan de transporte" reads Government announces new transportation plan; in a full sentence it would be El gobierno anuncia un nuevo plan de transporte. Second, they use the present tense for things that already happened, to sound immediate: "Lluvias dejan tres heridos en el sur" (Rains leave three injured in the south) describes yesterday’s storm. Third, they use short formal verbs you rarely say aloud: (to announce), aprobar (to approve), rechazar (to reject), dejar with a number (to leave, as in leave injured), subir and bajar (to rise, to fall) for prices and temperatures.

Fourth, headlines love numbers and place names. "Sube 8% el precio de la gasolina" (Gas price rises 8%). "Chicago celebra el Desfile de la Independencia Mexicana en La Villita" (Chicago celebrates the Mexican Independence Parade in Little Village). Once you know these four rules, a headline becomes a sentence with the small words removed. Put them back and read it aloud: subject, verb, what, where.

Everything in a newspaper is formal register. News articles use usted in interviews, full names with titles, and no slang. Reading one headline a day, in a paper from a country you are curious about, is one of the best habits a Spanish student can have. You will learn the vocabulary of the (news story), and you will see how the same language handles Buenos Aires, Madrid, Mexico City and Chicago in one style.

Think it through
Unpack the Headline

Headline: Alcaldía aprueba nuevo parque en Pilsen; vecinos celebran. (City government approves new park in Pilsen; neighbors celebrate.) As full sentences: La alcaldía aprobó un nuevo parque en Pilsen. Los vecinos celebraron. What changed: two articles returned (la, un, los), and the present tense (aprueba, celebran) became the preterite (aprobó, celebraron), because the event already happened. Now try this one on your own: Baja la temperatura y cierran escuelas en el norte de Illinois.

1. Spanish is an official language in how many countries?

Why: Vivís is the vosotros present form (and also the vos form). Viven is ustedes, vivimos nosotros, vivid the command.

4. A speaker from Mexico City pronounces the z in zapato

Why: Latin America uses the seseo: z and soft c sound like s. Most of Spain uses the th sound.

5. Which word for bus would you hear in Puerto Rico?

Why: Guagua is the Caribbean word for bus. Colectivo is Argentina, micro is Chile, and ordenador is Spain's word for computer.

6. Which word came into Spanish from Nahuatl?

Why: Tomate is Nahuatl, like chocolate and aguacate. Almohada is Arabic, hamaca is Taíno, papa is Quechua.

7. Which word came into Spanish from Taíno and then into English?

Why: Huracán is Taíno and became hurricane in English through Spanish. Azúcar is Arabic, chile is Nahuatl, palta is Quechua.

8. Which Chicago neighborhood is the center of the city's Puerto Rican community?

Why: Humboldt Park, with Paseo Boricua on Division Street, is the heart of Puerto Rican Chicago. Pilsen and Little Village are largely Mexican American.

9. Which sentence correctly addresses a teacher you have just met?

Why: Usted takes the él/ella form tiene. Tienes, tenés and tenéis are all informal forms.

10. Which greeting opens a formal letter correctly?

Why: Estimado agrees with a man, the title is lowercase, and the greeting ends with a colon. Querido and Hola are informal; Estimada is feminine.

11. Which request is the most polite?

Why: The conditional podría plus por favor is the softest form. Tráigame is a command; quiero states a want; me trae is polite but plainer.

12. "Estoy muy embarazada" said by a student who arrived late means

Why: Embarazada means pregnant. A student who meant embarrassed should say avergonzada or me da mucha vergüenza.

13. A sign reads "Se busca cajero." It means

Why: Se busca plus a job title is a help-wanted sign. The impersonal se has no specific subject.

14. After a boss says "Puedes tutearme," which sentence is correct?

Why: Tutear means to use tú, so the verb becomes estás. Está tú mixes registers, and estáis is vosotros.

15. The headline "Sube el precio del pan en Chicago" tells you that

Why: Subir means to rise. Headlines use the present tense and short verbs like subir and bajar for prices.

Escribe dos textos sobre el mismo trabajo de verano: una carta formal de 6 a 8 oraciones a la dueña de una empresa (Estimada, usted, tu experiencia, tu disponibilidad, Atentamente) y un mensaje informal de 3 a 4 oraciones a un primo (tú, ¿qué tal?, un abrazo).   • In the letter, use usted forms only: tiene, puede, su; in the message, use tú forms only: tienes, puedes, tu.
