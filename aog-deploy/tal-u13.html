Reading Real Passages: Bava Metzia and Sanhedrin

Texts Close Up

This unit reads two famous passages up close. The first is Bava Metzia 59b, the story of the oven of Akhnai. A rabbi calls on miracles and a voice from heaven, and the majority still rules against him. The second is Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 and Makkot 7a, where the rabbis teach that one life is a whole world and argue about whether a court should ever put anyone to death.

Both passages show the course's two threads. Argument: you will trace how each sugya moves through question, proof, objection and answer. Mercy: you will see whom each ruling protects, from the teacher who lost a vote to the person on trial. You will read verses in the public-domain JPS 1917 translation and rabbinic passages in careful paraphrase.

By the end you will be able to cite a passage by tractate and page, explain what it says, and describe how Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and academic readers have read it. This course describes those readings. It does not tell you which one is right.

The big question

How does the Talmud balance the authority of the law with mercy for the person the law affects?

Bava Metzia 59b: The Oven of Akhnai and Wronging with Words

Bava Metzia
Big questionWhen a majority wins an argument, what does it owe the person who lost?
The story

Heaven Laughs

A rabbi meets the prophet Elijah and asks what God did on the day the sages outvoted a miracle.

The story is told in the Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 59b. Rabbi Nathan, a sage of the second century, is walking when he meets Elijah the prophet. In rabbinic stories Elijah often appears to sages and answers their questions. Rabbi Nathan has a question about a famous quarrel in the house of study.

The quarrel was about an oven. Rabbi Eliezer said the oven was ritually pure. The other sages said it was impure. Rabbi Eliezer brought every argument he had. When they did not accept them, he called on a carob tree, a stream and the walls of the study house to prove him right. Each one moved. Then a voice from heaven agreed with him.

Rabbi Joshua stood up and quoted Deuteronomy: it is not in heaven. The sages ruled against Rabbi Eliezer anyway. So Rabbi Nathan asks Elijah what God did in that hour. The Gemara gives Elijah's answer: God smiled and said, My children have defeated me, my children have defeated me.

But the story does not end with a smile. The sages burn the things Rabbi Eliezer had called pure, and they ban him. A great teacher weeps. Harm follows in the world, and a leader dies. The Gemara tells all of this inside a chapter about hurting people with words. This chapter reads the whole passage and asks why.

Talk about itWhy might the Talmud place a story about a winning majority inside a discussion of hurting people with words?
Section 1

The Frame: Wronging with Words

25.1

A Chapter About Fair Dealing

Main ideaBava Metzia 59b sits inside a chapter about ona'ah, wronging people in trade and in speech.

Bava Metzia means the Middle Gate. It is the middle of three tractates about damages and property. Its fourth chapter deals with ona’ah, a Hebrew word for wronging or cheating. The Mishnah first discusses overcharging: how far a price may stray from fair value before a sale can be undone.

Then Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:10 turns from money to speech. It says that just as there is wronging in buying and selling, there is wronging in words. Its examples are small and sharp. Do not ask a seller the price of something if you have no plan to buy it. Do not say to someone who has changed their ways, remember what you used to do.

The Mishnah also warns against reminding a convert of what their ancestors did. The Gemara that follows gathers teachings on this theme. The oven story comes in the middle of that discussion. So the editors placed it in a chapter whose subject is the harm done by careless or cruel words.

From the source
Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:10
Just as there is wronging in buying and selling, there is wronging in words. A person should not ask, How much is this? when they do not mean to buy. To someone who has turned their life around, do not say, Remember your former deeds.
Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:10, paraphrased from the Hebrew (compare Danby, The Mishnah, 1933) (paraphrased)

Which of the Mishnah's examples seems smallest to you, and why might the Mishnah still count it as a wrong?

25.2

Shame Like Shedding Blood

Main ideaThe Gemara treats public shaming as a very grave harm, comparing it to shedding blood.

Just before the oven story, on Bava Metzia 58b, the Gemara reports a teaching heard in the study house. Whoever makes a friend’s face go pale in public is like one who sheds blood. The image is physical. When a person is shamed, the red leaves their face and it turns white, as if blood were spilled.

The Gemara piles up related sayings. One says that verbal wronging is worse than cheating in money, because money can be paid back and shame cannot. Another says a person should rather be thrown into a furnace than shame someone in public. The sages who taught these lines were using strong words on purpose.

Readers have taken this passage in different ways. Many Jewish teachers use it in schools and sermons as a rule of daily kindness. Legal writers ask how it applies in business or courts. Either way, it sets the mood for what follows. The oven story is told right after it, and its readers are primed to watch for shame.

Think it through
Two kinds of harm

Case one: a seller overcharges you by a few dollars. Case two: someone mocks you in front of your class. The Gemara on Bava Metzia 58b says the second harm is greater, because money can be returned but lost face cannot.

Do you agree with the Gemara's reasoning? What could repair each harm?

Check yourself

1. On Bava Metzia 58b, making someone's face go pale in public is compared to what?

Why: The Gemara says whitening a friend's face in public is like shedding blood.

2. Why does the Gemara say verbal wronging is worse than cheating in money?

Why: The passage contrasts money, which can be repaid, with shame, which cannot.

25.3

The Verses Behind the Rule

Main ideaThe rabbis ground verbal wronging in Leviticus 25:17 and care for the stranger in Exodus 22:20.

The rabbis ask where the ban on hurtful words comes from. Leviticus 25 has two verses that both say, do not wrong one another. Verse 14 speaks of buying and selling. The Gemara reads verse 17 as a second command, and since money was already covered, it applies this one to words.

This is a typical rabbinic move. The rabbis assume the Torah does not repeat itself for nothing. When a command seems to appear twice, they look for a new meaning in the second one. Here the extra verse becomes the source of a whole field of ethics about speech.

The Gemara also brings Exodus 22:20 about the stranger, the ger. In rabbinic reading, ger usually means a convert to Judaism. The Gemara teaches that someone who wrongs a convert breaks more than one command. Care for the vulnerable, the person without family or standing in the community, is built into the law.

From the source
Leviticus 25:17
And ye shall not wrong one another; but thou shalt fear thy God; for I am the LORD your God.
Leviticus 25:17, The Holy Scriptures, Jewish Publication Society, 1917

The Gemara reads this verse as a rule about words. What in the verse's wording supports or resists that reading?

Section 2

The Oven and the Voice

25.4

An Oven in Pieces

Main ideaThe debate began over whether an oven built from segments with sand between them could become impure.

The Mishnah in tractate Kelim 5:10 describes an oven cut into rings, with sand placed between the pieces. Rabbi Eliezer said such an oven is pure, meaning it cannot receive ritual impurity. The sages said it is impure. The laws of purity mattered for food and for the Temple, and they were studied long after the Temple fell.

Why would the pieces matter? In rabbinic law, a broken vessel is not a vessel and cannot become impure. Rabbi Eliezer may have seen the ringed oven as broken pieces. The sages saw that the plaster or sand bound it into one working oven. Both views have a logic, and the Gemara does not mock either.

The Gemara explains the oven’s odd name. Akhnai is related to the Aramaic for snake. The Gemara says the sages surrounded the oven with arguments like a snake coiled around it and declared it impure. The name already hints at a debate that wraps around a person and holds on.

Think it through
A broken or a whole oven?

An oven is cut into rings. Sand is packed between the rings, and plaster covers the outside. It still bakes bread. Rabbi Eliezer calls it pure. The sages call it impure.

Give the strongest reason for each side. Which fact does each side treat as most important?

25.5

Miracles on Trial

Main ideaRabbi Eliezer calls on a tree, a stream, the walls and a heavenly voice, and the sages refuse them all.

The Gemara says that on that day Rabbi Eliezer answered every possible argument, and the sages did not accept them. Then he said, if the law is as I say, let this carob tree prove it. The tree was uprooted and moved a hundred cubits; some say four hundred. The sages replied, one does not bring proof from a carob tree.

He asked the stream to prove it, and the water flowed backward. He asked the walls of the study house, and they began to lean. Rabbi Joshua scolded the walls: when scholars argue about law, what business is it of yours? The Gemara says the walls did not fall, in honor of Rabbi Joshua, and did not straighten, in honor of Rabbi Eliezer. They are still leaning, it adds.

Finally a bat kol, a heavenly voice, declared that the law follows Rabbi Eliezer everywhere. Many readers note the humor in the leaning walls. They also note the pattern: each proof is bigger than the last, and each is answered the same way. The question is not whether miracles happen in the story. It is whether they count as legal proof.

Think it through
The walls that kept leaning

The walls of the study house begin to fall. Rabbi Joshua scolds them. They stop halfway: not falling, out of respect for him, and not standing straight, out of respect for Rabbi Eliezer.

What does this picture say about how the story wants readers to treat both rabbis?

Words to know

literally daughter of a voice; in rabbinic stories, a voice from heaven
aggadah

cubit
an ancient length, roughly the length of a forearm
Check yourself

1. What did the sages say after the carob tree moved?

Why: The sages refused to treat the miracle as legal proof.

2. Why did the walls of the study house stay leaning, according to the Gemara?

Why: The Gemara says they did not fall for Rabbi Joshua's honor and did not rise for Rabbi Eliezer's.

25.6

It Is Not in Heaven

Main ideaRabbi Joshua answers the heavenly voice with Deuteronomy 30:12, reading it to mean the law is decided on earth.

When the heavenly voice speaks, Rabbi Joshua stands and says, it is not in heaven. He is quoting Deuteronomy 30:12. In its setting, Moses tells Israel that the commandment is not too far away. It is not up in heaven, so no one needs to climb up and fetch it. It is near, in their mouth and heart.

The Gemara asks what Rabbi Joshua meant. Rabbi Jeremiah explains: the Torah was already given at Mount Sinai, so the sages do not follow a heavenly voice. The Torah itself says to incline after the majority, a rabbinic reading of Exodus 23:2. In the story, the Torah is handed over, and people must now interpret it.

Readers have drawn different lessons. Many Orthodox teachers read it as a statement about the authority of the sages who carry the tradition. Conservative and Reform writers often cite it for the idea that law develops through human interpretation. Academic readers note that the story is told by the rabbis about their own authority, and they study how it builds that claim.

From the source
Deuteronomy 30:12
It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say: 'Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, and make us to hear it, that we may do it?'
Deuteronomy 30:12, The Holy Scriptures, Jewish Publication Society, 1917

In Deuteronomy the verse says the law is near. How does Rabbi Joshua use it for a new purpose?

25.7

My Children Have Defeated Me

Main ideaIn the story, Elijah reports that God smiled and said, My children have defeated me.

The Gemara now turns to Rabbi Nathan meeting Elijah. Rabbi Nathan asks what the Holy One did in that hour. Elijah says that God smiled and said, My children have defeated me, my children have defeated me. The line is doubled in the text, as if repeated with delight.

The word for defeated, nitzchuni, has been read in more than one way. Many read it as victory: the students won the argument, and the teacher is proud. Some later commentators read it as eternalized, from the same Hebrew root: my children have made me last forever. Both readings are offered in Jewish tradition.

This part of the passage is aggadah, a story rather than a ruling. It describes God as a parent who enjoys being outargued. Readers across Jewish movements quote it to celebrate study and debate. Others warn against reading it alone, because the next lines of the Gemara are not joyful at all.

From the source
Rabbi Nathan meets Elijah
Rabbi Nathan asked Elijah: What did the Holy One, blessed be He, do in that hour? Elijah said: He smiled and said, My children have defeated me, my children have defeated me.
Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 59b, paraphrased (compare Rodkinson, New Edition of the Babylonian Talmud, 1896–1903) (paraphrased)

Section 3

The Cost of Winning

25.8

The Ban on Rabbi Eliezer

Main ideaAfter the vote, the sages burn what Rabbi Eliezer called pure and ban him, and Rabbi Akiva carries the news gently.

The Gemara continues. That day the sages brought everything Rabbi Eliezer had declared pure and burned it. They voted to place him under a ban, cutting him off from the community of scholars. Then they asked who would go and tell him. The news had to reach a great teacher, and it would hurt.

Rabbi Akiva, his student, said he would go, so that no unfit person would tell him and destroy the world. Akiva dressed in black and sat at a distance of four cubits, signs of mourning and of the ban. Rabbi Eliezer asked what was different about today. Akiva answered carefully: it seems to me your colleagues are keeping away from you.

Rabbi Eliezer tore his clothes, took off his shoes and sat on the ground, and he wept. Readers notice the gentleness of the messenger and the weight of the moment. The majority won the law, but the story now shows a great teacher in grief. The words of the sages, however right, have wounded a person.

25.9

Harm in the World

Main ideaThe Gemara describes damage that follows Rabbi Eliezer's grief, including a storm that threatens Rabban Gamliel.

The Gemara says that after the ban, the world was struck: a third of the olives, a third of the wheat and a third of the barley. Some say even dough in a woman’s hands spoiled. Wherever Rabbi Eliezer set his eyes, it burned. The story treats his pain as powerful.

Rabban Gamliel, the head of the academy who had led the ban, was traveling on a ship. A great wave rose to drown him. He said, it seems to me this is because of Rabbi Eliezer. He stood and prayed: you know that I did not do this for my own honor, but for yours, so that disputes would not multiply in Israel. The sea grew calm.

Readers point out that the story takes both sides seriously. Rabban Gamliel’s reason is real: a community needs a way to settle law. Yet the story still shows the cost of the ban. It does not say the majority was wrong about the oven. It says that winning does not erase the hurt.

25.10

The Gates of Wounding

Main ideaImma Shalom's words close the story: every gate may be locked except the gate of those who are wounded.

The last scene takes place in Rabbi Eliezer’s home. His wife, Imma Shalom, was Rabban Gamliel’s sister. The Gemara says she did not let her husband fall on his face in the supplication prayer, fearing his prayer of pain would harm her brother. One day, a poor man came to the door, and she went to give him bread.

Rabbi Eliezer fell on his face in prayer. When she returned she said, get up, you have killed my brother. Just then a shofar sounded from Rabban Gamliel’s house to announce his death. Rabbi Eliezer asked how she knew. She said she had a tradition from her father’s house: all the gates are locked, except the gates of ona’ah.

Now the frame closes. The chapter began with wronging in words and ends with it. Many readers, including the scholar Jeffrey Rubenstein in Talmudic Stories (1999), argue that this ending shows the editors’ purpose: the tale is finally about the pain of Rabbi Eliezer’s shaming. Others emphasize the teaching about majority rule. The Talmud keeps both in view.

Section 4

Reading It Whole

25.11

Authority and Mercy Together

Main ideaThe passage holds two lessons at once: the majority decides the law, and the one who loses must still be treated with care.

Read quickly, the passage seems to teach one thing: the sages, by majority vote, decide the law. That idea became central in Jewish legal thought. But read in its setting, a second lesson runs alongside. The ban and its cost are told in detail, and the chapter’s subject is hurting people with words.

The Talmud often works this way. It does not always say which lesson is the main one. It places them side by side and leaves the reader to weigh them. The minority view of Rabbi Eliezer is not erased either. It remains in the Mishnah in Kelim, named and recorded.

This is where the course’s two threads meet. Argument: the story shows how a law is settled, even against a miracle. Mercy: it shows what a community owes the person who loses. A student who reads only one half has missed the other. The editors seem to want readers to hold both.

25.12

How Modern Readers Disagree

Main ideaOrthodox, Conservative, Reform and academic readers use the oven story in different ways without one reading replacing the others.

Many Orthodox writers read the story as teaching the authority of the rabbinic tradition. The law is decided by qualified sages through the rules of interpretation. A heavenly voice cannot overturn it. In this view, the chain of teachers carries the Torah faithfully.

Conservative writers have often cited the story to show that Jewish law is shaped by human interpretation over time, within a community of scholars. Reform writers sometimes use it to honor debate and individual conscience, and some point to Rabbi Eliezer as a voice for the one who stands alone. Each movement finds support in the text.

Academic scholars, such as Izhak Englard in a 1975 article and Jeffrey Rubenstein in 1999, study how the story was built and when its parts were added. Some compare it with a shorter version in the Jerusalem Talmud. This course does not choose a winner. It shows how one passage can carry many readings.

Sanhedrin 37a and Makkot 7a: One Life, and a Court That Rarely Kills

Sanhedrin
Big questionHow can a legal system that allows the death penalty also work hard to keep it from being used?
The story

If We Had Sat on the Court

Two famous rabbis say that under them, no one would ever have been executed.

The Mishnah in tractate Makkot, chapter 1, is discussing courts. It gives a surprising line. A Sanhedrin that puts a person to death once in seven years is called destructive. Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah goes further: once in seventy years. The Mishnah is describing a court that almost never kills.

Then two great sages speak. Rabbi Tarfon and Rabbi Akiva say that if they had been on the Sanhedrin, no person would ever have been executed. The Gemara on Makkot 7a asks how. It answers with the kind of questions they would ask witnesses, questions so exact that testimony would almost always fail.

But the Mishnah gives one more voice. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel answers: they too would multiply murderers in Israel. A court that never punishes, he warns, may fail to protect people. The Mishnah does not settle the debate. It ends with both views standing.

This chapter reads two passages. One is Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, with its famous line that one life is a whole world. The other is Makkot 7a. Together they show a body of law that keeps capital punishment on the books, surrounds it with protections, and argues about whether to use it at all.

Talk about itRabban Shimon ben Gamliel worries about the victims of crime. Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Tarfon worry about the accused. How could both concerns come from mercy?
Section 1

One Life, a Whole World

26.1

Warning the Witnesses

Main ideaMishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 describes how judges warned witnesses in capital cases about the weight of a human life.

Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 begins in a courtroom. In a capital case, the judges bring in the witnesses and warn them. Perhaps you are speaking from guesswork, or from rumor, or from what another witness said. Perhaps you did not know we would question you closely.

Then the Mishnah explains why capital cases are different. In money cases, a false witness can pay money back. In capital cases, the blood of the accused and of his descendants that might have been is on the witness until the end of the world. The warning is meant to make a witness afraid of lying or guessing.

The Mishnah then reads Genesis 4:10, where God tells Cain, the voice of your brother’s blood cries. In Hebrew the word for blood is plural, bloods. The Mishnah takes this as meaning Abel’s blood and the blood of all his descendants who would never be born. One death cuts off a future.

From the source
The warning to witnesses
Know that capital cases are not like money cases. In money cases, a person can pay and be forgiven. In capital cases, the blood of the accused and of his offspring hang on the witness to the end of the world.
Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrased from the Hebrew (compare Danby, The Mishnah, 1933; Babylonian Talmud Sanhedrin 37a) (paraphrased)

Why would the court want witnesses to feel this weight before they speak?

26.2

Why One Person Was Created

Main ideaThe Mishnah teaches that humanity began with a single person, so that destroying one life is like destroying a world.

The Mishnah continues: for this reason a single human being was created. It points to Adam, the first person in Genesis. Because all of humanity came from one person, each person carries a whole world within them. The Mishnah draws a legal and moral rule from the creation story.

Its most famous line follows. Whoever destroys a single soul, Scripture counts it as if they destroyed a full world. Whoever saves a single soul, Scripture counts it as if they saved a full world. This line is quoted on buildings, awards and memorials, and in films and speeches around the world.

The Mishnah lists other reasons too. People cannot say, my ancestor was greater than yours, since all share one ancestor. And the Mishnah uses a picture: a human king stamps coins with one seal and they all look alike. The Holy One, it says, stamped every person with the seal of the first human, yet no two are alike.

Think it through
The coin and the person

A mint stamps a thousand coins with one die, and every coin is the same. The Mishnah says each human is stamped with the first human's seal, yet each one is different.

What point about human worth does this picture make? Why use money as the example?

Words to know

2. In the Mishnah's picture, how is God's seal different from a king's?

3. Which social lesson does the Mishnah draw from one ancestor?

Why: A shared ancestor means shared dignity.
26.3

Two Versions of the Line

Main ideaManuscripts and printed editions differ on whether the line says one soul or one soul of Israel.

The famous line exists in two versions. Many printed editions of the Babylonian Talmud read: whoever destroys a single soul of Israel. Some important manuscripts of the Mishnah and the Jerusalem Talmud read simply: a single soul. Scholars have compared these texts for more than a century.

Many scholars think the shorter, universal wording is earlier, since the Mishnah’s argument is about Adam, the ancestor of all people. Others note that the longer wording appears in many traditional editions and has been read and taught for centuries. Both versions are part of the history of the text.

The line also has a parallel outside Judaism. The Qur’an, in Surah 5:32, speaks of the Children of Israel being told that killing one person is like killing all people, and saving one is like saving all. Muslim and Jewish readers have discussed this connection. This lesson simply notes the parallel and its source.

Section 2

How the Court Protected the Accused

26.4

Twenty-Three Judges

Main ideaThe Mishnah requires a court of twenty-three for capital cases and builds rules that tilt toward acquittal.

Mishnah Sanhedrin 1:4 says capital cases are judged by a court of twenty-three. Money cases need only three. The Great Sanhedrin, with seventy-one, handled the gravest national questions. The larger court for life-and-death cases meant more minds and more chances to find doubt.

Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:1 lists the differences. In capital cases, the court opens with arguments for acquittal. A majority of one is enough to acquit, but conviction needs a majority of two. A judge who argued to acquit may not switch to convict, though a judge who argued to convict may switch to acquit.

Other rules follow. A verdict of acquittal can be given the same day, but conviction must wait until the next day. In capital cases the youngest judges speak first, so they are not swayed by their elders, according to Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:2. Each rule makes an execution harder to reach.

26.5

Questioning the Witnesses

Main ideaThe Mishnah requires close questioning of witnesses, and any contradiction on key points ends the testimony.

Mishnah Sanhedrin 5:1 says witnesses were examined with seven questions. In which seven-year cycle did it happen? In which year, month, day of the month, day of the week, hour and place? If the two witnesses disagreed on these, their testimony was thrown out.

The Mishnah also describes other checks. Did the witnesses recognize the accused? Did they warn him first? The rabbis read the Torah’s rules, such as the need for two witnesses in Deuteronomy 17:6, very strictly. Each rule narrowed the path to a conviction.

The Mishnah even says the more a judge examines, the more praiseworthy he is. It tells how Ben Zakkai once questioned witnesses about the stems of figs under the tree. Details like these could reveal a false or mistaken witness. Such careful questioning protected the accused.

From the source
Two witnesses
At the mouth of two witnesses, or three witnesses, shall he that is to die be put to death; at the mouth of one witness he shall not be put to death.
Deuteronomy 17:6, The Holy Scriptures, Jewish Publication Society, 1917

How does this verse already limit the death penalty before the rabbis add their own rules?

26.6

If All Convict, Acquit

Main ideaThe Gemara records the striking view that if every judge votes to convict, the accused goes free.

On Sanhedrin 17a, the Gemara reports a teaching from Rav Kahana, a Babylonian amora. If the Sanhedrin votes unanimously to convict, the accused is acquitted. At first this sounds backward. Surely a unanimous court is the most certain? Many students react that way on a first reading.

The Gemara’s explanation is brief. The common explanation in later commentary is that a court with no one arguing for the accused has lost a needed voice. Without debate, the judges may be in a rush to judgment. Rashi and others explain that a court needs someone who searches for merit.

Legal historians find this rule remarkable. It treats disagreement as a safeguard. It fits the whole Talmud, which keeps minority opinions alive. Readers debate how practical it was, since the Talmud itself says capital cases had largely ceased by its editing.

Think it through
A unanimous vote

Imagine twenty-three judges. Every one of them votes guilty. Not a single judge found a reason to doubt. Rav Kahana says: acquit.

Is a unanimous verdict more trustworthy or less? Give a reason for each side before choosing.

Words to know
amora
a rabbi of the Gemara era, c. 200–500 CE
unanimous

zekhut
merit; an argument in favor of the accused

Section 3

A Court That Rarely Kills

26.7

Once in Seven Years

Main ideaMishnah Makkot 1:10 calls a court that executes once in seven years destructive, and some say once in seventy.

Mishnah Makkot 1:10 describes a Sanhedrin that executes once in seven years. It is called destructive. Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah says once in seventy years. The Gemara on Makkot 7a asks whether this means once in seven, or even less often. Either way, a court that kills often is condemned.

Then Rabbi Tarfon and Rabbi Akiva say that if they had been on the Sanhedrin, no one would ever have been executed. The Gemara asks how. Its answer is that they would have asked questions like: did you see whether the victim was already mortally wounded? Such questions could make a conviction almost impossible.

Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel responds that they would increase murderers in Israel. The Mishnah records his worry and does not answer it. The passage gives three positions: rare executions, no executions, and a warning that justice must still protect the public.

From the source
The destructive court
A Sanhedrin that executes once in seven years is called destructive. Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah says: once in seventy years. Rabbi Tarfon and Rabbi Akiva say: had we been on the Sanhedrin, no one would ever have been executed. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says: they would multiply murderers in Israel.
Mishnah Makkot 1:10, discussed at Babylonian Talmud Makkot 7a, paraphrased (compare Danby, The Mishnah, 1933) (paraphrased)

26.8

The Law Against Its Own Use

Main ideaScholars describe rabbinic capital law as a system written so strictly that it almost never allowed an execution.

Put the pieces together. The Torah names crimes that carry the death penalty. The Mishnah keeps those laws. But it surrounds them with rules: two witnesses, warning beforehand, close questioning, a large court, a tilt to acquittal. The result, many scholars say, is a law that works against its own use.

History matters here. The Talmud reports that the Sanhedrin stopped hearing capital cases about forty years before the Temple fell in 70 CE (Sanhedrin 41a). Under Roman rule, Jewish courts likely lacked that power. So much of this law was studied, not practiced, by the rabbis who shaped it.

Readers disagree about what that means. Some see the rules as ideals from a time without such power. Others see them as a real legal philosophy. Many Jewish groups have drawn on these passages in modern debates about the death penalty. Their positions differ, and the course simply records the texts they cite.

26.9

Mercy and Justice in Tension

Main ideaThe sugyot keep two kinds of mercy in view: mercy for the accused and protection for possible victims.

Both chapters of this unit end the same way. The Talmud does not give one simple answer. In Makkot, Akiva and Tarfon protect the accused, and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel protects the public. In Bava Metzia, the majority decides the law, and the story defends the one who lost.

Each side has its own idea of mercy. Mercy for the accused asks that no innocent life be taken. Mercy for victims asks that the community be kept safe. Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 supports both, since it teaches that every life, the victim’s and the accused’s, is a whole world.

Many teachers today use these passages to train students in argument. They ask learners to state the strongest version of each side before choosing. That is the method of the chavruta, the study partnership. It fits a text that keeps both voices on the page.

Think it through
Steelman both sides

Partner A defends Rabbi Akiva: better to free the guilty than kill the innocent. Partner B defends Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel: a court that never acts leaves victims unprotected.

With a partner, state the other side's best argument before your own. Which parts of Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 does each side use?

justice
