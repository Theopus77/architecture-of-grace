Capstone: Present and Defend

Capstone

You have spent years collecting pieces of Spanish: the present tense and its stem changes, the preterite and the imperfect, the future and the conditional, the subjunctive in two tenses, tú and usted, the connectors that hold a paragraph together. This unit asks you to put all of it to work at once. In the first chapter, two seniors in Cicero edit each other's essays and rebuild the whole map of tenses and moods as a set of questions you can ask about any verb. In the second, a senior in Humboldt Park builds, delivers and defends a five-minute talk in Spanish about why her family's language matters.

The review chapter is not a list of rules to memorize again. It walks through real sentences from real drafts, shows why one form fits and another does not, and teaches a six-pass routine for catching your own errors before anyone else does. The presentation chapter follows a talk from topic to thesis (Sostengo que…), from three supporting reasons with evidence to transitions and a conclusion, and finally to the questions, where you learn to say Entiendo su punto, pero… and mean it.

By the end you will be able to choose the right verb form from context in a longer passage, edit a page of Spanish with a routine you can repeat, and stand in front of a room with five index cards and a claim you can defend. That is the capstone: not a test of what you remember, but a demonstration of what you can do.

The big question

How do you bring everything you know about Spanish together to write, speak and defend an idea of your own?

The story

Two Red Pens

Two seniors trade essays on a Tuesday afternoon and discover that finding someone else's mistakes is the fastest way to learn your own.

Marisol and Andrés sit at a corner table in the library of their high school in Cicero, just west of Chicago. Their Spanish teacher has paired them up to edit each other's essays before the final draft is due. Marisol's topic is her grandmother's bakery in Little Village. Andrés wrote about the soccer league at his park. Each of them holds a red pen and a printed page, and neither one wants to go first.

Andrés starts reading Marisol's second paragraph aloud. "Cuando yo era niña, mi abuela abrió la panadería." (When I was a girl, my grandmother opened the bakery.) He stops. "Era for the background, abrió for the event. That is right." Then he reads the next line: "Ella trabajaba allí por veinte años." He circles trabajaba. "Twenty years, finished, counted. That should be trabajó." Marisol groans, then nods. She has heard both forms her whole life; she has just never had to choose one on paper.

Marisol takes her turn with his essay. "Es importante que los niños tienen un lugar seguro." (It is important that the kids have a safe place.) She underlines tienen. "Es importante que is a trigger. It wants tengan." Andrés looks at the sentence for a long moment. "I know that rule," he says. "I just did not see it while I was writing." She finds two more: a sentence that begins with *sin embargo* (however) but adds a point instead of contrasting one, and *por lo tanto* (therefore) glued to a sentence it does not follow from.

By the end of the hour, each essay has a dozen circles and every circle has a reason next to it. Their teacher stops by and reads over their shoulders. "You know more grammar than you think," she tells them. "The problem is never one sentence. The problem is keeping the whole map in your head at once." This chapter is that map: the tenses, the moods, the connectors, and a routine for catching your own errors before a classmate has to.

The Tense Map

39.1

Present and Present Perfect

Main ideaThe present tense states what is happening or what is usually true, while the present perfect (he hablado) reports what has happened up to now.

Marisol’s first paragraph opens with two sentences that look alike but do different work. "Mi abuela trabaja en la panadería todos los días" (My grandmother works in the bakery every day). "Mi abuela ha trabajado allí toda su vida" (My grandmother has worked there her whole life). The first is the simple : a habit, a fact that is true right now. The second is the present perfect, built from the helper verb plus a past participle, and it reaches from the past up to this moment.

The helper is easy to memorize because it is short: he, has, ha, hemos, habéis, han. The participle is the verb’s -ado or -ido form: trabajar → trabajado, comer → comido, vivir → vivido. Put them together and you have he trabajado, has comido, hemos vivido. The participle never changes for the subject; only the helper does. English speakers sometimes write "he trabajo" because English drops the ending in "I have worked" only in speech, not in the verb. In Spanish the -ado is not optional.

A handful of participles are irregular, and they show up constantly in essays: hecho (done, made), dicho (said), escrito (written), visto (seen), puesto (put), vuelto (returned), abierto (opened), roto (broken), muerto (died). "He escrito tres borradores" (I have written three drafts). "¿Has visto la película?" (Have you seen the movie?) Writing "he escribido" is the single most common participle error, because the regular pattern is so strong.

When do you choose the perfect over the simple present or the preterite? Use it for experience up to now ("Nunca he estado en Guatemala," I have never been to Guatemala) and for recent events still connected to the present ("Hoy he terminado el ensayo," Today I have finished the essay). Speakers in much of Latin America prefer the preterite for that second use, "Hoy terminé el ensayo," and both are correct. The rule that never changes: nothing goes between the helper and the participle. "No lo he visto," never "he no visto" or "he lo visto."

39.2

Preterite Beside Imperfect

Main ideaThe preterite reports a completed event, the imperfect paints background and habit, and a longer passage needs both working together.

Andrés circled trabajaba in Marisol’s essay, and he was right. "Ella trabajó allí veinte años" (She worked there for twenty years) counts a finished stretch of time, and a counted, finished stretch takes the preterite. The trabajaba would say she used to work there, with no edges on the time. Both tenses talk about the past; the difference is whether the writer is looking at an event with a beginning and an end, or at a scene that was going on.

The quickest way to keep them apart in an essay is to sort every past verb into two jobs. Job one, the imperfect: what things were like, what people used to do, age, time, weather, and what was happening when something else happened. "Era invierno. Hacía frío. Yo tenía ocho años. Mi abuela amasaba el pan." (It was winter. It was cold. I was eight. My grandmother was kneading the bread.) Job two, the preterite: what happened next, one event after another. "Entró un cliente. Pidió veinte conchas. Mi abuela sonrió." (A customer came in. He ordered twenty conchas. My grandmother smiled.)

The two jobs meet in one sentence all the time, and that sentence is the one students get wrong most. "Mi abuela amasaba el pan cuando entró el cliente" (My grandmother was kneading the bread when the customer came in). The imperfect sets the scene; the preterite interrupts it. Reverse them and the sentence stops making sense. Writing "amasó… cuando entraba" would say she finished kneading at some point during his slow entrance, which is not what happened.

A few verbs shift meaning between the two tenses, and an editor should know them. Sabía means I knew; means I found out. means I knew a person; means I met them. Quería means I wanted; quise means I tried, and no quise means I refused. "Conocí a mi mejor amigo en la panadería" (I met my best friend at the bakery) is a single event, so it takes the preterite. Choosing conocía there would tell the reader you already knew him, which contradicts the story.

39.3

Future and Conditional

Main ideaThe future (hablaré) and the conditional (hablaría) share one stem and one set of irregulars; the future says what will happen, the conditional what would happen.

Marisol ends her essay by looking ahead. "El próximo año mi prima abrirá una segunda panadería" (Next year my cousin will open a second bakery). Then she softens it: "Yo trabajaría con ella si tuviera tiempo" (I would work with her if I had time). Abrirá is the future; trabajaría is the conditional. Both are built the same way: keep the whole infinitive, then add the ending. Future endings are é, ás, á, emos, éis, án. Conditional endings are ía, ías, ía, íamos, íais, ían.

Because the endings attach to the full infinitive, every -ar, -er and -ir verb behaves alike: abriré, abrirás, abrirá; comería, comerías, comería. That makes these two tenses the most regular in the language. The accent marks are not decoration. Hablara without an accent is a subjunctive form; hablará with one is the future. Hablaria is not a word; hablaría is. When an editor sees a future or conditional without its accent, the form is wrong.

Twelve common verbs shorten their stem, and the shortened stem serves both tenses: tener → tendr- (tendré, tendría), poner → pondr-, salir → saldr-, venir → vendr-, poder → podr-, saber → sabr-, querer → querr-, haber → habr-, hacer → har-, decir → dir-, caber → cabr-, valer → valdr-. "Habrá más clientes" (There will be more customers). "Diría la verdad" (I would tell the truth). Students who learn the irregulars for the future get the conditional for free.

Spanish also uses these tenses to guess. "¿Qué hora es? Serán las cinco" (What time is it? It must be about five). "¿Dónde estaba tu tío? Estaría en el taller" (Where was your uncle? He was probably in the shop). The future guesses about now; the conditional guesses about the past. In everyday speech, ir a plus an infinitive often replaces the future for plans: "Voy a abrir la panadería a las cinco." Both are correct. In a formal essay, the one-word future sounds more polished.

The Subjunctive in Review

39.4

Triggers and the Present Subjunctive

Main ideaA trigger phrase plus que plus a change of subject sends the second verb into the present subjunctive, built from the yo form with the opposite vowel.

Marisol underlined tienen in "Es importante que los niños tienen un lugar seguro." The sentence has all three parts of the subjunctive pattern. A trigger: es importante. The word . A new subject: los niños. When those three line up, the verb after que changes its shape: "Es importante que los niños tengan un lugar seguro." The subjunctive is a mood, not a tense; it does not say when something happens, it says the speaker is wishing, judging, doubting or feeling rather than reporting.

Building it is a two-step routine. Step one: take the yo form of the present and drop the -o. Tener → tengo → teng-. Hablar → hablo → habl-. Conocer → conozco → conozc-. Step two: add the opposite vowel endings. For -ar verbs use e: hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen. For -er and -ir verbs use a: tenga, tengas, tenga, tengamos, tengáis, tengan. Starting from the yo form is what keeps the irregular stem: tenga, conozca, salga, diga.

Six verbs do not follow the routine, and every essay uses at least two of them: ser → sea, estar → esté, ir → vaya, haber → haya, saber → sepa, dar → dé. "Espero que sea fácil" (I hope it is easy). "No creo que haya tiempo" (I do not think there is time). Writing "es" or "hay" after those triggers is the mistake to hunt for.

The triggers fall into families you can list on a card. Wishes and wants: querer que, esperar que, ojalá que. Emotions: me alegra que, temo que, es triste que. Impersonal judgments: es importante que, es necesario que, es mejor que. Recommendations: recomendar que, sugerir que, pedir que. Doubt and denial: dudar que, no creer que, no es verdad que. One check catches most errors: if the subject does not change, there is no que and no subjunctive. "Quiero tener un lugar seguro" (I want to have a safe place) keeps the plain infinitive.

1. Es importante que los niños ___ (tener) un lugar seguro.

39.5

Past Subjunctive and If

Main ideaThe imperfect subjunctive comes from the ellos preterite with -ra endings, follows past triggers, and pairs with the conditional in imagined if-sentences.

Marisol’s last paragraph has the sentence "Yo trabajaría con ella si tuviera tiempo." Andrés checks the second half. Tuviera is the imperfect subjunctive, and it is built from a form students already know: the ellos form of the preterite. Tener → tuvieron → drop -ron → tuvie- → add -ra. Hablar → hablaron → hablara. Ir and ser → fueron → fuera. Decir → dijeron → dijera. Because the base is the preterite, every preterite irregular comes along automatically.

The endings are -ra, -ras, -ra, -ramos, -rais, -ran, with one accent in the nosotros form: habláramos, tuviéramos. That accent falls on the vowel before -ramos. Missing it is a small error, but it is the kind an editor is paid to catch. Spain and formal writing also use a second set of endings in -se (hablase, tuviese); they mean the same thing, and the -ra set is what most Chicago classrooms teach.

The imperfect subjunctive has two main jobs. Job one: the same triggers as before, but in the past. "Mi abuela quería que yo aprendiera a hacer pan" (My grandmother wanted me to learn to make bread). "Era importante que los niños tuvieran un lugar seguro." When the trigger verb is in the preterite or imperfect, the verb after que shifts to the past subjunctive to match.

Job two is the if-sentence about an imagined world. The pattern is fixed: si plus the imperfect subjunctive, comma, the conditional. "Si tuviera tiempo, trabajaría con ella." "Si la liga tuviera más dinero, compraría luces" (If the league had more money, it would buy lights). Two mistakes to hunt for: the conditional inside the si clause ("si tendría" is never correct) and the present indicative where an imagined world is meant. "Si tengo tiempo, trabajo con ella" is a fine sentence, but it says the time is a real possibility, not a wish.

39.6

Choosing the Form from Context

Main ideaBefore picking a verb form, ask three questions in order: when, is it finished or background, and is it a fact or a wish, doubt or judgment.

Andrés and Marisol were not short on rules; they were short on a way to apply them fast. Their teacher gave them three questions to ask about any verb in an essay. First, when: past, present or future? Second, if past, is it a completed event or a scene in the background? Third, is the verb reporting a fact, or does a trigger before que turn it into a wish, judgment, doubt or emotion? Answering in that order narrows a dozen forms down to one.

Try it on a line from Andrés’s essay: "Cuando yo (ser) niño, mi padre siempre me (llevar) al parque, y allí (conocer) a mi mejor amigo." Ser: past, background, fact → era. Llevar: past, habit marked by siempre, fact → llevaba. Conocer: past, one completed event, fact → conocí. "Cuando yo era niño, mi padre siempre me llevaba al parque, y allí conocí a mi mejor amigo." Three past verbs, two tenses, no guessing.

Now a line with triggers: "Mi padre quería que yo (jugar) todos los sábados, y hoy espero que mi hijo (hacer) lo mismo." Jugar: past trigger quería que, new subject → jugara. Hacer: present trigger espero que, new subject → haga. Notice that the same question, is there a trigger, sends one verb to the past subjunctive and one to the present subjunctive, and the difference is only the tense of the trigger.

Context also settles cases where the form alone is ambiguous. "Cuando llegue mi tío, arreglará el horno" (When my uncle arrives, he will fix the oven): llegue is subjunctive because the arrival is still ahead. "Cuando llega mi tío, siempre arregla algo" (When my uncle arrives, he always fixes something): llega is indicative because it is a habit. The word cuando did not decide; the timeline did. Readers of a whole essay feel these choices even when they cannot name them, which is why a single wrong tense can make a paragraph feel confusing.

Connecting Ideas

39.7

Contrast Connectors

Main ideaSin embargo, en cambio and aunque all signal contrast, but each one joins ideas in a different shape and aunque can change the mood of the verb after it.

Marisol found a (however) in Andrés’s essay that was doing the wrong job. "La liga tiene cien niños. Sin embargo, tiene veinte entrenadores." (The league has a hundred kids. However, it has twenty coaches.) Nothing in the second sentence contradicts the first; the connector promised a turn that never came. A connector is a word or phrase that tells the reader how the next idea relates to the last one. Use the wrong one and the reader stumbles even when every verb is right.

Three contrast connectors carry most essays. opens a new sentence and reverses expectation: "La liga tiene cien niños. Sin embargo, no tiene un campo con luces." (The league has a hundred kids. However, it has no field with lights.) It is always followed by a comma. (on the other hand, instead) sets two things side by side: "Mi barrio no tiene canchas; en cambio, Pilsen tiene tres." (My neighborhood has no courts; Pilsen, on the other hand, has three.) Pero (but) works inside one sentence and is the everyday choice; sin embargo is its formal cousin.

(although, even though) is the one connector that can change a verb. With the indicative it concedes a fact: "Aunque llueve, jugamos" (Even though it is raining, we play). With the subjunctive it concedes a possibility: "Aunque llueva, jugaremos" (Even if it rains, we will play). English separates the two with even though and even if; Spanish keeps one word and moves the difference into the verb, which is a pattern you have seen all through this course.

An editor’s test for any contrast connector: cover it, read the two ideas, and ask whether the second pushes against the first. If it does not, replace the connector with además (besides, moreover) or simply start the sentence plainly. Andrés fixed his line to "La liga tiene cien niños; sin embargo, solo tiene veinte entrenadores" by adding solo, which turns twenty coaches into a shortage and earns the however.

Adding and Concluding

Main ideaAdemás adds a point, por lo tanto draws a conclusion, and a connector should only appear where the logic between two ideas truly exists.

The second connector Marisol circled was (therefore) glued onto a sentence it did not follow from. "El parque está cerca de la escuela. Por lo tanto, los niños juegan fútbol." (The park is near the school. Therefore, the kids play soccer.) Therefore promises that the second idea is caused by the first. Being near the school does not cause soccer. Andrés meant "Por lo tanto, muchos niños llegan caminando" (Therefore, many kids arrive on foot), and once he wrote that, the connector made sense.

Addition connectors stack evidence. (besides, moreover) opens a sentence and adds a new reason: "El campo no tiene luces. Además, la cerca está rota." (The field has no lights. Moreover, the fence is broken.) También (also) sits inside the sentence next to the verb: "La cerca también está rota." Asimismo (likewise) is the formal version you will see in newspapers. All three add; none of them contrast or conclude.

Conclusion connectors close a chain of reasoning. and (that is why) both mean the previous ideas lead here; por lo tanto is more formal. En conclusión and (in summary) open a final paragraph. "El campo no tiene luces y la cerca está rota. Por lo tanto, la liga necesita apoyo de la ciudad." (The field has no lights and the fence is broken. Therefore, the league needs support from the city.) The conclusion is earned because two reasons came before it.

Two habits make connectors work. First, punctuation: sin embargo, además, por lo tanto and en cambio at the start of a sentence take a comma after them. Second, variety with restraint. A paragraph with a connector on every sentence reads like a checklist. Use one where the logic turns or lands, and let plain sentences carry the rest. The Error Checklist

Main ideaMost essay errors fall into five families, and checking for one family at a time catches more than reading for everything at once.

When Marisol and Andrés listed every circle on their two essays, the mistakes sorted into five piles. Agreement: a noun and its article or adjective did not match. Ser and estar: the wrong verb for a description. Verb endings: the -o ending on a subject that was not yo, or the preterite and imperfect swapped. Accents: a missing mark that changed the word. English word order: an adjective in front of its noun, or a missing personal a. Five families cover most of what an editor will ever find.

(agreement) errors come from writing fast. "Los panaderías" should be las panaderías; "una problema" should be un problema, because problema is masculine despite its -a. Adjectives follow the noun and take its gender and number: "las conchas frescas," not "las frescas conchas" and not "las conchas fresco." Ser and estar errors come from meaning: "La panadería está en la calle 26" (location, estar), "La panadería es pequeña" (a lasting trait, ser), "El pan está caliente" (a state right now, estar).

Accent errors are silent on the page but loud in meaning. "Hablo" is I speak; "habló" is he or she spoke. "Esta" is this; "está" is he or she is. "Si" is if; "sí" is yes. "Mi" is my; "mí" is me. A reader who meets "mi abuela hablo con el cliente" has to stop and rebuild the sentence. The fix is mechanical: every preterite yo and él form of a regular verb carries an accent, and every question word (qué, cómo, dónde, cuándo) does too.

The last family is English sneaking in. Spanish puts the personal before a person who is the object of the verb: "Conocí a mi amigo," "Vi a mi abuela." It uses tener, not ser, for age: "Tengo diecisiete años." It says "me gusta el pan" with the thing liked as the subject, so the plural is "me gustan las conchas." Reading your own draft aloud is the best test for this family, because a sentence that follows English order will sound like translation.

Main ideaIn a real passage, the verb forms and connectors do work you can trace; reading for them is how you learn to write them.

Tiene and ha ganado bring the story to now. Aprenda and se jubile are present subjunctives after quiere que and antes de que. Tuviera and sería are the if-sentence. Trabajaré is a plan.

A Routine for Self-Correction

Main ideaEditing your own writing works when it is a fixed routine: one pass per error family, read aloud, then check the connectors last.

The teacher’s last lesson before the final draft is not grammar; it is procedure. Nobody catches everything in one reading, so the routine is several short readings, each hunting one thing. Pass one: circle every verb and write its subject above it. Any verb ending in -o with a subject other than yo is a suspect. Pass two: mark every past verb P or I and ask the finished-or-background question. Pass three: find every que and look to its left for a trigger. If there is one, the verb to the right must be subjunctive, present or past to match.

Pass four is agreement and accents, and it goes fastest backward. Reading the essay from the last sentence to the first breaks the flow of meaning, so your eye stops on each word instead of gliding. Check each article against its noun, each adjective against both, and each preterite yo and él form for its accent. Marisol found her "una problema" this way; forward, she had read past it three times.

Pass five is reading aloud, and it is the pass heritage speakers skip and should not. Your ear has heard correct Spanish for years, and it will flag "no creo que viene" or "me gusta las conchas" before your grammar knowledge does. Read at a normal speaking pace and stop wherever you stumble; the stumble is usually a real error. Pass six, the last, is connectors: cover each one, read the two ideas, and ask whether the relationship it promises is really there.

The whole routine takes about fifteen minutes for a one-page essay. Andrés timed it. Choosing a Topic and a Thesis

40.1

A Topic You Can Stand Behind

Main ideaA good presentation topic about the Spanish-speaking world is narrow enough to cover in five minutes and personal enough that you have something to say.

Lucía’s first idea was "la cultura latina" (Latino culture). Her teacher asked her how she would cover that in five minutes, and Lucía admitted she could not. Her second idea was "la comida puertorriqueña" (Puerto Rican food), better but still a book. Her third was the one she kept: why her own family’s Spanish matters. It was narrow, it was hers, and she already knew the stories. A (topic) for a five-minute talk should pass that test: you could explain it to a friend in the hallway, and you would want to.

Topics about the Spanish-speaking world come in three shapes, and each one can be narrowed. A place: not "México" but "por qué mi familia celebra el Día de los Muertos en Chicago" (why my family celebrates Day of the Dead in Chicago). A practice: not "la música" but "cómo la salsa llegó a Nueva York" (how salsa came to New York), if you have a source you trust. A question: "¿Debería ser obligatorio el español en las escuelas de Illinois?" (Should Spanish be required in Illinois schools?). The question shape is the easiest to defend, because it forces a thesis.

Two warnings. First, a topic should not claim more than you can prove. "El español es la lengua más bonita del mundo" (Spanish is the most beautiful language in the world) cannot be supported with evidence; "el español me conecta con mi abuela" can. Second, avoid topics where your only knowledge is a half-remembered fact. If you are not sure when or how something happened, either look it up in a source you can name or choose a topic closer to home.

Heritage speakers sometimes think a personal topic is too easy. It is the opposite. A talk about your own family requires you to turn feelings into claims and memories into evidence, which is the hardest kind of thinking. Lucía’s teacher told her that the best capstone talks she had heard in twenty years were about a grandmother’s kitchen, a neighborhood corner, or a word that only one family used. Writing the Thesis

Main ideaA thesis is one sentence that takes a position, opened with a phrase like En mi opinión or Sostengo que, and everything else in the talk exists to support it.

Lucía’s index card holds one sentence: "Sostengo que el español de mi familia es una herencia que vale la pena proteger." That sentence is her (thesis): the claim the whole talk defends. It has three parts. An opener that marks it as a position: (I hold that, I maintain that). A subject: el español de mi familia. A claim about that subject that someone could disagree with: es una herencia que vale la pena proteger. If nobody could disagree, it is a fact, not a thesis.

Spanish gives you several openers, and they differ in strength. (in my opinion) is the gentlest. Creo que (I believe that) and pienso que (I think that) are the everyday choices. and afirmo que (I assert that) are the formal, confident ones you want in a capstone. All of them take the indicative after que, because the speaker is standing behind the claim: "Sostengo que el español es una herencia." Do not let the formality trick you into the subjunctive; "sostengo que sea" is wrong.

The negative versions flip the mood, and that is worth one careful sentence. "No creo que el inglés sea suficiente" (I do not think English is enough) takes the subjunctive sea, because no creo que expresses doubt. Lucía uses that shape once, in her second reason, and it is the sentence a classmate later challenges. Knowing why sea is there lets her answer calmly.

Test a thesis by asking what evidence would prove it and what a skeptic would say. Lucía’s skeptic says, "English gets you into college, not Spanish." Her thesis does not claim Spanish matters more than English; it claims Spanish is worth protecting. The skeptic’s point does not touch it. A thesis that is narrow and clear defends itself. Write yours on a card, say it aloud ten times, and it will be there when your notes fail you.

Main ideaTransitions such as en primer lugar, en segundo lugar and por último tell the audience where they are in the talk, so they can follow without a page in front of them.

A reader can look back at a page; a listener cannot. That is why a spoken talk needs transiciones (transitions): short phrases that announce where the speaker is going. Lucía’s first draft jumped from her grandmother’s story to the number of Spanish speakers with no warning, and her practice audience got lost. Her second draft added five phrases, and the same audience could repeat her three reasons back to her afterward.

The workhorse set is ordinal. (in the first place, first) opens reason one. En segundo lugar (second) opens reason two. (finally, lastly) opens the third and signals that the end is near. Spanish speakers also use primero, segundo and finalmente in the same slots, and para empezar (to begin with) as a warmer version of en primer lugar. Pick one set and keep it; mixing them is not wrong, but it makes the structure harder to hear.

Between the thesis and the first reason, and between each reason and its evidence, other phrases do the pointing. "Para apoyar esta idea" (to support this idea) introduces evidence. "Por ejemplo" (for example) introduces an example. "Esto demuestra que" (this shows that) returns from the evidence to the thesis. "Pasemos ahora a" (let us move now to) is a clean bridge between reasons, and it uses the nosotros command form, which invites the audience along instead of ordering them.

Write each transition at the top of its index card, in larger letters than the rest. When Lucía lost her place in the real talk, she looked down, saw "POR ÚLTIMO" and knew exactly where she was. Transitions do the same thing for the audience that connectors did for the reader in the last chapter, and the test is the same: cover the phrase, and ask whether it promises the relationship that really follows.

40.5

The Conclusion

Main ideaA conclusion restates the thesis in new words, gathers the three reasons in one breath, and ends with a line the audience will carry out of the room.

The last thirty seconds of a talk are the ones the audience remembers, and they are the seconds most speakers throw away with "y eso es todo" (and that is all). A has three moves. First, restate the thesis in different words, so the audience hears it fresh: Lucía opened with "una herencia que vale la pena proteger" and closes with "un tesoro que no pienso perder" (a treasure I do not intend to lose). Second, gather the reasons in one sentence. Third, end on a line that lands.

The gathering sentence is where the connectors from the last chapter earn their place. "Por lo tanto, por las historias de mi abuela, por los millones de personas que hablan esta lengua y por las puertas que abre en mi barrio, sostengo que…" (Therefore, for my grandmother’s stories, for the millions of people who speak this language, and for the doors it opens in my neighborhood, I hold that…). One sentence, three reasons, one por lo tanto that has been earned by everything before it.

The final line can be a question, a callback or a short quotation. Lucía chose a callback: she returned to the coquí rhyme and said its first line in Spanish, then stopped. Silence for two seconds, then "Gracias." A question works too: "¿Qué historia de su familia se perdería si se perdiera la lengua?" (What story of your family would be lost if the language were lost?) Notice the if-sentence pattern, imperfect subjunctive in the si clause and conditional in the result, carrying real weight in a real talk.

Two things to avoid. Do not introduce a new reason in the conclusion; the audience cannot weigh it, and it makes the three reasons feel unfinished. Do not apologize: "perdón por mi español" (sorry for my Spanish) tells the audience to doubt everything they just heard. Say your last line, say gracias, and stand still until the questions start.

40.6

Notes, Not a Script

Main ideaIndex cards with a few key words per part let you speak to your audience; a full script makes you read to a page.

"No lo leas. Habla." The teacher’s note on Lucía’s first draft is the whole lesson. When a speaker reads, the eyes drop, the voice flattens, and the audience hears a page instead of a person. When a speaker talks from (notes), the sentences come out a little rougher and a lot more alive. The grammar may be slightly less polished than in the essay; the rubric knows that, and it rewards communication over perfection.

The tool is the index card, la ficha. One card per part: thesis, reason one, reason two, reason three, conclusion. On each card, the transition in capitals at the top, then no more than five key words or short phrases, then any number or quotation you must get exactly right. Lucía’s card three reads: "EN SEGUNDO LUGAR — veinte países — según el Instituto Cervantes — una puerta, no una cerca." That last phrase, a door not a fence, is the one line she wanted word for word.

Rehearse from the cards, not from the essay, and rehearse out loud at least three times. The first run will be awful; that is what it is for. By the third run, the sentences that connect the key words will have settled into shapes you can reach for. Time each run. If a five-minute talk comes in at seven, cut a reason’s evidence, not the thesis or the conclusion. If it comes in at three, you are rushing; add a pause after each transition.

Heritage speakers often speak Spanish more comfortably than they write it, and the cards let that strength show. Andrés from the last chapter, who needed a six-pass routine to edit his essay, gave his talk with one stumble. Speaking is a different skill from writing, and for many students it is the stronger one. Trust it, keep the cards in your hand, and look at the people you are talking to.

Delivering and Defending

40.7

Voice, Pace and Pauses

Main ideaSpeak more slowly than feels natural, pause after each transition, and replace English fillers with Spanish ones or with silence.

In Lucía’s first rehearsal she finished in three minutes and forty seconds, and her practice audience caught about half of it. Nerves speed everyone up. The fix is not to speak slowly all the way through, which sounds unnatural, but to plant pauses in fixed places: after the thesis, after every transition, and before the last line. Two seconds of silence feels like ten to the speaker and like nothing to the audience. Mark each pause on the card with a slash.

Fillers are the sounds a speaker makes while thinking. English fillers, um and like, are the first thing a listener notices in a Spanish talk, because they announce that the speaker is translating. Spanish has its own: este (roughly um), pues (well), (I mean), bueno (well, okay). A few of these sound natural; a dozen sound unprepared. The better choice is silence. Close your mouth, find the next key word on the card, and go on.

Volume and eyes matter more than most students expect. Speak to the person in the back row, not to the card. Look up at the end of every sentence; that is when the audience decides whether you mean it. Heritage speakers sometimes drop into a fast, quiet register they use at home, which is warm across a kitchen table and lost across a classroom. Lucía’s teacher asked her to imagine she was telling the coquí story to her grandmother, who sits in the last row and does not hear well.

Pronunciation is a smaller worry than students think, and the rubric weighs it lightly, but two things help. Say every vowel fully; Spanish vowels do not fade to uh the way unstressed English vowels do. And put the stress where the accent mark tells you: "está," "también," "herencia" on the e, "opinión" on the last syllable. A misplaced stress is the pronunciation error most likely to make a Spanish speaker pause.

40.8

Answering Questions

Main ideaA good answer repeats the question, replies in two or three sentences, and links back to the thesis; not knowing is allowed if you say so honestly.

The five minutes end and the hard part starts. Three classmates and the teacher will each ask Lucía a question in Spanish, and she has to answer without a card. Her teacher gives her a three-step shape. Step one: repeat or rephrase the question. "Me preguntas si el inglés importa más para la universidad" (You are asking me whether English matters more for college). Repeating buys thinking time, confirms you understood, and lets the rest of the audience hear the question.

Step two: answer in two or three sentences, not ten. A long answer wanders away from the thesis and invites a follow-up you did not plan for. Step three: link back. "Y por eso sostengo que las dos lenguas abren puertas diferentes" (And that is why I hold that the two languages open different doors). The por eso is a small connector doing large work: it tells the audience the answer belongs to the talk.

Some questions will ask for something you do not know. The honest answer is the strong one. "No tengo ese dato, pero puedo buscarlo" (I do not have that figure, but I can look it up). "No estoy segura, pero creo que…" (I am not sure, but I think that…). Notice segura agrees with Lucía; a male speaker says seguro. Guessing a number aloud is worse than admitting a gap, because a classmate may know the real one.

Questions in Spanish use forms you know well: ¿por qué? (why), ¿cómo? (how), ¿qué pasaría si…? (what would happen if…), ¿no crees que…? (do you not think that…). That last one is a trap for the mood: "¿No crees que el inglés sea más importante?" invites the subjunctive in the question, but your answer stands behind a claim, so it takes the indicative: "Creo que las dos son importantes." Rehearse the questions you fear most with a partner, and write your two-sentence answers on a sixth card you never look at during the talk.

40.9

Disagreeing Politely

Main ideaDefending a thesis means acknowledging the other view first, then holding your ground with a reason, using phrases like Entiendo su punto, pero…

The classmate who asked whether English matters more was not being rude; he was doing his job. A capstone talk is defended, and defending means someone pushes and you do not fall over. Lucía’s answer had the shape her teacher taught: "Entiendo tu punto, pero creo que las dos lenguas abren puertas diferentes." Acknowledge, then disagree, then give the reason. Skipping the first step sounds combative; skipping the third sounds stubborn.

The acknowledgment phrases are worth memorizing, and they come in two registers. To a classmate, tú: entiendo tu punto (I understand your point), tienes razón en parte (you are partly right). To a teacher or an outside judge, usted: , tiene razón en parte, respeto su opinión (I respect your opinion). The pivot word is pero or, more formally, sin embargo. The reason follows with a connector from the last chapter or a plain porque.

Two phrases handle stronger pushback. "No estoy de acuerdo, porque…" (I do not agree, because…) is direct and fine as long as the porque comes with it. "Es cierto que…, sin embargo…" (It is true that…, however…) concedes a fact and then turns. Lucía used the second when the teacher asked whether her cousin’s job was really about Spanish: "Es cierto que también tenía experiencia; sin embargo, la clínica buscaba a alguien bilingüe." (It is true that he also had experience; however, the clinic was looking for someone bilingual.) Note the personal a before alguien.

What defending is not: raising your voice, repeating the thesis louder, or agreeing with everything to make the questions stop. "Sí, tienes razón" to every challenge means you did not believe your own thesis. The audience respects a speaker who can say "tienes razón en parte" and then explain which part. Politeness and firmness are not opposites in Spanish; the phrases in this lesson hold both in one breath.

The Rubric

40.10

Reading the Rubric

Main ideaA rubric names what the grader is listening for, so reading it before you build the talk tells you where to spend your time.

Lucía’s teacher handed out the (rubric) the same day she assigned the talk, and the class read it before choosing topics. A rubric is a table: rows for what is being judged, columns for how well. This one has five rows, each worth four points, for twenty in all. Organization: thesis, three reasons, conclusion, transitions. Content: evidence that fits and a source for any number. Language: verb forms and agreement that are mostly right, and connectors used correctly. Delivery: notes not script, pace, eye contact. Defense: answers that acknowledge, reply and link back.

Reading the rubric tells you where the points are. Language is one row out of five; a talk with perfect grammar and no evidence scores lower than a talk with three small errors and a clear thesis. That is a relief for students who freeze over the subjunctive, and a warning for students who think fluent Spanish is enough. The rubric rewards the shape of the talk as much as the words in it.

The column descriptions matter as much as the rows. A four in Organization reads, in the teacher’s Spanish, "La tesis es clara, los tres argumentos se distinguen y las transiciones guían al público" (The thesis is clear, the three reasons are distinct, and the transitions guide the audience). A two reads "Hay una tesis, pero los argumentos se mezclan" (There is a thesis, but the reasons blur together). Read your own rehearsal against those sentences and you will know your score before the teacher does.

Use the rubric while you build, not after. Lucía taped it above her desk and checked a row each night: Monday organization, Tuesday content, and so on. By Friday every row had a check mark. The rubric is not a trap set by the teacher; it is the teacher telling you, in writing, exactly how to succeed.

Main ideaScoring a classmate's rehearsal against the rubric, with one comment per row in Spanish, sharpens your ear for your own talk.

The week before the capstone, the class runs rehearsals in pairs. Each student scores the other on the rubric and writes one comment per row in Spanish. The comments follow the register lessons from the last unit: to a classmate, tú forms and direct suggestions. "Tu tesis es clara. Te recomiendo que hagas una pausa después de cada transición." (Your thesis is clear. I recommend that you pause after each transition.) Note hagas, the subjunctive after te recomiendo que, built from hago.

Good comments are specific and point to a fix. "Bien" (good) helps nobody. "Tu segundo argumento no tiene evidencia; añade un ejemplo o un número con fuente" (Your second reason has no evidence; add an example or a number with a source) helps immediately. The command añade is the affirmative tú form of añadir. For a suggestion softer than a command, use the subjunctive: "Sugiero que añadas un ejemplo."

Scoring someone else trains the ear. When Lucía scored Andrés, she noticed he said "en primer lugar" and then never said "en segundo lugar," so his second reason arrived unannounced. That night she checked her own cards and found the same gap. When Andrés scored Lucía, he counted seven este fillers in five minutes; she had not heard a single one. The rubric row for Delivery went from a two to a four in the next rehearsal.

The last step is to receive comments the way you want your thesis to be received: acknowledge, weigh, decide. "Entiendo tu punto sobre las pausas, y tienes razón." Some comments you will accept, some you will explain your reasons for rejecting, and both are fine. On the day of the capstone, Lucía’s five cards, three reasons, one thesis and one coquí rhyme add up to a talk her grandmother, in the last row, hears every word of.

11. Which phrase would open the last supporting reason of a talk?

Why: Por último means finally and announces the last reason.

12. What is the polite first step when a classmate challenges your thesis?

Why: Defending politely means acknowledging first, then disagreeing with a reason.

13. Te recomiendo que ___ (mirar) al público al final de cada oración.

Why: Te recomiendo que is a recommendation trigger, so mirar takes the present subjunctive mires.

14. Which reason is supported by evidence the way this unit teaches?

Why: A rounded figure with a named source is honest evidence; guesses and slogans are not.

15. What does "No lo leas. Habla." tell a speaker to do?

Why: No leas is the negative tú command of leer, and habla is the affirmative command of hablar: do not read, speak.
