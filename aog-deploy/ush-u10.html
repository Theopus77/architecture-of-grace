America in a Changing World

1969–Present

In 1969, Americans watched two astronauts walk on the Moon. The country was still locked in a Cold War with the Soviet Union. Most homes had one telephone, attached to the wall. Today, people carry more computing power in their pockets than NASA used to reach the Moon. The Soviet Union is gone. The nation looks different too, with more people whose families came from Latin America, Asia and Africa.

This unit covers the years from 1969 to the present. You will read about a scandal that led a president to resign, and about gas lines and rising prices. You will see the Cold War end and a new era of computers and the internet begin. You will also learn about the September 11 attacks, two long wars, a deep recession, a pandemic and several very close elections.

Because this history is recent, people who lived through it often still disagree about what it means. That is normal in a democracy. As you read, look for the facts, notice what supporters and critics argued, and form your own view based on evidence.

The big question

How has the United States changed since 1969, and how have Americans worked through their disagreements about its future?

Big questionHow did Americans' trust in government, and their ideas about its job, change between 1969 and 1990?
The story

The Night the Wall Opened

On November 9, 1989, a mistake at a press conference opened a wall that had split a city for 28 years.

For 28 years, a concrete wall cut the city of Berlin in two. East Germany's Communist government built it in 1961. Its goal was to stop its own people from escaping to West Berlin, which was free and tied to the West. The wall had guard towers, bright lights and armed soldiers. Families were split apart. Many people who tried to cross were caught, and some were killed.

By 1989, change was sweeping across Eastern Europe. The Soviet Union's leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, had said he would not send tanks to keep Communist governments in power. In East Germany, huge crowds marched in the streets. Every week the marches grew. Thousands of East Germans fled to the West through other countries, such as Hungary. The government knew it had to do something.

On the evening of November 9, an East German official named Günter Schabowski held a press conference on live TV. He read a note about new travel rules. A reporter asked when the rules started. Schabowski looked at his papers. He seemed unsure. Then he said that, as far as he knew, they started immediately. In fact, the rules were supposed to begin the next day, with careful controls.

People all over East Berlin heard the news. Thousands rushed to the checkpoints in the wall. At the Bornholmer Street crossing, the crowd grew larger and louder. The guards had no orders. They were badly outnumbered. Late that night, the officer in charge decided to open the gate. People streamed through, laughing and crying. Strangers from West Berlin hugged them and handed out flowers.

Over the next days, people climbed on top of the wall and danced. Some chipped off pieces with hammers to keep. Less than a year later, in October 1990, East and West Germany became one country again. For Americans, who had spent more than 40 years in the Cold War, the open wall was a sign that a long, dangerous era was ending.

The fall of the wall came at the end of two busy decades in the United States. Americans walked on the Moon, argued over a scandal that brought down a president, waited in gas lines and watched prices climb. They also debated what government should and should not do. This chapter tells that story.

The Nixon Years

28.1

Footprints on the Moon

Main ideaIn July 1969, Apollo 11 astronauts became the first people to walk on the Moon, a high point of the space race.

Richard Nixon became president in January 1969. He was a Republican from California who had been Dwight Eisenhower’s vice president. Nixon took over a country divided by the Vietnam War and upset by protests and violence. Just six months later, the nation shared a moment of pride.

On July 20, 1969, the module of Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. Astronaut Neil Armstrong climbed down a ladder and stepped onto the dusty ground. Buzz Aldrin soon joined him. Michael Collins circled above in the command ship. About 600 million people around the world watched on television.

The Moon landing was a win in the space race with the Soviet Union. President John F. Kennedy had set the goal in 1961. Thousands of engineers, scientists and workers made it happen. Some were Black women mathematicians, such as Katherine Johnson, who did key calculations at NASA. Over the next few years, 12 American astronauts walked on the Moon. The last returned in 1972.

28.2

Talking With Rivals

Main ideaNixon eased Cold War tensions through détente, a surprise visit to China and arms talks, and he ended the military draft.

Nixon was known as a strong anti-Communist. Yet he surprised the world. With his adviser Henry Kissinger, he followed a plan called . Détente means an easing of tension between rivals. Nixon believed talking with Communist powers could make the world safer.

In February 1972, Nixon visited the People’s Republic of China. No U.S. president had gone there since the Communists took power in 1949. He met the Chinese leader Mao Zedong and toured the Great Wall. Soon after, Nixon went to Moscow. He and Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev signed an arms treaty called SALT I. It limited some nuclear weapons.

Nixon also slowly pulled U.S. troops out of Vietnam. The last American combat troops left in 1973. That same year, the ended. Before, young men could be ordered into the military. Now the country moved to an all-volunteer army. The draft had been a big cause of anger during the Vietnam War.

28.3

Protecting the Environment

Main ideaIn the early 1970s, public concern about pollution led to Earth Day, the EPA and major laws to clean the air and water.

By the late 1960s, pollution was easy to see. Smog hung over cities. Factories dumped waste into rivers and lakes. In 1969, the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland, Ohio, caught fire because it was so full of oil and trash. Many Americans decided something had to change.

On April 22, 1970, about 20 million Americans joined the first . Senator Gaylord Nelson of Wisconsin helped start it. Students held teach-ins. People cleaned parks and marched in streets. The event showed that protecting the had wide support from both political parties.

Congress and President Nixon acted. In 1970, the government created the Environmental Protection Agency, or EPA. The Clean Air Act of 1970 set limits on air pollution. The Clean Water Act of 1972 worked to clean up rivers and lakes. The Endangered Species Act of 1973 protected animals and plants at risk of dying out, such as the bald eagle. Over time, the air and water in many places became much cleaner.

A Crisis of Trust

28.4

The Watergate Scandal

Main ideaWatergate began as a break-in and grew into a cover-up that led Richard Nixon to resign in 1974, the only president ever to do so.

In June 1972, police arrested five men inside the Democratic Party’s offices at the Watergate building in Washington, D.C. The burglars had ties to Nixon’s reelection campaign. Nixon won the 1972 election in a landslide. But reporters kept digging. Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein of the Washington Post wrote story after story about the break-in.

The real trouble was the . People in the White House tried to hide what had happened and paid money to keep the burglars quiet. A Senate committee held hearings on TV. Then Americans learned that Nixon secretly taped talks in his office. Nixon refused to hand over the tapes. In July 1974, the Supreme Court ruled that he had to.

The tapes showed that Nixon had helped plan the cover-up soon after the break-in. A House committee had already voted to him. Nixon knew he would likely be removed from office. On August 9, 1974, he resigned. Watergate showed that no one, not even a president, is above the law. It also left many Americans less trusting of their leaders.

Ford and Hard Times

Main ideaGerald Ford took over a shaken nation facing an energy crisis, rising prices and slow growth.

Gerald Ford had been a congressman from Michigan. He became vice president in 1973 and president in 1974. He was the only president never elected as president or vice president. Ford promised honesty. But a month later he Nixon, so Nixon could not be tried for any Watergate crimes. Ford said the nation needed to heal. Many Americans were angry.

Ford also faced money troubles. In 1973, Arab oil-producing nations stopped selling oil to the United States. They were angry about U.S. support for Israel in a war. Gas prices jumped. Drivers waited in long lines at gas stations. This showed how much the country depended on oil from other places.

Prices for many goods kept rising. This is called . At the same time, many people lost jobs. In the Midwest, some factories and steel mills began to close or cut workers. Americans started to call the region the Rust Belt. For many families, the 1970s were a time of worry about money.

Carter: Peace and a Hostage Crisis

Main ideaJimmy Carter helped make peace between Egypt and Israel but struggled with inflation and the Iran hostage crisis.

In 1976, Jimmy Carter, a Democrat and former governor of Georgia, won the presidency. He was a peanut farmer and a Navy veteran. He promised to be honest with the American people. Carter made a big part of his foreign policy. He spoke out against governments that jailed or hurt their own people.

Carter’s greatest success came in 1978. He invited Egypt’s president, Anwar Sadat, and Israel’s prime minister, Menachem Begin, to Camp David in Maryland. The two countries had fought several wars. After 13 days of hard talks, they agreed on a plan for peace. The led to a peace treaty between Egypt and Israel in 1979.

But troubles grew. Prices kept rising fast. In November 1979, students in Iran took over the U.S. embassy in Tehran. They held 52 Americans as . A rescue attempt failed in 1980. The hostages were held for 444 days. They were freed on January 20, 1981, the day Carter left office.

The Reagan Years

28.7

A New Direction in 1980

Main ideaRonald Reagan won the 1980 election by promising smaller government, lower taxes and a stronger military.

Ronald Reagan grew up in small towns in Illinois, including Dixon. He went to Eureka College in Illinois. He became a radio announcer and then a movie actor. Later he served two terms as governor of California. Reagan was a skilled speaker. People called him the Great Communicator.

In 1980, Reagan ran against Carter. He asked voters if they were better off than four years before. Many said no. Reagan won in a landslide. He was part of a growing movement. Conservatives wanted a smaller federal government, lower taxes and fewer rules on business. Many also stressed religion and traditional family values.

Reagan’s win changed American politics. Some voters who used to support Democrats, including many factory workers, now voted for him. People called them Reagan Democrats. Reagan was nearly killed by a gunman in March 1981 but recovered. That same year, he named Sandra Day O’Connor as the first woman on the Supreme Court.

Tax Cuts and Debates

Main ideaReagan cut taxes and business rules and built up the military, and Americans still debate the results.

In 1981, Congress passed large that Reagan asked for. Reagan believed lower taxes would let people and businesses keep more money. He said they would spend and invest it, and the economy would grow. Supporters called this supply-side economics. Reagan also pushed , or cutting government rules on business. Carter had already begun this with airlines and trucking.

At first, the economy got worse. In 1982, many people were out of work. The Federal Reserve had raised interest rates very high to stop inflation. It worked. Inflation fell sharply. By 1983, the economy was growing again, and it kept growing for years.

Reagan also spent much more on the military. With lower taxes and higher spending, the government borrowed a lot. The national nearly tripled while Reagan was president. Supporters said his policies beat inflation, created jobs and helped win the Cold War. Critics said the tax cuts helped the rich most, cut programs for poor people, and left huge debts. Americans still argue about this.

A Changing Population

Main ideaNew immigration, movement to the Sun Belt and new leaders changed who Americans were and where they lived.

A 1965 law ended old limits that favored immigrants from Europe. After that, more immigrants came from Latin America and Asia. After the Vietnam War ended in 1975, many refugees came from Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. The Refugee Act of 1980 set up a system for welcoming people fleeing danger. In 1986, a new law gave legal status to about 2.7 million immigrants who had come without permission.

Americans were also on the move. Many left the Northeast and Midwest for the , the warm states of the South and Southwest. Jobs, cheaper housing and air conditioning drew them. States like Texas, Florida and Arizona grew fast. They gained seats in Congress.

New voices rose in politics. In 1983, Chicago voters elected Harold Washington as the city’s first Black mayor. In 1984, Geraldine Ferraro became the first woman chosen by a major party to run for vice president. Latino and Asian American leaders won more offices too. The nation was becoming more .

28.10

Reagan and Gorbachev

Main ideaAfter a tense arms buildup, Reagan and Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev built trust and signed a treaty to destroy some nuclear weapons.

In his first years, Reagan took a hard line against the Soviet Union. He built up the military and called the Soviet Union an evil empire. He also proposed a system to shoot down nuclear missiles from space. Critics called it Star Wars. Many people feared the arms race could lead to nuclear war.

In 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev became the Soviet leader. He was younger than earlier leaders and wanted change. The Soviet economy was failing. Gorbachev allowed more open talk, called . He also tried to reform the economy, called .

Reagan and Gorbachev met several times. At first they could not agree. But they came to trust each other. In 1987, they signed the INF Treaty. For the first time, the two sides agreed to destroy a whole type of nuclear missile. Reagan said the U.S. would trust but verify, which meant checking that the Soviets kept their word.

28.11

Walls Come Down

Main ideaIn 1989, Communist governments in Eastern Europe fell, and in 1991 the Soviet Union itself broke apart, ending the Cold War.

In 1989, change spread across Eastern Europe. In Poland, a workers’ movement called , led by Lech Wałęsa, won free elections. Hungary opened its border to Austria. Huge peaceful protests filled the streets of East Germany and Czechoslovakia. Gorbachev did not send troops to stop them.

Inside the Soviet Union, the republics that made it up wanted to be free. In December 1991, the Soviet Union broke apart into 15 independent countries, including Russia and Ukraine. Gorbachev resigned. The Cold War, which had lasted more than 40 years, was over. The United States was now the world’s only .

In January 1991, the coalition attacked. The Persian Gulf War ended in about six weeks, and Kuwait was freed. Saddam Hussein, however, stayed in power in Iraq.

Bridge to the 21st Century

1990–Present
Big questionHow have technology, conflict and a changing population shaped the United States since 1990?
The story

New Americans Take the Oath

Every year, hundreds of thousands of people raise their right hands and become U.S. citizens.

Picture a courtroom in downtown Chicago on a weekday morning. Rows of chairs are full. Families squeeze in along the walls, holding small American flags. The people in the front rows were born in dozens of countries. Some came from Mexico, India or the Philippines. Others came from Poland, Nigeria or Iraq. Today, they will become citizens of the United States.

Some of them first arrived as refugees. A refugee is a person forced to flee home because of war or danger, and who cannot safely go back. Before coming, refugees go through long interviews and security checks. The wait can take years. When they land, local groups often help them find an apartment, enroll children in school and look for work. In the Chicago area, several such groups have helped families from places like Syria, Afghanistan, Congo and Burma.

Becoming a citizen is a long road. Most people must first be lawful permanent residents, often called green card holders, usually for five years. Then they fill out an application, get fingerprinted and have an interview. They must show they can read, write and speak basic English. They also take a civics test with questions like: What is the supreme law of the land? Who was the first president?

Now, at the ceremony, a judge or official asks everyone to stand. Together, they read the Oath of Allegiance. They promise to support and defend the Constitution. When they finish, they are Americans. People cheer. Children wave flags. Some new citizens cry. Many stop at a table on the way out to register to vote.

Scenes like this take place across the country all year long. They are one small part of a bigger story. Since 1990, the United States has changed fast. New technology connected the world. The nation faced terrible attacks, wars, storms, a deep recession and a pandemic. Through it all, people kept arriving, and the country kept debating what it means to be American.

The 1990s: Peace and Growth

29.1

After the Cold War

Main ideaWith the Cold War over, Americans turned their attention to problems at home and elected Bill Clinton in 1992.

George H.W. Bush was president when the Cold War ended. His popularity soared after the Persian Gulf War in 1991. But then the economy slowed down. Many people lost jobs. Bush had promised not to raise taxes, but he agreed to a budget deal that did. Some voters felt he had broken his word.

In 1992, Bill Clinton, the Democratic governor of Arkansas, ran against Bush. A businessman named Ross Perot also ran as an candidate. Perot won almost one-fifth of the popular vote, a strong result for a third candidate. Clinton won the election. Clinton called himself a New Democrat, a who wanted to mix ideas from both parties.

With the Soviet Union gone, the United States faced a new kind of world. There was no single rival superpower. But conflicts still broke out. Main ideaThe 2000 presidential election was so close in Florida that the Supreme Court's decision in Bush v. Gore settled the outcome.

In 2000, Vice President Al Gore, a Democrat, ran against Texas governor George W. Bush, a Republican. Bush was the son of the former president. On election night, the result came down to Florida. Whoever won Florida’s would win the presidency. The count there was extremely close.

Florida began a . Some ballots were hard to read, and the rules for counting them were unclear. Lawyers for both sides went to court. For weeks, the country did not know who its next president would be. The Florida Supreme Court ordered more hand counts.

On December 12, 2000, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 in . The decision stopped the recount. Bush was declared the winner of Florida by 537 votes. That gave him the presidency, even though Gore won more votes nationwide. Supporters of the ruling said it ended a chaotic process that treated ballots unequally. Critics said every vote should have been counted. Gore accepted the result.

Hurricane Katrina

Main ideaIn 2005, Hurricane Katrina flooded New Orleans, and the slow response raised hard questions about government and fairness.

In late August 2005, Hurricane Katrina slammed into the Gulf Coast of Louisiana and Mississippi. In New Orleans, the storm caused to fail. Levees are walls built to hold back water. About 80 percent of the city flooded. Some neighborhoods were under many feet of water.

Many people had left before the storm. But thousands could not, often because they had no car or money to travel. Many of them were poor, elderly or Black. People waited on rooftops for rescue. Help from local, state and federal governments was slow and poorly organized. The head of the Federal Emergency Management Agency, or , resigned. Many Americans asked why the richest nation in the world could not do better. Volunteers came from across the country. Many New Orleans residents moved away for good, and rebuilding took years.
