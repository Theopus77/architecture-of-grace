Moses and the Torah

Shemot Stories

The second book of the Torah is called Shemot. That means "Names." Its English name is Exodus. It tells how Jacob's family became a people. It tells how they were slaves in Egypt. And it tells how they walked out free.

Jewish families tell this story every spring at Passover. Seven weeks later, at Shavuot, they remember a mountain. The Torah says the people heard ten sayings there. In this unit we learn what the Torah says. We learn what Jewish families do to remember. We do not decide if the story is true. We listen and we learn.

By the end, you will be able to tell the story of baby Moses and the burning bush. You will know why matzah is flat. You will know what the ten sayings are. And you will know what a mitzvah is.

The big question

What does the Torah say about leaving Egypt and the mountain, and how do Jewish families remember it every year?

The story

The Youngest Child Asks

It is the first night of Passover, and the smallest person at the table has the biggest job.

It is spring in Chicago. Sam's family sits at a long table. There is flat bread on a plate. It is called matzah. There is a cup of wine. There is a plate with a green herb and a bitter herb. Sam is the youngest child at the table.

Sam's grandma nods at him. It is time. Sam stands up. He asks, "Why is this night different from all other nights?" Everyone smiles. This is the question the youngest child asks every year.

Sam's uncle opens a small book. It is called the Haggadah. That means "the telling." He says, "Tonight we tell a story from the Torah. Long ago, our people were slaves in Egypt. Then they walked out free."

Sam listens. He hears about a baby in a basket. He hears about a bush on fire. He hears about a king who said no. He hears about a sea that opened. Sam looks at the flat bread. "Why is it flat?" he asks. "Wait," says Grandma. "That is part of the story."

Talk about itWhy do you think the youngest child gets to ask the big question at the Passover table?

Main ideaThe Torah says Jacob's family grew into a people, and a Pharaoh made them slaves.

The second book of the Torah is called Shemot. That means "Names." Its English name is . It starts with a list of names. They are the sons of Jacob who came to Egypt.

The Torah says the family grew and grew. It became a people. Then a new king ruled Egypt. A king of Egypt is called a . The story says this Pharaoh was afraid of the people.

Main ideaThe Torah says God spoke to Moses from a burning bush and sent him back to Egypt.

Moses grew up. He left Egypt and became a shepherd. One day he saw a strange thing. A was on fire. But the fire did not burn it up.

The Torah says God spoke to Moses from the bush. God told Moses to go back to Egypt. God said to tell Pharaoh to let the people go.

Moses was scared. He said, "Who am I?" The Torah says God told him, "I will be with you." Moses’s brother Aaron went with him to help him speak.

5.4

Pharaoh Says No

Main ideaThe Torah says Moses told Pharaoh to let the people go, and ten plagues came when he said no.

Moses and Aaron went to Pharaoh. They said, "Let my people go." Pharaoh said no. He made the people work even harder.

The Torah says ten came to Egypt. A plague is a big trouble. The river turned red. Frogs hopped everywhere. Bugs came. Animals got sick. Hail fell. Locusts ate the plants. The sky went dark.

After each plague, Moses asked again. Pharaoh still said no. The last plague was the saddest of all. Then, the Torah says, Pharaoh finally said, "Go."

Section 3

The Night They Left

5.5

Bread With No Time to Rise

Main ideaThe Torah says the people left Egypt so fast their bread had no time to rise; Jewish families eat matzah to remember.

The Torah says the people left Egypt at night. They had to hurry. Their bread dough had no time to rise. So it baked flat. Flat bread like this is called .

That night is called . In Hebrew it is Pesach. The Torah says the people put a mark on their doors. The last plague passed over their homes.

Every spring, Jewish families eat matzah for a week. They do this to remember. Flat bread means "we left in a hurry."

Think it through
Two Kinds of Bread

Look at two plates. On one plate is a tall, soft loaf of bread. It took hours to rise. On the other plate is matzah. It is flat and crisp, with little holes. It baked in a few minutes.

Which bread would you take if you had to leave right now? Why does the Passover table have that one?

5.7

Telling It Every Year

Main ideaJewish families retell the Exodus story every year at a Passover meal called the Seder.

Every spring, Jewish families sit down for a special meal. It is called the . Seder means "order." The meal goes in a set order, step by step.

At the Seder, the youngest child asks four questions. The first is, "Why is this night different?" Then the family tells the story. A baby, a bush, a king, a sea.

There is matzah. There is a bitter herb for the hard years. There is a sweet mix for the bricks. Many Jewish families in Chicago hold a Seder every year.

Mount Sinai and the Ten Sayings

Sinai
Big questionWhat does the Torah say the people heard at the mountain, and how do Jewish families keep those words today?
The story

One Rule for Our Room

A class writes its own rules, and then hears about a mountain where the Torah says ten sayings were given.

Ms. Ortiz's class sits on the rug. She holds up a big sheet of paper. It is blank. "Today we make rules for our room," she says. "Each of you will write one rule. It should make our room a better place."

Leo writes, "Take turns." Ana writes, "Tell the truth." Jamal writes, "Do not take what is not yours." Priya writes, "Be kind to helpers." Ms. Ortiz tapes each rule to the paper. Soon the paper is full.

"Rules are not just for our room," says Ms. Ortiz. "Long ago, the Torah says, a whole people stood at a mountain. They heard ten sayings. Some of them sound like yours." She reads one: "Honor your father and your mother."

Ana raises her hand. "Do people still keep those rules?" Ms. Ortiz nods. "Jewish families do. They even have a holiday to remember that day. It is called Shavuot." Leo looks at the class rules. "Ours should have a holiday too," he says.

Talk about itWhich rule would you write for your room? Why would it make the room a better place?

6.3

One God and a Day of Rest

Main ideaThe first sayings are about one God and about resting on Shabbat.

Many people call them the Ten Commandments. Jewish tradition calls them the Ten . The first saying starts, "I am the LORD your God." It says God brought the people out of Egypt.

The next sayings say: there is one God. Do not make statues to bow to. Do not use God’s name in a bad way.

Then comes . Remember the seventh day. Rest on that day. Do not work. Jewish families keep this day every week with candles and bread.

6.4

Family, Truth and What Is Yours

Main ideaThe other sayings are about how people treat each other.

The first sayings are about God. The rest are about people. The fifth saying is to your father and your mother. To honor is to treat with care and respect.

Next: do not hurt or kill anyone. Be true to your family. Do not take what is not yours. Do not tell lies about your neighbor.

The last saying is, do not wish for what belongs to someone else. Jewish tradition teaches all ten sayings together. They are about God and about people.

Section 3

Keeping the Words

6.5

What a Mitzvah Is

Main ideaA mitzvah is a commandment in the Torah; Jewish tradition counts many of them.

The ten sayings are not the only rules in the Torah. Each rule is called a . That means a commandment. Something you are told to do, or not to do.

Some mitzvot are about God. Light candles for Shabbat. Some are about people. Help a person who is poor. Be kind to a stranger. Jewish tradition counts 613 mitzvot in the Torah.

Many Jewish people say "doing a mitzvah" when they do a good deed. Helping a friend carry books is a mitzvah. So is sharing.

6.6

Shavuot: Remembering the Mountain

Main ideaJewish families celebrate Shavuot to remember the day the Torah says the ten sayings were given.

Seven weeks after Passover comes a holiday. It is called . That means "weeks." Jewish tradition connects this day to Mount Sinai.

On Shavuot, many Jewish families go to the synagogue. They hear the ten sayings read aloud. Many stand up to listen.

Many families eat foods made with milk and cheese. Some decorate with flowers and green plants. Some stay up late to study the Torah. It is a happy day.

The Torah Scroll in the Synagogue

Main ideaIn a synagogue, the Torah is a big scroll kept in a special cabinet and read aloud.

A is where Jewish people meet to pray and learn. At the front is a special cabinet. It is called the ark. Inside the ark is the Torah .

The scroll is rolled on two wooden poles. It has a cover like a coat. When it is carried, people stand up. A reader uses a little pointer to keep the place.

The words are written in Hebrew by hand. The reader chants them out loud. Each week a new part is read. The whole scroll takes one year.

Think it through
Look at the Scroll

Look at the front of the room. The doors of the ark open. Inside stands a tall scroll in a velvet cover. A silver crown sits on top. A person lifts it out. Everyone in the room stands up. The cover comes off. The scroll is rolled open on a table.

Why do you think people stand up when the scroll is carried?
