Literary Devices and American Voices

Literature

A story from 1843 opens with a man insisting he is not mad, and every sentence he adds to prove it makes you surer that he is. That gap, between what a text says and what it means, is where literature does its most interesting work. This unit begins with the tools writers use to open that gap and the tools readers use to cross it: structure, unreliable narration, irony, ambiguity, satire, motif, symbol, archetype, allusion, and the stream of a mind on the page. You will also learn what to do when two careful readers see different things in the same lines, and how to write an interpretation that argues instead of reporting.

The second half of the unit turns those tools on three centuries of American writing, from a Puritan woman's love poem in 1650 to a Black poet's mask in 1895. Bradstreet, Edwards, Paine, Emerson, Thoreau, Hawthorne, Poe, Melville, Whitman, Dickinson, Douglass, Jacobs, Twain, Crane, Chopin, London, Wharton and Dunbar did not write in isolation. They read one another and argued back. The preacher who threatened hell is answered by the boy who chooses it for a friend. The essayist who said trust yourself is answered by a sea that does not care what you trust.

By the end you will be able to name a device and, more importantly, say what it does; to weigh two readings of a text and defend the stronger; and to hear American literature as a long conversation about what this country is and who it includes. Every writer here is public domain, which means every text is free to you, and the argument they started is one you are allowed to join.

The big question

How do writers make texts mean more than they say, and how have American writers used that power to argue about what America is?

The story

The Man Who Says He Is Not Mad

A story from 1843 opens with a narrator arguing with you before you have said a word, and the argument is the whole point.

The first sentence of Edgar Allan Poe's story The Tell-Tale Heart is a defense. The narrator admits he has been nervous, dreadfully nervous, and then turns on the reader: why will you say that I am mad? Nobody has said it yet. The story is one paragraph old, and already the man telling it is answering an accusation that only he can hear. That is the first clue, and Poe hands it to you on purpose.

The narrator goes on to prove his sanity in the strangest way possible. He explains how carefully he planned to kill an old man he lived with, a man who had never wronged him. He hated the old man's pale blue eye, which he calls a vulture eye. For seven nights he eased the door open at midnight and shone a single ray of lantern light on the sleeping face. On the eighth night the eye was open. He killed the old man, cut up the body and hid the pieces under the floorboards. Then he sat the police down in chairs right over the spot, calm and smiling.

Every detail he offers to show how clever and calm he was shows the opposite. A sane man does not count his slow, silent steps toward a stranger's bed as evidence of his good judgment. The reader is doing two things at once: hearing the story the narrator tells, and building a second story, the true one, out of what he lets slip. That gap between the teller's version and the reader's version is where much of the power of literature lives.

The ending closes the gap. Sitting with the police, the narrator begins to hear a low, dull, quick sound, like a watch wrapped in cotton. He decides it is the dead man's heart beating under the boards. The officers hear nothing. The sound grows until he cannot bear it, and he tears up the floor and confesses. The heart he hears is, of course, his own. He never understands that. You do.

This chapter is about the tools writers use to make readers see more than characters see: unreliable narrators, irony, structure, symbols, allusion, satire. It is also about what to do when two careful readers see different things in the same lines. Poe's narrator wants you to accept one reading. The story quietly insists on another. Learning to weigh the two is what literary interpretation means.

Talk about itThe narrator gives evidence of his sanity and the reader takes it as evidence of the opposite. What kind of evidence would have actually convinced you he was sane?

46.1

Structure Carries Meaning

Main ideaThe order in which a text gives you its events is a choice, and the choice changes what the events mean.

Read these two openings of the same story. First: Maria was born in Pilsen in 1998, went to school on Eighteenth Street, and learned to sew from her grandmother. Second: The dress was finished at three in the morning, and Maria sat on the floor beside it and cried, because her grandmother would never see it. Both openings are true to the same story. The first starts at the beginning and marches forward. The second starts at the end of something and leaves you wanting to know why.

The arrangement of a text’s parts is its . A writer can tell events in the order they happened, which is order, or begin in the middle of the action, a device with a Latin name, . A writer can jump backward in a , hold back a key fact until the last page, or tell the same night from three characters’ points of view. None of these choices is neutral. Starting with the finished dress makes grief the subject; starting with the birth makes a whole life the subject.

When you analyze structure, ask two questions. What do I know at each point, and what does the character know? Poe’s narrator tells his story after the crime, so he knows the ending, and his attempts to prove himself calm are shadowed by what we sense is coming. A mystery hides the answer until the end so the reader hunts. A tragedy often tells you the ending in the prologue so you watch the characters walk toward it. The gap between what you know and when you learn it is a tool the writer controls.

A useful habit: after you finish a text, sketch its skeleton. Where does it start in time? Where does it jump? Where does it slow down and give a single moment three pages, and where does it cover ten years in a sentence? The places a writer spends the most words are usually the places the writer thinks matter most. Structure is an argument about importance, made without a single sentence of explanation.

46.2

The Narrator You Cannot Trust

Main ideaAn unreliable narrator tells a story the reader must read against, using the teller's own slips as evidence.

Here is a narrator: I only borrowed my sister’s phone for a minute, and it is not my fault it fell in the pool, and anyway she is always leaving it around. You have not met this person, but you already suspect that the minute was longer, the fall was not an accident, and the sister is not the problem. A narrator whose version of events the reader learns to doubt is an . The unreliability is not a mistake by the author. It is the author’s design.

Narrators can be unreliable for different reasons. Some lie on purpose to look better. Some are children who do not understand what they are seeing. Some are mad, like Poe’s narrator, who describes his own madness in the act of denying it. Some are simply too close to the events to judge them. In every case the author plants that points past the narrator: a fact that does not fit, a reaction that is too strong, another character who sees things differently.

Reading an unreliable narrator well means keeping two ledgers. In one, write what the narrator claims. In the other, write what the details show. Poe’s narrator claims he acted with the calm of a healthy mind. The details show a man who spent seven nights creeping into a room to stare at an eye, then heard a dead heart through a floor. When the two ledgers disagree, the second one is usually the truth of the story, and the distance between them is the story’s meaning.

Be careful, though. Not every first-person narrator is unreliable, and calling every narrator a liar is a lazy reading. The test is evidence. Can you point to specific lines where the narrator’s account cracks? If you can, you have an interpretation. If you cannot, you have only a suspicion, and a suspicion is not yet an argument. Suspicion is where reading starts, not where it ends.

46.3

Setting and Order as Choices

Main ideaWhere and when a writer places a story, and in what order the reader meets its parts, are decisions that shape everything the story can mean.

Put the same argument between a mother and a son in three places: a kitchen at dinner, a hospital hallway at two in the morning, a parked car outside a courthouse. The words can stay exactly the same. The meaning cannot. is the time and place of a story, and it is never just scenery. It tells the reader what is at stake, what the characters can and cannot do, and what the world around them expects.

Setting works on more than one scale. The largest scale is the historical moment: a story set in Chicago in 1919, the summer of the race riot, carries a weight that the same story set in 2019 does not. The middle scale is the social world, such as a small town where everyone knows everyone, or a city where no one does. The smallest scale is the room. Poe locks his narrator and the old man in one dark house, and the closeness makes the story feel like a trap.

Order works with setting. A writer who shows you the empty house first and the family second has decided that loss is the frame. A writer who shows the family first lets you love them before you lose them. Ask of any text: why here, why now, and why in this order? If you can imagine the same events in another place or another sequence, and you can say how the meaning would change, you have understood the author’s choice instead of simply accepting it.

A short exercise makes this real. Take a scene you know, from a story or from your own life, and move it. Change the season. Change the city. Tell the ending first. Notice what breaks and what suddenly becomes visible. Writers do this all the time in revision, and readers who can do it in their heads read with the writer’s eyes.

46.4

Three Kinds of Irony

Main ideaIrony is a gap between what is said or expected and what is meant or true, and it comes in verbal, situational and dramatic forms.

A student walks into class soaked from the rain and says, Beautiful day. That is : the words say one thing and mean the opposite, and both speaker and listener know it. Sarcasm is verbal irony with an edge meant to wound. But irony is much bigger than sarcasm, and its other forms do the heaviest work in literature.

is a gap between what is expected and what happens. In O. Henry’s story The Gift of the Magi, from 1905, a poor young wife sells her long hair to buy a chain for her husband’s watch, while he sells the watch to buy combs for her hair. Each gift is made useless by the other. The irony is not in anyone’s words; it is in the shape of events. Readers feel it as a small shock followed by understanding.

is a gap between what a character knows and what the audience knows. When Poe’s narrator sits the police over the body and chats calmly, the reader knows what is under the floor and the officers do not. In Shakespeare’s Romeo and Juliet, the audience knows Juliet is only drugged when Romeo finds her and believes she is dead. Dramatic irony makes the reader an anxious witness who wants to shout at the page.

To find irony, look for gaps: between words and meaning, between expectation and result, between the character’s knowledge and yours. Then ask what the gap is for. Irony can be funny, cruel, or heartbreaking. O. Henry’s gap ends in tenderness, since the failed gifts prove the love. Poe’s gap ends in horror. Naming the kind of irony is the first step; explaining its effect is the interpretation.

46.5

Ambiguity on Purpose

Main ideaAmbiguity is a text's deliberate openness to more than one meaning, and it differs from vagueness because each meaning is supported by the words.

Robert Frost’s poem The Road Not Taken, published in 1916, ends with a speaker saying he took the road less traveled by, and that it has made all the difference. Millions of people have read that as a cheer for bold choices. But earlier in the same poem, the speaker admits that the two roads were worn really about the same. So did he take the harder road, or is he an old man telling himself a flattering story about a choice that was never that different? The poem supports both readings. That is .

Ambiguity is not the same as confusion. A sentence has no clear meaning because the writer was careless: The thing happened somewhere and it was bad. An ambiguous line has two or more clear meanings, each supported by the words, and the writer meant the reader to hold both. The test is evidence. If you can point to lines that support reading A and other lines that support reading B, the text is ambiguous. If you cannot support either, it is vague.

Writers use ambiguity because life is ambiguous. Frost’s speaker will tell the story with a sigh, ages and ages hence, and the poem does not say whether the sigh is satisfaction or regret. A reader who wants a single answer will be frustrated. A reader who can say, this poem is about how we turn our choices into stories afterward, has found the meaning that includes both readings. Often the best interpretation is the one that explains why the ambiguity is there.

When you meet an ambiguous passage, do not pick a side too fast. Write both readings down, in plain sentences, so you can see them side by side. Find the evidence for each. Then ask what the text gains from leaving the question open. That question is usually the door into the text’s deepest subject.

46.6

Satire and Its Targets

Main ideaSatire uses exaggeration, irony and mock seriousness to expose something the writer wants the reader to condemn.

In 1729 the Irish writer Jonathan Swift published a short pamphlet calmly proposing that the poor of Ireland could ease their poverty by selling their one-year-old children as food for the rich. He included recipes and cost estimates. He was not a monster. He was furious at English landlords and policies that were, in his view, already devouring the Irish poor, and he wanted readers to feel that fury through their own disgust. The pamphlet, A Modest Proposal, is the most famous piece of in the language.

Satire is writing that attacks a target, usually a human folly, a vice or an institution, by making it look ridiculous. Its main tools are , which blows a flaw up until it cannot be missed, and irony, especially the mock-serious voice that says appalling things in a reasonable tone. Swift’s narrator never winks. The horror comes from the reader’s realization that the calm math of the proposal matches the calm math of the real policies.

Mark Twain’s Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, published in the United States in 1885, is satire with a gentler surface. Huck, a boy raised in a slaveholding society, believes helping his friend Jim escape is a sin. When he decides to help anyway, he says he will go to hell for it. Twain’s target is not Huck; it is the society that taught a good-hearted boy that kindness was wickedness. The reader is meant to see what Huck cannot.

To read satire, ask three questions. What is the target? What tools make the target look absurd? And what does the writer want instead? Swift wanted fair treatment for the poor; Twain wanted readers to see that a whole society’s moral teaching could be upside down. Satire that misses its target becomes mere mockery. Satire that hits it changes how readers see the world, sometimes for centuries.

Motif and Symbol

Main ideaA motif is a repeated element that builds meaning across a text; a symbol is a concrete thing that stands for something larger.

Here is a short model story, written for this lesson. Ana counted the locks on the apartment door: three. Her father had added the third after the break-in. At school she found the locker jammed and kicked it until the lock gave. At night she lay awake listening to the deadbolt turn as her mother came home, and only then could she sleep. In the last scene, moving out at eighteen, she leaves her key on the counter and pulls the door shut behind her without locking it.

The locks are a : an element, such as an object, phrase, image or sound, that repeats across a text and gathers meaning as it repeats. One lock is a detail. Three locks, a jammed locker and a deadbolt are a pattern, and a pattern is a message. Each time the motif returns, the reader carries the earlier appearances with it. That is why the final unlocked door means so much: it is the first time the pattern breaks.

A is a concrete thing that stands for something beyond itself. In Ana’s story, the locks come to symbolize fear and the wish for safety, and the unlocked door symbolizes trust or release. The difference between motif and symbol is roughly the difference between a pattern and a meaning: the motif is what repeats, the symbol is what the repetition comes to stand for. Often the same object is both.

Two cautions. First, a symbol must be earned by the text; a reader who declares that every bird means freedom is not interpreting, only labeling. Ask whether the text itself connects the object to the idea, through repetition, placement or a character’s attention. Second, symbols are rarely equations. The locks do not mean exactly one thing. They mean fear, and care, and the father’s love, and the daughter’s cage, all at once. Good interpretation keeps that fullness.

46.8

Archetypes

Main ideaAn archetype is a character, situation or image that recurs across many cultures and centuries of stories, and recognizing it lets you see how a text uses or breaks the pattern.

A young person leaves home, meets a guide, crosses into a dangerous world, faces a test, and returns changed. You have read this story many times, with a hobbit, a Jedi, a girl from Kansas, a boy with a lightning scar. It is older than any of them. It is in the story of Odysseus and in folk tales collected in every part of the world. A pattern this widespread is an : a character type, plot or image that appears across cultures and centuries, as if human beings keep needing to tell it.

Common archetypal characters include the hero, the mentor, the trickster, the outcast and the tyrant. Common archetypal situations include the journey, the fall from grace, the return home and the sacrifice. Common archetypal images include light and dark, the garden, the flood, the desert. None of these belongs to any one writer. A writer draws on them the way a musician draws on a familiar chord.

Recognizing an archetype is useful only if you go one step further and ask how this text uses it. Huck Finn is a journey story, but Huck’s guide is an enslaved man the society calls property, and the river carries the two of them deeper into slave territory, not toward safety. Twain takes the oldest pattern and turns it so that the reader feels the wrongness of the world the pattern moves through. The interest is never in the archetype alone. It is in the variation.

A caution: archetypes are broad, so almost any story can be forced into one. If you claim a character is a trickster, show the tricks and what they cost or reveal. If you claim a journey, show the crossing, the test and the return, or show which of those the text refuses to give. The pattern is the starting point. The evidence is still the work.

46.9

Allusion

Main ideaAn allusion is a brief reference to another text, person or event that borrows that reference's meaning for the new text.

Herman Melville’s Moby-Dick, from 1851, begins with three words: Call me Ishmael. Readers in 1851 knew their Bibles, and they knew Ishmael as the son of Abraham cast out into the wilderness with his mother, an outsider who survived. Without a sentence of explanation, Melville’s narrator has told you he is a wanderer, an outcast, and a survivor. That is an : a brief reference to a person, place, event or text outside the work that brings its meaning along.

Allusions are a kind of compression. A writer who calls a character a Judas has said in one word what would otherwise take a paragraph about betrayal by a friend. Writers allude to scripture, to mythology, to Shakespeare, to history, to songs and to other novels. The reference works only if the reader catches it, which is why allusions from a century ago can sail past us today, and why annotated editions and a quick search matter.

To read an allusion, do three things. Identify the reference: who or what is being named? Recall what that reference means in its own source: Ishmael, the outcast who lived. Then ask how that meaning changes the new text: a narrator who calls himself Ishmael expects to be cast out and to endure, and Moby-Dick ends with him as the only survivor. The allusion at the first sentence has predicted the last page.

Allusions can also be ironic, when the writer sets a small thing beside a great reference to make the small thing look absurd, or a great reference beside a small thing to make it heroic. A teenager’s lost phone described in the language of Paradise Lost is comic. A cleaning woman’s day told with echoes of Odysseus is something else. In each case the borrowed meaning does work, and the interpretation is in saying what work.

Weighing Two Readings

Main ideaWhen a text supports more than one interpretation, the stronger reading is the one that accounts for more of the text and forces less of it.

Two students read The Tell-Tale Heart. One says the story is about guilt: the narrator’s own conscience, in the form of the beating heart, forces him to confess. The other says it is about madness: there is no guilt, only a diseased mind hearing sounds that are not there. Both readings are reasonable. Both cite the text. So which is right? The honest answer is that interpretation is not a coin flip between equal guesses. Some readings are stronger than others, and there are tests.

The first test is coverage: how much of the text does the reading explain? The guilt reading explains the confession, but it must work hard to explain why the narrator feels no guilt at all before the police arrive. The madness reading explains the seven nights of staring and the hearing of a dead heart, and it also explains the confession, since the narrator cannot tell his own heartbeat from the corpse’s. A reading that accounts for more, with less strain, is stronger.

The second test is versus outside assumption. If a reading depends on something the text never shows, such as the old man secretly deserving it, the reading is standing on air. The third test is whether the reading can survive its best objection. State the opposing reading fairly, then show what it cannot explain. A reading that has never faced an objection has not been tested.

Notice that the two readings of Poe may not be enemies. Perhaps the story is about a mind that experiences guilt as sound, so that madness and conscience are the same thing. A synthesis that keeps the strengths of both is often the strongest interpretation of all. But it must still meet the tests: coverage, evidence, and survival under objection. The rule is not that all readings are equal. The rule is that every reading must earn its place.

Writing an Interpretation

Main ideaAn interpretive essay argues how a text means, with a debatable claim, evidence from the text, and reasoning that connects the two.

A weak thesis says: The Tell-Tale Heart uses an unreliable narrator. That is true and no one would argue with it, which is exactly the problem. A strong thesis says: In The Tell-Tale Heart, the narrator’s insistence on his own calm is the very evidence Poe uses to convict him, so that the story makes the reader the judge the narrator fears. Now someone could disagree. Now there is something to prove. An interpretation is an , and an argument needs a claim that could be wrong.

The claim should be about how the text means, not merely what it contains. Not: the story has a symbol. Rather: the heartbeat works as a symbol of guilt the narrator cannot name, and Poe times its return so that it grows as the narrator’s confidence grows. The second version connects a device to an effect. That connection, device to effect to meaning, is the spine of every interpretive paragraph.

Evidence means quotation and close description. Quote short, then explain. Do not let a quotation do your arguing; a quotation is a fact, and the reasoning is where you show what the fact proves. A useful paragraph shape: claim for this paragraph, quoted or described evidence, two or three sentences explaining how the evidence supports the claim, and a sentence linking to the thesis. Then treat the strongest objection honestly in its own paragraph and answer it.

Comparing two texts on a theme follows the same rules with one addition: the claim must be about the relationship. Not: both Poe and Frost write about the mind. Rather: Poe’s narrator and Frost’s speaker both rewrite their pasts, but Poe punishes the rewriting while Frost leaves it open, and that difference reveals two views of self-knowledge. Then every paragraph must keep both texts in play. An interpretation that argues is not a report on a book. It is your best case for a way of seeing it, made with the text in your hand.

The story

One Night in the Concord Jail

In the summer of 1846 a man who lived in a cabin by a pond refused to pay a tax, spent a night in a cell, and turned the night into an essay that reached Gandhi and King.

In July 1846 Henry David Thoreau walked into Concord, Massachusetts, to pick up a mended shoe. He was living alone in a small cabin he had built beside Walden Pond, a mile or so from town, trying to find out how little a person needed in order to live well. On the street he met the town's tax collector, who reminded him that he had not paid his poll tax, a flat tax charged to each adult man, in several years. Thoreau said he would not pay it. The collector locked him up.

The refusal was not about money. The United States had just gone to war with Mexico, a war that Thoreau and many others in New England saw as a scheme to extend slavery into new territory. Massachusetts also returned escaped slaves to their owners under federal law. Thoreau did not believe he could pay into a government that did those things and still call himself an honest man. Paying, he thought, would make him a small partner in the wrong.

He spent one night in the jail. By his own account he found it oddly pleasant: he talked with his cellmate, listened to the sounds of the town through the window, and felt he was seeing Concord from the inside for the first time. In the morning someone, probably a relative, paid the tax without asking him, and the jailer let him go. He collected his shoe, joined a huckleberry party, and was back in the woods within the hour.

Two years later he gave a lecture about the night, and in 1849 it was printed under the title Resistance to Civil Government. Later editors called it Civil Disobedience, and the name stuck. In it Thoreau argues that a person's conscience outranks the law, that a majority can be wrong, and that when a government does injustice, the right place for a just person may be its prison. He wrote it as an argument, not a diary, and it turned one night in a small-town cell into a permanent American position.

That is what this chapter is about: American writers arguing with their country and with one another. A Puritan poet writing love poems in a colony that suspected poetry. A preacher trying to frighten a congregation into grace. A pamphlet writer telling soldiers that the times try men's souls. A formerly enslaved man explaining how he learned to read against the law. A poet in a white dress who published almost nothing. Each of them answers a question the last one asked, and the argument is still going.

Anne Bradstreet and Puritan Poetry

Main ideaThe first published American poet was a Puritan woman whose plain, personal poems reveal both her faith and her doubts.

In 1630 an eighteen-year-old named Anne Bradstreet crossed the Atlantic with her husband and parents to the Massachusetts Bay Colony. She later wrote that her heart rose against the new world at first, and then she submitted to it. She raised eight children in a settlement where winters killed and books were few. She also wrote poetry, at night. In 1650 a collection of her poems was printed in London under the title The Tenth Muse. It was the first published book of poems by a writer living in what became the United States.

The who founded the colony believed that God had made a covenant with them. Scripture was the final authority, and every person’s soul was in daily danger. They distrusted ornament, in churches and in writing. They prized the : clear, direct language that served truth rather than showing off. Bradstreet’s best poems are plain in that sense. When her house burned in 1666 she wrote about walking past the ruins. She remembers the trunk and the chest where treasures had stood, and then reminds herself that her true home is in heaven.

The poems are also honest about the pull between this world and the next. In her poem to her husband she says that if two people were ever one, they are. If a man was ever loved by a wife, he is. That is not the voice of a woman who found this world easy to give up. The tension between loving what is here and trusting what is promised runs through her work. It is the first version of a tension that American writers have never stopped exploring.

Read Bradstreet for two things. First, for the plain style at its best, which later writers from Thoreau to Hemingway would return to. Second, for the sound of a real person inside a rigid system, keeping faith and keeping her own voice at the same time. That is a very American situation, and she got there first.

Main ideaEdwards's famous sermon uses vivid images and relentless logic to make an abstract doctrine feel like physical danger.

On July 8, 1741, in Enfield, Connecticut, a minister named Jonathan Edwards read a sermon in a level voice. By several accounts, the congregation began to weep and cry out before he finished. Its title was Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God. Edwards was one of the most brilliant minds in the colonies, a philosopher as well as a preacher. He was part of the , a wave of intense religious revival that swept the colonies in the 1730s and 1740s.

The sermon’s argument is simple: nothing but God’s choice keeps any sinner out of hell at this moment, and God owes no one that choice. What makes the sermon literature is its . Edwards compares the listener to a spider held over a fire by a thread, to a person walking on a rotten covering over a pit, to water dammed up and rising. Every image turns an idea about the soul into a body in danger. The listener does not think about damnation; the listener feels the thread.

Notice the method, because it is a method every persuasive writer can use. Edwards states a doctrine, then finds a physical picture for it, then repeats the picture with variations until the mind cannot escape it. He also speaks directly to the listener, in the second person, so that no one in the room can assume he means someone else. The sermon is an argument aimed at the body as much as the mind.

Edwards and Bradstreet share a faith and a colony but not a tone. She writes quietly of love and loss; he writes to terrify people toward grace. Reading them together you meet the two faces of Puritan writing: the private conscience and the public warning. American literature would keep both voices, and later writers would argue with each.

Main ideaThomas Paine's pamphlets turned the argument for independence into plain, urgent prose that ordinary readers could repeat.

In January 1776 a forty-seven-page pamphlet appeared in Philadelphia. It was called Common Sense, its author was unnamed, and within months it had sold more copies, for its population, than almost any publication in American history. The author was Thomas Paine, an Englishman who had arrived in the colonies barely a year earlier. His argument was that the colonies should not merely resist the king’s taxes but separate from Britain entirely, and that the idea of kings was itself absurd.

A is a short, cheap, unbound printed argument, and in the 1770s it was the fastest way to reach a public. Paine’s genius was his style. Where lawyers and ministers wrote in long, learned sentences, Paine wrote as if talking across a table: short claims, homely comparisons, direct challenges. He called hereditary monarchy a joke played by nature, and asked why an island should govern a continent. Readers who had never read a political argument read his aloud in taverns.

The summer soldier and the sunshine patriot quit when it gets hard. Against them stands the one who stands it now and deserves the love and thanks of man and woman.

The argument for independence was made not only in the Declaration’s formal periods but in language a farmer or a private could carry in his head. Later American writers who trusted plain speech, from Lincoln to Twain, were working in a tradition Paine helped make. Notice, too, that Paine answers the Puritans: for them, the danger was the soul; for him, the danger was the king, and the remedy was in human hands.

Emerson, Thoreau and the Self

Main ideaThe Transcendentalists argued that truth comes through the individual's own mind and conscience, not through institutions or tradition.

In the 1830s a former minister in Concord, Massachusetts, began giving lectures that made his listeners feel both thrilled and scolded. Ralph Waldo Emerson told them that every person had direct access to truth through intuition and nature. Institutions, he said, were the lengthened shadows of single people, and imitation was suicide. His 1841 essay Self-Reliance is a string of such sentences, each one a small dare. He and his circle were called . They believed the deepest truths transcended, or went beyond, what the senses and logic alone could prove.

Transcendentalism was America’s version of , the European movement that valued feeling, imagination, nature and the individual over reason, rules and society. But the Concord writers gave it a practical edge. If conscience is the highest authority, then a person must act on it. Emerson’s most famous student took him at his word. Henry David Thoreau built the cabin at Walden Pond in 1845 to test how simply one could live, and wrote Walden, published in 1854, about the two years he spent there.

Thoreau’s night in jail produced Civil Disobedience, and its central claim answers Edwards directly: the conscience that Edwards would terrify into submission, Thoreau treats as the one authority a person must never surrender. His sentences are built for quoting. He writes that under a government which imprisons anyone unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison. A century later Gandhi in India and Martin Luther King Jr. in the American South read the essay and used it.

The Transcendentalists could be naive; they trusted the self more than the self may deserve. But their argument set the terms for much of what followed. Whitman’s celebration of himself, Dickinson’s private universe, even Huck Finn’s decision to follow his heart against his teaching, all work out what Emerson and Thoreau started. In America, they argue, the individual conscience is where the real authority lives.

The Dark Side of Romanticism

Main ideaHawthorne, Poe and Melville shared the Romantics' interest in the inner life but saw guilt, madness and obsession where Emerson saw light.

Not every American Romantic believed in the sunny self. Nathaniel Hawthorne lived in Concord for a time and knew Emerson, but his fiction is full of secret sin and public shame. His 1850 novel The Scarlet Letter follows Hester Prynne, forced by a Puritan town to wear a red letter A for adultery. The father of her child, a respected minister, hides his guilt until it destroys him. Hawthorne’s own ancestor had been a judge in the Salem witch trials. He used the Puritan past to ask what a community does to the people it condemns.

Edgar Allan Poe, who died in 1849, went inward and down. His narrators bury enemies alive, watch themselves go mad, and cannot escape their own minds. Poe also invented the modern detective story and wrote about how a story should produce a single effect in the reader, planned from the first sentence. Where Emerson trusted the individual mind, Poe made it the most frightening place in the world.

Herman Melville’s Moby-Dick, from 1851, sets a Transcendentalist temptation on a whaling ship. Captain Ahab has lost a leg to a white whale and hunts it across the oceans as if it were evil itself. His crew follows a single will to destruction. The narrator, Ishmael, survives to tell it. The novel takes Emerson’s confident self and asks what happens when that self is wounded, obsessed and in command of other people.

Critics sometimes call these three writers . They are not anti-Romantic; they share the movement’s fascination with the individual, with nature and with the imagination. But they answer Emerson’s optimism with a question: what if the self, looked at honestly, contains things that cannot be trusted? Guilt in Hawthorne, madness in Poe, obsession in Melville. The argument between these two sides of Romanticism is one of the great conversations in American writing.

Main ideaWhitman and Dickinson invented two opposite poetic voices, one vast and public, one compressed and private, and both broke the rules of verse.

In 1855 a printer and journalist from Brooklyn published a thin book of poems with no author’s name on the cover. There was only a picture of a bearded man in work clothes with his hat tilted. The book was Leaves of Grass, and the man was Walt Whitman. Its long poem, later titled Song of Myself, opens by celebrating the speaker’s own self. It declares that every atom belonging to him belongs equally to the reader. Whitman wrote in , lines without regular meter or rhyme, each as long as a breath. He cataloged everything in America he could see: carpenters, prostitutes, presidents, the runaway slave he shelters in one section.

About the same time, in Amherst, Massachusetts, Emily Dickinson was writing poems no one would read for decades. She wrote nearly eighteen hundred; fewer than a dozen were published in her lifetime, and those against her wishes or altered by editors. Her poems are short, packed, and strange, full of dashes, unexpected capital letters, and , where sounds nearly match but not quite. She wrote about death, God, pain and the mind in the meter of hymns, and then broke the hymn from inside.

Set the two side by side. Whitman’s line spills across the page and includes everyone; Dickinson’s line stops short and admits almost no one. Whitman writes I am large, I contain multitudes; Dickinson writes I’m Nobody. Yet both are doing what Emerson asked: trusting the self absolutely and making a new form to fit it. Both were also reworking the earlier American voices, Whitman answering Paine’s public prose with public poetry, Dickinson answering Bradstreet and Edwards with a private faith that questions everything.

Nearly all later American poetry descends from one of them. Poets who want to include the whole country in a long line look back to Whitman. Poets who want to say the most in the fewest words look back to Dickinson. Learn to hear both, and you will hear the two poles of the American voice.

47.7

Frederick Douglass Learns to Read

Main ideaDouglass's Narrative uses the story of his own literacy to argue that slavery depends on keeping minds in the dark.

Frederick Douglass was born enslaved in Maryland around 1818. He escaped to the North in 1838, and in 1845 he published Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave, Written by Himself. Those last three words were an argument. Many white readers doubted that a formerly enslaved man could write such a book. Douglass put his own name on the title page and named the people who had held him, at real risk of being captured and returned.

A is a first-person account of life in slavery and escape from it, and hundreds were published before the Civil War. Douglass’s is the most famous because it is a work of literature as well as testimony. Its center is the story of reading. His mistress in Baltimore began teaching him letters until her husband stopped her, saying that learning would make a slave unmanageable. Douglass writes that this speech was his first clear lesson: the pathway from slavery to freedom ran through the alphabet. He then taught himself, trading bread to poor white boys for lessons.

The Narrative is built as an argument as much as a memoir. Each incident is chosen to prove a point. Slavery corrupts the enslavers. Religion was used to justify cruelty. An enslaved person’s mind was fully human and fully capable. The book’s most famous sentence turns the story on its hinge. The reader has seen how a man was made a slave, and will now see how a slave was made a man. Douglass fights back against a brutal overseer named Covey. From that day, he says, he was free in spirit whatever his legal status.

Douglass is answering everyone before him. Against Edwards, he shows religion used for evil and demands a better one. Against Paine and Jefferson, he asks how a nation founded on liberty could hold him. Alongside Emerson and Thoreau, he shows self-reliance not as a lecture topic but as a fight in a farmyard. In 1893 he spoke at the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago, an old man still arguing with his country.

47.8

Harriet Jacobs and the Hidden Years

Main ideaJacobs's narrative tells what Douglass's could not: how slavery bore down on women, and how she hid seven years in a tiny attic to free her children.

Harriet Jacobs was born enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina, in 1813. Her 1861 book, Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl, was published under the name Linda Brent, with the names of people and places changed. Its subject was too dangerous to print openly: the sexual pursuit of enslaved women by the men who owned them. Jacobs addressed her book directly to the free women of the North. She asked them to imagine her situation before judging her choices.

The book’s most astonishing section describes her hiding place. To escape her owner and to be near her two children, Jacobs hid in a crawlspace under the roof of her grandmother’s house. The space was about nine feet long and seven wide, with a sloping roof so low she could not stand. She stayed there for nearly seven years. She watched her children through a small hole she bored in the wall, unable to speak to them. She wrote later that her body suffered badly. Still, she would rather live in that loophole than in the comfort of slavery.

Douglass’s story moves outward and upward: from ignorance to reading, from a farmyard fight to escape to a lecture platform. Jacobs’s moves inward and stays: into a garret, for years, for her children. The two narratives together show that slavery was not one experience. Douglass writes of manhood won; Jacobs writes of motherhood defended. Reading both, you see what each account leaves out and what the other supplies.

For many years scholars doubted that Jacobs had written her own book, assuming a white editor had. Research in the 1980s, using her letters, established that the book was hers. That history is itself part of the American argument: whose voices get believed, and what proof is demanded of them. Jacobs, like Douglass, wrote her way into the record, and the record eventually caught up.

Outside the wall, your children playing, who do not know you are there. Now picture staying for seven years.

Jacobs said she would rather live in that loophole than in the comfort of slavery. What does her choice tell you about the word comfort as it was used to defend slavery?

47.9

Twain and the Realist Turn

Main ideaRealism after the Civil War aimed to show ordinary life and ordinary speech truthfully, and Twain made a boy's dialect carry a moral argument.

After the Civil War, American writers turned away from the Romantic interest in the ideal and the strange, toward ordinary people in ordinary places, speaking the way they actually spoke. This turn is called . Mark Twain, born Samuel Clemens in Missouri in 1835, spent his youth on the Mississippi River as a printer, a riverboat pilot and a journalist. He had an ear for American speech that no writer before him had shown on the page.

Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, published in the United States in 1885, is narrated by an unschooled boy in his own , the regional speech of the Missouri river country. That choice was itself a Realist statement: the voice of the book belongs to the least respectable person in it. Huck floats down the river with Jim, an enslaved man escaping to freedom, and the book’s moral center is the moment Huck decides not to turn Jim in. He has been taught that helping Jim is a sin. He tears up the letter that would betray him and says, all right, then, I’ll go to hell.

The sentence is the whole argument of the book. Huck’s conscience, shaped by a slaveholding society, tells him one thing; his feeling for Jim tells him another, and he chooses the feeling and accepts damnation. Twain is answering Edwards, whose sermon threatened exactly that hell. He answers Emerson by showing that conscience itself can be corrupted by teaching. He answers Douglass by putting a white boy through the discovery that a Black man is a man.

The novel is not simple. Its final chapters, in which Jim is treated as a plaything in a boy’s game, have troubled readers for more than a century, and its use of a racial slur hundreds of times makes it painful to read aloud. Those problems are part of studying it honestly. Realism promised the truth about American life, and the truth included the ugliness that Twain both exposed and, at times, fell into.

47.10

Naturalism: Crane, London, Chopin, Wharton

Main ideaNaturalist writers showed people shaped and often crushed by forces they cannot control: nature, society, heredity and chance.

In January 1897 Stephen Crane was aboard a ship carrying guns to Cuba when it sank off Florida. He spent thirty hours in a ten-foot dinghy with three other men before reaching shore. His story The Open Boat, published that year, begins with the men unable to see the sky because they are watching the waves. The sea in the story is not evil and not kind; it simply does not care whether they live. That indifference is the heart of , a movement that grew out of Realism and pushed it toward the forces beyond human control.

Naturalist writers treated people almost as scientists treat specimens, shaped by heredity, environment and chance. Jack London’s To Build a Fire, in its 1908 version, follows a man in the Yukon who ignores an old-timer’s warning. He tries to travel alone at seventy-five degrees below zero. His fingers freeze, his fire fails, and the cold kills him without malice. His dog trots off toward the camp where there is food and warmth. Crane had done something similar in The Red Badge of Courage in 1895. He showed a soldier’s courage and cowardice as reactions no nobler than instinct.

Society was a force too. Kate Chopin’s The Story of an Hour, from 1894, gives a wife one hour of believing her husband is dead, an hour in which she whispers to herself that she is free. Then he walks in unharmed and she dies, and the doctors call it joy. Chopin’s 1899 novel The Awakening, about a woman who refuses the roles of wife and mother, was condemned so widely that her career never recovered. Edith Wharton’s The House of Mirth, from 1905, watches New York society destroy a woman who cannot afford to belong to it and will not sell herself to stay.

Naturalism answers the Transcendentalists most sharply of all. Emerson said trust yourself and nature; Crane’s sea and London’s cold do not care about the self, and Chopin’s and Wharton’s societies punish it. Yet the best Naturalist stories are not simply bleak. The men in the open boat feel a brotherhood they never felt on land. Chopin’s wife has her hour. What these writers deny is the guarantee that the self will win. What they keep is the dignity of the attempt.

47.11

Dunbar and the Voices to Come

Main ideaPaul Laurence Dunbar became the first widely famous Black American poet, and his poem about the mask points toward the Harlem Renaissance.

Paul Laurence Dunbar was born in Dayton, Ohio, in 1872, the son of parents who had been enslaved in Kentucky. He was the only Black student in his high school class and edited the school paper. After graduation the only work he could find was running an elevator. In 1893 he traveled to Chicago for the World’s Columbian Exposition, where he met Frederick Douglass. Douglass hired him as a clerk and praised him publicly. By 1896 Dunbar was famous across the country, the first Black American poet to reach a wide national audience.

Dunbar wrote in two voices. Many of his most popular poems used a Black Southern that white readers found charming and that Dunbar came to feel trapped by. His poems in standard English were often more bitter and more direct. The most famous, We Wear the Mask, from 1895, describes a mask that grins and lies, hiding cheeks and eyes, worn over torn and bleeding hearts. The poem never names race. It does not have to. Every Black reader in 1895 knew what mask it meant, and the poem’s restraint is its force.

Dunbar died of tuberculosis in 1906 at thirty-three. Fifteen years later a young poet named Langston Hughes published his first major poem, The Negro Speaks of Rivers, in 1921. Hughes belongs to the , the flowering of Black art, music and writing in the 1920s, which is beyond this chapter’s range and you will study elsewhere. But its writers read Dunbar, argued with his dialect poems, and carried his mask forward. Dunbar is the hinge between Douglass’s generation and theirs.

Here the chapter’s long argument comes round. Bradstreet kept her voice inside a rigid faith. Douglass and Jacobs wrote their way into a record that doubted them. Dunbar wrote of a mask that the country made him wear. Each is asking what America is and whether it includes them. The Harlem Renaissance would answer with a whole movement. For now, the thing to hear is how the question got there: passed from writer to writer, each one refusing the last one’s silence.
