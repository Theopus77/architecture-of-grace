Room 104

The Year We Looked Up · Grades 9–10 · direct-instruction curriculum

EditionUniversal Edition
CohortMs. Calloway (now high school SEL coordinator) · Ms. Wong (counselor) · Mr. Lin (geometry) · the same ten · plus Dr. Ramirez (Kezia’s therapist), Rachel (a student Theo notices)
Register9–10 register: the cohort speaks in first-person voice now. The construct work is internalized in real time, in real rooms, mostly alone. The cohort is no longer the daily container — high school scatters them across schedules. The Mistake Wall becomes a public artifact for 200+ freshmen. Theo’s noticing-fatigue mirrors what teachers themselves experience in this curriculum.

This is the universal edition: the full curriculum for this grade band, with no faith content. The crosswalk matrix, marker guide and Grace in Practice chart need no faith background. The Neuro-Affirming Adjustment column is core practice, not an extra.

01

Crosswalk Matrix

Each key scene, matched to its lesson, its anchor chart and a clear neuro-affirming adjustment. Print on cardstock and keep it at the front of your teacher binder.

Ch. 1

The First Day of High School

★ HIGH RISK

The cohort arrives at Lincoln Park High. The school is bigger, the halls louder, the schedule scatters them. The cohort that has been their daily container for eight years is now a fragmented thing they will only see in pieces.

Core SEL themeIdentity · Continuity Under Transition
Lesson alignmentU1·L1 (9-10) — Identity in a Scattered Container
Anchor chart trigger

WHO AM I WHEN THE COHORT IS NOT IN THE ROOM (9-10) — the self continues; the Mask is now optional in every room, every period. “Identity is what you bring INTO each room — not what each room makes of you.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

High school transition is the highest-risk identity moment for autistic students: fewer predictable adults, more unpredictable peers, escalating masking demands. Build an Identity Anchor Card — “Things that stay true about me regardless of the room.” Five lines. Carried in the wallet. Re-read at every transition.

Ch. 2

Room 104

Ms. Calloway is the SEL coordinator now; her room is Room 104. The Mistake Wall moves with her — it will be in this building for four years. The cohort knows the Wall; the other 200 freshmen are about to learn it.

Core SEL themeAll Four · The Container Travels
Lesson alignmentPacing Anchor — Wall as Permanent Infrastructure
Anchor chart trigger

THE WALL (9-10) — public anchor at the entrance to Room 104. “Anyone may write a card. Cards are anonymous unless signed. Repair is always the second line. The Wall does not get messed with. The Wall keeps the building honest.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

For students walking past the Wall daily, predictability is the gift. The cards may change; the rules do not. Print a wallet-card with the four rules: “anonymous-okay, repair-on-the-card, never-mocked, kept-by-everyone.”

Ch. 3

The Inner Critic Goes to High School

★ HIGH RISK

Priya (first-person) catches the Critic twice in one geometry class. The Critic is louder in the new room because the cohort is not there to catch her. “The catching, in this room, has to be entirely mine.”

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · Solo Critic-Catching
Lesson alignmentU2·L1 (9-10) — The Critic in New Rooms
Anchor chart trigger

CATCHING THE CRITIC SOLO (9-10) — “The cohort built the muscle. The high-school room is where the muscle works alone. The practice was for this.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Provide a wallet-sized Critic Catch Card: three trigger words (ALWAYS, NEVER, TOO MUCH) and three quick counters — (1) name the tactic, (2) state one fact, (3) breathe four counts. The card is the cohort-in-portable-form for moments the cohort is not present.

Ch. 4

Amara at the Locker

★ HIGH RISK

Second week of school. Amara, alone between classes, finds a girl she does not know crying at a locker. She stops. She opens the lock, invites Olivia to lunch, and is twelve minutes late to class. ‘The architecture is built out of small things.’

Core SEL themeIdentity · The Small Thing in Front of You
Lesson alignmentU1·L1 (9-10) — Mask vs. Mirror
Anchor chart trigger

REFUSING THE MASK (9-10) — “The Mask becomes habitual under pressure. You can refuse it in the first three days, or spend four years removing it. Refuse early.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

★ MASKING IS DOCUMENTED SURVIVAL BEHAVIOR ★ Masking pressure peaks at high-school entry. “You can stim. You can be quiet. You can opt out of small talk. The new rooms can learn. You do not owe new rooms a curated self.”

Ch. 8

Theo Notices

Theo, the noticing kid since first grade, starts noticing a freshman named Rachel being quietly excluded at lunch. He tells Sofia, says one kind sentence to Rachel in the hallway, and routes it to Ms. Calloway, who says: Notice. Route. Put down. But Theo is noticing more rooms than one fourteen-year-old can sustainably hold.

Core SEL themeForgiveness · Empathy at Cost
Lesson alignmentU3·L1 + Caregiver-Fatigue Strand
Anchor chart trigger

NOTICING IS A GIFT — AND A LOAD (9-10) — “The kids who see what others miss are valuable. They are also at risk of carrying more than is sustainable. Noticing alone, for too long, breaks the noticer.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Many autistic students ARE the noticing kid — the same pattern-recognition that makes them autistic makes them see what others miss. “Your noticing is real and costly. You may route what you notice to a trusted adult and then put it down. You do not have to fix what you notice. The fixing belongs to the adults.”

Ch. 9

Kezia Carries Something

★ HIGH RISK

An estranged relative writes Kezia’s grandmother. The letter sits on the counter. Kezia stops sleeping for three weeks, fails a biology quiz, lies to Ms. Calloway in an email. Ms. Calloway finds her in the hall: “What is going on.” Kezia tells her everything.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · Carrying What Cannot Yet Be Put Down
Lesson alignmentU2·L6 (9-10) — When Self-Forgiveness Is Hard
Anchor chart trigger

CARRYING ALONE IS HEAVIEST (9-10) — “You are nine years old, in some part of you, right now. The old version of you who carried this is awake in your body. That is normal. Both versions of you deserve compassion. Both need help.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

★ FOR STUDENTS WITH SHUTDOWN HISTORY ★ When something old surfaces, the autistic nervous system often returns to its earliest survival state — shutdown, mutism, self-care regression. This is NOT failure; it is protection. “Your body has done this before. It knows how to wait this out. The wait does not have to be alone.”

Ch. 11

The Party I Did Not Go To

Sofia, who has had a worried stomach since first grade, is invited to a freshman party. She does not go, and thinks for a week she made the wrong choice. Ms. Calloway: “Not going because your body told you it was not safe is not failure. That is your nervous system doing its job. Listen to it.”

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · Honoring the Body
Lesson alignmentRegulation & Repair — Body as Information
Anchor chart trigger

THE BODY IS INFORMATION (9-10) — “Your stomach, heart rate, shoulders, sleep — not problems to override. Messengers. The override-the-body protocol of adolescence is wrong. The honor-the-body protocol is right.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

★ HIGH SALIENCE ★ Autistic interoception is often atypical and reliable signals are easily overridden by social pressure. “Sensory overwhelm, racing heart, GI distress, sudden exhaustion are NOT social problems to push through. They are data. If your body says no to a party, the no is legitimate. You do not need to justify it — including to yourself.”

Ch. 13

The Wall in February ★ KEY CHAPTER (Theo)

February. The Wall has a hundred cards. Theo stands in front of it at 7:42 a.m. for four minutes and writes a card: “I have been the noticing kid since first grade. I am, this year, more tired than I have ever been. I do not know how to stop noticing.” He signs it “— T.” Ms. Calloway sees it at 8:15. She knows.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · The Caregiver Burns Out
Lesson alignmentU2·L2 — The Weight We Carry (Caregiver Strand)
Anchor chart trigger

THE NOTICING KID (9-10) — “The kid who watches the room is a gift. The kid who watches the room is also at risk. Watching alone, for too long, costs the watcher more than the watched.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

★ AUTISTIC CAREGIVERS ★ The noticing IS the autistic pattern-recognition working overtime, so burnout risk is high. Three permissions: (1) route what you notice and stop tracking it. (2) take a noticing-break — a period, a week — off duty. (3) tell an adult: “I am full. Please redirect anyone who comes to me this week to someone else.” The role is not the identity.

Ch. 14

The Old Story

★ HIGH RISK

Amara, in Ms. Calloway’s self-forgiveness unit, writes about the two months in second grade when she watched Iris be excluded and did nothing. She answers three questions — how old was I, what did I know, what did I not yet know — and forgives eight-year-old Amara.

Core SEL themeSelf-Compassion · Identity After Mask Removal
Lesson alignmentU1·L1 / U2·L6 — Identity After the Mask
Anchor chart trigger

WHO AM I WITHOUT THE MASK (9-10) — a blank Identity Web. “You will not know immediately. You will not know in a week. You will know across years. The not-knowing is the beginning of the knowing.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

★ MASKING AND IDENTITY DEVELOPMENT ★ For students who have masked their whole conscious life, removing the Mask is a slow excavation, not one decision. “You may not know who you are without masking. That is masking having been very effective. Start with one room. One identifiable preference. One stim you stop hiding. Build from there.”

Ch. 17

The Girl Who Was Mean to Priya in Fourth Grade

Priya, fifteen, realizes the Inner Critic has been using fourth-grade Olivia’s words for five years. On the forgiveness paper she writes ‘Dear Olivia … your words are revoked.’ The Critic goes quiet for a week — and stays quieter through May.

Core SEL themeForgiveness · The Letter Applied
Lesson alignmentU3·L6 (9-10) — The Agency of Release
Anchor chart trigger

THE LETTER IN PRACTICE (9-10) — four sections: what you did; what it cost me; what I am putting down; what I am keeping. “The fourth section is the hinge. Without ‘what I am keeping,’ the letter is loss. With it, the letter is gain.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Many autistic students carry decade-old harms because memory is detailed and forgiveness is conscious rather than spontaneous. This is an asset here: “Your memory held this for years because it was real. The same brain that remembered the harm can write the release.”

Ch. 18

The End of the Season

The freshman season ends 8–10. Coach Henley names one specific thing for every kid; for Darius it is the unseen work — passes, screens, eleven assists. Darius realizes good adults across professions do what Ms. Calloway does: they see you specifically.

Core SEL themeGrace · Being Known
Lesson alignmentGrief Strand (cross-cutting U2 and U3)
Anchor chart trigger

GRIEF IN COMMUNITY (9-10) — “Grief is not a problem to be solved. Grief is a weight to be carried in company. The cohort that learned to carry stones together can carry grief together.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

★ AUTISTIC GRIEF MAY LOOK DIFFERENT ★ It may show as routine disruption, sensory regression, special-interest focus, or apparent flatness — not necessarily tears or talk. None of these are inappropriate. “There is no right way to grieve. Your way is your way. The cohort will sit with whatever your way is.”

Ch. 19

Grandfather’s Brush

Maya’s grandmother gives her the paintbrush her grandfather Augusto painted with in secret for fifty years; he died when Maya was three. “The brush is what connects you. The brush is the handoff.”

Core SEL themeGrace · Generational
Lesson alignmentU4·L1 — Grace as Gift (Generational)
Anchor chart trigger

GRACE ACROSS GENERATIONS (9-10) — “What was given to you that you did not earn. What you give to the next person that they will not earn. The line of grace is what makes a family, a cohort, a community.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

For students whose family relationships have been complicated by miscommunication or sensory differences: “You can receive a gift from someone you never met. You can give a gift to someone who does not yet exist. The line does not require a perfect relationship. The line just requires the gift.”

Ch. 20

The Sentences Come Back

In the second week of May, Ms. Calloway hands back the one-sentence commitments the class wrote on the first day. Sofia’s — ‘I have asked at least one person, at least once, for help’ — came true in December. Around the U, each cohort kid finds their sentence came true.

Core SEL themeAll Four · The Long Plant
Lesson alignmentYear-Long Arc — The Sentences That Return
Anchor chart trigger

THE SENTENCES COME BACK (9-10) — “What was read to you at six does not always work at six. Sometimes it works at fifteen. Sometimes at forty. The reading was not wasted. The reading was the planting.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

★ LONG TIMESCALES ★ Many autistic students absorb language in ways that surface later — sometimes years later. “You may not have shown you were learning. You were. The fact that you are using it now is the proof. The teacher who planted it is not in the room. The teacher is in the sentence.”

Ch. 21

The Last Day

Freshman year ends. The cohort, scattered all year, gathers in Room 104 on the last day. Ms. Calloway: “You have learned that the framework works even when the cohort isn’t in the room. That is the test of the framework. You passed.”

Core SEL themeAll Four · Portable Integration
Lesson alignmentU4·L7 — Community Charter (high school edition)
Anchor chart trigger

ROOM 104 LEARNED (9-10) — “The cohort is not the curriculum. The curriculum is the cohort’s gift to itself. We carry it without each other in every room. That is the whole point.”

Neuro-affirming adjustment

Portable take-home: the Year-End Card now layers every grade-band’s tools. Same card structure as Room 12, 18, 36, now 104. “Same architecture. New rooms.”

02

In-Margin Teacher Marker Guide

Print on labels or sticky notes and put each one where it says in your classroom copy of the novel. Each note has a Teacher Script and a Neuro-Affirming Prompt.

▸ Ch. 3 · The moment Priya catches the Critic twice in one geometry class.
Teacher script

“Look at what Priya just did. She caught the Critic. She named her. She countered her with one fact. She breathed. She solved the problem. Four steps, three of them silent, in two minutes, with no one helping. This is what we built the muscle for. The cohort is not in this room. Priya is the cohort for herself, in this room. That is the work.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Take out your Critic Catch Card. Three trigger words: ALWAYS, NEVER, TOO MUCH. Three quick counters: (1) name the tactic. (2) state one fact. (3) breathe four counts. The card fits in your wallet and travels to every room where the cohort is not. If you have not made yours — make it this week.

▸ Ch. 8 · When Theo notices Rachel being excluded at lunch.
Teacher script

“Theo is doing what he has done since first grade. He sees what others miss. He routes it to an adult. Notice also — he says one thing to Rachel and then hands the weight to an adult. He routes it. The intervention is structural, not personal. That is wisdom.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

If you are a noticing kid, you have three options when you see something: (1) route it to a trusted adult who can act. (2) small private kindness without making it a project. (3) tell another cohort kid so the noticing-work is shared. You do NOT have to be the lone hero. The lone hero burns out.

▸ Ch. 9 · Ms. Calloway finding Kezia in the hallway.
Teacher script

“Look at how she asked the question. ‘What is going on.’ Same way she asked it in third grade. Same office. The infrastructure of trust she built in third grade is what makes the disclosure possible at fourteen. Five years of trust = one moment of truth.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

If something old surfaces, your nervous system may regress to the age you were when the harm first happened. That is normal. You are not nine again as a sign of failure — you are nine again because the nine-year-old you is doing what she did then: surviving. The fourteen-year-old you can hold her. So can a trusted adult. So can the cohort.

▸ Ch. 13 · Theo, fourteen, stands in front of the Wall at 7:42 a.m.
Teacher script

“This is one of the most important chapters in the curriculum. Theo stands at the Wall for four minutes and decides to write a card — he has never written one. He has WATCHED the Wall for eight years. Today he USES it. That is not weakness. That is the Wall doing exactly what it was built to do.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Read Theo’s card twice. Then ask yourself: have I been carrying anything that looks like this? ‘I have been ___ since ___. I am, this year, more tired than I have ever been. I do not know how to ___.’ The frame is portable. The Wall takes the card. You can sign with just an initial. You can stay anonymous. The relief is the same.

▸ Ch. 11 · Sofia and the party she did not go to.
Teacher script

“Sofia listened to her body. Her body said: this is not safe. The worried-stomach is her oldest messenger — she has spent eight years learning to listen to it. Tonight, she listened. The kids who pushed through paid the body cost. Sofia is not weak. Sofia knows herself.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

If your body says no to something the cohort is doing — your body’s no is information. The no may be: (1) sensory — too loud, too crowded. (2) social — I cannot mask that long. (3) somatic — tired in a way I cannot explain. (4) intuitive — something does not feel right. All four are valid. You do not have to justify them — including to yourself.

▸ Ch. 17 · Priya writes the Dear Olivia letter for the girl from fourth grade.
Teacher script

“Priya remembered every detail. The girl does not remember being mean. That is the asymmetry of harm — the harmed remember, the harmer often does not. Priya does not need the girl’s apology to release her. Priya does not even need the girl to know. The Letter is for Priya’s nervous system. The girl is incidental.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Pick someone you have been carrying a long time. Not the biggest — a medium one. Write the Letter, four sections in order. Do not skip section four — what you are keeping. Read it once. Fold it. Put it in a drawer or burn it. The act is the practice; the artifact is incidental. If your body will not let you write — that is information. The tool will be available later.

▸ Ch. 20 · Maya, fifteen, hearing the second-grade sentence come back.
Teacher script

“A sentence Ms. Calloway said in second grade — ‘Sammy already knows you are here. That is enough for today.’ — came back to Maya at fifteen, exactly when she needed it. The reading-aloud did not look like it was doing anything at the time. The seed went in. It grew underground for seven years. Today it pushed through.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Some of you wonder whether what we do in this curriculum is actually doing anything. Some of it is doing work you cannot yet see. Sentences are being planted. They will come back — in three months, three years, three decades. The fact that you cannot feel the work happening does not mean nothing is happening. Trust the planting.

▸ Ch. 21 · The cohort gathered in Room 104 on the last day.
Teacher script

“The cohort is no longer a daily classroom. They scattered all year. They came back here today because Room 104 is the room they need to come back to. That is the test of cohort architecture: it survives the scattering. The room continues. So do they.”

Neuro-affirming prompt

Take home your freshman-year card — the same architecture as Room 12, 18, 36, now 104. The cards stack. The architecture is portable. You will be in different schools, programs, cities someday. The architecture stays the same. The card travels.

03

Grace in Practice

What grace LOOKS like in a 6-12 classroom — when there is a real social cost

Grace at this age is not a feeling. It is a choice you make when the easier choice is in your hand. The cost is the proof.

1

REFUSE TO SHARE THE SCREENSHOT

Observable

A student receives a screenshot, a leaked text, an embarrassing video, or compromising information about a peer. They do not forward it. They delete it. They do not bring it up later, even to people who would have laughed.

Teacher cue

You will rarely see this. When you hear about it — usually long after the fact — name it: “I heard you deleted the screenshot. That decision had a social cost. You paid it. That was grace.”

Not this

Forwarding it once ‘as a joke’ is the failure mode. Pretending you didn’t see it but secretly enjoying it is the in-between. Grace is the actual deletion plus the actual silence.

2

DEFEND AN ABSENT PEER WHEN IT WOULD BE EASIER NOT TO

Observable

A student is in a group conversation where someone is being mocked or talked about behind their back. The student says something — naming the absent person as a real person, redirecting, or leaving the conversation. They pay the social cost: seen as uptight, no fun, on the wrong side.

Teacher cue

Often you will only hear about this through the affected peer thanking the defender months later. Reinforce it: “You did the hard thing in that conversation. That was grace under cost.”

Not this

Silently disapproving in your head = not grace. Telling the affected peer afterward that you disagreed = not grace. Speaking up in the moment, when speaking up costs you, is grace.

3

HOLD A LIMIT AND EXTEND KINDNESS

Observable

A student has been hurt by a peer, family member, or friend. They have set a limit (less contact, no longer hanging out). They continue to NOT speak unkindly of that person. They do not retaliate. They maintain the limit, with kindness toward the person at a safe distance.

Teacher cue

Name it when you observe it: “You have not been hanging out with Cassidy this semester. I have not heard you say one unkind thing about her. That is the difference between a limit and a grudge. That is grace.”

Not this

Cutting someone off AND running them down to others = retaliation, not grace. Pretending nothing happened and continuing to be hurt = being a doormat. The discipline of distance + kindness is the actual move.

4

GIVE CREDIT TO SOMEONE WHO DID NOT GIVE IT TO YOU

Observable

A student knows that an idea or a piece of work originated with another peer (or a teacher, or a sibling) who is not present. When they present or build on that work, they give credit by name — even though no one would know if they did not.

Teacher cue

Name it loudly when you see it: “You just said ‘this was Sofia’s idea.’ Sofia is not in this class. No one would have known. You said it anyway. That is grace.”

Not this

Adults call this integrity. It is also grace — because the un-earned gift here is the credit you could have kept for yourself.

5

SHOW UP FOR SOMEONE WHO CANNOT RECIPROCATE

Observable

A student attends the funeral of someone they barely knew, sits with the new kid at lunch on a Tuesday in October when the new kid has nothing socially to offer back, calls an elderly grandparent with dementia who will not remember the call. The act is one-directional. There is no return.

Teacher cue

Name it privately and specifically: “I heard you went to your neighbor’s grandmother’s funeral. You barely knew her. You went anyway. That is grace.”

Not this

Showing up because you expect to be recognized for it = social currency, not grace. The mark of grace at this age is the absence of return. The accounting does not balance. That is the point.

Five behaviors. Each one has a real cost. Each one is the moment when grace stops being a vocabulary word and becomes a fact about who the student is becoming. Grace at this age is detected by behavior under cost. A senior who has practiced these five behaviors across four years has built the architecture this curriculum has pointed at since first grade. The architecture is invisible. It is also load-bearing. They will need it. They have it.
