Literary Devices

The last band taught you to catch a writer arguing. This one starts with the devices that are not arguing at all — or rather, are arguing about something a sentence cannot say directly. Irony, juxtaposition, motif, foreshadowing, ambiguity, an unreliable narrator, a frame: these are structural rather than local, which means you cannot find them in a line. You find them by noticing that two parts of a text are in a relationship. That is the upgrade — the unit of analysis stops being the sentence and becomes the distance between two places in a work. Five tabs — cards for the devices and how each creates meaning from a gap, hints with the distance test, a quiz that makes you say what a juxtaposition claims, and a workshop where you sort devices, order an analysis, label a structure and argue what a gap is doing.

The meaning is in the gap

Chapter nine and chapter ten of one novel. Read them separately and each is just a scene. Now read the three details under each heading.

These devices do not live in a line — they live in the distance between two places in a text, and the gap is where the meaning is made. So the analysis changes shape: quote BOTH places, name the relationship between them, and say what the reader has to supply to close the gap. Then test it — if joining the two with a because would change nothing, there was no device.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

I found the device and named it. That is the analysis.

Naming it is step zero here, because these devices are relationships rather than things. “This is a juxtaposition” does not tell anyone which two places, what relationship holds between them, or what the reader had to supply — and all three of those are the answer. Quote both, name the relationship, state the reader’s work.

You cannot quote a relationship with one quotation.

⚠️ People often think…

This novel is ambiguous, so I will decide which reading is right.

When a text sustains two readings and adjudicates neither, the refusal is the claim — usually that the question cannot be settled from where the characters, and therefore you, are standing. Choosing a side answers something the author deliberately left open, and your essay then describes a simpler book than the one on the table.

Show both are supported, then say what the refusal gained.

⚠️ People often think…

I listed every appearance of the water. That is the motif essay.

That is a frequency report. A motif’s meaning accumulates, so the analysable thing is the trajectory: what water meant in chapter one, what it means after the drowning, and what the text did in between to move it. Stated that way it becomes a claim with evidence — the same shape as a main idea with details under it, run across four hundred pages.

Write about the change, not the thing.

Both places, the relationship, the reader’s work

1 Watch one

Analyse: a prologue tells you nobody survived the journey; chapter one shows the family happily planning it.

  1. Name both places: the prologue’s statement, and the planning scene. The two are twenty pages apart, which is the point.
  2. Name the relationship: you know something the characters do not. That is dramatic irony, and it exists only because the author told you first.
  3. Say what the reader supplies: every cheerful line now carries a second meaning the characters cannot hear. You are doing that work, continuously.
  4. Test it: move the prologue to the end and the whole first half becomes ordinary. So the placement was the device, not the information.

Helpful Hints

⚡ The one idea

These devices do not live in a line. They live in the distance between two places in a text, and the meaning is made by the gap. Irony is a gap between what is said and what is true. Juxtaposition is a gap between two things set side by side with no connective, so the reader supplies the connection. Dramatic irony is a gap between what a character knows and what you know. So the analytical move changes: instead of quoting a phrase, you quote two things and name the relationship between them.

📐 Three ironies

KindThe gap is betweenExample
Verbalwhat is said and what is meant“Lovely weather,” in a storm.
Situationalwhat is expected and what happensThe fire station burns down.
Dramaticwhat the character knows and what you knowShe trusts the man you saw lie.

🪜 The distance test

  • 1 · Name both places. Quote the two things that are in relationship.
  • 2 · Name the relationship. Contrast? Echo? Contradiction? Prophecy?
  • 3 · Say what the reader must supply to close the gap.
  • 4 · Ask what would be lost if the two were separated, or joined by a because.

🔁 Motif against symbol

A symbol is one thing standing for an idea. A motif is a recurring element whose meaning accumulates — it does not stand for something on first appearance and may not stand for anything fixed at all. Ask: does this object mean the same thing every time it appears? If yes, symbol. If its meaning changes as the book goes on, motif, and the change is what you write about.

🎭 The unreliable narrator

Not a narrator who lies to you — a narrator the text lets you see past. The signal is a mismatch between what they report and what their own details imply. When a narrator insists he is calm and describes his hands shaking, the author has handed you evidence against the narrator, and the space between the two is where the novel actually happens.

🎯 How you will be asked

  • “What is the effect of juxtaposing these two scenes?”
  • “Identify the irony in lines 30–36 and explain its function.”
  • “How does the motif of water develop across the novel?”
  • “What evidence suggests the narrator is unreliable?”
  • “Why does the author frame the story within another story?”

✅ Before the test, can you…

  • Quote BOTH parts of a device and name the relationship?
  • Tell the three ironies apart by naming the two things in the gap?
  • Say what a motif meant early and what it means later?
  • Point at the detail that betrays an unreliable narrator?
  • Say what the reader has to supply for a juxtaposition to work?
