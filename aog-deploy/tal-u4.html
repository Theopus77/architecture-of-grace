From Sinai to the Mishnah

Origins

Every rule needs explaining. The Torah says rest on Shabbat, but it does not say what rest means. It says dwell in booths, but not how tall a booth should be. For hundreds of years, Jewish teachers passed explanations from one person to the next, out loud, by memory. Rabbinic tradition calls this the Oral Torah.

This unit follows that chain from Mount Sinai to a book. You will meet Ezra reading the Torah to a crowd, the Pharisees and Sadducees arguing about tradition, and Hillel and Shammai, who disagreed and are both remembered. Then you will watch a city burn, a rabbi escape in a coffin, and a scholar named Judah the Prince gather everything into the Mishnah.

By the end, you will be able to tell the Written Torah from the Oral Torah, name the six orders of the Mishnah, and explain why the rabbis kept the opinions that lost. You will also practice the course's habit: say who holds a belief, and describe what a text says without deciding whether it is true.

The big question

How did the Oral Torah travel from memory to the Mishnah, and why did the rabbis write it down?

Written Torah and Oral Torah

Oral Torah
Big questionHow did Jewish teachers pass on the explanations of the Torah before anyone wrote them down?
The story

The Chain of Remembering

One teacher tells one student, who tells the next, for hundreds of years.

Picture a long line of people. At the front stands a very old teacher. Jewish tradition calls him Moses. He turns to the person behind him and says the rules of the Torah out loud. He also explains what the rules mean. The person behind him listens hard and remembers every word.

That person grows up and becomes a teacher too. He turns to a student behind him and says the same words. He adds the same explanations. The line goes on and on. Each person is a student first, then a teacher. Nobody in the line writes the explanations down. They keep them by memory.

This is the story rabbinic tradition tells about the Oral Torah. The word oral means spoken. Rabbis in the Talmud say that Moses received two things at Mount Sinai. One was the Written Torah, the five books kept on a scroll. The other was the Oral Torah, the explanations that go with it.

The first chapter of Pirkei Avot, a book of the Mishnah, lists the chain. It names Moses, then Joshua, then the elders, then the prophets. Then it names the Men of the Great Assembly. After them come pairs of teachers, all the way to Hillel and Shammai. Jewish tradition holds that the chain never broke.

In this chapter you will walk along that chain. You will meet Ezra, who read the Torah aloud to a crowd. You will meet the Pharisees and the Sadducees, who argued about whether the Oral Torah was real. You will find out why a short rule, like rest on Shabbat, needed so much explaining.

Talk about itHave you ever passed a message down a line of people? What helped the message stay the same, and what changed it?
Section 1

Two Torahs, One Mountain

7.1

The Torah on a Scroll

Main ideaThe Written Torah is the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, kept as a scroll and read aloud.

The word means teaching. In Jewish tradition it names the first five books of the Hebrew Bible. They are Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. They tell how the world began. They tell about Abraham’s family. They tell how Moses led the people out of Egypt. They also hold many rules for daily life.

A is a Jewish house of worship. There the Torah is kept as a . A scribe writes it by hand on long sheets of parchment. The sheets are sewn together. Then they are rolled around two wooden poles. On Shabbat and many other days, a reader unrolls the scroll. She or he chants a part of it aloud.

Rabbinic tradition calls this the Written Torah. It is written, so anyone who can read Hebrew can look at the words. The rabbis of the Talmud treated every word of it with care. They also noticed something. Many of its rules are short. A short rule leaves a lot of questions. That is where the next lesson begins.

7.2

The Torah That Was Spoken

Main ideaRabbinic tradition holds that Moses also received an Oral Torah, the explanations, which was passed on by memory.

The Talmud tells of a second Torah. Rabbinic tradition holds that at Mount Sinai, Moses received the Written Torah and also an . Oral means spoken. The Oral Torah was the set of explanations that went with the written words. It told how to do what the Written Torah said.

According to this tradition, the Oral Torah was not written down for a very long time. A teacher said it to a student. The student learned it by heart. Then that student taught it to the next one. The rabbis called this passing on a , something handed from one person to the next.

Not everyone reads this story the same way. Orthodox Jews hold that the Oral Torah truly came from Sinai. Many Conservative and Reform Jews see it as the wisdom of teachers over many centuries. Historians study when each part was written down. In this course we describe what the tradition says and how its readers understand it.

7.3

Why a Rule Needs Explaining

Main ideaShort rules like rest on Shabbat raise questions, and the Oral Torah is where the rabbis worked out answers.

The Written Torah says to remember , the seventh day, and keep it holy. It says not to do any work on that day. That sounds simple. But try to use the rule. Is cooking work? Is carrying a book work? Is walking to a friend’s house work? The Torah does not give a list.

The rabbis said the Oral Torah answers these questions. The Mishnah, in tractate Shabbat, lists thirty-nine kinds of work. Planting, baking, sewing and writing are on the list. So is carrying from one place to another. Jewish tradition links them to building the , the tent of worship in the desert.

This is the pattern for the whole course. A short rule in the Written Torah leads to a . The question leads to a discussion. The discussion leads to a ruling, and often to a second opinion that is kept too. The rabbis believed that asking a good question was a way of honoring the Torah.

Section 2

Ezra and the Great Assembly

7.4

Ezra Reads to the Crowd

Main ideaThe Bible tells that Ezra read the Torah aloud to the people, and helpers explained it, around 450 BCE.

Long before the Talmud, the Jewish people came home from in Babylon. Their first Temple in Jerusalem had been destroyed. They built a second one. Then a scribe named came from Babylon. The Bible says he had set his heart on studying the Torah and teaching it.

The book of Nehemiah tells what happened next. Ezra stood on a wooden platform in a square in Jerusalem. He opened the scroll, and the whole crowd stood up. He read from morning until midday. Men, women and children old enough to understand were there. Helpers stood among the people and explained the reading so everyone understood.

Historians date this reading to about 450 BCE, though the exact year is debated. Rabbinic tradition loves this scene. It shows the Written Torah being read aloud and the meaning being explained at the same time. To the rabbis, Ezra looked like one of them. Later teachers said that if Moses had not come first, Ezra would have been worthy to receive the Torah.

7.5

The Men of the Great Assembly

Main ideaRabbinic tradition names a group after Ezra, the Men of the Great Assembly, as a link in the chain of teachers.

Pirkei Avot lists the chain of teachers. After the prophets, it names the . Rabbinic tradition pictures them as a large council of scholars in the time of Ezra and after. The tradition holds that they kept the Oral Torah alive and helped shape Jewish prayer.

Avot says the Men of the Great Assembly taught three sayings. Be careful in judgment. Raise up many students, called disciples. Make a fence around the Torah. A fence is a rule that keeps you a safe step away from breaking a bigger rule. For example, a fence might say to stop work a little before Shabbat begins.

Historians know very little about this group. The Talmud and the Mishnah are the main places it is described. So some scholars treat the Great Assembly as a name for the many teachers of that time. Jewish tradition, in turn, treats it as a real council. In this course, both readings are worth knowing.

Think it through
A Fence Around a Rule

A school rule says: do not run in the hall. A teacher adds a second rule: walk on the right side of the hall. The second rule is not the same as the first. But it makes the first one easier to keep. Rabbinic tradition calls a rule like the second one a fence around the Torah.

7.6

The Second Temple

Main ideaThe Second Temple in Jerusalem stood for about 500 years and was the center of Jewish worship while the Oral Torah was passed on.

The was the great house of worship in Jerusalem. Jewish tradition says King Solomon built the first one. The Babylonians destroyed it in 586 BCE. When the people came home, they built a second one. Historians date its finishing to about 515 BCE. It was later made much larger by King Herod.

In the Second Temple, priests offered gifts of animals, grain and wine. Each gift was a sacrifice. People came from far away for three big festivals each year. Passover, Shavuot and Sukkot all brought crowds to Jerusalem. The Torah gave rules for each festival, and the Oral Torah, tradition holds, explained how to keep them.

The Second Temple stood for about five hundred years. During those years the chain of teachers went on. The Written Torah was read. The Oral Torah was taught. Different Jewish groups grew up in Jerusalem, and they did not all agree. The next section meets two of them.

Section 3

Who Believed in the Oral Torah?

7.7

Pharisees and Sadducees

Main ideaIn the time of the Second Temple, the Pharisees accepted the Oral Torah and the Sadducees did not.

Near the end of the Second Temple period, several Jewish groups lived in the Land of Israel. Two of them argued about the Oral Torah. The taught that the traditions handed down from the elders were part of God’s teaching. The said that only the Written Torah counted.

The Jewish historian Josephus, who lived in the first century CE, wrote about both groups. He says the Pharisees followed many rules passed down from their ancestors that were not written in the Torah of Moses. He says the Sadducees rejected those rules. Many Sadducees were priests and wealthy families. Many Pharisees were teachers of ordinary people.

When the Temple was destroyed in 70 CE, the Sadducees lost their center. The Pharisees’ way of study continued. The rabbis of the Mishnah and Talmud saw themselves as heirs of the Pharisees. That is one reason the Talmud is full of the Oral Torah, the traditions the Pharisees had defended.

Think it through

A Pharisee answers: our teachers received explanations from their teachers, and those explanations tell us. Both are pointing to the Torah. They disagree about where its meaning comes from.

7.8

Hillel and Shammai

Main ideaHillel and Shammai, two teachers around the end of the first century BCE, were the last pair in the chain named in Avot.

Pirkei Avot names five of teachers after the Men of the Great Assembly. The last pair is and . Historians place them around the end of the first century BCE, when Herod was king. Tradition says Hillel came from Babylon to study in Jerusalem and became a leading teacher there.

The Talmud tells many stories about the two. Hillel is shown as patient and gentle. Shammai is shown as strict and quick. In one story, a man asks each of them to teach the whole Torah while he stands on one foot. Shammai sends him away. Hillel says: what is hateful to you, do not do to another; the rest is explanation, go and learn.

Each teacher had students. Their students formed two schools, the House of Hillel and the House of Shammai. The schools disagreed on hundreds of rules. The Mishnah records both sides. Rabbinic tradition holds that the law usually follows the House of Hillel, but it keeps the House of Shammai’s view on the page too.

7.9

How to Keep a Holiday

Main ideaThe Oral Torah explains how to keep the Torah's holidays, and the rabbis' disagreements about them are part of the tradition.

The Written Torah names holidays but leaves out many details. It says to dwell in booths on for seven days. It does not say how big a booth must be. It does not say what the roof is made of. It says to eat unleavened bread on . It does not say what to do with bread you forgot in a cupboard.

The Mishnah fills in the details. Tractate Sukkah begins with how tall a booth may be. The Houses of Hillel and Shammai even argue about a booth’s roof. Tractate Pesachim tells how to search the house for leavened bread. This happens the night before Passover, by the light of a lamp.

This is how the Oral Torah worked, as rabbinic tradition tells it. Rules were passed down, questions were asked, and answers were argued. The details were still in memory when the Second Temple fell. In the next chapter, a rabbi carried out of a burning city helps keep them from being lost.

Mishnah Sukkah 1:1, paraphrased from the Hebrew Mishnah (paraphrased)

The story

A Coffin and a Question

A rabbi leaves a city under siege the only way the guards will let him: as a dead man.

The year is 70 CE. Roman soldiers surround Jerusalem. Inside the walls, people are hungry, and fighters will not let anyone leave. A teacher named Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai wants to get out. He believes the city will fall. He wants to save something before it does.

The Talmud, in tractate Gittin, tells how he did it. His students spread the word that their teacher had died. They laid him in a coffin. Two of them, Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Joshua, carried it toward the gate. The guards wanted to check with a sword. The students said: do you want the Romans to say Jews stab their own teachers? The guards let the coffin pass.

Outside the walls, the rabbi climbed out. He asked to see the Roman general, Vespasian. The story says he greeted the general as a king. Vespasian was not yet emperor, so he was annoyed. Just then a messenger arrived from Rome with news: the old emperor was dead, and Vespasian was to take his place.

The general said the rabbi could ask for something. Rabbi Yochanan did not ask for the city. He asked for Yavneh and its scholars, a small town where teachers could keep studying. He asked for the family of Rabban Gamliel to be spared. He asked for a doctor for a sick rabbi. Vespasian said yes.

That is how the Talmud remembers it. Historians say the story grew in the telling, but Yavneh really did become the center of Jewish learning after the Temple burned. In this chapter you will see what the teachers at Yavneh feared, and how, about 130 years later, Rabbi Judah the Prince answered that fear with a book.

After the Temple Fell

8.1

Rome Burns the Temple

Main ideaIn 70 CE the Roman army destroyed the Second Temple, and Jewish life had to find a new center.

For about a hundred years, had ruled the Land of Israel. Roman governors taxed the people and put down protests. In 66 CE a broke out. Jewish fighters drove out the Roman soldiers. Rome sent a large army. The general Vespasian led it, and later his son Titus.

In the summer of 70 CE, after a long , Roman soldiers broke into Jerusalem. They set fire to the Temple. Jewish tradition remembers that day with a fast. It falls on the ninth of the month of Av. The historian Josephus was there with the Roman side. He wrote a long account of the fall of the city.

The Temple had been the center of Jewish worship. Sacrifices could only be offered there. Now it was gone. Rabbinic tradition tells that Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai comforted a student who wept at the ruins. He said that acts of kindness could also bring people close to God. Prayer, study and kindness became the heart of Jewish life.

8.2

Yavneh, a Town of Scholars

Main ideaAt Yavneh, Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai and his students rebuilt Jewish teaching around study and prayer.

was a small town near the coast, south of today’s Tel Aviv. After 70 CE it became a center of Jewish learning. Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai taught there. His students, and their students, are the first rabbis whose names fill the Mishnah. The word means my teacher.

The teachers at Yavneh made rules for a world without a Temple. Tradition says Rabban Yochanan made a ruling about the shofar, a ram’s horn. It could be blown on Rosh Hashanah at Yavneh, as it once was in the Temple. Later, Rabban Gamliel led the school. Tradition holds that he helped set the order of daily prayer.

Historians see Yavneh as the start of , the form of Judaism built on the rabbis’ teaching. The Talmud tells its story with legends and details. What historians can say for sure is smaller. Both views agree that after the Temple fell, study and prayer became central.

8.3

The Fear of Forgetting

Main ideaWar and persecution made the rabbis fear the Oral Torah would be forgotten, and that fear pushed them to gather it.

The Oral Torah lived in . Every teacher held a piece of it. If a teacher died before passing it on, that piece could be lost. In the years after 70 CE, many teachers died. War, hunger and Roman punishment took them. The rabbis began to worry.

In 132 CE a second revolt broke out, led by Simon . It lasted about three years and ended in defeat. Rabbinic tradition says Rome then forbade the teaching of Torah. It tells that Rabbi Akiva kept teaching and was put to death. Many of his students died too. The rabbis moved north to the Galilee, to towns like Usha.

The teachers who survived did something new. They began to gather the teachings into short, clear sayings that could be learned by heart. Each saying was called a , from a Hebrew word for repeating. Students repeated them until they knew them cold. These collections became the raw material for the book you meet next.

Section 2

Rabbi Judah and His Book

8.4

The Man Called Rabbi

Main ideaRabbi Judah the Prince, leader of the Jews in the Land of Israel around 200 CE, gathered the Oral Torah into the Mishnah.

was born, tradition says, around the time of the Bar Kokhba revolt. He came from the family of Hillel. He became the , the prince or leader of the Jewish community under Roman rule. He was wealthy, respected by Roman officials, and a great scholar. The Talmud calls him simply Rabbi, the teacher.

Rabbi Judah lived in the Galilee, first in Bet She’arim and later in Sepphoris. He gathered the teachings of the earlier rabbis, the tannaim. He sorted them by subject. He chose which sayings to keep and how to word them. He kept many disagreements, naming who said what. Historians date the finished Mishnah to about 200 CE.

Was the Mishnah written on a scroll or held in memory? Scholars debate this. Some say Rabbi Judah arranged it to be memorized. Others say it was written down early. Either way, from about 200 CE on, the Mishnah was a fixed text. Every rabbi after him studied the same six orders in the same words.

From the source
Rabbi's Own Saying
Pirkei Avot records a saying of Rabbi, that is, Judah the Prince. He asked: which is the right path for a person to choose? One that brings honor to the one who walks it and honor from other people. He also said: be as careful with a small mitzvah as with a big one, since you do not know the reward for each.
Mishnah Avot 2:1, retold from Charles Taylor, Sayings of the Jewish Fathers, 1877 (paraphrased)

8.5

Six Orders on a Shelf

Main ideaThe Mishnah is arranged in six orders by subject, and each order holds several tractates.

The Mishnah is sorted into six orders. In Hebrew each is a seder, the same word used for the Passover meal. The orders are Seeds, Festivals, Women, Damages, Holy Things and Purities. Together they hold sixty-three tractates, or books, each on one subject.

Seeds is about farming and blessings. Festivals is about Shabbat and the holidays. Women is about marriage and family. is about courts, money, harm and fairness. Holy Things is about the Temple and its offerings. Purities is about what is clean and unclean for Temple purposes. Pirkei Avot sits inside the order of Damages.

The orders show what the rabbis cared about. Two whole orders are about a Temple that no longer stood. The rabbis kept studying its rules anyway. Jewish tradition holds that studying the rules counts as a kind of keeping them. Historians see it as a way of keeping the Temple alive in memory.

8.6

Short, Clear and in Hebrew

Main ideaThe Mishnah is written in short Hebrew sentences that state rules and record who disagreed.

Open the Mishnah and you find short sentences. Each one states a rule or a case. The language is , a plain and clear Hebrew that scholars call Mishnaic Hebrew. There is very little story and very little explaining. The Mishnah says what the rule is. It rarely says why.

The Mishnah also records . A rule will be stated, and then: Rabbi Judah says otherwise. Or: the House of Shammai say this, and the House of Hillel say that. The first tractate, Berakhot, starts with a question about when to say the evening Shema. Three answers are given, from three teachers.

Why keep the losing side? Rabbinic tradition gives an answer in the Mishnah itself. It says a court might one day agree with the lost opinion, so it must be on the page. The Mishnah is short because it was made to be remembered. It keeps the arguments because, to the rabbis, the arguing was part of the Torah.

From the source
The Mishnah's First Lines
The Mishnah opens by asking from what time the evening Shema may be recited. It answers: from the hour when the priests go in to eat their offering, until the end of the first watch of the night. That is the view of Rabbi Eliezer. The sages say until midnight. Rabban Gamliel says until the dawn comes up.
Mishnah Berakhot 1:1, paraphrased from the Hebrew Mishnah (paraphrased)

Section 3

The Seed of the Talmud

8.7

A Book That Asks for More

Main ideaBecause the Mishnah states rules without reasons, later rabbis kept asking why, and their discussion became the Gemara.

The Mishnah solved one problem and made another. The Oral Torah was now safe in a fixed text. But the text was short. It said what the rule was, not why. A student who read that the evening Shema is said when the priests go in to eat would ask: why the priests? What time is that?

So the rabbis after Rabbi Judah studied the Mishnah the way earlier rabbis had studied the Torah. They asked what each line meant. They compared one mishnah to another. They brought in old teachings that were left out of the Mishnah. Each one is called a . This discussion, in the Land of Israel and in Babylon, went on for about three hundred years.

That discussion is the . The word means completion or learning in Aramaic. Mishnah plus Gemara equals . So the Mishnah is like a seed. The Gemara grew from it, and the Talmud is the whole tree. The next unit follows that growing.

Think it through
From Seed to Tree

Picture a page. In the middle is a short paragraph in Hebrew: the Mishnah. Around it grows a much longer text in Aramaic: the Gemara, asking what the short paragraph means. Around that, in smaller print, grow later explanations. The short paragraph in the middle is about the size of your hand. The page is as big as a tray.

Why would a short, clear rule book lead to a much longer book of discussion?

2. What is a baraita?

8.8

What the Mishnah Protects

Main ideaMany of the Mishnah's rules protect people who could be hurt: the worker, the poor, the stranger and the person on trial.

The Mishnah is not only about Temple and festival. Much of it is about fairness between people. In the order of Damages, one tractate says that a must be paid on time. Another is about lost objects. If a lost thing has a sign on it, the finder must announce it. Then the owner can claim it.

The order of Seeds has a rule for farmers. Leave the corners of the field for the and the . This comes from the Torah. The Mishnah works out how big a corner should be. It treats these gifts as the poor person’s right, not just as kindness.

Tractate Sanhedrin, about courts, is careful with people on trial. When a life is at stake, it says, witnesses must be warned first. One person, it says, is like a whole world. Rabbinic tradition reads these rules as built into law. In this course, mercy and argument are the two threads to follow.

From the source

Mishnah Sanhedrin says that a person was created alone at the beginning to teach that whoever destroys one life is counted as if he destroyed a whole world, and whoever saves one life is counted as if he saved a whole world. Some old copies of the Mishnah read one life of Israel; others read simply one life.
Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrased from the Hebrew Mishnah (paraphrased)

This is said to witnesses before a trial for a person's life. Why would a court want witnesses to hear it first?

8.9

Two Chapters, One Chain

Main ideaFrom Sinai to the Mishnah, rabbinic tradition tells one long chain of passing on and writing down.

Look back at the two chapters of this unit. In rabbinic tradition, the starts with Moses at Sinai. It runs through Joshua, the elders, the prophets, the Men of the Great Assembly and the pairs. It reaches Hillel and Shammai. Then it reaches the teachers of Yavneh and, at last, Rabbi Judah the Prince.

Along that chain, the Oral Torah was spoken and remembered. Ezra read the Written Torah aloud and had it explained. The Pharisees defended the traditions of the elders. The Temple fell, and the rabbis feared forgetting. So Rabbi Judah gathered the teachings into a short, clear book: the Mishnah, c. 200 CE.

Different readers tell this chain differently. Orthodox tradition holds that every link goes back to Sinai. Many Conservative and Reform readers see a growing tradition shaped by people in each age. Historians test each link against evidence. All of them agree that the Mishnah became the for everything the Talmud built next.

Some of these are people, some are groups, and some of the dates are guesses. That is how Pirkei Avot lays out the chain.
