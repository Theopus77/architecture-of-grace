Literature: Close Reading

Literature

A woman sits at an open window for one hour and whispers a word she is not supposed to feel. A walker stands where two roads split and, years later, tells a story about it that the poem quietly contradicts. A man stands over a dead body in a Roman square and, without accusing anyone, turns a city into a mob. Three pages, twenty lines, one scene. Each is small, and each holds more than most whole books.

This unit is about reading small things slowly. Fiction first: what a story says, how it says it, why the writer chose every word, and how characters, structure and point of view carry a theme. Then poetry: line breaks, meter, sound, image, speaker, tone and the turn where a poem changes its mind. Then drama: acts and scenes, soliloquy and aside, Shakespeare's language decoded, tragedy's shape, and what happens to a scene when someone stands up and plays it.

By the end you will be able to take a paragraph, a stanza or a speech, say exactly what it does, point to the words that do it, and explain why it matters, in writing that someone could argue with. That skill is called close reading. It works on Chopin and Shakespeare, and it works on a text message, a contract and a campaign ad. It is the difference between being told what a text means and finding out for yourself.

The big question

How do writers make a few chosen words carry meaning that a reader can find, prove and argue about?

The story

One Hour in a Closed Room

A woman is told her husband died in a train wreck. Sixty minutes later, the front door opens.

In 1894 Kate Chopin published a story that fits on three pages. It begins with a warning: Louise Mallard has a weak heart, so the news must be broken gently. Her sister Josephine tells her, in broken sentences, that her husband Brently has died in a railroad accident. Louise weeps at once, wildly, in her sister's arms. Then she goes upstairs alone, shuts the door of her room, and sinks into a chair by an open window.

Here the story slows down. Outside the window it is spring. Trees shake with new leaves. Someone is singing far off, and sparrows twitter in the eaves. Patches of blue sky show between the clouds. Louise sits very still. Something is coming toward her, she feels, out of the sky and the sounds and the smell of rain. She is afraid of it. She tries to push it back. Then she stops trying, and a word slips out of her lips, whispered over and over. The word is free.

Chopin does not tell us that Louise hated her husband. She tells us the opposite: he was kind, he loved her, and she had sometimes loved him. What Louise sees, in that chair, is the long line of years ahead that will belong to her alone. No one will bend her will to theirs. She prays for a long life, and only yesterday she had shuddered at the thought of a long life. Josephine kneels outside the locked door, begging her to open it. Louise finally rises, and the story says she walks out of the room like a goddess of victory.

At the bottom of the stairs, a key turns in the lock. Brently Mallard walks in, holding his umbrella, a little dusty from travel. He had been nowhere near the accident. He had not even known there was one. Josephine screams. Louise's heart stops. The last sentence of the story reports what the doctors said: she died of heart disease, of joy that kills.

Read fast, the story is a twist ending. Read slowly, sentence by sentence, it is something stranger and sadder. The doctors are wrong about the joy, and the reader is the only person in the house who knows it. This chapter is about reading that slowly: what a story says, how it says it, and why a writer might choose every one of those words.

Talk about itThe doctors say Louise died of joy. What do you think killed her, and what sentence in the story would you point to?
Section 1

Reading Closely

34.1

What the Text Says

Main ideaClose reading starts with the plain facts of the text: who, what, where, and in what order, before any interpretation.

Read this sentence from a story written for this lesson: "Marcus set the letter on the table, unopened, and went out to shovel the walk for the third time that morning." Before you decide what it means, get the facts straight. A man named Marcus has a letter. He does not open it. He goes outside to shovel snow. He has already shoveled twice today. Those are the things the sentence says. Everything else is something you add.

This first step is called reading for what the text . It sounds too easy to bother with, and that is exactly why readers skip it and go wrong. A reader who is moving fast might remember that Marcus "threw the letter away" or "read the letter and got upset." The sentence says neither. When you analyze a story, every claim you make has to rest on what is actually on the page. If you start from a misreading, every conclusion after it is built on sand.

A useful habit is to a passage in one or two plain sentences, with no opinion in them. For the story of Louise Mallard, a fair summary would be: a woman with a heart condition is told her husband died; alone in her room she feels a rush of freedom; her husband walks in alive, and she dies. Notice what the summary leaves out. It does not say she was glad, or that she was a bad wife, or that the ending is ironic. Those ideas may be true, but they are the next step, not the first one.

Try it now with a longer piece. When you read any story in this chapter, pause after each page and ask three flat questions. Who is here? What just happened? What has changed since the last page? Write the answers in a margin or a notebook. You will be surprised how often the answer to the third question is the key to the whole story, and how often a reader who did not stop to ask it misses the point.

34.2

How the Text Says It

Main ideaOnce you know what a passage says, look at how it says it: word choice, sentence length, images, and what is repeated or left out.

Here are two sentences that say the same thing. First: "She was very sad when she heard the news." Second: "She wept at once, with sudden, wild abandonment, in her sister’s arms." The second is close to how Chopin describes Louise Mallard’s first reaction. Both report grief. But the second one has a body in it. It has arms and a sudden storm of crying, and it has the word wild. The choice of those words is the how of the sentence, and it is where a story’s meaning actually lives.

Close readers look at four things when they study how a text says something. First, , which means word choice: why wild and not sad? Second, sentence length and rhythm: is the sentence short and blunt, or long and winding? Third, images, the pictures a sentence puts in your mind: open window, new leaves, patches of blue sky. Fourth, patterns: what word or idea comes back again and again, and what is oddly missing. In Chopin’s story, the word free is whispered three times in a row, and the husband’s name barely appears while Louise sits at the window.

Look at this passage written for this lesson: "The kitchen was clean. The counters were clean. The floor, which she had mopped twice, was clean. Nobody had eaten anything in that house for two days." What the passage says is simple: a clean kitchen, no eating. How it says it is the point. The word clean lands three times like a hammer. The sentences are short and flat. The last sentence explains the first three without saying the word grief once. A reader who notices the repetition and the flatness understands the character better than any label could tell them.

When you write about a story, the how is your evidence. "Louise is happy" is a claim. "Chopin describes the sky outside her window as showing patches of blue and has her whisper the word free three times" is evidence for the claim. Practice moving from one to the other. Underline one striking word in a paragraph, and ask what would change if the writer had picked a plainer one.

34.3

Why the Writer Chose It

Main ideaThe last step of close reading asks why: what effect does a choice have on the reader, and what would be lost if the writer had done it differently?

Chopin could have opened her story with the train wreck. Smoke, screaming, a list of the dead. Instead she opens in a quiet house with a sentence about a woman’s weak heart. Why? Ask what the choice does. It makes the reader nervous for Louise before anything happens. It keeps our attention on her body and her feelings, not on the accident. And it plants the fact the last sentence will need. A writer’s choice is never just decoration. It sets up an , a change in what the reader knows, feels or expects.

The question why is the hardest of the three, and it is where students most often freeze. Here is a way in. Take the choice you noticed and imagine the , the thing the writer could have done instead. Then compare. Suppose Chopin had let Louise sit at a window facing a brick wall on a gray day. The word free would still be whispered, but it would sound like madness. With spring outside the window, it sounds like the world agreeing with her. The alternative shows you what the actual choice is doing.

Read this pair written for this lesson. Version one: "Dad came home at six. He said he lost his job. We ate dinner." Version two: "Dad came home at six, like always, and set his lunchbox on the counter, like always, and told us the plant was closing, and then he asked what was for dinner." Why does version two keep saying like always? Because the writer wants the reader to feel the ordinary evening cracking. The bad news arrives inside a routine that has not yet noticed it is over. Version one reports the same events and produces almost nothing.

When you write the why, use verbs that name an effect: the choice builds suspense, softens a blow, makes us trust a character, makes us doubt one, delays the truth. Then finish with what the reader ends up believing or feeling that they would not have otherwise. Says, how, why: that is close reading, and it works on a paragraph, a page or a whole book.

Character and Motivation

34.4

Complex Characters

Main ideaA complex character wants more than one thing, changes across the story, and cannot be summed up in one word.

Think of a character from a cartoon you watched as a small child. The villain wanted to be bad. The hero wanted to be good. You could describe each one in a single word, and nothing they did ever surprised you. That is a . Now think of a person you actually know: a friend who is generous and also jealous, a parent who is strict because they are afraid. Real people hold contradictions. Fiction that feels true builds characters the same way, and we call them or round characters.

Louise Mallard is complex. She loved her husband sometimes. She weeps real tears when she hears he is dead. And within the hour she is whispering that she is free. None of these cancels the others out. Chopin even tells us that Louise knows she will weep again when she sees his kind hands folded in death. A flat version of Louise would be a cold wife who is glad, or a devoted wife who is destroyed. The real Louise is both grieving and glad, and the story’s power comes from holding those two things in one body at one window.

Here is a short model written for this lesson. "Tasha had told her little brother she would come to his game. She had also told her manager she would take the extra shift, because rent was due Friday. At four o’clock she stood in the break room with her coat half on, listening to the clock." Tasha is not lazy or selfish; she is pulled. A reader who watches her stand there with her coat half on knows more about her than any list of traits could say.

To analyze a complex character, do not stop at one adjective. Track the character across the story and ask: what does this person want, what else do they want, and where do those wants collide? Then look for the moment of change, the place where they act differently than they would have on page one. Complex characters develop, and tracing that development is one of the main things a close reader does.

34.5

Conflicting Motivations

Main ideaMotivation is the reason a character acts; when two motivations pull in opposite directions, the story's conflict often lives inside one person.

In Edgar Allan Poe’s story "The Tell-Tale Heart," published in 1843, a man insists that he is not mad, then explains how he murdered an old man because of the old man’s pale blue eye. He says he loved the old man. He says the old man had never wronged him. And still he creeps into his room every night for a week. The narrator’s , the reason behind his actions, is the puzzle at the center of the story. He claims one thing and does another, and the gap between them is where a reader has to work.

Most great stories put opposing motivations inside a single character. In Nathaniel Hawthorne’s "Young Goodman Brown," from 1835, a young husband leaves his wife at sunset to keep a mysterious appointment in the forest. He wants to go; he also feels he should turn back, and he says so several times. He keeps walking. What he finds in the forest, or dreams he finds, ruins the rest of his life. The story never settles whether the night was real, but the conflict between his curiosity and his conscience is real on every page.

Here is a model written for this lesson. "Jamal wanted to tell the coach who had broken the window. He also wanted to be the kind of person who did not tell. He sat in the locker room after everyone left, tying and untying the same shoe." Notice that the writer never uses the word torn. The shoe does the work. When you read for motivation, watch what characters do with their hands, where they linger, and what they say twice. Those are the places where two motives are fighting.

To write about conflicting motivations, name both pulls and then point to the moment one wins. Louise Mallard’s love for her husband pulls one way; her hunger for a life of her own pulls the other. At the window, the second wins, and Chopin marks it with the whispered word. Later, at the bottom of the stairs, the story does not tell us which feeling stops her heart. That silence is a choice too, and a close reader notices it.

34.6

Point of View

Main ideaWho tells the story, and how much they know, shapes everything the reader is allowed to see.

Charlotte Perkins Gilman’s "The Yellow Wallpaper," from 1892, is told by a woman writing in a secret journal. Her husband, a doctor, has confined her to an upstairs room to rest after the birth of her baby. He forbids her to write. She writes anyway, in short entries, and over weeks she becomes convinced that a woman is trapped behind the pattern of the room’s yellow wallpaper. The reader never gets the husband’s side. We see only what the narrator sees, and by the end we are watching her mind come apart from the inside. That is the power of first-person point of view.

Point of view is the position from which a story is told. In first person, a character tells it using I, and we know only what that character knows or admits. In , a narrator outside the story uses he or she but stays inside one character’s head; Chopin’s story stays close to Louise this way. In third-person omniscient, the narrator knows everything about everyone and may move from mind to mind. Each choice trades something. First person gives intimacy but takes away certainty. Omniscient gives certainty but can feel distant.

A narrator can also be , meaning the reader has reason to doubt what they say. Poe’s murderer in "The Tell-Tale Heart" says he is calm and sane while describing a crime that proves the opposite. Gilman’s narrator reports the wallpaper moving. The story never announces that these narrators are wrong. It leaves clues, such as facts that do not add up or feelings the narrator denies too loudly, and trusts the reader to see around the narrator’s shoulder.

Read this model written for this lesson: "I did not take the money. I want that clear. I was in the office, yes, and the drawer was open, and I looked, because anyone would look. But I left with the same twenty dollars I came in with, and the fact that nobody believes me is their problem." What point of view is this? First person. Is the narrator reliable? Watch the sentence about the twenty dollars. A close reader hears a defense that has been rehearsed.

34.7

Cultural Context

Main ideaKnowing when and where a story was written helps you see what its characters could and could not do, and why the writer's choices were bold.

Why does Louise Mallard whisper free instead of shouting it? Part of the answer is the year: 1894. A married woman in most of the United States then had few legal rights of her own. She could rarely sign a contract, keep her own wages, or vote. Divorce was a scandal. Chopin’s readers would have understood, without being told, that Louise’s only door to a life of her own was the one death had just opened. The story’s , the time, place and beliefs around it, is the air the characters breathe.

Context also explains "The Yellow Wallpaper." Gilman’s narrator is being treated with a real medical fashion of the 1880s called the rest cure. Doctors told women with nervous illness to lie still, avoid work, and stop thinking. Gilman herself was given this treatment and later wrote that it nearly destroyed her. Knowing that, the husband’s gentle orders in the story stop sounding like care and start sounding like a cage. A reader who does not know the context may blame the narrator. A reader who knows it understands the room.

Context is not an excuse to stop reading the text. It is a tool for reading it better. Here is a model written for this lesson: "Her grandmother had never learned to read, and she signed her name with an X on the day the deed was recorded. My mother framed that page." Without context, this is a family scrapbook. With the fact that reading was once forbidden to many enslaved people in this country, the framed X becomes something else: proof of a history and a claim on the land in the same mark.

When you read older fiction, ask two context questions. What could this character do in this time and place, and what could they not? And what did the writer risk by writing this? Chopin’s stories about women’s inner lives drew criticism in her own day. Reading with context lets you see that a quiet story of one hour in a closed room was, in its moment, a daring thing to publish.

Theme and Structure

34.8

Theme Built From Details

Main ideaA theme is an idea about life that a story develops; you find it by tracing how specific details keep pointing the same way.

Ask a class what "The Story of an Hour" is about and someone will say "a woman whose husband dies." That is the topic. A is different. A theme is what the story says about its topic, an idea about life that could be true outside the story too. One theme of Chopin’s story might be that even a loving marriage can feel like a loss of self. Notice that a theme is a full sentence, not a single word. "Freedom" is a topic. "Freedom can be more frightening than grief" is a theme.

You do not find a theme by guessing. You find it by watching details pile up in one direction. In Chopin’s story, the window is open. The trees are full of new spring life. The sky shows blue. Louise’s pulse beats fast and warms her body. Every detail in that room points toward life beginning, not ending. Then the story ends with a death that the doctors call joy. Put the pattern of details next to that ending, and a theme starts to take shape: the life Louise glimpsed was real, and losing it is what killed her.

Details also a theme, which means they make it sharper and more specific. Early in the story you might think the theme is simply that Louise wanted to be free. Then Chopin adds that her husband was kind, that his face had only ever looked on her with love. That detail refines the idea. The story is not about escaping a cruel man. It is about how even kindness, when it comes with the power to bend another person’s will, can be a kind of prison. The added detail makes the theme both truer and harder.

Here is a model written for this lesson. "Every morning Ana’s father drove her to school, past the bus stop where her friends stood laughing. He said the bus was not safe. He said it the way he said everything: gently, finally." One detail, gently, finally, refines the whole picture. The father is loving, and the love has a lock on it. When you write about theme, trace the details in order and show how each one adjusts the idea.

34.9

Order and Pacing

Main ideaWriters control the order in which events are told and how fast they move, and both choices shape what the reader feels.

Chopin’s story covers one hour, but the story does not treat every minute the same. The news arrives in a few sentences. The husband’s return takes a paragraph. The stretch in between, Louise alone at the window, takes up most of the story’s length. Time stretches when the writer slows down to look closely, and it snaps shut when the writer hurries. This control of speed is called , and it is one of the most powerful tools a storyteller has.

Order is the other tool. Most stories move in , from first event to last. But a writer can start in the middle and go back, a move called , or hint at what is coming, called . Poe’s narrator in "The Tell-Tale Heart" tells his story after the crime, so we know from the first line that something terrible happened, and we read to find out how. The first sentence of Chopin’s story foreshadows the last one: a weak heart, mentioned before anything else, is the fact the ending will turn on.

Here is a model written for this lesson, two versions of the same moment. Slow: "The envelope was thin. She turned it over. Her name was spelled right, which was rare. She slid a finger under the flap and stopped, and looked at the ceiling, and then she opened it." Fast: "She tore it open. Rejected." The slow version makes the reader wait with her, and the waiting is the emotion. The fast version is a slap. Neither is better; they do different jobs, and a good writer picks the speed that fits the moment.

Parallel Plots and Pattern

Main ideaSome stories run two lines of action side by side, or repeat a pattern, so that one part comments on another.

Stephen Crane’s "The Open Boat," published in 1897, puts four men in a tiny lifeboat after a shipwreck: a captain, a cook, an oiler, and a correspondent. For most of the story they row toward a shore they can see but cannot reach. Crane builds the story out of repetition. The men row, they trade places, they see the shore, they get no closer. The same phrases return. The repetition is the point: the sea does not care about them, and the story’s , its shape, makes the reader feel that indifference in their own tired arms.

Crane based the story on something that happened to him. In January 1897 the ship he was on sank off the coast of Florida. He spent about thirty hours in a small boat before reaching land. Knowing that does not tell you what the story means, but it explains why the details feel so exact: the way a wave looks from below, the way an oar blisters the hands. Close reading of structure asks what the repeated parts have in common and what changes, if anything, from one repetition to the next.

A second structural choice is the , two story lines that run beside each other and reflect one another. Here is a model written for this lesson. "On the third floor, Mr. Reyes graded essays until the building went dark. Usually the answer is contrast, or an echo: the same problem faced by two people, or the same night lived two ways. When you meet repetition, ask what it makes you feel. In Crane’s boat the repetition is exhaustion and dread. In a comedy the same device might be a running joke. Structure does not have one meaning; it has whatever effect the writer built it to have.

Claim and Evidence

Main ideaA literary analysis states a claim that could be argued, then supports it with specific evidence from the text.

Here is a sentence that is not a claim: "The Story of an Hour is about a woman whose husband dies." No one could argue with it, so there is nothing to prove. Here is a sentence that is a : "Chopin makes the reader believe that Louise’s death is a loss of freedom, not a shock of joy, even though the doctors say otherwise." Someone could disagree with that. It takes a position on how the story works. A good analysis begins with a sentence like the second one. Every paragraph after it exists to earn it.

The claim must be about the text, not about your feelings. "I liked the ending" is not a claim. "The ending works because the reader knows more than the doctors do" is. Notice that the second sentence names a technique, the gap between what the reader and the doctors know, and links it to an effect. A strong claim often has that shape: the writer does X, which makes the reader Y.

Evidence is what earns the claim. It means specific words and moments from the story, quoted or precisely described. For the claim about Louise, the evidence might include the open window and the spring outside it, the whispered word free, Louise’s prayer for a long life, and the fact that the doctors never saw her at the window. Each piece of evidence should be followed by , a sentence or two that explains how this evidence supports the claim. Evidence without reasoning is just a list of quotes.

Try building one paragraph. Start with the claim. Give one piece of evidence, quoted or described exactly. Explain what that evidence shows. Then a second piece, and its explanation. End by saying what the reader now understands. Here is the skeleton written for this lesson: "Chopin sets Louise’s discovery in spring. The window is open, the trees are full of new life, and the sky shows blue. These details surround the word free with images of things beginning. When Louise dies an hour later, the reader has been taught to read her death as a life cut off, not a joy too great to bear."

The Line Everyone Gets Wrong

A poem about a fork in the woods gets printed on posters and read at graduations, and almost nobody notices what it actually says.

In 1916 Robert Frost published a poem that opens with a walker standing where a path splits in two in a yellow autumn wood. He looks down one path as far as he can. He takes the other. The poem's last lines say that he will tell this story someday with a sigh: he took the road less traveled by, and that has made all the difference. You have probably seen those last lines on a coffee mug, a yearbook page or a graduation speech. They sound like a lesson about being brave and different.

Now go back and read the middle of the poem, the part the mugs leave out. The walker says the second road was grassy and wanted wear. Then, in the very next line, he takes it back: as for that, the passing there had worn them really about the same. The stanza after that says both roads lay equally covered in leaves that no step had turned black. In plain words, the two roads were the same. Neither was less traveled. The speaker chose one for no reason he can name.

So why does he say, at the end, that he took the road less traveled? Look at the tense. He is not saying it now. He says he shall be telling this someday, ages and ages hence, with a sigh. He is predicting that his older self will turn a coin flip into a story about courage. Frost said he wrote the poem about a friend who could never decide which path to take on their walks and always sighed afterward about the one he had missed. The poem is a gentle joke about the stories we tell about our own lives.

Frost knew the poem was slippery. Audiences kept hearing it as a hymn to independence, and he once warned that he had to be careful with it. That is the strange thing about a poem. Every word is in plain sight, and readers still see what they expect. The only defense is to read slowly, line by line, and let the poem say what it says instead of what the poster says.

This chapter is about reading that slowly. You will look at how lines are built, how they sound, what pictures they make, who is speaking, where the tone turns, and how the shape of a poem carries its meaning. By the end you will be able to read a poem like the one about the two roads and notice the line everyone else skips.

Lines and Stanzas

Main ideaA poem is built from lines and stanzas, and where a line breaks is a choice that changes how the words are read.

Read these two versions of the same words, written for this lesson. Version one: "My brother left the porch light on for me every night for a year, and then he stopped." Version two: "My brother left the porch light on / for me / every night for a year / and then / he stopped." The words are identical. But in version two your eye pauses at the end of each , and the pauses do something. "For me" sits alone. "And then" hangs in the air before the last two words fall. The slashes mark line breaks, and the breaks are the first thing that makes a poem a poem.

Prose fills the page from margin to margin, and the writer does not control where a line ends. Poetry does. A poet decides where every line stops, and the end of a line is a small silence. Words at the end of a line get extra weight because the reader lands on them. When a sentence spills over the end of one line into the next, it is called , and it pulls the reader forward. When a line ends where the sentence ends, it is , and it feels settled, like a door closing.

Lines are grouped into , which are the poem’s paragraphs. A stanza of two lines is a couplet, of four lines a quatrain. Frost’s poem about the two roads has four stanzas of five lines each. When you meet a new poem, count first. How many lines? How many stanzas? Are the lines long or short, even or ragged? Where does the poet break a sentence across a line, and which word ends up alone at the edge? These are not trivia questions. A poem’s shape is its first meaning, and you can see it before you understand a single word.

Main ideaMeter is a poem's pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables, and the most common English pattern is the ten-syllable line called iambic pentameter.

Say your own name out loud. One syllable is louder than the others: MAR-cus, ta-SHA, jo-SE-fina. Tap it out: shall-I com-PARE thee-TO a-SUM mer’s-DAY. Soft, loud, soft, loud, five times. That soft-loud pair is called an , and five of them in a row make , the line Shakespeare used in his sonnets and most of his plays.

Iambic pentameter is close to the rhythm of ordinary English speech, which is why it can sound natural even when it is strict. Listen to a sentence you might say: "I left my keys inside the other car." Tap it: i-LEFT my-KEYS in-SIDE the-OTH er-CAR. You just spoke a line of iambic pentameter without trying. When a poem has kept a steady beat for ten lines and suddenly stumbles, the poet almost always did it on purpose, and the stumble is pointing at something. Meter is not a test to pass. It is a rhythm to feel, and then a place to look for the moment the rhythm changes.

Main ideaPoets repeat sounds on purpose, through rhyme, alliteration and assonance, so that the ear connects words the mind might not.

Read this line written for this lesson: "The bus was late, and the light was low, and the boys at the back were bored." Say it aloud. Hear the b sounds bumping along: bus, back, boys, bored. That repeated first sound is . Now hear the long o inside light, low and boys, and the long a in late. Repeated vowel sounds inside words are . None of these are accidents. A poet, or any careful writer, uses sound to tie words together and to give a line a texture you can feel in your mouth.

The best-known sound device is , matching the ending sounds of words, usually at the ends of lines. Frost’s stanzas about the two roads rhyme in a pattern: the first, third and fourth lines share one sound, the second and fifth another. It does not. It means concrete, physical detail that lets the reader’s body understand what the speaker’s body felt.

Look at the opening of Frost’s poem about the two roads: a yellow wood. Not a forest, not autumn, just the color. It puts you in a specific light. Then ask what feeling those images add up to, and notice that the poem probably never named the feeling directly. When you write about imagery, do not just list the images. Figurative Language

Main ideaMetaphor, simile and personification say one thing is another, or is like another, so that the reader understands both differently.

Robert Burns wrote in 1794 that his love was like a red, red rose newly sprung in June. He did not mean she had thorns. He meant that something about her was fresh, bright and new, and he trusted the reader to carry those qualities from the rose to the woman. That comparison, using like or as, is a . Take away the like and say the thing directly, "my love is a rose," and it becomes a . Both are : words that mean something other than their plain, literal sense.

Dickinson’s hope is a metaphor. She does not say hope is like a bird; she says it is the thing with feathers. Shakespeare’s most famous sonnet opens by asking whether he should compare someone to a summer’s day, and then spends the poem finding all the ways the person is better than summer. In his sonnet that begins by saying his mistress’s eyes are nothing like the sun, he mocks the whole habit of comparison. He lists all the flattering similes other poets use and denies each one, then says at the end that his love is as rare as any woman who has been praised with false comparisons.

The Speaker Is Not the Poet

Main ideaThe voice in a poem is a speaker the poet created, and the two may agree, disagree or be worlds apart.

Read this poem written for this lesson: "I have been driving this truck for thirty years / and I have never once looked in the mirror / to see what I left behind." A student reading it might say that the poet has been a truck driver for thirty years. But the poet might be nineteen. The poet might take the bus. The I in a poem is the , a voice the poet builds the way a fiction writer builds a narrator. Sometimes the speaker is very close to the poet. Sometimes the speaker is a character the poet would never want to be.

Robert Browning’s poem "My Last Duchess," from 1842, is spoken by a duke showing a visitor a painting of his dead wife. He complains that she smiled at everyone, not only at him, and he hints, calmly, that he gave orders and the smiles stopped. Browning was not a murderer. He built a speaker who reveals himself as one without meaning to, and the reader’s horror comes from hearing the duke’s polished voice say what he does not know he is saying.

Even when the speaker seems to be the poet, keep the distinction. Whitman’s "Song of Myself" begins by celebrating himself and says that every atom belonging to him belongs to the reader too. That I is enormous, containing crowds and continents. Whitman the man lived in Brooklyn and worked as a printer and nurse. The I of the poem is a made thing, and treating it as the poet’s diary shrinks it. Dunbar’s mask poem says we, and the we is African Americans facing a country that demanded a smile. 35.7

Hearing the Tone

Main ideaTone is the speaker's attitude toward the subject, and you hear it in word choice, rhythm and what is left unsaid.

Your friend texts "great" after you tell her the plan. Is she pleased, or is she rolling her eyes? You cannot tell from the one word. You need the context, the timing, whether she used a period. In a poem, that attitude is the , and finding it is one of the hardest and most important parts of reading. Tone is not what the poem is about. It is how the speaker feels about what it is about: tender, bitter, amused, grieving, mocking, calm.

You hear tone in diction first. Read this pair written for this lesson. "My father’s chair still sits beside the stove." And: "My father’s chair is still taking up space by the stove." Same chair, same stove. The first is quiet and sad; still sits is gentle. The second is irritated; taking up space treats the chair as an obstacle, and maybe the father too. The tone lives in three words. Change them and the whole poem changes.

Tone also lives in rhythm and in restraint. Dickinson’s carriage poem describes Death, a grave and eternity, in a steady hymn rhythm with almost no drama. The calmness is the tone, and it is unsettling precisely because the subject is not calm. Frost’s poem about fire and ice, from 1920, takes the end of the world and handles it with a shrug: some say fire, some say ice, and the speaker allows that either would do. The offhand tone about the largest possible subject is the joke and the chill at once.

To name a tone, avoid the vague words first: happy, sad, serious. Push for something exact: wistful, defiant, weary, tender, sardonic. Then prove it. Point to the two or three words that carry the attitude, and to the rhythm, and to anything the speaker refuses to say. Two readers may hear different tones in the same lines. That is fine, as long as each can point to the words that made them hear it.

35.8

The Turn

Main ideaMany poems pivot at a certain line, changing tone, direction or meaning, and finding that turn is the key to reading the poem.

Shakespeare’s sonnet about the summer’s day spends eight lines on what is wrong with summer: it is too short, too hot, too cloudy, and it fades. Then line nine begins with the word but. From there the poem changes direction. The beloved’s summer will not fade, because the poem itself will keep it alive as long as people can read. That pivot is the , sometimes called by its Italian name, the . Nearly every sonnet has one, and learning to spot it is like finding the hinge of a door.

A sonnet is fourteen lines of iambic pentameter. The kind Shakespeare wrote has three quatrains and a final couplet, and the couplet often delivers the turn or a last twist after it. In the sonnet that mocks flattering comparisons, twelve lines deny that the mistress is like the sun, coral, snow, roses or music. Then the couplet turns: and yet, the speaker says, his love is as rare as any woman ever praised with false comparisons. The insult was a setup. The turn reveals the real tenderness underneath.

Turns are not only for sonnets. Dunbar’s mask poem describes the grin and the lie for a stanza, then turns to a cry: why should the world be wise about our pain? Frost’s two roads poem turns at the last stanza, when the speaker leaps into the future and starts telling the story his older self will tell. Here is a model written for this lesson: "I said I did not need the ride. / I said it twice, and meant it once. / You drove off slow enough to let me change my mind." The turn is the last line, where the speaker’s stubbornness meets someone else’s patience.

To find the turn, look for signal words: but, yet, and yet, still, then, now. Look for a change in tense, from past to future, or in address, from talking about someone to talking to them. Look for the place where the tone shifts, where a mocking voice softens or a calm voice cracks. Then ask what the poem means after the turn that it did not mean before. Often the whole poem was built to reach that line.

35.9

Structure Makes Meaning

Main ideaThe shape of a poem, its stanzas, repetitions and ending, is not a container for the meaning; it is part of the meaning.

Go back to Frost’s poem about the two roads and look at where things fall. Stanza one: the fork. Stanza two: the choice, and the admission that the roads were about the same. Stanza three: both roads lay equal that morning, and the speaker doubts he will ever come back. Stanza four: the future story, told with a sigh. The poem’s walks the reader from an honest present into a dishonest future, one stanza at a time. The four identical stanzas march the reader forward while the speaker, in the last one, starts rewriting the walk.

Frost’s snowy woods poem does something similar with its ending. Three stanzas describe stopping, the horse, the quiet snow. The last stanza says the woods are lovely, dark and deep, but the speaker has promises to keep and miles to go before he sleeps. Then it says the last line again. Word for word. The of that line is the whole poem’s weight. The first time, it is about a journey. The second time, because it is repeated and the poem stops there, sleep starts to sound like something larger, and the speaker seems to be talking himself out of staying in the dark.

Structure includes what a poet does with length. A short poem that ends abruptly can feel like a door slammed. A poem that circles back to its opening image can feel like a life closing. Here is a model written for this lesson: "Monday, the phone. / Tuesday, the phone. / Wednesday, the phone, and I let it ring. / Thursday, nothing." Four lines, one per day, and the meaning is entirely in the pattern: repetition, a small act of refusal, then silence, which is worse.

When you write about structure, describe the shape before you interpret it. How many parts? What does each part do? Where does the poem repeat, and where does it break the pattern? Then say what the shape makes the reader feel that the words alone would not. Frost could have written a paragraph saying that people invent brave stories about arbitrary choices. Instead he built four stanzas that let the reader watch it happen.

From Paraphrase to Paragraph

35.10

Paraphrase First

Main ideaBefore you analyze a poem, put it into plain prose in your own words so that you know what it literally says.

Students often jump from reading a poem to writing about its deep meaning in a single step. The writing wobbles, because the first step was skipped. The first step is : restating the poem, line by line or stanza by stanza, in your own ordinary sentences, with nothing added. A paraphrase of Frost’s last stanza might be: "Years from now, I will tell people, with a sigh, that when the road split I took the one fewer people used, and that this changed everything." That is all the stanza says. The analysis comes after.

Paraphrasing forces you to solve the poem’s puzzles instead of stepping around them. What does thou ow’st mean in Shakespeare’s line about eternal summer? You have to find out that ow’st means own or possess before you can paraphrase it: your beauty will not lose what it has. Old words, inverted sentences and dropped subjects all get untangled when you have to say the line plainly. If you cannot paraphrase a line, you do not yet know what it says, and you are not ready to say what it means.

Here is a stanza written for this lesson, followed by a paraphrase. Poem: "My mother kept the receipt for everything, / a shoebox of proof that we had paid, / as if the world might someday ask." Paraphrase: "The speaker’s mother saved every receipt in a shoebox, as evidence that the family had paid for things, because she seemed to fear that someone might one day demand proof." Notice the paraphrase is longer than the poem. That is normal. Poems compress; prose unpacks.

A paraphrase is not an analysis, and you should not hand it in as one. But you should write it, keep it, and read it next to the poem. The gap between the two is where the poem’s art is. The paraphrase says the mother saved receipts. The poem says a shoebox of proof and as if the world might someday ask. Everything in that gap, the box, the fear, the tone, is what your paragraph will be about.

Main ideaA strong paragraph about a poem makes one claim about how the poem works, quotes the exact words that show it, and explains the effect.

A paragraph about a poem has the same bones as a paragraph about a story. Claim, evidence, reasoning. The difference is that a poem’s evidence is smaller and denser. A single word, a line break, a repeated sound can carry the argument. Here is a claim written for this lesson: "In Frost’s poem about the two roads, the last stanza does not celebrate a brave choice; it shows the speaker preparing to tell a flattering story about a choice that made no difference." That claim could be argued against, which is what makes it a claim.

Now the evidence. Quote the poem’s own admission that the roads had been worn really about the same, and point out that this line comes before the famous ending. Quote the future tense, I shall be telling this, and the phrase ages and ages hence. Quote the sigh. Each piece of evidence gets a sentence of reasoning: the sigh could be regret or satisfaction, and the poem refuses to say which; the future tense means the speaker is predicting a story, not reporting a fact. Keep quotations short and set them inside your own sentences.

Here is a full model paragraph written for this lesson about the receipt poem. "The speaker’s mother is not simply organized; she is afraid. The poem calls her shoebox ’proof that we had paid,’ and proof is a word for courtrooms, not kitchens. The last line, ’as if the world might someday ask,’ turns the world into an accuser waiting at the door. The line break before ’as if’ leaves the receipts hanging for a moment, and then the fear arrives. By the end, a box of paper has become a defense against a world the mother does not trust." Claim, three pieces of evidence, reasoning for each, and a closing sentence in the writer’s own words.

Before you hand in a paragraph about a poem, check four things. Does the first sentence make a claim someone could dispute? Does every quotation stay under a line and sit inside your sentence? Does each quotation get at least one sentence explaining its effect? Does the paragraph say speaker and not poet? If all four are yes, you have written literary analysis, and you have done it with a form most people are afraid of.

Two Speeches and a Crowd

In the middle of Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, two men speak over the same dead body, and the second one turns a city upside down.

Antony climbs up with Caesar's body. He says he has come to bury Caesar, not to praise him. He says Brutus has called Caesar ambitious, and that Brutus is an honorable man. Three times Caesar refused a crown. Was that ambitious? Yet Brutus says so, and Brutus is an honorable man. Each time Antony says the word, it means a little less.

Antony never accuses anyone. He just keeps asking questions and repeating Brutus's word until the crowd begins to answer the questions themselves. He shows them Caesar's cloak with the holes the knives made. He shows them the body. He mentions there is a will, and then says he had better not read it, because it would inflame them. They beg him to read it. It leaves every citizen money and Caesar's gardens to the public. By the time Antony stops talking, the crowd is screaming for the killers, and it pours out of the square to burn their houses.

Read the two speeches on the page, and you can see the machinery: Brutus in plain prose, Antony in rolling verse; Brutus explaining, Antony asking; one word said again and again until it turns inside out. That is what a play does that a story cannot. It puts the words in bodies and puts you in the room.

36.1

Acts and Scenes

Main ideaA play is divided into acts and scenes, and the divisions mark changes in time, place and pressure that a reader should track.

Open a Shakespeare play and the first thing you see is Act 1, Scene 1. A is a stretch of continuous action in one place. When the location changes or time jumps, the scene ends. An is a group of scenes that together form one large movement of the story. Shakespeare’s plays as we print them have five acts. Romeo and Juliet, Julius Caesar and Macbeth all have five, and in all three the killing that changes everything happens in the middle, in Act 2 or Act 3.

The five-act shape has a logic. Act 1 sets the situation and the problem. Act 2 raises the stakes. Act 3 holds the turning point, the act that cannot be undone. Act 4 shows the consequences spreading. Act 5 brings the ending, a death or a wedding or a new king. Not every play follows this exactly, but knowing the shape helps a reader stay oriented. If you are in Act 3, expect the knife. If you are in Act 4, ask what is falling apart.

Here is the opening of a scene written for this lesson. "Act 2, Scene 3. A school hallway, after the last bell. Lockers slam offstage. DEVON stands alone at his locker, holding a folded note he has not opened. MS. ORTIZ enters, keys in hand." In four lines you know the time, the place, who is there and what is at stake. A scene heading and its first stage directions are the play’s way of telling you what a novel would spend a page describing. Read them slowly. They are not skippable.

Keep a simple scene map as you read. For each scene, write the act and scene number and the place. Add who is there. Then write one sentence for what changes. In Julius Caesar, Act 3 Scene 1 is the Senate and the murder; Act 3 Scene 2 is the square and the two speeches. Seeing them side by side on your map shows you what the playwright did: he put the killing and its consequences right next to each other, in the same act, so the audience feels the crowd turn while Caesar’s blood is still fresh.

Main ideaA play has no narrator; everything a reader learns comes from what characters say and from the stage directions that tell bodies what to do.

Everything else, every feeling, every motive, every lie, has to be figured out from those two things. That is why reading a play is harder than reading a story and also why it is more like real life. Nobody narrates your day either.

Shakespeare’s stage directions are famously thin. The most famous one in all his plays, from The Winter’s Tale, reads simply: Exit, pursued by a bear. Most of his directions are just Enter and Exit and a name. That means the dialogue itself has to carry information a modern script would put in directions. When Romeo says that it is the east and Juliet is the sun, the line tells the actor where to look and the audience that a light has appeared above. The words do the staging.

Read this exchange written for this lesson. "MS. ORTIZ: You are still here. DEVON: I am waiting for my ride. MS. ORTIZ: Your mother called the office an hour ago. But the reader learns that Devon is lying about the ride, that his mother is worried, that the note matters, and that Ms. Ortiz has decided not to push. The single stage direction, putting the note away, says as much as a paragraph.

When you read dialogue, ask three questions of every line. What does this character want from the person they are speaking to? What are they not saying? And what does the other person’s reply show they heard? Read stage directions as events, not as formatting. An exit in the middle of a scene is a decision. A character who enters with a knife has changed the room. In a play, the action is always somebody doing something, and the reader has to see it.

Soliloquy and Aside

Main ideaIn a soliloquy a character alone on stage speaks their real thoughts; in an aside a character speaks to the audience without other characters hearing.

Macbeth is alone. He is about to go murder the king who is sleeping as his guest, and he sees a dagger floating in the air in front of him, handle toward his hand. He asks whether it is real. He tries to grab it and cannot. He is talking to no one, and so he is telling the truth. This is a : a speech by a character alone on stage, saying what they really think. Since a play has no narrator, the soliloquy is the playwright’s way of letting the audience inside a mind.

The convention is that a soliloquy is always honest. Characters lie to each other constantly in Shakespeare, but they do not lie to the audience when they are alone. That is why Macbeth’s dagger speech matters. To everyone else he is a loyal host. To us, in the empty room, he is a man watching his own hand reach for a murder. Later, after his wife dies, he is alone again and says that tomorrow creeps in this petty pace from day to day until the last syllable of recorded time. The soliloquy shows a man for whom life has stopped meaning anything.

An is shorter and sneakier. Other characters are on stage, but one character turns and says something the others are understood not to hear. Early in Macbeth, the king announces that his son will be his heir, and Macbeth, in an aside, says that this is a step he must either fall down at or leap over. The other characters keep celebrating. The audience alone has heard the ambition. Here is a model written for this lesson: "COACH: Devon, you have been a leader for this team. DEVON (aside): He has not looked at me once all season. DEVON: Thank you, Coach."

When you read a soliloquy, treat it as evidence of the highest quality. It is the one place a character cannot be performing for someone else. When you read an aside, ask what the character is hiding from the people in the room, and why the playwright wants you to know it now. Both devices create a bond between you and the character: you know what the others do not, and you carry that knowledge into every scene that follows.

36.4

Decoding the Language

Main ideaShakespeare's English is hard for four fixable reasons: old pronouns, shifted word meanings, reordered sentences and dense figures of speech.

Juliet stands at her window and asks, wherefore art thou Romeo. Most students hear where are you, Romeo. But wherefore means why, not where. She is asking why he has to be Romeo, a member of the family her family hates. She is not looking for him. Once you know that one word, the speech changes from a girl searching a garden to a girl arguing with fate. Most of Shakespeare’s difficulty is like that: a small number of words and habits that, once learned, unlock whole scenes.

Start with the pronouns. Thou, thee, thy and thine all mean you and your, in the form people used with family, close friends, children and social inferiors. You was the polite or formal form. So when a character switches from you to thou, something has changed: they have grown closer, or they are showing contempt. Verbs that go with thou take an ending: thou art, thou hast, thou dost. Add a few common words that have shifted, such as wherefore for why, soft for wait or hush, and anon for soon, and you have most of the vocabulary problem handled.

The next barrier is word order. Modern English almost always goes subject, verb, object: I saw the ghost. Shakespeare often rearranges for rhythm or emphasis: the ghost I saw, or saw I the ghost. When a line confuses you, find the verb, then ask who is doing it and to what, and rebuild the sentence in modern order. Here is a model written for this lesson: "Of all the friends I had, thee only trust I." Rebuilt: "Of all the friends I had, I trust only you." The meaning was always there; the pieces were just in a different order.

The last barrier is that Shakespeare packs figures of speech tightly. Juliet is the sun; the moon is envious; night’s candles are burnt out. When a line still resists after you have fixed the pronouns and the order, ask what two things are being compared and why. Read with a good edition that glosses hard words at the bottom of the page, read aloud, and give yourself permission to go slowly. Nobody, including your teacher, read Shakespeare fast the first time.

36.5

Character Through Speech

Main ideaSince a play has no narrator, a character's way of speaking, in prose or verse, plain or ornate, is the main evidence of who they are.

Brutus speaks to the crowd in , ordinary sentences with no meter. He asks them to hear him for his cause and to believe him for his honor. His speech is balanced, logical, built on if-then reasoning: if any of you loved Caesar, know that Brutus loved him too. It is the speech of a man who believes people are persuaded by reasons. Antony speaks in , unrhymed iambic pentameter, the heightened line Shakespeare gives to passion and power. Before you know a word of what either says, the form of their speech has told you who they are.

Shakespeare uses this contrast on purpose throughout his plays. Nobles usually speak verse; servants, clowns and characters in low or comic scenes usually speak prose. When a noble character drops into prose, watch closely: they may be pretending to be mad, or joking, or their world may be breaking. When a character rises into verse, they are moved. Brutus choosing prose for the biggest speech of his life is a choice by Shakespeare, and it tells the audience that Brutus has misjudged the moment. He brings an argument to a funeral.

Listen also to what a character repeats. Antony says honorable again and again, and each repetition is a small turn of the screw. Macbeth says tomorrow three times in a row when he has run out of reasons to live. Here is a model written for this lesson. "DEVON: I am fine. I said I am fine. Why does everyone keep asking if I am fine?" The word fine appears three times, and by the third time it means the opposite. A character reveals more by what they cannot stop saying than by anything they announce.

When you analyze a character in a play, gather three kinds of evidence. What form do they speak in, verse or prose, and does it change? What words do they repeat or avoid? And how do they speak differently to different people, formally to a king, tenderly to a wife, in short bursts when afraid? Put those together, and you have a portrait built entirely from speech, which is the only material a playwright has.

36.6

Dramatic Irony

Main ideaDramatic irony is when the audience knows something a character does not, and it turns ordinary lines into knives.

Before Romeo and Juliet begins, a speaker steps out and tells the audience the whole story in fourteen lines. Two families hate each other. Two children of those families fall in love. They die, and their deaths end the feud. Then the play starts. Why would a playwright give away the ending first? Because from that moment on, every hopeful line the lovers say lands differently. When Juliet plans a future, we know she has none. That gap between what the audience knows and what the character knows is .

The device works hardest at the end of the play. Juliet has taken a potion that makes her seem dead so she can escape her family. Romeo never gets the message. He arrives at her tomb, sees her still and pale, and believes she is gone. The audience knows she will wake in minutes. Every line Romeo speaks over her body is unbearable, not because of what he says but because of what we know and he does not. When she wakes and finds him dead, the irony completes its circle.

Dramatic irony is different from the kind of irony people usually mean. is when a speaker says one thing and means another, as Antony does when he calls Brutus honorable. is when events turn out the opposite of what was expected, as when Brutus lets Antony speak in order to seem fair and loses everything by it. Dramatic irony belongs to the audience: we hold the knowledge. In Macbeth, King Duncan arrives at Macbeth’s castle and remarks on how pleasant the air is. We have just heard the plan to kill him there.

Here is a model written for this lesson. Earlier in the play, the audience saw Devon’s mother put a letter from a hospital on the kitchen table. Now Devon comes home and says, "Nothing ever happens in this house." The line is flat on its own. With what the audience knows, it hurts. When you read a play, keep a list of what you know that each character does not. Then reread their lines with that list in mind. That is where the emotion of drama lives: not in what is said, but in the distance between what is said and what is true.

The Shape of Tragedy

Main ideaA tragedy follows a person of standing whose own choice, driven by a flaw, leads to a downfall the audience sees coming and cannot stop.

Macbeth is a war hero at the start of his play. The king loves him. Three witches tell him he will be king himself. Within an act he has murdered that king in his sleep. By the end he has killed his friend, ordered the slaughter of a family, and lost his wife and his mind, and he dies fighting in a castle full of people who hate him. He was not destroyed by bad luck. He was destroyed by a choice, and the choice came from something in him, a hunger for the crown that the witches only named. That is the shape of a .

The pattern is old. Ancient Greek tragedies followed it, and the philosopher Aristotle described it: a person of high standing, a or error of judgment, a reversal of fortune, and a moment of recognition when the character understands what they have done. The fall must come from the character’s own action, not from an accident, or it is only sad, not tragic. And the audience should feel both pity for the character and fear that the same thing could happen to them.

Macbeth’s flaw is ambition. Brutus’s flaw is a kind of honor so pure it cannot imagine that other people are less honest than he is. Romeo’s is haste; he falls in love, marries, kills and dies within days, and every disaster comes from moving too fast. Notice that none of these flaws is simply evil. Ambition, honor and passion are qualities we admire. Tragedy shows what happens when an admirable quality has no brake, and that is why it frightens us. We have these qualities too.

Here is a small tragedy written for this lesson. "Marisol was the fastest runner on the team and knew it. In the last race of the season she went out too hard on purpose, to break the other girl early. At the final turn her legs went, and she watched two runners pass her. Afterward she sat on the track and understood, for the first time, that being the fastest and being the smartest were different things." Standing, flaw, choice, fall, recognition. The whole shape fits in a paragraph.

36.8

The Point of No Return

Main ideaEvery tragedy has a moment after which nothing can be fixed, and reading closely around that moment shows what the play is about.

In Romeo and Juliet, the point of no return is not the balcony and not the wedding. It is Act 3, Scene 1, on a hot afternoon in the street. Tybalt, Juliet’s cousin, wants to fight Romeo. Romeo, now secretly married to Juliet, refuses and tries to make peace. His friend Mercutio, disgusted, fights Tybalt instead, and Tybalt kills him. Dying, Mercutio curses both families. Romeo, in a rage, kills Tybalt. In ten minutes of stage time, the play goes from comedy to tragedy, and it cannot go back.

Watch what Shakespeare does with the scene. Romeo’s attempt at peace is the very thing that gets Mercutio killed, since Romeo steps between the fighters and Tybalt strikes under his arm. Romeo’s love, the best thing in the play, causes the death. Then his grief causes another. The scene is built so that every good impulse produces a disaster. When you find a play’s , read the scene around it as closely as you read a poem, because the playwright has usually packed the whole meaning of the play into it.

In Julius Caesar the point of no return is the assassination, but the scene the play really turns on is the one after it, when Brutus lets Antony speak. Caesar’s death could have led to a republic. Brutus’s fairness gives Antony the platform to turn it into a war. In Macbeth, the killing of Duncan is the act that cannot be undone, and Shakespeare puts it offstage; we see Macbeth come back with bloody hands and hear him say he could not say amen when he tried. The horror is in his face, not in the deed.

When you write about a tragedy, find its hinge and build your analysis there. Ask what the character wanted at that moment, what they chose, and what the choice revealed. Here is a model written for this lesson. "Marisol’s point of no return was the first hundred meters. She could have run her own race. She chose to run against the other girl instead, and that choice showed that what she wanted was not to win but to make someone else lose." Find the hinge, and the character opens.

36.9

Performance Choices

Main ideaA script leaves most decisions open, and every production makes choices about voice, movement, pause and setting that change the scene's meaning.

Take one line from Antony’s speech: and Brutus is an honorable man. On the page it is seven words. An actor has to decide how to say it. Straight, as if he means it? With a tiny pause before honorable? With a glance at the body? Louder each time or softer? Every one of those is a , and every one changes what the crowd hears and what the audience understands. The script is not the play. The script is a set of instructions for making a play, and the instructions leave most things open.

Directors and actors make choices at every level. Where is the scene set: ancient Rome in togas, or a modern city in suits? What is the pace: does Antony rush, or let silences do the work? Where do people stand: is Antony above the crowd on a platform, or down among them? What does the crowd do while he talks: stand silent, mutter, begin to move? None of these are in Shakespeare’s text. All of them decide whether the audience sees a cynical manipulator, a grieving friend, or both.

Here is a model written for this lesson. Two actors play Devon’s line, "Then I am waiting for something else." The first says it fast, looking at the floor, and pockets the note as if hiding it. Devon is scared. The second says it slowly, looking straight at Ms. Ortiz, and folds the note carefully into his pocket. Devon is daring her to ask. Same words, same stage direction, two different characters. A reader who only reads the script has to imagine both and decide which the words support better.

This is why reading a play is a creative act, not just a receiving one. As you read, hear the line several ways. Ask which delivery the surrounding lines support. Ask what the character wants, and how a body wanting that would stand and move. When you watch a production afterward, notice which choices the company made and which you would have made differently. Neither of you is wrong. The script allows both, and the disagreement is where the thinking happens.

36.10

A Scene on Its Feet

Main ideaStanding up and playing a scene reveals things about it that silent reading hides: who has power, where the bodies go, and what the silences hold.

Read the fight in Romeo and Juliet silently and it takes two minutes. Get four students up, hand them rolled paper for swords, and walk through it, and you discover things. Where does Romeo stand when he steps between them? How does Tybalt reach Mercutio under Romeo’s arm? Is Mercutio joking when he calls his wound a scratch, and when do the others realize he is not? Questions that never occurred to you on the page become urgent when there are bodies in the space. This is what theater people mean by getting a scene on its feet.

Playing a scene shows you power. On the page, two characters take turns speaking, and they look equal. In the room, one of them is standing and one is sitting; one moves toward the other and the other steps back; one keeps talking and the other cannot get a word in. , the planned movement of actors on stage, is a language of its own. When Antony brings Caesar’s body down among the crowd, he is not just showing it; he is putting the dead man at their feet, and the crowd’s bodies bend toward it.

Playing a scene also shows you silence. A script prints the words, but the pauses between them are where much of the meaning lives. Here is a model written for this lesson. "MS. ORTIZ: Your mother called the office an hour ago. (DEVON does not answer. He looks at the note. He looks at the door. MS. ORTIZ waits.) DEVON: Then I am waiting for something else." On the page the pause is a line of directions. In the room it is eight seconds of two people not speaking, and the audience holds its breath through all of them.

You do not need a stage. Push the desks back. Read the lines from the script, and give each actor one thing to want. Then run it again with a different choice, and talk about what changed. Even a scene you thought you understood will show you something new the moment someone has to decide where to look. Drama was made to be done, and doing it is a form of close reading that uses your whole body.

36.11

Comparing Page and Stage

Main ideaComparing a scene as written with a scene as performed shows which meanings are in the text and which a production added, cut or changed.

You have read Antony’s speech. Now you watch a production of it. Antony, in this staging, is calm and quiet, almost gentle, and only at the very end does his voice crack. The crowd turns slowly, one citizen at a time. Afterward you go back to the text and find that the script never says calm, never says quiet, never says one at a time. The production added all of that. Is it wrong? No. It is an , and your job is to compare it to the text and judge how well the words support it.

Comparing page and stage means asking three things. What did the production keep? What did it cut or change? And what did it add? Productions cut lines constantly; a full Shakespeare play can run four hours. They add music, costumes, a set, a period. A Julius Caesar in modern dress, with the crowd holding phones, is saying something about the play’s meaning today. A Romeo and Juliet with the families divided by race or religion is making an argument about what the feud is. Every addition is a claim about the text, and you can evaluate it like any other claim.

Here is a model written for this lesson. In the script, Devon’s scene ends with Ms. Ortiz saying, "I will wait with you," and sitting down. In the production, the actor playing Ms. Ortiz says the line, then takes out her own phone and quietly texts someone. The text does not say that. What does the addition suggest? That she is calling his mother, or her own family to say she will be late, or someone at the hospital. The production has made a choice the script left open, and a viewer can argue about whether the choice deepens the scene or explains too much.

When you write a comparison, do not just list differences. Choose one significant choice the production made and argue whether the text supports it. Quote the lines. Describe the staging exactly. Then say what the choice reveals about the scene, or what it hides. This is the same close reading you have practiced all unit, with one more layer: now the text has been read by other people, out loud, with their bodies, and you are reading their reading.
