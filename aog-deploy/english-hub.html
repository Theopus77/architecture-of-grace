Sounds and Letters
A letter has a name and a sound, and they are not the same thing. Stretch a word and hear its first, middle and last sound; push three sounds together and a word comes out. The five short vowels, the three pairs that make one sound, and why no English word gets by without a vowel.
Open
Words You Know by Sight
Sounding out works on most words, and then you meet said, was, of and one. The surprise is that they are not all strange: in said only the middle is odd. Find the one part that breaks a rule and the rest of the word behaves.
Open
A Sentence Is a Whole Thought
A sentence is not any group of words and it is not a long one — it is a whole thought, with somebody in it and something happening. Four words can be a piece; two words can be a sentence. Count the parts, not the words.
Open
Who, Where, What Happens
Every story is holding three things: somebody it is about, a place and time it happens in, and the things that change. Name those three and you can retell anything — and tell a real event from a detail that is just sitting there.
Open
Beginning, Middle, End
A story sets you up, then something goes wrong, then somebody does something about it. The end is not where the story stops — it is where the trouble is settled. The middle is the biggest part, and the part most retellings skip.
Open
True Books and Story Books
Some books tell a story; some tell you what is true. They are not read the same way — a story front to back, a true book opened anywhere. Headings, labels, captions and a contents page, and how to take one fact out without reading it all.

Word Parts
A long word is not one thing — it is a root with pieces bolted on. Find the root first, then read the front for a prefix and the end for a suffix. Un-, re-, dis-, pre-, and the suffixes that change a word’s job without changing what it means.
Open
Context Clues
Writers know which words are hard, and they leave help nearby on purpose. Five kinds of clue and the signal words that announce each one — including the contrast clue you have to read backwards, and the substitute-and-check step that separates a guess from a wild guess.
Open
Main Idea and Details
The topic is a word or two and cannot be true or false. The main idea is what the paragraph SAYS about it, and it takes a whole sentence. Everything else holds it up. Where the main idea hides, how to build one that is never stated, and why both wrong answers are usually true.
Open
Story Elements and Theme
Character, setting, problem, resolution — and the theme a story never states out loud. Build it from what the character wanted, what stood in the way, and what changed. A theme is a full sentence about people in general, and there is no name in it.
Open
How a Text Is Built
Nobody writes facts down in the order they thought of them. Five shapes — sequence, cause and effect, compare and contrast, problem and solution, description — the signal words that announce each, and why the same three facts arranged two ways leave a reader with two different conclusions.
Open
The Paragraph: a Claim and Reasons
Five rooms of taking writing apart; this one you build. A claim that could be wrong, reasons with something checkable under each, a shape chosen before drafting, and a close that says more than the opening. Then the hard part — cutting the sentence you liked that held nothing up.

Context Clues and Word Parts
At this level the word you do not know is usually the one carrying the argument. Roots and affixes as a system that predicts whole families, the difference between what a word means and what it is doing, and the habit of naming the neutral word a writer refused.
Open
Figurative Language
Every figure is a transfer: it carries some properties across and silently refuses to carry the rest. The eight figures you will be asked to name, and one method that works on all of them — say what crossed, say what did not, and propose the rival comparison.
Open
Tone and Voice
Tone is never stated, so it can only be proved. Telling tone from mood and voice, the four sources it is built from — diction, figures, sentence length and what is left out — a precise tone vocabulary, and finding the line where a passage turns.
Open
Rhetoric: How a Writer Persuades
Tone is attitude with no plan; rhetoric is attitude with one. Ethos, pathos and logos, the devices that carry them, and the three questions that come before any of it — who is the audience, what are they being asked to do, and why have they not done it already.
Open
Punctuation That Changes the Meaning
Not a room about neatness. Two commas change how many brothers you have; a pair of quotation marks calls a word a lie without arguing. The removal test, what each mark claims, and four ways to repair a comma splice that each say something different.
Open
Clauses, Parallel Structure and Usage
Where a sentence is actually built. What sits in the main clause is asserted; what is tucked into a subordinate one is handed over as already agreed — so the swap test shows you what the structure was doing. Plus parallelism, danglers, agreement and the usage pairs worth owning.

Literary Devices
The unit of analysis grows: not a word or a sentence but the distance between two places in a text. Irony, symbol, motif, foreshadowing, allusion and structure — each one a claim made by arrangement, and each one provable only by naming both places.
Open
Diction and Syntax
Where the actor goes to disappear. The agentless passive and the nominalization delete the person who acted while leaving a perfectly grammatical sentence behind — so the repair is always the same: restore the agent and read what you get.
Open
Reasoning and Fallacies
Premises, conclusion, and the warrant nobody writes down. Validity is not truth and soundness is not persuasiveness; the named fallacies are a vocabulary, not a verdict — and the fallacy fallacy is the trap waiting for anyone who has just learned their names.
Open
Advanced Punctuation
The punctuation of quotation, treated as what it is: an ethical matter. Ellipsis, brackets, block quotation and the semicolon — and the honest-cut test, which asks whether the writer you quoted would accept the sentence you built from their words.
Open
Concision and Style
Concision is not brevity. The rule is that every word does work — because the quickest way to shorten a sentence is to delete a qualification, which makes it briefer and false at once. The deletion test, deadwood, buried verbs, and the hedge that carries the load.
Open
Capstone: Research, Cite, Defend
The last room. Judge the source, size the claim to the evidence, quote so the author would accept your use of their words, and answer the objection you would raise against yourself. Every check is easy to skip, because skipping leaves no mark on the page.
