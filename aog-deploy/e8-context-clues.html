Context Clues

Word parts got you close. Now the sentence finishes the job. A writer who uses a hard word almost always leaves a clue nearby — a definition tucked in, an example, a word like but that flips the meaning, or a cause that gives it away. There are five kinds of clue and a handful of signal words that announce them, and once you know them you stop skipping words. Five tabs — cards for the five kinds, hints with the signal words on one page, a quiz that asks you to name the clue you used, and a workshop where you sort sentences by their clue, put the guess-and-check steps in order, label a sentence and say why skipping a word costs more than guessing.

A writer who uses a hard word usually leaves a clue nearby on purpose. Find the signal word, use the clue to make a guess — reading a CONTRAST backwards — then put your guess back in place of the word and check the sentence still makes sense.

Two things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

I do not know that word, so I will skip it and keep reading.

Skipping looks free and is the most expensive thing you can do: the word was holding the sentence up, and nothing warns you it has gone. A guess costs five seconds and is testable — substitute it, and the sentence either stands or it does not.

A guess announces itself when it fails. A skip never does.

⚠️ People often think…

The clue word is near the hard word, so it must mean roughly the same thing.

Only if the signal word says so. With or, which is or such as, read forwards and take it. With but, unlike or however, the clue is the OPPOSITE — in “He was timid, unlike his bold sister,” taking bold as the meaning gets you exactly backwards.

The signal word tells you which direction to read.

Signal, guess, substitute, check

1 Watch one

Work out what arid means in: “The soil was arid, so nothing would grow.”

  1. Read to the end of the sentence first — the clue here comes after the hard word.
  2. Find the signal word: so. That announces a cause and its effect.
  3. Work backwards from the effect: nothing would grow. What kind of soil does that? Very dry soil.
  4. Substitute and check: “The soil was very dry, so nothing would grow.” It stands up, so the guess is good.

Helpful Hints

🧭 The unit in one line

Read to the end of the sentence → look for a signal word → use the clue to make a guess → put your guess back in place of the hard word and see whether the sentence still makes sense.

Guess, then substitute and check. Never skip.

🔑 The one idea

A writer who uses a hard word usually knows it is hard, so they leave a clue nearby on purpose. Your job is to find the help they already left you.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • Do not skip the word. Skipping costs you the sentence, and often the paragraph — a guess you check costs you five seconds.
  • The clue is not always in the same sentence. Look at the one before and the one after before you give up.
  • A signal word like but or unlike means the clue is the OPPOSITE of the word. Read it backwards or you will get the meaning upside down.
  • Always substitute your guess back in. A guess that sounds fine on its own can break the sentence it came from.

📐 The five clues — the chart

ClueSignal wordsExample
Definitionis, means, or, which isA herbivore, or an animal that eats only plants, …
Examplesuch as, like, including, for exampleCitrus fruits such as lemons and limes …
Contrastbut, unlike, however, instead ofHe was timid, unlike his bold sister.
Cause and effectbecause, so, since, as a resultThe soil was arid, so nothing would grow.
General sensenone — you use the whole pictureShe trudged up the hill with the heavy pack.
