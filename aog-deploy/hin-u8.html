The Vedas

Shruti I

Long before anyone in India wrote a book, families of priests carried a huge body of poetry in memory. These are the Vedas. Many Hindus regard them as shruti, sacred sound that was heard by ancient seers. Scholars study them as the oldest texts of South Asia, composed beginning around 1500 BCE.

In this unit you will read hymns from the Rig Veda to fire, storm, dawn and sun, including the Gayatri mantra and the creation hymn that ends with a question. Then you will look at all four Vedas, their layers, the fire ritual, and the remarkable ways reciters kept every syllable safe.

By the end, you will be able to name the four Vedas and their layers, explain how Hindus use Vedic verses today, and describe how scholars and Hindu tradition date these texts differently. You will also see how the question of who may learn the Vedas has changed.

The big question

What are the Vedas, and how have Hindus kept them alive and used them for over three thousand years?

Hymns of the Rig Veda

Rig Veda
Big questionWhat do the hymns of the Rig Veda praise and ask, and why have Hindus kept them alive for so long?
The story

Words Older Than Writing

At dawn, a priest lights a fire and sings a hymn that was never first written on paper.

It is still dark in a courtyard in Kerala. A priest sits cross-legged beside a square fire altar made of brick. He strikes a flame, feeds it small sticks and a spoon of clarified butter, called ghee. The fire jumps up, bright orange against the gray sky.

He begins to chant. His voice rises and falls on three set pitches. The words are Sanskrit, but an old form of it. They are addressed to Agni, the god of fire, whom the hymn calls a priest and a messenger. The priest learned every syllable from his father, who learned it from his.

Scholars think hymns like this one were composed more than three thousand years ago. For most of that time, no one wrote them down. Families of priests carried them in memory, word by word, sound by sound. Some scholars say the recitation today is remarkably close to how it sounded long ago.

As the sky turns pink, the priest reaches a hymn to Ushas, the dawn. The hymn praises her as a young woman who wakes every living thing. Outside, birds begin to call. The priest does not look up. He keeps chanting, exactly as he was taught.

Talk about itThe priest learned the hymns by listening, not reading. What would it take to keep a long text perfectly in memory for hundreds of years?
Section 1

Meeting the Rig Veda

15.1

The Oldest Hindu Text

Main ideaThe Rig Veda is a collection of 1,028 hymns in ten books, and scholars date it to about 1500–1000 BCE.

Open an English Rig Veda and the first line praises fire. Hymn after hymn follows, more than a thousand in all. The is the oldest of the four Vedas. Hindus count it among the most sacred of their texts.

The Rig Veda holds 1,028 hymns, arranged in ten books. Each book is called a , which means circle. Books 2 through 7 are called the family books, because tradition links each one to a family of poet-seers. Book 10 is thought to be one of the latest.

Scholars date most of the hymns to about 1500–1000 BCE, though they debate the exact range. Many Hindus hold a traditional view that the Vedas are eternal and were not made by any human author. In this view, the seers, or , heard the hymns rather than composed them.

Both views agree on one thing. The Rig Veda is very old, and it was first passed down by mouth. It was written down much later. The oldest surviving manuscripts are about a thousand years old or less.

15.2

Hymns as Poems and Ritual Words

Main ideaThe Rig Veda's hymns are both poetry and words spoken in rituals.

A Rig Veda hymn might describe the dawn as a woman in bright clothes. Another might compare prayers to cows streaming toward a calf. These are poems, full of images. Scholars admire their skill and their puzzles.

The hymns were also working words. Priests chanted them during the , the fire ritual. Offerings of ghee, grain and the drink called soma were poured into the fire. The hymns praised the gods and asked for rain, cattle, children, long life and victory.

Most hymns follow set meters, or patterns of syllables. The meters helped with memory and with chanting. The same hymn can be read today as poetry in a college class and chanted as sacred sound at a Hindu wedding.

15.3

Griffith's English Rig Veda

Main ideaRalph T.H. Griffith published a full English translation of the Rig Veda in the 1890s, now in the public domain.

In the 1890s, an English scholar named Ralph T.H. Griffith finished a complete English Rig Veda. He had taught in Benares (Varanasi). His Hymns of the Rigveda came out in volumes, with a revised edition in 1896.

Griffith wrote in an old-fashioned English style, with words like thou and hath. Some of his choices are now out of date, and modern translators often disagree with him. Still, his work is free to read and quote because it is in the .

Newer translations exist. Stephanie Jamison and Joel Brereton published a full modern translation in 2014. Readers often compare translations, because each one shows a different way of reading the old Sanskrit.

Gods of the Hymns

15.4

Agni, the Fire

Main ideaAgni, the fire, is praised as a priest and messenger who carries offerings to the other gods.

Picture a Vedic priest pouring ghee into a flame. Where does the offering go? The hymns answer: carries it. Agni is both the fire on the altar and a god. His name means fire.

The hymns call Agni a priest, a guest in every home and a messenger between people and the gods. He is born each time the priests rub fire-sticks together. About 200 hymns of the Rig Veda are addressed to him.

Agni remains part of Hindu life. At a Hindu wedding, the couple walks around a sacred fire. Many Hindus see Agni as a witness to their vows. Fire is also part of the funeral rites of many Hindu families.

15.6

Ushas, the Dawn

Main ideaThe hymns to Ushas, the goddess of dawn, are among the Rig Veda's most admired poems.

Every morning, Ushas arrives. The hymns picture her as a young woman, shining, driving a bright chariot. She wakes sleepers, sends workers to their tasks and reveals the world again. About twenty hymns praise her.

The dawn hymns are full of feeling. They notice that Ushas is old and young at once, returning each day while people grow older. Many readers find in them both joy and a sense of time passing.

Scholars often point to the Ushas hymns when they talk about the Rig Veda as literature. They show how closely the poets watched nature. Ushas is one of several goddesses in the Vedas, along with Sarasvati, a river goddess.

15.7

The Gayatri Mantra

Main ideaThe Gayatri mantra, Rig Veda 3.62.10, is a prayer to the sun that many Hindus recite daily.

Every morning, millions of Hindus repeat the same short verse. It is the , from Rig Veda 3.62.10. It is named for its meter, the gayatri. It is addressed to Savitar, the sun as the one who stirs life.

A is a sacred word or verse. Hindus believe mantras have power when spoken correctly. Traditionally, the Gayatri was taught to boys of certain groups at a coming-of-age rite called the sacred thread ceremony.

Many Hindus today, girls and boys alike, learn and recite the Gayatri. Some reform movements, like the Arya Samaj, taught it widely. You can hear it sung in temples, homes and music recordings around the world.

15.8

The Creation Hymn

Main ideaRig Veda 10.129 asks how the world began and ends by wondering whether anyone truly knows.

Most creation stories tell what happened. Rig Veda 10.129, often called the Nasadiya hymn, asks instead. It begins before there was being or non-being. There was no air, no sky, no death and no night.

The hymn says that out of darkness, desire arose, and from it came the world. Then it turns to questions. Who really knows how it happened? The gods came later than the world, so how could they know?

The last verse wonders if even the one who watches from highest heaven knows, or perhaps does not. Hindu thinkers, scholars and scientists have admired this humility. Some readers see it as deep faith, and others as honest doubt.

15.9

Hymns About Society

Main ideaThe Purusha hymn, Rig Veda 10.90, names four social groups, and readers have long argued about what it means.

Rig Veda 10.90 describes a cosmic being, , offered in a great sacrifice. From his body the world is made. The hymn says four groups of people came from his mouth, arms, thighs and feet.

These four groups, the , are priests, rulers and warriors, traders and farmers, and servants. Later texts built on this idea, and it became linked with caste, a system of birth-based groups. Caste has caused great harm and unfair treatment over many centuries.

Readers respond in many ways. Some scholars think the verse was added late. Many Hindu reformers, such as Swami Vivekananda and Mahatma Gandhi, rejected birth-based ranking. B.R. Ambedkar, a leader born into a group treated as untouchable, criticized this hymn sharply. India’s constitution outlaws untouchability.

15.10

When Was It Composed?

Main ideaScholars and traditional Hindus give different dates for the Rig Veda, and the debate continues.

How old is the Rig Veda? Most scholars say its hymns were composed in the northwest of South Asia, about 1500–1000 BCE. They base this on the language, which is close to old Iranian texts, and on places and things the hymns mention.

Some Hindu scholars and writers argue for much older dates. Many Hindus hold that the Vedas are eternal and so have no date of composition at all. The traditional view speaks of when the Vedas were revealed and arranged, not invented.

There are also debates about where the Vedic people came from. Most scholars think the language arrived from outside South Asia. Others disagree. This course describes these views and does not decide between them. Using "c." for dates reminds us that they are estimates.

Four Vedas, Ritual and an Unbroken Voice

The Vedic Corpus
Big questionHow were the four Vedas organized, used and passed down, and who has been allowed to learn them?
The story

Eleven Ways to Say One Verse

A teacher and a student chant one verse forward, backward and braided together.

In a Vedic school in Maharashtra, a boy of twelve sits facing his teacher. Between them there is no book. The teacher chants one line of a hymn. The boy chants it back. They repeat it until every sound, pitch and pause is exact.

Then the teacher breaks the line apart. He says each word alone. Next he pairs the words: one and two, two and three, three and four. Then he chants a pair forward, then backward, then forward again. The boy follows, frowning hard.

"Why so many ways?" the boy finally asks. The teacher smiles. "If you forget one word, the pattern will catch it," he says. "The words can travel a thousand years this way and not lose a letter." Tradition counts eleven ways of reciting, some simple and some like braids.

UNESCO, a United Nations agency, has recognized Vedic chanting as part of the world's heritage. Scholars marvel at the method. For the boy, it is simply his morning. He will practice for years before he knows one Veda by heart.

Talk about itThe teacher says the patterns will catch mistakes. How is this like the ways you check your own work in math or spelling?

Four Vedas

16.1

Rig, Sama, Yajur and Atharva

Main ideaHindus recognize four Vedas, each with its own job: praise, song, ritual formulas and everyday prayers.

Picture a Vedic ritual with four kinds of priests. One recites hymns of praise. One sings. One handles the offerings while speaking short formulas. One watches over the whole rite. Each priest relies on a different Veda.

The Rig Veda is the book of praise hymns. The sets many of those verses to melodies for singing. The holds the formulas spoken while doing the ritual. The has hymns and charms for everyday life, such as healing, protection and peace at home.

The Atharva Veda was accepted as a Veda later than the other three, and some older texts speak of only three Vedas. Today Hindus generally count four. Together they are the core of , the texts Hindus regard as heard or revealed.

16.2

Four Layers in Each Veda

Main ideaEach Veda grew in layers: Samhitas, Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads.

Think of a house with four floors, built over many generations. Each Veda is like that. The oldest floor is the , the collection of hymns or formulas. When people say Rig Veda, they often mean just its Samhita.

Next come the . These are prose texts that explain the rituals and what they mean. Then the Aranyakas, or forest books, which think about the ritual in deeper and more hidden ways.

The last layer is the Upanishads. They ask about the self and ultimate reality. Because they come at the end, they are called Vedanta, the end of the Veda. Scholars date this whole growth from about 1500 BCE to about 500 BCE or later.

16.3

Shruti and Smriti

Main ideaHindus often divide sacred texts into shruti, what is heard, and smriti, what is remembered.

A Hindu teacher might hold up two books. One is a Veda. The other is the Ramayana. She explains that Hindus often place them in two groups. The Vedas are , heard. The Ramayana is , remembered.

Smriti texts include the epics, the Puranas and law books. Many Hindus see them as having human authors, like Valmiki and Vyasa, and as explaining the Vedas’ teachings. In the traditional ranking, shruti has the highest authority.

In daily life, though, smriti often matters more to many people. Far more Hindus know the Ramayana and the Gita than the Vedic hymns. The Gita is smriti, yet many Hindus honor it as highly as the Upanishads.

The Fire Ritual

16.4

Yajna: Offerings to the Fire

Main ideaThe Vedic yajna is a fire ritual in which offerings are given to the gods through Agni.

Priests build a fire altar of bricks in a set shape. They light the fire and chant from the Vedas. They pour ghee, grain and other offerings into the flames. This is the , the central Vedic ritual.

The Vedic texts say the gods receive offerings through Agni, the fire. The Brahmanas explain that the ritual helps keep the world in good order. Large public rites could last many days and need many priests.

Large yajnas are rare today, but some are still held. A smaller home fire rite, often called or havan, is common. Families in Illinois may hold a havan to bless a new house or begin a new venture.

16.5

The Vedas at Weddings and Life Rites

Main ideaVedic verses are still chanted at Hindu weddings and other life-cycle rites called samskaras.

At a Hindu wedding, the bride and groom take seven steps together around a sacred fire. This is called . A priest chants Vedic verses. Many couples make a promise at each step. Some wedding verses come from Rig Veda book 10.

The wedding is one of the , rites that mark stages of life. Other samskaras include naming a baby, a child’s first solid food, a sacred thread ceremony and funeral rites. Many of them use Vedic mantras.

Families adapt these rites. In the United States, a wedding may be shortened, explained in English or held in a hotel ballroom. Yet the fire and the Sanskrit verses often remain at the center.

16.6

Changing Views of Ritual

Main ideaOver time, Hindu thinkers asked what ritual means, and many turned inward toward knowledge and devotion.

The Upanishads sometimes treat ritual in a new way. Instead of an outer fire, they speak of an inner one, like breath or thought. The Aranyakas began this turn toward inner meaning.

The Bhagavad Gita, too, speaks of sacrifice broadly. It says any work done without selfishness can be an offering. In Gita 9.26, Krishna says he accepts even a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water offered with love.

Some later schools, like Mimamsa, defended the Vedic ritual as the heart of dharma. Others stressed knowledge or bhakti. Hindu practice today includes all of these. A family may hold a havan and also sing devotional songs.

16.7

Memory Before Writing

Main ideaThe Vedas were passed down orally for many centuries before they were written down.

Today we write important words down. For many centuries, Vedic priests did the opposite. They kept the Vedas in memory. Some texts even warned against learning them from a book. This is .

Scholars think writing came into use in India long after the Rig Veda was composed. Even once people wrote, the chanted Veda stayed the true one for many teachers. The oldest surviving Rig Veda manuscripts date from about 1000 years ago or later.

Oral learning was hard. A student often lived with a teacher, a , for years. The student listened, repeated and practiced every day. Many families passed the same Veda down over dozens of generations.

16.8

The Patha Methods

Main ideaVedic reciters use patterned methods, called pathas, that make mistakes easy to catch.

Take the words a b c d. In the plain recitation, the samhita patha, you say a b c d. In the , you say each word separately. In the , you say overlapping pairs: ab, bc, cd.

Harder methods braid the words. In the , you say ab, ba, ab, then bc, cb, bc. The hardest, the ghana patha, forms long chains forward and backward. Tradition counts eleven methods in all.

Because each word is said in several patterns, a mistake in one pattern shows up against the others. Reciters also keep pitch accents, marked by raising or lowering the voice. Scholars see these methods as a remarkable way of guarding a text.

16.9

Who Could Learn the Vedas?

Main ideaIn the past, Vedic learning was limited mostly to men of certain groups, but that has changed in many places.

For many centuries, Vedic learning was closely guarded. Law books like the Manusmriti said it was for the three upper varnas, and mostly for boys. Women and people of lower groups were often shut out.

There were exceptions. The Upanishads name women thinkers such as Gargi and Maitreyi. Still, the general rule kept many people from learning the Vedas. Scholars and Hindu teachers today discuss this history openly.

In the 1800s and 1900s, reformers pushed for change. The , founded by Swami Dayananda Saraswati in 1875, taught the Vedas to women and to people of all castes. Today many Hindu women chant the Vedas, and some serve as priests.

16.10

Vedas in the Wider World

Main ideaVedic chanting is honored today as world heritage and heard in homes, temples and recordings.

In 2003, UNESCO recognized the tradition of Vedic chanting as a masterpiece of the oral and intangible heritage of humanity. It later joined UNESCO’s list of the world’s .

Some Vedic schools in India still train students the old way. Other students now learn online or from recordings. Temples in the United States hold classes where children learn Vedic verses like the Gayatri mantra.

Scholars keep studying the Vedas too. They compare recordings with manuscripts and older descriptions. For many Hindus, though, the Vedas are above all sacred sound, to be heard and chanted with care.

Think it through
Heritage You Can't Hold

A temple is heritage you can walk into. A chant is heritage you can only hear. It lasts only as long as people keep learning it.

Why might protecting a chant be harder than protecting a building? What helps keep it alive?
