The Classics Through History

History and Travel

For two thousand years, the classics shaped who held power in China. They filled exam halls, guided emperors and spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam. They also shaped who was left out. Then, starting in the 1600s, they traveled west, into Latin, French and English.

In this unit you will follow the classics through the imperial age: the Han, the arrival of Buddhism, the exams, Zhu Xi and Neo-Confucianism, and the voices of women like Ban Zhao. Then you will follow them west with Jesuits, Enlightenment writers, James Legge and Wang Tao, and into American classrooms.

By the end, you will be able to explain how the exams worked and whom they excluded. You will be able to describe how translators shaped Western views of the classics. And you will be able to connect this history to Chinese American life, including the Exclusion Act.

The big question

How did the classics shape power in East Asia, and what changed as they traveled to the West?

Empire, Exams and Neo-Confucianism

The Imperial Age
Big questionHow did the classics shape who held power in imperial China and East Asia, and who was left out?
The story

The Last Exam

In 1905 an imperial order ends an exam system that had shaped China for thirteen centuries.

September 1905. In the Qing capital, Beijing, officials read an imperial edict. Beginning at once, it says, the civil-service examinations will stop. The country will build modern schools instead. The order is short. Its effect is enormous.

Across China, hundreds of thousands of men had been studying for the next round. Some were teenagers. Some were grandfathers who had taken the exams again and again. Their years of memorizing the Four Books and Five Classics would no longer lead to office.

For thirteen centuries, since about 605 CE, the exams had been the main road to government service. They had carried Zhu Xi's reading of the classics into every village school. They had made a scholar-official class that ruled China, and inspired similar exams in Korea and Vietnam.

Reformers cheered the change. China, they said, needed science, engineering and law to face foreign powers. Others grieved a world that was ending. The classics did not vanish, but they lost the place they had held at the center of Chinese life.

Three Teachings in One Empire

21.1

Han State Confucianism

Main ideaThe Han dynasty used Confucian learning to train officials and to explain the emperor's role, while keeping strict laws.

The Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) gave Confucian learning official support. Officials were expected to know the Five Classics. The scholar Dong Zhongshu described the emperor as a link between Heaven, Earth and humanity. Disasters, he taught, could be signs from Heaven that a ruler was going wrong.

The Han also made , respect and care for parents, a public value. Officials were sometimes recommended for office because of their devotion to parents. The short Classic of Filial Piety became widely taught.

Historians describe this as : the government using Confucian ideas to support its rule. Some Confucians welcomed it. Others worried that serving the state could bend the teaching. Han law still owed much to Legalism.

21.2

Buddhism Arrives

Main ideaBuddhism came to China from India along trade routes by the first century CE and became one of the 'three teachings' with Confucianism and Daoism.

began in India with the Buddha, around the 400s BCE. Monks and merchants carried it to China along the Silk Roads. Chinese tradition dates its arrival to the first century CE and links it to the White Horse Temple near Luoyang.

At first, translators used Daoist words to explain Buddhist ideas. Over time Buddhism grew into a major Chinese religion, with monasteries, schools of thought such as Chan (Zen), and millions of followers. Some Confucians criticized it, since monks left their families.

By the Tang dynasty (618–907), people often spoke of the : Confucianism, Daoism and Buddhism. Emperors sometimes favored one over another. Many ordinary people drew on all three. A popular saying held that the three teachings are one.

The Examination Road

21.3

How the Exams Worked

Main ideaFrom the Sui and Tang to 1905, the civil-service exams tested knowledge of the classics in several levels, from local to palace.

The Sui dynasty began exams for officials around 605 CE. The Tang used them, and the Song (960–1279) expanded them greatly. Printing made books cheaper, so more families could prepare. Exams became the main road to office.

By the Ming and Qing, there were several levels. Candidates passed local exams, then provincial exams held every three years, then the metropolitan exam in the capital. The best faced a before the emperor. Few reached the top degree, the jinshi.

Candidates sat in tiny cells for days. Graders copied essays so handwriting could not reveal names, to prevent favoritism. Cheating still happened. Some candidates wore undershirts covered with tiny copied classics, which museums preserve today.

21.4

Zhu Xi Becomes the Standard

Main ideaIn 1313 Zhu Xi's commentaries on the Four Books became the exam standard, spreading Neo-Confucian readings across China.

is a modern name for the renewed Confucian thought of the Song and later dynasties. Its thinkers answered Buddhism and Daoism with their own big picture of the universe. Zhu Xi (1130–1200) was its greatest systematizer.

Zhu Xi taught that everything has , principle, the pattern that makes it what it is, and , the stuff or energy of which it is made. Human nature is principle and is good, he said, as Mencius taught, but cloudy qi can hide it. Study of things and self-cultivation clear it.

In 1313, the Yuan government made Zhu Xi’s commentaries the exam standard. This lasted to 1905. Later, Wang Yangming (1472–1529) challenged Zhu Xi, teaching that principle is found in one’s own mind and that knowing and acting are one.

21.5

Who Was Left Out

Main ideaThe exams claimed to reward merit, but women and several groups were barred, and few poor families could afford years of study.

Confucius taught, in Analects 15.38 (15.39 in some editions), that in teaching there should be no distinction of classes. The exams were praised as open to talent. In practice, many people could not take them.

Women were barred from the exams for their whole history. Sons of some groups, such as actors and certain hereditary servants, were also excluded. Poor farm families rarely could spare a son for years of study or pay a teacher. Most passing candidates came from families with land or learning.

Historians still debate how much the exams allowed , the chance to rise from one’s birth. Some studies find a real share of degree-holders had no recent ancestors with degrees. Others stress how limited the system.

Women, Neighbors and Change

21.6

Ban Zhao's Lessons for Women

Main ideaBan Zhao, a Han scholar, wrote Lessons for Women (c. 100 CE), which taught humility and duty and also argued that girls should be educated.

(c. 45–c. 117 CE) was one of the most learned people of the Han. She helped finish the History of the Former Han that her brother Ban Gu had begun, and she taught at the imperial court.

Around 100 CE she wrote (Nüjie), advice for the young women of her family. It teaches humility, hard work and obedience to husbands and in-laws, following the Confucian idea of the five relationships. It became a standard text for girls for centuries.

Readers have taken it in different ways. Many modern critics see it as teaching women to accept a lower place. Others note that Ban Zhao argued girls, like boys, should be taught to read, and that her own life showed a woman as a scholar. Historians read it in its Han context.

21.7

Korea, Japan and Vietnam

Main ideaNeo-Confucian learning shaped government and education in Joseon Korea, Tokugawa Japan and Vietnam, each in its own way.

In Korea, the dynasty (1392–1910) made Neo-Confucianism its guiding philosophy. The national academy, Seonggyungwan, trained scholars. Thinkers such as Yi Hwang (Toegye) and Yi I (Yulgok) debated Zhu Xi’s ideas of principle and qi in depth. Ancestral rites spread widely.

In Japan, the Tokugawa shoguns (1603–1868) supported Confucian scholars such as Hayashi Razan. Japan did not use exams to choose officials, since rank was mostly hereditary. Still, Confucian ideas shaped schools and samurai ethics.

In Vietnam, the in Hanoi, founded in 1070, honors Confucius. Stone tablets on the backs of stone turtles list doctoral laureates from the 1400s onward. Vietnam held classical exams until 1919.

21.8

Critics and Revivals

Main ideaIn the 1900s, Chinese reformers attacked Confucianism, a 1970s campaign targeted Confucius, and later decades saw a revival of interest.

After 1905, many young reformers blamed Confucian tradition for China’s weakness. In the May Fourth Movement of 1919, writers such as Lu Xun criticized family rules and the treatment of women. A popular slogan called for overthrowing the "Confucian shop."

Under Mao Zedong, the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976) attacked old traditions. In 1973–74, a political campaign called "Criticize Lin Biao, Criticize Confucius" denounced Confucius. Red Guards damaged temples and tombs, including at Qufu, in 1966.

Since the 1980s, interest has returned. The Qufu temple was restored. Scholars in China, Taiwan, Korea and the West study "New Confucianism." Some welcome the ; others worry that governments use it to promote obedience. The debate continues.

The Classics Travel West

Translation
Big questionWhat happens to a classic when it is carried into another language and culture?
The story

Two Scholars at One Desk

In Hong Kong in the 1860s, a Scottish missionary and a Chinese scholar work together on the classics.

Hong Kong, 1863. In a study above the harbor, a Scottish missionary named James Legge rises before dawn, as he does most days. He opens the Book of Documents and a pile of Chinese commentaries. He has promised himself to translate all the major classics into English.

Beside him works Wang Tao, a Chinese scholar who had fled Shanghai after being accused of helping the Taiping rebels. Wang Tao knows the commentaries deeply. He explains hard passages, gathers notes from many editions, and argues with Legge about meanings.

Legge believes the classics must be understood on their own terms before a Westerner can judge them. His notes are long and careful. He prints the Chinese text, the English and his commentary on the same page, so readers can check his work.

In 1867 Wang Tao even travels with Legge to Scotland to continue the work. The volumes of The Chinese Classics appear from 1861 to 1872. Legge later becomes Oxford's first professor of Chinese. Wang Tao returns to Hong Kong and becomes a pioneering newspaper editor. Their translations are still read today.

Jesuits and the First Latin Confucius

22.1

Matteo Ricci in China

Main ideaThe Jesuit Matteo Ricci learned classical Chinese, studied the classics and presented Christianity in terms that respected Confucian learning.

(1552–1610) was an Italian Jesuit priest. He arrived in China in 1582 and settled in Beijing in 1601. He learned to read and write classical Chinese and dressed as a Confucian scholar.

Ricci believed that early Confucianism was compatible with Christianity. He argued that the ancient classics spoke of a supreme Lord on High (Shangdi) and that ancestor rites could be understood as honoring, not worshiping. He made friends with scholars such as Xu Guangqi, who became a Christian.

Ricci’s approach is sometimes called : adapting to local culture. It sparked the . In 1715, Pope Clement XI ruled against allowing Chinese Christians to take part in ancestor and Confucian rites. The Kangxi emperor then restricted missionaries.

22.2

Confucius in Latin, 1687

Main ideaIn 1687, Jesuits in Paris published Confucius Sinarum Philosophus, the first major Western translation of Confucian classics.

Jesuit scholars spent decades translating Confucian texts into , the shared learned language of Europe. In 1687 they published (Confucius, Philosopher of the Chinese) in Paris.

The book included Latin versions of the Great Learning, the Doctrine of the Mean and the Analects, with a life of Confucius. Its editors, including Philippe Couplet and Prospero Intorcetta, presented Confucius as a wise moral philosopher who taught by natural reason.

The book shaped how Europe first saw Confucius. It also shows how carries the translator’s aims. The Jesuits wanted to show that China’s ancient wisdom fit with Christian belief. Later translators made different choices.

22.4

Legge and Wang Tao

Main ideaJames Legge, helped by Wang Tao and other Chinese scholars, translated the Chinese classics into English between 1861 and 1891.

(1815–1897) was a Scottish missionary with the London Missionary Society. He worked in Malacca and then in Hong Kong from 1843. He decided that missionaries needed to understand the classics deeply.

His series appeared from 1861 to 1872. It included the Four Books, the Book of Documents, the Songs, and the Spring and Autumn with its Zuo commentary. Each page printed the Chinese text, Legge’s English and extensive notes.

Legge had help. (1828–1897) contributed research on commentaries and worked with him in Hong Kong and Scotland. Scholars today stress that the translations were a joint achievement, though only Legge’s name is on the title pages.

22.5

Oxford and the Sacred Books

Main ideaLegge became Oxford's first professor of Chinese and translated Confucian and Daoist texts for the Sacred Books of the East.

In 1876 Legge became the first Professor of Chinese at Oxford University. He kept translating into his seventies. His students were few, but his books reached readers far beyond Oxford.

The scholar Max Müller edited a series called the (1879–1910), 50 volumes of Asian religious texts in English. Legge wrote six volumes. They included the Classic of Filial Piety, the Book of Changes, the Book of Rites, and, in 1891, the Daodejing and the Zhuangzi.

Legge’s translations are now in the in the United States, so anyone may copy them. That is why this course quotes them. Scholars also note their limits: old-fashioned English, Victorian spellings like Tao Teh King and Kwang-ze, and some Christian assumptions.

22.6

A Hundred Daodejings

Main ideaThe Daodejing has been translated into English more than any other Chinese book, and each version reflects the translator's choices and aims.

After Legge, English versions of the Daodejing multiplied. Scholars count well over a hundred. Some are careful scholarly translations. Others are loose by writers who do not read Chinese and work from earlier English versions.

Each type serves a purpose. Scholarly versions note manuscript differences, such as the Mawangdui and Guodian copies. Popular versions may speak to modern readers about calm or leadership. Critics warn that some popular versions change the meaning a great deal.

Students can compare versions carefully. Ask: Does the translator read Chinese? Which Chinese text did they use? Where do they add their own ideas? A good names the translator and year.

The Classics in America

22.7

Emerson, Thoreau and the Dial

Main ideaAmerican writers of the 1800s, such as Emerson and Thoreau, read and quoted Confucian sayings from early translations.

In the 1840s, the New England writers called read Asian texts in translation. Their magazine, The Dial, printed selections of Confucian sayings in 1843. Ralph Waldo Emerson admired Confucius as a moral teacher.

Henry David Thoreau quoted Confucius several times in (1854). He drew on the Analects and the Great Learning to support his ideas about simple living and self-improvement.

These writers read the classics through their own interests. Historians note they often mixed Confucian, Hindu and Buddhist sources. Still, they introduced many Americans to Chinese thought.

Exclusion and Community

Main ideaWhile Americans read Chinese classics, the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred most Chinese laborers from entering the United States.

Chinese immigrants came to the United States in large numbers from the 1850s. They worked in gold mines, built the western part of the first transcontinental railroad, and opened businesses. Many faced violence and unfair laws.

In 1882 Congress passed the . It barred most Chinese laborers from immigrating and blocked Chinese immigrants from becoming citizens. It was the first U.S. law to ban immigration by a specific national group. It was repealed in 1943.

Chinese Americans built communities anyway, including Chicago’s Chinatown. Families kept festivals, temples, churches and family associations. Historians note the : the same years that Americans praised Confucius, Chinese people were shut out.

The Classics in Classrooms Today

Main ideaToday the Chinese classics are studied in American schools and universities as philosophy, literature and history, alongside living communities who keep them.

Today, American students may read the Analects or the Daodejing in world history, philosophy or literature classes. Universities teach classical Chinese. Scholars compare Confucian ethics with Aristotle, and Daoist ideas with other traditions.

Students also learn from living communities. Chinese American, Korean American and Vietnamese American families keep festivals and values linked to the classics. Some families are Christian, Buddhist or have no religion, and still honor Confucian ideals of family and learning.

Studying the classics well means using good , naming translations, and attributing ideas. It also means listening to the many people, in Asia and in America, for whom these books are part of their .
