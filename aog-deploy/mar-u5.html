A School with a Rulebook

Origins

In the 1800s, Japan had many small jujutsu schools. Each kept its own lessons on private scrolls. In 1882 a young teacher named Kanō Jigorō opened a school in Tokyo called the Kodokan. He named his subject judo, the gentle way.

Kanō gave judo things the old schools did not share: numbered ranks, a goal of education, and written contest rules. Teachers carried judo into Japanese schools. A world federation formed in 1951, and judo reached the Olympics in Tokyo in 1964.

In this unit you will learn how judo began and how its rulebook works. You will be able to name its ranks, its referee's words and its scores. You will compare its code with boxing's Queensberry Rules of 1867.

The big question

How does a written code turn an old fighting art into a school sport that people everywhere can share?

The story

Notes on a Temple Desk

In 1882 a young teacher in Tokyo sorts the notes of two old schools into one new plan.

It is 1882 in Tokyo, Japan. Kanō Jigorō is 21 years old. He has just finished his studies at Tokyo University. He rents rooms at Eishōji, a small Buddhist temple in the city.

Kanō has studied jujutsu for years. He trained in two old schools, Tenshin Shin'yō-ryū and Kitō-ryū. Each school kept its own lessons and its own secrets. Each teacher passed them to a few chosen students.

Kanō wants something different. He wants lessons that any student can learn in order. He wants a name, a plan and ranks that anyone can read. He calls his new school the Kodokan and his subject judo.

Only a few students come at first. The room is small and the mats are few. But Kanō keeps writing and teaching. Within a few years, schools and police across Japan know the Kodokan's name.

Before Judo

9.1

Many Old Schools

Main ideaBefore judo, Japan had many separate jujutsu schools, each called a ryū.

In Japan from the 1600s to the 1800s, many teachers taught . The word means the gentle art or the soft skill. It was a set of ways to handle an attacker without a weapon, or with a small one.

Each teaching family was called a , which means a school or a stream. A ryū had its own name, founder and lessons. Records list many different jujutsu ryū in this long era, called the Edo period.

A ryū often gave written scrolls to its best students. These scrolls named the lessons and the teachers before them. The scrolls were private. Outsiders could not easily read them or join.

9.2

When the Samurai Class Ended

Main ideaAfter 1868, Japan's new government ended the samurai class, and many jujutsu schools lost students.

In 1868 a new government took power in Japan. This change is called the . The new leaders wanted railways, factories, an army like Europe’s and public schools for all children.

The new government ended the old ranks of society. The samurai, a warrior class, lost their special place. In 1876 a law banned most people from wearing swords in public.

Many old arts looked out of date in this fast new Japan. Some jujutsu teachers lost their students and their pay. Some performed for money. Some ryū closed, and their lessons were nearly lost.

A School Called the Kodokan

9.4

Eishōji Temple, 1882

Main ideaIn 1882 Kanō opened the Kodokan at a Tokyo temple and named his subject judo.

In 1882 Kanō opened a training room at , a Buddhist temple in Tokyo. It had only a few mats. He called his school the . The name means a place for teaching the way.

Kanō named his subject judo, the gentle way. The word ju is the same one in jujutsu. Do means a way or path. Kanō chose do to show that judo was a way of living and learning, not only fighting.

9.5

Judo as Education

Main ideaKanō said judo should train the body, the mind and good character, like a school subject.

Kanō was a teacher first. In 1889 he gave a talk in Tokyo on judo and education. He said judo could train three things: the body, skill in contests, and the mind and character.

In 1922 Kanō began the Kodokan Culture Society. He used two short phrases to sum up judo. Seiryoku zen&#x27;yō means best use of energy. means mutual welfare and benefit, or you and others doing well together.

These ideas made judo sound like a school subject. Parents and schools could see a goal beyond winning. Kanō wanted students to use judo lessons in study, work and daily life.

9.6

Ranks Anyone Can Read

Main ideaThe Kodokan used numbered kyū and dan ranks, and the black belt became the sign of dan rank.

Old ryū often marked progress with private scrolls. Kanō used numbered ranks instead. Beginners held grades. Advanced students held grades, counted first dan, second dan and up.

In 1883 Kanō gave the first dan grades. Two of the first were his students Saigō Shirō and Tomita Tsunejirō. Over time, a black belt became the visible sign that a student held a dan rank.

A number is easy to read. A student from one judo club could tell another club what rank they held. The Kodokan kept records of each promotion. This made ranks public, not secret.

9.7

Teaching the Teachers

Main ideaKanō led a school that trained teachers, which helped carry judo into Japan's schools.

Kanō worked in education for most of his life. For many years he was the head of the Tokyo Higher Normal School. A is a college that trains new teachers.

Teachers trained there went on to schools across Japan. In the early 1900s, Japanese middle schools began to offer judo and kendo, the sword art, as school subjects. Judo now had a place in the school timetable.

Kanō also looked beyond Japan. In 1909 he became the first member from Asia on the International Olympic Committee, called the IOC. He traveled to Europe and the United States many times to speak about judo.

Why a Code Matters

Judo
Big questionHow does a written rulebook turn a contest into a school sport that strangers in many countries can share?
The story

The Page Says Begin

Two athletes in white and blue wait at the edge of a mat until a rule lets them start.

A judo hall is full and quiet. Two athletes stand at the edge of a square of mats. One wears a white jacket. One wears a blue jacket. They come from different countries and speak different languages.

Neither athlete steps onto the mat yet. The International Judo Federation's rules say how a contest opens. The athletes walk to their marks. They bow to each other, a standing bow the rules call for at the start of every contest.

Then the referee says one Japanese word: hajime. It means begin. A clock starts. Three officials watch. Every score, penalty and stop will follow the same written rules used in Paris, Tokyo or Rio de Janeiro.

When the contest ends, the two athletes return to their marks and bow again. The rulebook gave the contest a start, an end and a way to name the winner. That page is older than either of them.

10.2

A Contest Code on Paper

Main ideaAround 1900 the Kodokan wrote contest rules, and in 1951 the IJF began world rules.

In judo’s early years, many contests were held between Kodokan students and students of older jujutsu schools. Each side could argue about what was fair. Written rules helped settle those arguments before a match began.

Around 1900 the Kodokan wrote down its own contest rules. The rules named how a match was scored and which moves were not allowed. The Dai Nippon Butokukai, a national martial arts body founded in Kyoto in 1895, also worked on shared rules.

After the International Judo Federation formed in 1951, it wrote the rules for world contests. The IJF still updates its , usually every few years. A club in Illinois and a club in Japan can read the same rules.

10.4

Ippon Ends the Match

Main ideaIn judo, an ippon is a full point that ends the contest at once.

Judo rules name different scores. The highest is , which means one full point. When a referee calls ippon, the contest ends right away, even if time is left on the clock.

A smaller score is called . The IJF rules have changed how waza-ari counts several times. The rules also removed a smaller score called yuko in 2017, to make scoring simpler to follow.

Senior IJF contests today last four minutes. If the score is tied when time runs out, the contest goes to golden score. In golden score, the first athlete to score wins.

10.5

Sorting by Weight

Main ideaWeight classes let athletes compete against others of similar size, and their number has changed over time.

Early judo contests in Japan often did not sort athletes by weight. A small athlete could face a much larger one. Kanō taught that skill could overcome size, and stories of small winners were told proudly.

Modern contest rules use weight classes for fairness and safety. At the 1964 Olympics, men’s judo had three weight classes and an open class. The number of events grew in the years after.

The open class was dropped from the Olympics after 1984. Since 2000, Olympic judo has had seven weight classes for men and seven for women. Each class has a set top weight, weighed before the contest.

Penalties and Fair Play

Main ideaJudo rules name penalties, from a small shido to a hansoku-make that ends the contest.

A rulebook does more than list scores. It also says what is not allowed. Judo rules have always named some moves as banned in contests, because they are too dangerous. Referees stop a contest when one is tried.

A small penalty is called , which means guidance. A referee may give shido when an athlete avoids contact or steps out of the contest area. Too many shido can cost an athlete the match.

The biggest penalty is , which means defeat by a serious rule break. It ends the contest. An athlete can also be given hansoku-make for acting against the spirit of judo, such as disrespect.

10.8

An English Code, 1867

Main ideaThe 1867 Queensberry Rules did for boxing what Kodokan rules later did for judo: they put the contest on paper.

Fifteen years before the Kodokan opened, boxing got a written code in England. The were published in 1867. John Graham Chambers wrote them, and the Marquess of Queensberry gave them his name.

The rules called for a ring 24 feet across, rounds of three minutes and padded gloves. A boxer who went down had ten seconds to get up. The Marquess was a Scottish nobleman, John Sholto Douglas.

Judo and boxing grew in different lands and have different rules. But both codes did the same big job. They wrote down a start, an end, what was banned and how a winner was named.

From the source

Rules That Keep Changing

Main ideaJudo's code keeps changing, from blue jackets in the 1990s to a mixed team event in 2021.

For most of judo’s history, both athletes wore white. Referees and viewers could mix them up. In the late 1990s, the IJF began using one blue jacket and one white jacket in top contests.

Rule changes also add new events. At the Tokyo Olympics, held in 2021, judo added a event. Each team had both men and women from one country.

Some people in judo argued against changes like the blue jacket. They said white was part of Kanō’s tradition. The IJF decided that clear viewing mattered more. A living code is argued over and rewritten.

Photo one: two athletes in white jackets on a white-lit mat. It is hard to tell who is who. Photo two: one athlete in white and one in blue. A viewer far away can follow each athlete.
