Judaism, Christianity and Islam

Traditions

Judaism, Christianity and Islam are three of the world's biggest religions. They began in the same part of the world. All three tell stories about Abraham. All three teach that there is one God. Millions of people in Chicago follow one of them.

In this unit, you will visit a Passover table, an Easter church, and a Ramadan sunset. You will read short lines from the Torah, the Gospels and the Qur'an. You will learn what each tradition's people believe and do.

By the end, you will be able to name each tradition's holy book and main practices. You will explain what the three share and how they differ. And you will do it the way a scholar does: describe, do not judge.

The big question

How are Judaism, Christianity and Islam alike, and how are they different?

The story

Two Tables, One Story

Two cousins sit at two different holiday tables in the same spring week, and both hear about Moses.

Maya and Leo are cousins. They live in Chicago, two streets apart. Maya's family is Jewish. Leo's family is Christian. One spring week, both families get ready for a big holiday meal.

At Maya's house, it is Passover. The table has a special plate. It holds a bone, bitter herbs, and a green vegetable. There is flat bread called matzah. Maya's grandpa reads from a book called the Haggadah. It tells how Moses led the Israelites out of slavery in Egypt.

A few days later, Leo's family eats an Easter dinner. Before the meal, they went to church. The pastor talked about Jesus. She also read the story of Moses and the escape from Egypt. Leo is surprised. "That's Maya's story too!" he whispers.

The next day, the cousins compare notes. Both families know Moses. Both read the same old books. But Leo's family also reads books about Jesus. Maya's family does not see those books as part of their Bible. "So we share some stories," Maya says. "But not all of them."

The Story of the Jewish People

9.1

Abraham and One God

Main ideaJews, Christians and Muslims all tell stories about Abraham, who Jews see as the first of their ancestors.

The Hebrew Bible tells a story about a man named . It says God told him to leave his home and go to a new land. God promised to make his family into a great people. Jewish tradition calls Abraham the first father of the Jewish people.

In the story, God and Abraham make a . A covenant is a serious promise between two sides. Jews believe God promised to care for Abraham’s family. Abraham’s family promised to follow God. Jews believe this promise still links them to God today.

Abraham is important to Christians and Muslims too. That is why Judaism, Christianity and Islam are sometimes called the Abrahamic religions. All three teach that there is only one God. But each tradition tells Abraham’s story in its own way.

Moses and Mount Sinai

Main ideaJews tell how Moses led the Israelites out of Egypt and how God gave the Torah at Mount Sinai.

Every spring, Jewish families retell a story at . The Torah says the Israelites were slaves in Egypt long ago. A leader named asked the king to let them go. In the story, God helped them escape. Passover celebrates that freedom.

After the escape, the story says, the people camped by a mountain called Sinai. Jews believe God gave Moses the Torah there. The Ten Commandments are part of it. They include rules like "Do not steal" and "Honor your father and mother."

At the Passover meal, called a seder, families eat special foods. Flat bread called matzah reminds them the Israelites left in a hurry. Bitter herbs remind them how hard slavery was. Children ask questions, and adults answer with the story.

The Synagogue and Shabbat

Main ideaMany Jews gather at a synagogue and rest on Shabbat, from Friday sunset to Saturday night.

On Friday evening, just before sunset, a family lights two candles. They say a blessing over bread and grape juice or wine. This begins , the Jewish day of rest. Shabbat lasts until Saturday night, when three stars can be seen.

The Torah says God rested on the seventh day after making the world. It tells people to rest too. Many Jews do not work on Shabbat. They spend time with family, pray, eat together and read. Different families keep Shabbat in different ways.

Many Jews go to a on Shabbat. A synagogue is a Jewish house of prayer and learning. A rabbi is a teacher who leads the community. Jews may be Orthodox, Conservative or Reform. These groups keep the old laws in different ways.

Kosher Food and the Jewish Year

Main ideaKosher rules guide what many Jews eat, and holidays like Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur mark the Jewish year.

The Torah has rules about food. Food that follows these rules is called . For example, kosher rules do not allow pork. Meat and milk are not eaten together. Some Jews keep every rule. Others keep some or none.

The Jewish year has special days. Rosh Hashanah is the Jewish New Year, in the fall. People eat apples dipped in honey for a sweet year. Ten days later comes . On that day, many adults fast and pray. They ask forgiveness for wrongs they did.

Other holidays remember stories. Hanukkah lasts eight nights in winter. Families light candles on a menorah. They remember a time long ago when, as Jewish tradition tells it, a little oil burned for eight days in the Temple.

Muhammad in Mecca

Main ideaMuslims believe Muhammad was the last prophet, and that God first spoke to him through an angel in a cave near Mecca.

Muslims tell the story of . He was born in Mecca, in Arabia, around 570 CE. His parents died when he was young. He grew up to be a trader known for being honest. People called him "the trustworthy one."

Muhammad often went to a cave on a mountain to think and pray. Muslims believe that one night, in about 610 CE, the angel Gabriel came to him there. The angel brought words from God. Muslims believe Muhammad is God’s last .

Muhammad began to teach that there is only one God. Some people in Mecca did not like his message. In 622 CE, he and his followers moved to the city of Medina. This move is called the Hijra. The Islamic calendar counts its years from it.

Giving and Fasting

Main ideaThe third and fourth pillars are giving to people in need and fasting during Ramadan.

The third pillar is . It means giving to people in need. Muslims who have enough give a set share of their savings each year, often about 2.5 percent. The money helps the poor, the hungry and others in need.

The fourth pillar is fasting in . Ramadan is a month in the Islamic calendar. From dawn to sunset, healthy adults do not eat or drink. Children, sick people and travelers do not have to fast. They can join in other ways.

Muslims believe fasting helps them grow closer to God. It helps them feel thankful and remember hungry people. Each evening, families break the fast with iftar. Many start with a date and water, as Muhammad did.

Eid: Two Big Celebrations

Main ideaMuslims celebrate two Eids: Eid al-Fitr after Ramadan and Eid al-Adha at the time of the hajj.

When Ramadan ends, families wake up early. They put on new clothes. They go to a big morning prayer, sometimes in a park. Afterward, people hug and say "Eid Mubarak!" This means "blessed celebration." The day is .

Eid al-Fitr celebrates the end of the fast. Before the prayer, Muslims give food or money to the poor. Then comes a feast with family and friends. Children often get gifts or money.

The second Eid is . It comes at the time of the hajj. It remembers the story of Abraham’s faith in God. Many families share meat in three parts: for the family, for friends and for the poor.

Sunni and Shia, Near and Far

Main ideaMuslims live all over the world, and most are Sunni or Shia, two big families within Islam.

Islam began in Arabia. Today about 1.9 billion Muslims live around the world. The country with the most Muslims is Indonesia, in Asia. Many Muslims also live in Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, Egypt, Nigeria and the United States.

After Muhammad died in 632 CE, Muslims disagreed about who should lead them. This led to two big families. Muslims are the larger group, about 85 to 90 percent. Muslims are the rest. Many Shia live in Iran and Iraq.

Sunni and Shia share the Qur’an, the Five Pillars and belief in one God. They differ on some leaders and customs. The Chicago area is home to many Muslims. Their families came from South Asia, the Middle East, Africa and beyond, and many are Black Americans.
