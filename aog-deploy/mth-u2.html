Adding and Subtracting

Operations

You have two cookies. A friend gives you three more. How many now? Your sister takes one. How many are left? These are adding and subtracting questions. You meet them every day, at lunch, at recess and at the store.

In this unit you will put groups together and take groups apart. You will learn what the equal sign really means. You will find a missing number in any spot. Then you will learn quick tricks: doubles, making ten and fact families.

By the end, you will know your facts within 20. You will solve story problems with two steps. And you will check your answer a second way, so you know it is right.

The big question

How do we put numbers together, take them apart, and know the answer is right?

How we figured it out
Step 1

Put two groups together and count them all

Step 2

Count on from the first number instead of starting at 1

Step 3

Take some away and count what is left

Step 4

Compare two groups to find how many more

Step 5

Split a whole into two parts with a number bond

Step 6

Read the equal sign as the same as, and find a missing number

Step 7

Learn doubles and the pairs that make ten

Step 8

Use near doubles and make ten to add within 20

Step 9

Think addition to subtract, and build fact families

Step 10

Solve two-step stories and check the answer a second way

The story

Nine Ducks on the Pond

Count the ducks before and after some fly away.

Nine ducks float on a pond in a Chicago park. A girl named Rosa counts them from the path. One, two, three, all the way to nine. She writes 9 on her hand with her finger so she will not forget.

A dog barks. Four ducks flap their wings and fly away. Rosa looks again. Some ducks are gone. How many stay on the water? She counts them: five ducks. Nine take away four is five.

Later, two ducks come back. Rosa counts again. Five and two more. Now there are seven ducks. The number went down, then it went up. Taking away and putting together, right there on the pond.

Talk about itHow could Rosa know there are 5 ducks without counting every duck again?

Putting Together

3.1

Adding Puts Groups Together

Main ideaAdding puts two groups together to find how many in all.

Mia has 3 red apples. Ben has 2 green apples. Put them in one basket. Count them all: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. There are 5 apples in all. We the two groups.

We write it like this: 3 + 2 = 5. The + sign means put together. The answer is called the . Five is the sum of 3 and 2.

Try 4 + 3. Start with 4 blocks. Add 3 more blocks. Count them all. You get 7. So 4 + 3 = 7.

Adding More to a Group

Main ideaWhen more come, say the first number and count on.

Six birds sit on a fence. Two more birds land. How many birds now? Start at 6. Count on: 7, 8. There are 8 birds. 6 + 2 = 8.

You do not need to count from 1. Say the first number. Then count on for the other part. This is called . It is faster.

Try 5 + 3. Say 5. Count on three times: 6, 7, 8. The sum is 8. Check it with fingers. Five fingers and three more make 8.

Taking Away

Main ideaSubtracting takes some away and finds how many are left.

Sam has 7 grapes. He eats 3. How many are left? Start with 7. Take away 3. Count what is left: 4. We . 7 − 3 = 4.

The − sign means take away. The answer is called the . Four is the difference between 7 and 3.

Try 9 − 4. Draw 9 circles. Cross out 4. Count the circles not crossed out. There are 5. So 9 − 4 = 5.

How Many More?

Main ideaSubtract to compare two groups and find how many more.

Jo has 8 stickers. Kim has 5. Who has more? Jo. How many more? Line them up. Match each of Kim’s stickers to one of Jo’s. Three of Jo’s stickers have no match. Jo has 3 more.

We can subtract to . 8 − 5 = 3. The bigger number goes first. Then take away the smaller number. The difference tells how many .

Try it. A tower has 6 blocks. Another has 2. How many more in the tall tower? 6 − 2 = 4. The tall tower has 4 more blocks.

3.5

Parts and the Whole

Main ideaA number bond shows a whole and its two parts.

Hold up 7 fingers. Keep 3 down. The parts are 7 and 3. The is 10. A shows this. The whole sits on top. The two parts sit below. 10 splits into 7 and 3.

The parts always add up to the whole. 7 + 3 = 10. The whole minus one gives the other part. 10 − 7 = 3.

Find the parts of 5. 1 and 4. 2 and 3. 0 and 5. Each pair makes 5. Try it with 5 counters. Split them two ways.

3.6

What Equals Means

Main ideaThe equal sign means both sides are the same amount.

Two plates. One has 3 crackers and 2 more. The other has 5 crackers. Both plates hold 5. They are . 3 + 2 = 5. The = sign means the same as.

The equal sign is not just an answer sign. It says both sides match. 5 = 3 + 2 is . 4 + 1 = 3 + 2 is true too. Both sides make 5.

Is 6 = 2 + 3 true? Count 2 + 3. That is 5. Six is not 5. So it is false. Check both sides every time.

Find the Missing Number

Main ideaA missing number can be in any spot; use parts and wholes to find it.

Dan has 3 marbles. He gets some more. Now he has 7. How many did he get? 3 + ? = 7. Count on from 3 to 7: 4, 5, 6, 7. That is 4 jumps. So 3 + 4 = 7.

The can be in any spot. ? + 2 = 6. Think: what and 2 make 6? Four. Or 8 − ? = 5. Think: 8 take away what leaves 5? Three, since 5 + 3 = 8.

Doubles and Making Ten

Main ideaDoubles and pairs that make ten are facts worth knowing by heart.

Two hands. Five fingers each. 5 + 5 = 10. That is a . Both parts are the same. 2 + 2 = 4. 3 + 3 = 6. 4 + 4 = 8. Doubles are quick to learn.

Pairs that are handy too. Hold up 6 fingers. How many are down? Four. So 6 + 4 = 10. Other pairs: 9 and 1, 8 and 2, 7 and 3, 5 and 5.

What goes with 8 to make 10? Hold up 8 fingers. Two are down. 8 + 2 = 10. Check by counting on from 8: 9, 10. Yes.

The story

The Fact House on the Wall

A paper house holds four facts that all belong together.

Mr. Ortiz's first-grade class has a paper house on the wall. The roof says 10. The two windows say 6 and 4. The door has four lines on it. Each line is a number fact. The class reads the house every morning.

Deshawn reads the door out loud. 6 + 4 = 10. 4 + 6 = 10. 10 − 6 = 4. 10 − 4 = 6. Every line uses the same three numbers. That is why the facts live in one house. They are a family.

On Friday the roof number changes to 13. The windows say 8 and 5. Deshawn writes the four new facts by himself. He knows one fact, so he knows all four. The house makes facts easy to remember.

Talk about itWhy do the three numbers in the house make exactly four facts?

4.1

Count On to Add

Main ideaTo add a small number, start with the big number and count on.

Nine kids wait in line for the slide. Three more join. Say 9. : 10, 11, 12. There are 12 kids. 9 + 3 = 12.

Always start with the bigger number. For 2 + 8, say 8 first. Count on: 9, 10. The sum is 10. The does not change the sum. 2 + 8 = 8 + 2.

Use counting on for 1, 2 or 3. For bigger numbers, use another trick. Try 11 + 2. Say 11. Then 12, 13. So 11 + 2 = 13.

4.2

Count Back to Subtract

Main ideaTo take away a small number, start at the whole and count back.

Eleven cookies sit on a tray. Grandpa eats 2. Say 11. : 10, 9. Nine cookies are left. 11 − 2 = 9.

Count back only for 1, 2 or 3. Use your fingers to keep track. Each finger is one step back. For 13 − 3, say 13. Back: 12, 11, 10. The difference is 10.

A common mistake is to say the start number as a step. 11 − 2 is not 10. Start at 11. Step to 10, then to 9. Two steps. The answer is 9.

4.3

Near Doubles

Main ideaIf you know a double, you can find the fact right next to it.

You know 6 + 6 = 12. What is 6 + 7? Seven is one more than 6. So the sum is one more than 12. 6 + 7 = 13. That is a .

Try 8 + 9. Think 8 + 8 = 16. Nine is one more than 8. So 8 + 9 = 17. Or think 9 + 9 = 18 and take one away. That gives 17 again.

Know these doubles. 6 + 6 = 12. 7 + 7 = 14. 8 + 8 = 16. 9 + 9 = 18. 10 + 10 = 20. A near double is one more or one less.

Main ideaTo subtract, ask what you add to the part to reach the whole.

What is 13 − 9? Think: 9 plus what makes 13? from 9: 10, 11, 12, 13. Four jumps. 9 + 4 = 13. So 13 − 9 = 4.

Adding and subtracting each other. 9 + 4 = 13. So 13 − 9 = 4. And 13 − 4 = 9. All three facts share the same parts.

Section 3

Fact Families and Two Steps

4.6

Fact Families

Main ideaThree numbers make a family of two adding facts and two subtracting facts.

Take 4, 6 and 10. Ten is the whole. Four and 6 are the parts. Write four facts: 4 + 6 = 10. 6 + 4 = 10. 10 − 4 = 6. 10 − 6 = 4. This is a .

The whole is always the sum. The whole comes first in the subtracting facts. If you know one fact, you know all four.

Try 5, 8 and 13. Adding: 5 + 8 = 13 and 8 + 5 = 13. Subtracting: 13 − 5 = 8 and 13 − 8 = 5. A double like 7 + 7 = 14 has only two facts. Both parts match.

Words to know
fact family

Check yourself

1. Which fact is in the family of 3, 9 and 12?

Why: 12 is the whole. 12 − 3 = 9 is true. The whole comes first when subtracting.

2. A family has 2 + 7 = 9. Which subtracting fact belongs?

3. How many facts are in the family of 6, 8 and 14?

Why: 6 + 8, 8 + 6, 14 − 6 and 14 − 8. That is four facts.

by a second way. Ava got 5 and gave away 3. That is 2 more overall. 8 + 2 = 10. Same answer. Two ways, same answer, so it is right.
