Across first, then up

Start at the corner where the two lines meet. That corner is zero, and every trip begins there.

Across first, then up. Swap the two numbers and you land somewhere else entirely — (2, 3) and (3, 2) are two different places on the same grid.

Two things people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

(2, 7) means 7 across and 2 up.

The first number is always across. (2, 7) is 2 across and 7 up — a tall skinny trip. (7, 2) is a wide flat one, and it is a completely different point.

Walk the hallway before you climb the stairs.

⚠️ People often think…

An angle with long sides drawn is a bigger angle.

Only the opening counts. Stretch both sides out to a mile and the angle is exactly the same size. Think of a door: the door does not get wider when you open it further.

Decide acute or obtuse before you read any number.

Helpful Hints

🧭 Reading a protractor — the three steps

Center mark ON the vertex → baseline along one side, at 0° → read where the other side crosses the scale that STARTED at 0 on your first side → check: acute angles read under 90, obtuse angles read over 90.

Center, zero, read, check.

📍 Plotting a point — across, then up

(x, y): start at the origin (0, 0). The FIRST number is how far RIGHT along the x-axis. The SECOND number is how far UP. Put the dot, label it. (3, 5) and (5, 3) are two different points — that is why the pair is called ordered.

Cross the floor before you take the elevator.

⚠️ Traps the test loves

  • Reading the wrong protractor scale: 130° for an angle that is plainly acute. Decide acute or obtuse FIRST.
  • Judging an angle by how long its sides are drawn. Only the opening counts.
  • Swapping x and y: plotting (2, 7) as 7 across and 2 up.
  • Saying a square is not a rectangle, or a tilted square is a “diamond.” Names come from the rule, not the look.
  • Subtracting an angle from 100 instead of 90, or from 360 instead of 180.
  • Finding a distance by adding the coordinates. From (2, 6) to (9, 6) is 9 − 2, not 9 + 2.

🎯 How the test will ask

  • A drawing of an angle — “acute, right, obtuse or straight?” Compare it with a paper corner.
  • A protractor on an angle — “what is the measure?” Two scales; pick the one that started at 0 on the side.
  • A right angle or straight angle split in two — “find the missing piece.” Subtract from 90 or 180.
  • Lines in a picture — “parallel, perpendicular or intersecting?” Look for the square corner or for lines that never meet.
  • A shape — “which names fit?” Test every rule; a square passes four of them.
  • A grid — plot (x, y), name a point, find the fourth corner, or count the distance between two points on one line.
  • Two number rules — write the pairs, plot them, say what you notice.

✅ Before the test, can you…

  • Tell a line, a segment and a ray apart, and draw each one?
  • Name an angle as acute, right, obtuse or straight on sight?
  • Measure an angle with a protractor and get the right scale?
  • Find a missing angle inside a right angle or a straight angle?
  • Explain why a square is a rectangle, and a rhombus, and a parallelogram?
  • Plot a point from an ordered pair, and write the pair for a point?
  • Find the fourth corner of a rectangle on a grid, and the length of its sides?
