Muhammad, the Qur'an and the First Community

Islam
Big questionHow did one man's message in a mountain cave become the faith of a whole community?
The story

The Trustworthy One on the Mountain

A respected merchant of Mecca goes to a quiet cave to think, and Muslims say his life changed there.

Mecca, around the year 610. The city sits in a dry valley in western Arabia. Camel caravans come and go, carrying spices, cloth and leather. Traders from many tribes crowd its markets. At the center of the city stands the Kaaba, a cube-shaped building that Arabs from far away visit.

Among the merchants is a man of about forty named Muhammad. He lost his parents as a child and was raised by his grandfather and then his uncle. He works in the caravan trade. People in Mecca call him al-Amin, which means "the trustworthy." They leave their valuables with him because they know he is honest.

Muhammad often climbs a rocky mountain outside the city. Near the top is a small cave called Hira. There he spends nights alone, thinking and praying. According to Muslim tradition, one night in the month of Ramadan, the angel Gabriel (Jibril in Arabic) came to him in the cave.

Muslims tell that the angel gave one command: "Recite!" Muhammad answered that he was not a reciter. The command came again, and then the first words of what Muslims call the Qur'an. Shaken, Muhammad hurried home to his wife, Khadijah. She comforted him and, in Muslim tradition, became the first person to accept his message.

Over the next twenty-some years, Muslims believe, more revelations came. They were memorized, recited and later gathered into one book. Today about two billion people call themselves Muslims. This chapter follows the story as Muslims tell it and as historians study it.

Section 1

Arabia and the Prophet

19.1

Arabia Before Islam

Main ideaBefore Islam, Arabia was a land of trading towns and desert tribes, with Mecca and its Kaaba at the center of trade and worship.

Picture the Arabian Peninsula around 600 CE. Most of it is desert. People live in oasis towns or move with herds of camels and goats. They belong to , large family groups that protect their members. Loyalty to the tribe mattered more than loyalty to any king, because Arabia had no single ruler.

Mecca was a trading town. Caravans carried goods between Yemen in the south and Syria in the north. The powerful Quraysh tribe ran much of this trade. Mecca also held the , a stone shrine. Each year, people from many tribes came to worship there, and fighting was forbidden during certain months so pilgrims and traders could travel safely.

Most Arabs at this time worshipped many gods. In Muslim tradition, the Kaaba held hundreds of idols, or statues of gods. Some Arabs were Jews or Christians, and a few, called hanifs in Muslim sources, worshipped one God. Muslims call this time the Jahiliyya, meaning "the age of ignorance," because in their view it came before God’s final message.

19.2

The Cave and the Message

Main ideaMuslims believe that around 610 CE, the angel Gabriel brought Muhammad the first words of the Qur'an in the cave of Hira.

Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570 CE. He grew up as an orphan and became a trader. At about age twenty-five he married Khadijah, a widow who ran her own caravan business. As an adult, he often went to the cave of Hira to be alone and reflect.

Muslims believe that around 610 CE, the angel Gabriel came to him there with a message from God. They call these messages , words that God makes known. The first words, in Muslim tradition, are the opening of Surah 96: a command to recite in the name of the Lord who created.

For Muslims, this night made Muhammad a , a person God chooses to carry a message. The Qur’an describes him as the "seal of the prophets," the last in a line that Muslims say includes Abraham, Moses and Jesus. Jews and Christians have their own teachings about those figures and do not share this view of Muhammad.

19.3

From Mecca to Medina

Main ideaIn 622 Muhammad and his followers moved from Mecca to Medina, a journey called the hijra that starts the Islamic calendar.

Muhammad began to preach in Mecca. He taught that there is one God, that people will be judged for their deeds, and that the rich must care for the poor. Some people joined him. Many leaders of the Quraysh did not. His message challenged the gods of the Kaaba and the trade that pilgrims brought.

The early Muslims faced insults, boycotts and violence. In 622, people from the town of Yathrib invited Muhammad to lead them. He and his followers moved there. This move is called the , Arabic for "migration." Yathrib became known as Medina, "the city" of the Prophet.

In Medina, Muslims formed their first , or community. Muhammad was both its religious teacher and its leader. Medina also had Jewish tribes, and an agreement often called the Constitution of Medina set out rules for the town’s groups. Muslims later made 622 the first year of their calendar.

19.4

Return to Mecca

Main ideaIn 630 Muhammad returned to Mecca, and Muslims rededicated the Kaaba to one God; he died in Medina in 632.

For several years, Medina and Mecca were at war. Battles such as Badr (624) and Uhud (625) are well known in Muslim history. In 628 the two sides signed a truce at a place called Hudaybiyya. When the truce broke down, Muhammad marched toward Mecca with a large army in 630.

Mecca surrendered with little fighting. Muslim sources say Muhammad forgave most of his old enemies. He had the idols removed from the Kaaba. Muslims believe this returned the shrine to the worship of one God, as they say Abraham first intended. Many Arab tribes then joined the Muslim community.

In 632 Muhammad made a pilgrimage to Mecca and gave what Muslims call the . He died later that year in Medina. His followers faced a hard question: who should lead the community now? Their answers to that question still shape Islam today.

Section 2

The Qur'an

19.5

A Book Meant to Be Recited

Main ideaMuslims believe the Qur'an is God's word in Arabic, first memorized and recited, then gathered into one written book.

The word Qur&#x27;an means "recitation." Muslims believe it is the exact word of God, given to Muhammad through Gabriel over about 23 years. At first, followers memorized the verses and wrote some on bone, leather and palm leaves. Reciting aloud was the main way people learned it.

Muslim tradition says the verses were collected into one book soon after Muhammad died. Under the third caliph, Uthman (ruled 644–656), a standard copy was made and sent to major cities. Muslims hold that the text has been kept the same since then.

Muslims believe the Qur’an is fully the Qur’an only in Arabic. Translations help readers understand the meaning, but most Muslims treat them as explanations, not as the Qur’an itself. Many Muslims around the world pray in Arabic even when it is not their home language. A person who memorizes the whole book is honored with the title .

19.6

Surahs, Ayahs and the Sunnah

Main ideaThe Qur'an has 114 surahs made of ayahs, and Muslims also learn from the Sunnah, the example of Muhammad recorded in hadith.

The Qur’an is divided into 114 chapters called . Each surah is made of verses called . After the short opening surah, the surahs are arranged roughly from longest to shortest. So the Qur’an does not tell one story from start to end, the way a history book might.

The first surah, al-Fatiha ("The Opening"), is only seven verses long. Muslims recite it in every unit of daily prayer. It praises God as merciful and asks to be guided on the straight path. Short surahs near the end, such as Surah 112, are often the first ones children learn.

Muslims also learn from the , the example of how Muhammad lived. Reports of his words and actions are called . Scholars sorted hadith by how reliable their chains of reporters seemed. Sunni and Shia Muslims use partly different hadith collections.

Section 3

The Five Pillars

19.7

Faith and Prayer

Main ideaThe first two pillars are the shahada, the statement of faith, and salat, prayer five times a day.

Muslims describe five core practices as the of Islam. Like pillars holding up a building, they support a Muslim’s life. They appear in a well-known hadith. Sunni and Shia Muslims both keep these practices, though Shia teachers list their core duties a little differently.

The first pillar is the , the statement of faith: "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the messenger of God." Saying it sincerely, Muslims believe, is how a person enters Islam. Muslims repeat it in prayer and whisper it to newborn babies.

The second pillar is , prayer five times a day: at dawn, midday, afternoon, sunset and night. Before praying, Muslims wash in a set way. They face the Kaaba in Mecca, a direction called the qibla. Prayer includes standing, bowing and touching the forehead to the ground.

19.8

Giving and Fasting

Main ideaZakat is giving part of one's wealth to people in need, and sawm is fasting from dawn to sunset in the month of Ramadan.

The third pillar is , giving to those in need. Muslims who have savings above a set amount give a share each year, commonly 2.5 percent. The Qur’an lists who should receive it, including the poor. Muslims believe zakat purifies wealth and reminds them that it comes from God. Many also give extra charity, called sadaqa.

The fourth pillar is , fasting during , the ninth month of the Islamic year. From dawn until sunset, adult Muslims who are able do not eat or drink. Children, the sick, travelers and some others are excused. Many families wake before dawn for a meal and break the fast at sunset.

Muslims believe the Qur’an was first revealed in Ramadan. So many try to read or hear the whole Qur’an during the month, often at special night prayers. The month ends with Eid al-Fitr, the Festival of Breaking the Fast, with prayers, new clothes, gifts and meals.

19.9

The Pilgrimage to Mecca

Main ideaThe hajj is a pilgrimage to Mecca that Muslims who are able should make once in a lifetime.

The fifth pillar is the , the pilgrimage to Mecca. It takes place each year in Dhul Hijjah, the last month of the Islamic calendar. Every adult Muslim who is healthy and can afford it is expected to go at least once. In recent years, about two million people have made the hajj at once.

Pilgrims put on simple white clothing called . For men it is two unsewn white cloths. Muslims say this shows that everyone is equal before God, rich or poor. Pilgrims walk seven times around the Kaaba and walk between two small hills, remembering Hagar searching for water for her son Ishmael.

Pilgrims also stand in prayer on the plain of Arafat. Then Muslims everywhere celebrate Eid al-Adha, the Festival of the Sacrifice. It remembers the story of Abraham’s willingness to sacrifice his son, which appears in the Qur’an. Families share meat with relatives and with the poor.

Section 4

One Faith, Many Muslims

19.10

Sunni and Shia

Main ideaSunni and Shia Muslims share the Qur'an and the Five Pillars but disagree about who should have led after Muhammad.

After Muhammad died in 632, most of his companions chose Abu Bakr, his close friend, as the first caliph. Those who accepted this line of leaders became known as Muslims, from sunnah, "the way." Today most Muslims, about 85 to 90 percent, are Sunni.

Another group believed Muhammad had chosen his cousin and son-in-law, Ali, to lead. They became the , from shiat Ali, "the party of Ali." Shia Muslims honor a line of leaders called imams, starting with Ali. Most Shia live in Iran, Iraq, Bahrain, Azerbaijan and Lebanon, with many elsewhere.

In 680, Ali’s son Husayn was killed with his small band at Karbala, in Iraq. Shia Muslims mourn him each year on Ashura, the tenth day of the month of Muharram. Sunni and Shia Muslims pray, fast and make the hajj. Many Muslims stress what the two groups share.

19.11

The Mosque and the Moon

Main ideaMuslims gather at the mosque, especially for Friday prayer, and follow a lunar calendar whose months move through the seasons.

A is a place where Muslims pray together. The Arabic word masjid means "place of bowing down." Inside there are no statues or pictures of people. A niche in the wall, the mihrab, shows the qibla. Many mosques have a tall tower called a minaret, from which the call to prayer is given.

At midday on Friday, Muslims gather for jumu’ah, the congregational prayer. A leader gives a short sermon, then leads the prayer. Men are expected to attend. Women may attend and often pray in their own section. Mosques also hold classes, weddings, meals and community events.

The follows the moon. Each month begins with the new crescent moon, so the year has about 354 days. That is about 11 days shorter than the solar year. So Ramadan comes about 11 days earlier each year and moves through every season over about 33 years.

Islamic Civilization
Big questionHow did Muslim states, scholars and traders connect so much of the world, and how did Islam come to America?
The story

Gold and Books Across the Sahara

A caravan crosses the desert toward Timbuktu, carrying salt, gold, cloth and books.

The Sahara, around the year 1350. A long line of camels moves across the sand in the cool hours before sunrise. Some carry slabs of salt cut from desert mines. Others carry cloth, copper and paper. The guides watch the stars and the shapes of the dunes to find their way.

The traders are heading south, toward the Niger River and the empire of Mali. Mali is rich in gold. North African merchants trade salt for it, since salt is scarce in the lands farther south. Along with goods, the traders bring something else: books written in Arabic.

At the end of the road lies Timbuktu. The city has mosques built of sun-dried mud brick, with wooden beams sticking out of the walls. Students gather around teachers to study the Qur'an, law, grammar, astronomy and medicine. Families copy books by hand and keep them in wooden chests.

A generation earlier, Mali's ruler, Mansa Musa, had made the hajj to Mecca. Writers of the time said he carried so much gold that its price fell in Cairo when he passed through. He came home with scholars and builders. Timbuktu grew into a center of learning.

The same faith that began in Mecca had now reached West Africa, Spain, Persia and India. This chapter follows how that happened: through rulers and armies, but also through traders, teachers, translators and ordinary families.

The Caliphates

20.1

The First Four Caliphs

Main ideaAfter Muhammad, four caliphs led the Muslim community from 632 to 661, and Muslim rule spread quickly beyond Arabia.

A is a state led by a caliph, a successor to Muhammad as leader of the community. The first four caliphs were Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali. They ruled from 632 to 661. Sunni Muslims call them the , or "rightly guided" caliphs. Shia Muslims honor Ali as the first rightful imam.

In these years, Arab Muslim armies defeated much of the Byzantine and Persian empires. They took Syria, Iraq, Egypt and Persia. Historians note that most people in these lands did not become Muslim right away. Conversion happened slowly, over centuries.

Jews and Christians under Muslim rule were often treated as , protected peoples. They could keep their faith and their places of worship. They paid a special tax, the jizya, and faced some limits. How they were treated changed from ruler to ruler and from place to place.

20.2

The Umayyads of Damascus

Main ideaThe Umayyad caliphs ruled from Damascus from 661 to 750, over an empire stretching from Spain to Central Asia.

In 661 a governor named Muawiya founded the caliphate. He made Damascus, in Syria, his capital. Leadership now passed from father to son, like a royal family. The Umayyads ruled until 750.

Under the Umayyads, Muslim rule reached across North Africa and into Spain in 711. In the east it reached Central Asia and the Indus River. Arabic became the language of government. The Umayyads made the first Islamic coins with only words on them, no pictures.

The Umayyads built famous buildings. The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem was completed around 691. The Great Mosque of Damascus followed soon after. Both used mosaics of plants and patterns instead of pictures of people. Some Muslims criticized the Umayyads for living like kings, and in 750 a rival family overthrew them.

20.3

The Abbasids of Baghdad

Main ideaThe Abbasids ruled from 750 and built Baghdad, a round city that became one of the largest in the world.

In 750 the family overthrew the Umayyads. They moved the capital east to Iraq. In 762 the caliph al-Mansur founded Baghdad on the Tigris River. It was planned as a round city with the palace and great mosque in the middle.

Baghdad sat on trade routes between the Mediterranean, Persia, India and China. Ships and caravans brought silk, spices, paper and ideas. Historians estimate that by about 900 it was one of the largest cities in the world, with many hundreds of thousands of people.

Over time, Abbasid power weakened. Local rulers took control of many regions, though they often still named the caliph in Friday prayers. In 1258 the Mongols captured and destroyed much of Baghdad and killed the caliph. Historians often mark that year as the end of an age.

20.4

The House of Wisdom

Main ideaIn Abbasid Baghdad, scholars of many faiths translated Greek, Persian and Indian books into Arabic and added new ideas.

Under the Abbasids, especially in the 800s, Baghdad became a center of . Scholars turned books on philosophy, medicine, math and astronomy from Greek, Persian, Syriac and Sanskrit into Arabic. A library and study center often called the is linked to this work. Historians still debate exactly how large and formal it was.

The translators came from many backgrounds. Some were Muslims. Many were Christians, such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq, who translated Greek medical books. Others were Jews or followers of other faiths. They shared Arabic as a common language of learning.

Muslim scholars did not only copy old books. They checked, argued with and added to them. Many Muslims pointed to the Qur’an and hadith that praise knowledge. Centuries later, many of these Arabic books were translated into Latin in Spain and Italy and helped shape learning in Europe.

Algebra and the Stars

Main ideaScholars in the Muslim world advanced math and astronomy; the word algebra comes from al-Khwarizmi's book.

Around 820, a scholar in Baghdad named Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote a book on solving equations. Its title included the Arabic word al-jabr, meaning "restoring." That word became our word . The word algorithm comes from a Latin form of his name.

Scholars in the Muslim world also used the numerals that came from India, including zero. Arabic writers spread them west. Europeans learned them later and called them "Arabic numerals." These are the digits 0 to 9 you use today.

, the study of the stars and planets, had practical uses for Muslims. They needed to find the qibla from any place, set prayer times by the sun and spot the new moon to begin each month. Astronomers built observatories and improved the astrolabe, a tool for measuring the height of stars.

Medicine and Paper

Main ideaMuslim-ruled cities built hospitals, produced great medical books and spread paper-making from China to the West.

Many cities in the Muslim world had hospitals, called in Persian. Some cared for patients of any faith, for free, and trained doctors. The Persian scholar Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, wrote The Canon of Medicine around 1025. European medical schools used a Latin translation of it for centuries.

Paper-making moved west through Egypt and North Africa to Spain. From there it reached the rest of Europe. Centuries later, cheap paper helped make the printing press useful. The same roads that carried scholars also carried their tools.

West and South

20.7

Al-Andalus and Córdoba

Main ideaMuslims ruled much of Spain, called al-Andalus, for centuries, and Córdoba became a great city of learning.

In 711, Muslim forces from North Africa crossed into Spain. Muslims called the lands they ruled . When the Abbasids overthrew the Umayyads, one Umayyad prince escaped to Spain. In 756 he set up his own state with Córdoba as its capital. In 929 his descendant declared himself caliph.

Córdoba grew into one of Europe’s largest cities. Work on its Great Mosque began in 785, with rows of red-and-white striped arches. The city had libraries, markets and paved streets. Muslims, Jews and Christians all lived there. The Jewish thinker Maimonides and the Muslim thinker Ibn Rushd were both born in Córdoba in the 1100s.

People sometimes call this time , "living together." Historians say that is partly true. There was real sharing of ideas and art, and also times of conflict and harsh rule. Christian kingdoms slowly took back the land. Granada, the last Muslim kingdom, fell in 1492.

Mali, Mansa Musa and Timbuktu

Main ideaIslam spread to West Africa along trade routes, and Mali's ruler Mansa Musa made Timbuktu a center of learning.

Islam reached West Africa mostly through trade, not conquest. Muslim merchants crossed the Sahara with salt and goods, trading for gold. Some West African rulers and traders became Muslim. Many ordinary people kept older local practices for a long time, and some blended them with Islam.

The empire of became rich from gold. Its most famous ruler, Mansa Musa, made the hajj in 1324. Writers of the time reported that he brought huge amounts of gold, which lowered its price in Cairo. He returned with scholars and an architect who worked on mosques in Timbuktu and Gao.

20.9

The Ottoman Empire

Main ideaThe Ottomans took Constantinople in 1453 and ruled a large Muslim empire until after World War I.

The began as a small Turkish state in Anatolia around 1300. In 1453 Sultan Mehmed II captured Constantinople, the Byzantine capital. The city became the Ottoman capital, later known as Istanbul. The great church Hagia Sophia was turned into a mosque.

At its height in the 1500s, under Suleiman the Magnificent, the empire ruled Southeast Europe, Turkey, the Middle East and North Africa. It also governed Mecca and Medina. Ottoman sultans used the title caliph. The architect Sinan designed many famous mosques, with large domes and slim minarets.

The Ottomans organized many non-Muslim communities into groups often called . Greek Orthodox Christians, Armenian Christians and Jews each ran many of their own religious and family matters. The empire ended after World War I. Turkey abolished the office of caliph in 1924.

Islam in South and Southeast Asia

Main ideaToday most Muslims live in Asia, and Indonesia has more Muslims than any other country.

Many people picture Islam mainly in the Middle East. In fact, most Muslims today live in South and Southeast Asia. Islam reached India through conquest, trade and teachers. The emperors ruled much of India from 1526. Their buildings include the Taj Mahal, a tomb completed around 1653.

In India, , Muslims who seek to feel close to God through prayer and devotion, drew many followers. Their shrines became places of pilgrimage for people of several faiths. Today India, Pakistan and Bangladesh together are home to hundreds of millions of Muslims.

Islam reached the islands of Southeast Asia mainly through merchants and Sufi teachers, starting around the 1200s. Today has more Muslims than any other country. Its Muslims mix Islam with local languages, arts and customs. Malaysia and Brunei also have Muslim majorities.

20.11

Muslims in America

Main ideaMuslims have been in America since the time of slavery, and today Chicago has one of the country's largest Muslim communities.

The first large group of Muslims in America came in chains. Historians estimate that a meaningful share of the enslaved West Africans brought to the Americas were Muslim. One of them, , was enslaved in North Carolina. In 1831 he wrote a short life story in Arabic.

Later, Muslims came as immigrants from the Middle East, South Asia and Africa. In the 1900s, some Black Americans formed new movements. The Nation of Islam, founded in Detroit in 1930, later made Chicago its headquarters. Its teachings differed from mainstream Islam. In 1975 Warith Deen Mohammed led many members toward Sunni Islam.

Today Chicago and its suburbs are home to many mosques. They serve Muslims with roots in Palestine, Pakistan, India, Bosnia, Somalia, Nigeria and many other places, as well as Black American and convert Muslims. Many mosques run schools, food pantries and free clinics. Muslims in America are one of the country’s most diverse faith groups.
