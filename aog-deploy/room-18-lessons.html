BEFORE YOU TEACH

How to Use the Closed-Loop Routing Tables

A one-page “Score → Intervention Crosswalk” opens every unit of Book 2 · 3–5.

1 · Screen. Run the Grace Compass check-in. It scores three domains — A (Regulation & Well-Being), B (Self-Compassion & Growth), C (Social Competency & Repair).

2 · Route to the lowest domain. A student’s lowest-scoring domain is where support is needed most. Find that domain on the unit’s crosswalk; the rows beneath it are this unit’s targeted lessons, novel scenes, and anchor charts.

3 · Run the Teacher Move. The last column gives a one-line, low-prep way to deliver the row — usually a scaffold so students fill content, not structure.

4 · Mind the red flag. A red ★ (elevated) or ★★ (highest-sensitivity) badge means safety steps apply: counselor co-facilitation, posted crisis resources, and — for ★★ — pre/post consultation. Never route a flagged row without them.

Doing well? If all three domains score 75+, use the ◆ enrichment row — an integration capstone — instead of a support row.

Important: deliver lessons in order. The crosswalk tells you where a student’s need is met within the unit — it is not a license to reorder the curriculum. The same routing logic runs the Grace Compass digital screener, so print and screen always agree.

Who Am I?

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →This lesson’s worksheet is interactive — open it: type in it, turn it in, Print my copy / Print it blank →
DURATION

35–40 min · Week 1

OBJECTIVE

Students identify at least three personal strengths or values and articulate why each student is uniquely worthwhile.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness: identifying personal strengths and self-perception

VOCABULARY

identity, strength, value, character

MATERIALS

Journals · Drawing supplies · WS1 Identity Web · Character strengths list · Index cards

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 3–5 · LATE ELEMENTARY

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1B.2a Describe personal skills and interests that one wants to develop.

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2B.2a Identify differences among and contributions of various social and cultural groups.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

LESSON AT A GLANCE: The goal is not content coverage — it is trust and safety. Go slowly. Prioritize the opening story. Every name goes on the wall — the words on the card are the student’s choice.
▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01Opening Story0–3 MIN

Before you hand students anything, tell them what you are building: “Once a week, for about 40 minutes, we are going to work on the inside of your life — not your grades, but the part of you that decides how you treat yourself when things go wrong.” Promise the journal privacy contract. Establish: feelings are information, not weaknesses. Every person is more than their worst moment.

02Classroom Compact3–8 MIN

Ask: What do you need this space to feel like to be honest here? Write student responses on chart paper. Ensure these four are included: (1) What is shared here stays here. (2) You never have to share more than you choose. (3) Feelings are information. (4) The Inner Coach standard applies to how we speak to each other. Post permanently.

ANCHOR CHART · IDENTITY WEB — TEACHER DISPLAY MODEL

Post your completed example BEFORE distributing WS1. Five branches: Things I Love · Good At · Matter to Me · People Don't Know · Still Figuring Out.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
03Identity Web (WS1)8–18 MIN

Distribute WS1. Students write name in center and branch out. Grade 3: Drawing is a full response. Grades 4–5: Push toward values, not just interests.

04Strengths Inventory18–26 MIN

Display the character strengths list. Students circle three that feel most true and write one sentence for each. Grade 3: Circle only. Grades 4–5: Which strength required the most work to build?

05Partner Share26–32 MIN

Students tell one partner one thing from their identity web. Listen without commenting. Switch. “What did it feel like to just be heard?”

06Index Card + Class Display32–36 MIN

Students write one true thing about themselves that often goes unseen. Posting the card’s words is voluntary. A student who would rather not post can put up a card with just their name, so every name is on the wall. “Look at this wall. This is who we are.”

IN-LESSON SCAN

As students write their index cards: watch for prolonged blank staring, erasing repeatedly, or a student who writes and immediately crumples the card. These students may be carrying a strong internal voice that says nothing about them is worth posting.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During partner share: scan for any student who sits silently while their partner speaks, or who says “I don't know” to every prompt. A student unable to name a single thing about themselves is giving you early, significant data about self-perception.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
Keep identity web simple and drawing-focused. Reduce strengths list to 10–12. Every child must see their name on the wall by end of lesson.Encourage depth — push toward values, not interests. Introduce born strengths vs. developed strengths. The index card should name a value.

SCENARIO CARD 1-B · WHO AM I?

THE SITUATION

Amara is filling out her strengths inventory and cannot think of a single thing to write. She keeps crossing things out. “I'm not really good at anything,” she tells her partner.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

Why might Amara struggle to name her own strengths? What is the difference between a born strength and one developed through effort?

Which students struggled to identify strengths — defaulting to “I don't know” or minimizing? This is early data about self-perception that will matter all year.

Emotions Are Information

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →
DURATION

35–40 min · Week 2

OBJECTIVE

Students name at least five emotions and describe feelings as useful messengers rather than problems to avoid or hide.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness: identifying and labeling emotions; emotional expression

VOCABULARY

emotion, feeling, messenger, regulate, identify

MATERIALS

Journals · Emotions anchor chart · Emotion thermometer worksheet · Drawn face images

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 3–5 · LATE ELEMENTARY

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1A.2a Describe a range of emotions and the situations that cause them.

1A.2b Describe and demonstrate ways to express emotions in a socially acceptable manner.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

OPENING REVIEW
2 Minutes

Show the identity web from L1. “Last week we mapped who we are. Today we add something — our emotions. What is one emotion you felt since last week?” Take 4–5 responses. “Every one of those feelings — we're going to learn what it's trying to tell you.”

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01Hook — Faces Activity2–7 MIN

Show 4–5 drawn faces. “What might this person be feeling? What might have happened right before?” “Emotions don't appear randomly. They are always a response to something.”

ANCHOR CHART · EMOTIONS ARE MESSENGERS

Build WITH students — co-construction increases ownership. Add each emotion as students name it. For each: what message does it carry? This chart stays posted all year.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
02Direct Instruction7–16 MIN

Every emotion exists for a reason: FEAR = something feels dangerous. SADNESS = something I care about was lost. ANGER = a boundary was crossed. JOY = I am in alignment with what matters. EMBARRASSMENT = I care about belonging. LONELINESS = I need connection. “Emotions are not problems. They are data.”

03Co-build Anchor Chart16–25 MIN

Push beyond happy/sad/mad: frustrated, proud, embarrassed, relieved, confused, hopeful, lonely, overwhelmed, curious. For each: What might this emotion be trying to tell us?

04Emotion Thermometer25–32 MIN

Students fill in their thermometer — from lowest intensity to highest. Name what each level feels like in the body AND what it tells them. Personal — not collected.

05Journal32–38 MIN

Write about a time this week when you felt a strong emotion. What was it? What was it telling you? Drawing accepted. Private.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the faces activity: any student who consistently assigns negative emotions to every face, or who goes very still at a specific face, may be projecting something real. Note which emotion triggers the strongest reaction in the room.

IN-LESSON SCAN

While students complete the emotion thermometer: watch for a student who fills in only the very top or very bottom — indicating they may be experiencing emotional flooding or emotional numbness. Neither extreme is neutral. Note for reflection.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
Focus on 8–10 illustrated emotions. Keep the messenger concept concrete: “Fear is a guard dog — it barks when it thinks something is wrong.”Introduce emotional granularity — the difference between “I feel bad” and “I feel disappointed and a little embarrassed.”
SCENARIO CARD 1-C · EMOTIONS ARE INFORMATION

THE SITUATION

During a math test, Jayla's mind goes blank. She panics. After the test: “I hate when that happens — my feelings always ruin everything.”

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What message was Jayla's panic actually trying to send? Is the emotion the problem — or is it a messenger pointing at a different problem?

SCENARIO CARD 1-D · EMOTIONS ARE INFORMATION

THE SITUATION

Deon says “Fine” when asked about his day — but he actually felt lonely, left out, and invisible. He didn't have words for it.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What emotions was Deon actually feeling? Can you name them more precisely than “fine”? Why might it be hard to share emotions, even when we do have words?

EXIT TICKET

Journal: Name one emotion you felt today and what it was trying to tell you.

TEACHER REFLECTION

Which students showed the broadest emotional vocabulary? Which defaulted to “fine” or “mad”? Emotional vocabulary is strongly correlated with emotional regulation.

UNIT 1 · LESSON 3Lesson 3

Everyone Makes Mistakes

DURATION

30–35 min · Week 3

OBJECTIVE

Students articulate that mistakes are universal and identify the difference between a mistake and a character flaw.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness: growth mindset; accurate self-perception

VOCABULARY

mistake, failure, growth, learning, normal

MATERIALS

Mentor text (Mistakes That Worked or Beautiful Oops!) · Journals · Sticky notes

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 3–5 · LATE ELEMENTARY

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1A.2a Describe a range of emotions and the situations that cause them.

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3B.2b Generate alternative solutions and evaluate their consequences for a range of academic and social situations.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

OPENING REVIEW
2 Minutes

“We said emotions are messengers. What emotion do you feel when you make a mistake?” Take 3 responses. “Today we look at mistakes themselves — not the emotion, but what a mistake actually IS.”

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01Mentor Text Read-Aloud2–8 MIN

Read a page or two from the selected text. “Was this person bad because they made a mistake? Or were they just a person making a mistake? What is the difference?”

02Mistake vs. Flaw8–15 MIN

A mistake is something you DID. A flaw is something you ARE. “I got the answer wrong” = mistake. “I am stupid” = flaw. Run 4–5 examples — students call out: mistake or flaw? “The inner critic turns mistakes into flaw statements.”

03Mistake Gallery15–22 MIN

Students write or draw on sticky notes: a mistake they made and something they learned. Anonymous. Post on the board. “Look how much learning is in this room.”

04Private Journal22–30 MIN

Think of a mistake you are still hard on yourself about. Write: The mistake was ___. What I learned was ___. What I would do differently is ___. “You just gave it a learning frame instead of a shame frame.”

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the private journal: any student who writes for only 10–15 seconds, or conversely who writes intensely and then covers the page, may be carrying something significant. Do not press. Note for Unit 2 watch list.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the read-aloud: watch for students whose posture shifts or who look down at the word “mistake.” Physical cues of shame — clenching, withdrawing, covering the face — can signal that mistakes at home carry much heavier consequences than this lesson assumes.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
The mentor text is the primary anchor. Keep mistake/flaw distinction simple. Every student contributes before closing.Introduce “failure as feedback.” Ask: Can you think of a mistake that, looking back, was one of your most important learning experiences?
SCENARIO CARD 1-E · EVERYONE MAKES MISTAKES

THE SITUATION

Priya forgot her part of a group project. That night: “I always ruin everything. I am so irresponsible. I am a bad friend.”

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

Which of Priya's self-statements are about the MISTAKE, and which about her IDENTITY?

Inner Critic: “I always ruin everything.” Inner Coach: “I forgot something important. I need to apologize and be more reliable.”

SCENARIO CARD 1-F · EVERYONE MAKES MISTAKES

THE SITUATION

Jaylen missed the winning penalty kick. His older brother says: “Messi missed penalty kicks. Jordan missed shots. That doesn't make them bad players.”

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

Is the missed kick evidence Jaylen is a bad soccer player — or evidence that he is a soccer player? Why do we hold onto worst moments as if they define us?

EXIT TICKET

Journal: Complete this sentence: “Making mistakes means I am ___, not that I am ___.”

TEACHER REFLECTION

Which students seemed to genuinely believe a mistake means they are bad? Note these students for closer attention in Unit 2.

My Inner Critic vs. My Inner Coach

Build this lesson’s anchor chart live — the interactive board →This lesson’s worksheet is interactive — open it: type in it, turn it in, Print my copy / Print it blank →
DURATION

35–40 min · Week 4

OBJECTIVE

Students identify the inner critic voice, contrast it with the inner coach, and practice reframing one critical thought.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness · Self-Management: self-talk and reframing

VOCABULARY

inner critic, inner coach, self-talk, reframe, voice

MATERIALS

Journals · WS3 Two Voices · Anchor chart · Scenario cards

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 3–5 · LATE ELEMENTARY

Goal 1 — Self-Awareness & Self-Management

1A.2a Describe a range of emotions and the situations that cause them.

1A.2b Describe and demonstrate ways to express emotions in a socially acceptable manner.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

OPENING REVIEW
2 Minutes

“Who made a mistake this week?” (Most hands.) “Who was hard on themselves about it?” “Today we meet the voice that turns a mistake into a flaw statement. It has a name.”

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01Introduce the Inner Critic2–10 MIN

Do you have a voice that makes things about who you ARE, not just what you did? Examples: “You always mess up.” / “You're not as good as everyone else.” Pattern: the inner critic ATTACKS identity. It is global. It catastrophizes. Three trigger words: ALWAYS · NEVER · TOO MUCH. Ask: “Is that voice you — or is that the Inner Critic?”

02Introduce the Inner Coach10–17 MIN

The inner coach is honest AND kind. Three questions: WHAT happened? WHAT can I learn? WHAT is one next step? Post these permanently.

ANCHOR CHART · INNER CRITIC VS. INNER COACH — PERMANENT ANCHOR CHART

Build WITH students — both columns. Color-code: red for Critic, green for Coach. Post permanently. Students will use this language spontaneously by Week 8.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
03Build the Anchor Chart17–22 MIN

Two columns: INNER CRITIC language / INNER COACH language. Students help generate both sides.

04Scenario Card Reframes (WS3)22–32 MIN

Distribute WS3. For each scenario: (1) Write what the inner critic says. (2) Write what the inner coach says. (3) Which helps them do better next time? Debrief: “Was the coach softer? Did it ignore what happened?” [No — honest AND kind.]

05Personal Reframe Practice32–38 MIN

Journal + WS3 Part 2: Write one recent inner critic statement. Rewrite in the inner coach voice. “Which would you say to your best friend?” Private.

IN-LESSON SCAN

As you introduce the inner critic: students who immediately and emphatically say “yes” or who name very specific critic phrases without hesitation are telling you the critic is loud and very familiar. These students need Unit 2 most.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During personal reframe practice: a student who cannot write a single inner coach response — who genuinely cannot find a kinder voice — may have no internal model of kind self-talk. This is significant. Note for individual follow-up.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
Use the anchor chart as primary visual. Keep scenarios relatable to 3rd grade. Reframe can be done with a partner.Explore the origins of the inner critic — where did that voice come from? Seeing it as learned, not truth, is the key insight.

UNIT 1 · LESSON Strand

My Regulation Toolkit

DURATION

35–40 min · Week 5 (after Lesson 4, before Lesson 5)

OBJECTIVE

Students name their three regulation strategies — one body-based, one cognitive, one relational — and record them on WS-R1 (My Toolkit), which they will carry and reference all year.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Management: emotional regulation and self-monitoring · Self-Awareness: recognizing internal states

NEW ANCHOR CHART

Window of Tolerance (WOT-1) — introduced in this lesson. Must be posted before class begins.

WORKSHEET

WS-R1: My Regulation Toolkit — completed and owned by student. Not collected.

VOCABULARY

regulation, window of tolerance, activated, settled, strategy, toolkit

PLACEMENT NOTE

This lesson is delivered between Unit 1 Lesson 4 (My Inner Critic vs. My Inner Coach) and Lesson 5 (Guilt vs. Shame). Placement here is intentional: students have now met the inner critic — they know the voice that attacks identity after a mistake. This lesson gives them the physical and practical infrastructure to regulate the activation that the critic produces before it overwhelms them. The guilt/shame distinction in Lesson 5 will be significantly more accessible to students who have this toolkit in hand.

SAFETY NOTE

This is a standard-risk lesson. No counselor co-facilitation is required. However: some students have learned to be disconnected from their body as a protective response to trauma or chronic stress. For these students, body-awareness prompts can feel disorienting or activating. Monitor during the body strategy section. If a student appears distressed during any body-awareness activity, offer the option to observe rather than participate. Note for counselor awareness — this student may benefit from individual support before the deeper emotional content in Units 2 and 3.

2 Minutes

STEP 2 · Teach the 4-Count Breath · 5 minutes

“Put both feet flat on the floor. Sit up slightly — not rigid, just upright.”

ANCHOR CHART · THE REPAIR PROTOCOL · REP-1

Post this chart before students arrive alongside the Recovery Arc (ARC-1) and Window of Tolerance (WOT-1). The three charts form the complete Regulation & Repair visual system.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
1BOTH PEOPLE REGULATE FIRST
Each person checks their Window independently. Neither begins until both are inside or close to it. If one person is still in Phase 1 — wait.		“I need a few minutes before we talk about this. Can we do it at lunch?”
2ONE PERSON SPEAKS
The person who caused harm speaks first: specific action, honest tone. The person who was hurt listens without preparing their response. Then they switch.		“I told your secret. That was wrong. I want to hear what that did to you.”
3IMPACT ACKNOWLEDGED
The person who caused harm names the actual impact — not a summary or deflection. “I understand you felt bad” is not enough.		“I know that broke your trust. And I know it embarrassed you in front of people you care about.”
4REPAIR ACTION NAMED
What specifically will change? What will the person who caused harm do differently? This is not a promise of feeling — it is a commitment of action.		“Next time I am tempted to share something that isn't mine to share — I won't. And if you tell me something, I will ask first.”
5RE-ENTRY AGREED
What does the relationship look like going forward? This is not always returning to what it was — that depends on trust. Both people state explicitly what re-entry looks like.		“I'm not ready to be as close as before. But I'm willing to try again slowly. Can we start with just being okay in class?”

OPENING REVIEW

ANCHOR CHART · THE FORGIVENESS PATH — NON-LINEAR (ANONYMOUS STICKY DOT PLACEMENT)

Post this chart large before the lesson. After direct instruction, give each student a sticky dot — they place it where they are on the path. Anonymous. After placement: “Is that okay?” The answer is always yes.

Anchor Chart · Classroom Display — print on cardstock or 11×17, post as the lesson indicates
▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01The Forgiveness Process Chart2–10 MIN

Draw a squiggly — non-linear — path. Name the stations: Still hurting / Beginning to understand / Some days are better / Choosing to put it down today / The hurt is getting smaller / I am okay. “The path is not a straight line. Setbacks are not failure.”

02Anonymous Sticky Dot Placement10–18 MIN

Students receive a sticky dot and place it where they are — anonymously. “Is that okay?” Some dots are near the beginning. “Every dot represents someone doing the work.”

03The Setback Is Normal18–27 MIN

“Some days you will feel like you have forgiven something. Then it comes back. That is not failure. You put it down again.” Connect to U2 L5: “Remember the stone practice? The same is true for forgiving others.”

04Connect to Self-Compassion27–35 MIN

“You are not required to be further along the path than you are.” Journal: Where are you on the forgiveness path for something specific?

IN-LESSON SCAN

After dot placement: note where most dots land as a class pattern. A class where the majority of dots are at Station 1 — still hurting — needs more validation before any further forgiveness practice. Do not pressure students toward resolution.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the journal: watch for a student who places their dot at Station 5 or 6 but whose journal writing suggests they are still at Station 1. Some students perform resolution they have not yet reached.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
The visual path is the primary anchor. The sticky dot placement is anonymous. “It is okay wherever you are” is the key message.What factors accelerate or slow the forgiveness process?

OPENING REVIEW
2 Minutes

If Panel 3 was deferred: “Let's open with Panel 3 now — take 3 minutes.” If Panel 3 was completed: “Today we watch someone else do it — in a story — and then we practice what to do with your voice when you've been hurt.”

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01Mentor Text Read-Aloud + Discussion2–18 MIN

“As we read, notice: How did the character experience being hurt? What did they choose? What did it cost — and what did it give?” After: connect the character's journey to unit learning.

02Say It Straight Framework18–25 MIN

THREE STEPS: STEP 1: Name the moment, not the person. “When [specific thing] happened, I felt ___.” No “you always” or “you never.” STEP 2: Say what you need. “What I need going forward is ___.” STEP 3: Listen without defending. Give the other person a real turn.

03Pair Practice — Scenario Card Dialogue25–35 MIN

Person A (hurt) uses Say It Straight. Person B listens without defending. Switch. “Stay in Step 1 — name the moment, not the character.”

04Closing Discussion35–38 MIN

“Can you say it straight to someone and still forgive them? Can you forgive someone without ever saying anything?” Both are true — this gives students a choice, not a mandate.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During pair practice: any student who becomes tearful, who breaks the frame of the scenario and begins speaking from personal experience, or who refuses to switch roles may be processing a real situation. Do not press. Offer the Reflection Pass quietly.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the mentor text read-aloud: watch for students who become visibly emotional at specific moments. These moments may be resonating with personal experience. Note which part of the story triggers the strongest reaction.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
Focus entirely on Step 1 — naming a moment without character attacks.Why is Step 3 often hardest despite being the most passive?

THE LIMIT FORMULA

When ___, I feel ___ because ___.

Going forward, I will ___.

The limit is what YOU will do — not what you are demanding from the other person. The formula works whether or not the other person agrees, changes, or even hears it.

The most important word is the last one: I WILL.

BEFORE YOU SET A LIMIT · THREE TESTS

1. Is this from self-respect or retaliation?

2. Does the formula focus on what I will do?

3. Can I hold this even if they don't change?

If all three answers are yes — this is a limit from strength.

01Strength/Fear Distinction0–5 MIN

Two contrasting examples — same situation (a friend who repeatedly makes cutting jokes). Walk through both responses. Run the three tests on the strength version.

02Practice the Formula5–10 MIN

Build the formula on the board with students. “The last part is the hardest. A limit is in your control. 'I will leave.' 'I will not share information.' These are limits.”

03Card R4-A — Whole Class10–14 MIN

Sofia and the group chat. Students draft formula on WS-R4 Part 1. Test it. Refine together.

04Card R4-B — Pairs14–18 MIN

Marcus and the difficult teammate. Pairs draft the formula. Focus on the limit-vs-complaint distinction.

05Card R4-C — Individual18–20 MIN

Part 2 is always private. “If something real comes to mind, the formula works there too. I won't read it.”

IN-LESSON SCAN

OBSERVE — A student whose formula consistently ends with what the other person should do — even after the distinction has been taught.

MAY MEAN — This student may be having difficulty locating their own agency. It is also possible their own actions have had no effect on the harm they receive — making 'what I will do' feel futile.

ACTION — Approach quietly. “Your formula's last part — is that something you can control?” Receive without correcting. Note for counselor.

IN-LESSON SCAN

MAY MEAN — The student may be describing ongoing harm — where distancing is a safety response, not a limit from strength. The formula is not the right tool for unsafe relationships.

ACTION — After class: “If someone is genuinely hurting you, the right response is not just a limit formula. It's getting support. Can we talk?” Note for counselor today.

Grace Gap Field Assignment — Class Debrief

DURATION

40 min · Week 7

OBJECTIVE

Students debrief their five-day Grace Gap field assignment and articulate what grace as a practice (not a feeling) actually requires.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness: reflection on practice · Social Awareness: impact recognition

VOCABULARY

debrief, practice, sustain, observe, witness

MATERIALS

Students bring WS8 (completed) · Journals · Anchor chart

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 3–5 · LATE ELEMENTARY

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3C.2b Identify and perform roles that contribute to one’s local community.

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2C.2b Analyze ways to work effectively in groups.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

OPENING REVIEW
2 Minutes

“Two weeks ago you committed to five school days of one small act of grace. Today is the day we talk about what happened. Whatever happened — including 'nothing visible' — is real data.”

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01Pair Debrief — WS8 Reflection Logs2–15 MIN

In pairs, students share their five-day reflection logs. “What did you do? What did you notice in yourself? In the other person?” Listen first. No advice.

02Whole-Class Pattern Naming15–25 MIN

Without naming the recipient, students share what they noticed. Patterns to draw out: (1) Grace is often invisible to the recipient. (2) Grace changes the giver more than the receiver. (3) Five days is harder than expected. (4) Grace requires no thanks.

03The Big Insight25–35 MIN

“Most of you noticed: nothing visible happened. The world did not transform. The person you chose may not have even noticed. AND — you noticed something about yourself. That is what grace as a practice is. It changes you whether or not anyone sees it.”

04Closing35–40 MIN

“Who will continue past today? Raise your hand if you are committing to one more week.” Acknowledge: most who try this will continue. The practice has its own gravity.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the pair debrief: any student who reports the grace gap was rejected aggressively or who experienced backlash needs a private check-in. Some grace gaps reveal toxic dynamics. Adjust counsel if needed.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During whole-class sharing: a student who reports they did not do the assignment — note honestly. Do not shame. Some students have contexts where this assignment exposed something hard to navigate. Note for individual follow-up.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
The pair debrief is the primary structure. Drawing of the impact is acceptable.Push toward articulating what grace as a practice requires — and what makes it different from one-time kindness.

THE SITUATION

Maya thanked Ms. Linda by name every day for a week. By Day 5, Ms. Linda was visibly lighter. Maya noticed she felt more grounded all day after that small interaction.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

What changed for Ms. Linda? What changed for Maya? What is the curriculum's claim about grace and the giver?

SCENARIO CARD 4-L · GRACE GAP FIELD ASSIGNMENT

THE SITUATION

James made a positive comment about a quieter classmate every day. The classmate never said anything in response. James started to wonder if it was working.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

Is the absence of visible response evidence the practice is failing? What might be happening that James cannot see?

EXIT TICKET

TEACHER REFLECTION

Which students continued past Day 5 spontaneously? Which struggled to sustain? This is data about character formation in real time.

DURATION

45 min · Week 8

OBJECTIVE

Students retrieve and read their U2 future-self letters, co-create a class community charter, and close the year with reflection on growth across all four units.

CASEL FOCUS

Self-Awareness · Social Awareness · All four CASEL competencies (synthesis)

VOCABULARY

charter, community, growth, year, witness, celebrate

MATERIALS

Sealed letters from U2 L7 · Journals · Chart paper · All Unit anchor charts displayed

ILLINOIS SEL ALIGNMENT GRADES 3–5 · LATE ELEMENTARY

Goal 3 — Responsible Decision-Making

3C.2a Identify and perform roles that contribute to the school community.

Goal 2 — Social Awareness & Interpersonal Skills

2C.2b Analyze ways to work effectively in groups.

Aligned to the Illinois Social/Emotional Learning Standards (ISBE).

SAFETY CHECK — COMPLETE BEFORE DELIVERING

Counselor notified — minimum 1 week in advance. · Counselor reachable on the day. · Lesson scheduled Mon–Wed of final week only. · Reflection Pass pre-positioned. · Some students may find the future-self letter activating — past-self holding present-self carries emotional weight. Do NOT collect or read letters. Honor privacy.

OPENING REVIEW
2 Minutes

Display all anchor charts from all four units. “Look at what we built. Twenty-eight lessons and four strand lessons. Four units. Look at the visual record of nine months of brave honest work. Today we close it. And we open something else.”

▶ DIRECT INSTRUCTION

Scripted teaching sequence · timed sequence · follow the minute markers · read aloud or in your own words

01Retrieve Future-Self Letters2–15 MIN

Distribute the sealed letters students wrote at U2 L7 — back in December. “Your past self wrote this for you. Read it privately. Take your time.” Allow 8 full minutes of silence. Do not collect. Honor the privacy.

02Community Charter Co-Creation15–25 MIN

“On chart paper, we are writing the agreements for who this class has been. Not aspirational — actual. What do we have that we want to name?” Students contribute. Teacher records. Sign at the bottom — every student. This is the year's record.

03Year-End Reflection — Pair Share25–35 MIN

“Two questions. Take turns. (1) How are you different from who you were in September? (2) What is one thing you are taking with you?” Pairs share. No reporting back.

04Closing Circle35–43 MIN

Each student names one word for what they are taking with them. No comments. No judgment. Just the word. Teacher closes: “You are the architects of your own grace. The work continues. You know how now.”

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the future-self letter reading: any student who appears emotionally overwhelmed — tears, withdrawal, sudden silence — may be experiencing the weight of who they were. Receive this. Some students will find their past-self's letter heartbreaking.

IN-LESSON SCAN

During the closing circle: any student who cannot name a word, or whose word is markedly negative ('exhausted', 'nothing'), may be signaling something important about their year. Do not press in front of the group. Note for follow-up.

GRADE 3GRADES 4 — 5
The community charter is the primary anchor. The closing word is sufficient — drawing accepted.Push toward specificity in the pair share. The year-end reflection is the most sophisticated metacognitive task they will do.
EXIT TICKET

Journal: Final journal entry of the year: “Who I am at the end of fifth/fourth/third grade is ___. Who I am becoming is ___.”

TEACHER REFLECTION

This is the last day. There is no next lesson. Sit in what you saw across nine months. Write your own teacher reflection privately — it belongs to no one but you.

END OF YEAR

Teacher Close-Out Note

You taught a curriculum that asked students to do honest interior work. They did it. So did you.

Some of what you witnessed this year will stay with you. Some of what you witnessed should not stay with you alone — debrief with your counselor, your colleagues, your own support web.

The students who grew the most this year may not be the students who said the most. Trust what you saw.

Before the next school year: review the post-assessment data with your counselor. Identify any students whose post-assessment patterns warrant a warm hand-off to next year's teacher.

And: you taught grace this year. Extend it to yourself for the parts that did not go the way you wanted. Every part of this curriculum applies to you, too.
