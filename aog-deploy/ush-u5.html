Pushing National Boundaries

1821–1860

Between the 1820s and 1860, the United States changed faster than ever before. More white men gained the right to vote, and Andrew Jackson rode that wave into the White House. The country stretched west until it reached the Pacific Ocean. Wagon trains rolled toward Oregon, soldiers marched into Mexico, and gold seekers from around the world rushed to California.

Growth came at a heavy cost. Native nations were forced from their homelands, including along routes that crossed southern Illinois. Mexico lost half its land, and many Mexican Americans lost their property. At the same time, millions of immigrants arrived from Ireland and Germany, and some Americans turned against them. Cities such as Chicago boomed.

Many Americans also tried to make their country live up to its ideals. Reformers built public schools and hospitals. Abolitionists, Black and white, demanded an end to slavery, and freedom seekers escaped on the Underground Railroad. Women met at Seneca Falls to demand equal rights. As you read, ask who was included in the growing nation, and who was pushed aside.

The big question

As the United States grew bigger and more democratic, whose freedom grew and whose was taken away?

Big questionWhen more people gained a voice in government, why did others lose their rights and their homes?
The story

Sequoyah and the Talking Marks

A Cherokee silversmith who could not read invented a way for his whole nation to write.

In the early 1800s, a Cherokee man named Sequoyah lived in the hills of the southern Appalachian Mountains. He worked as a silversmith, making jewelry and other goods from silver. Sequoyah could not read or write in any language. But he noticed something about the white Americans he traded with. They made marks on paper, and other people could look at those marks and know exactly what had been said, even days later and miles away.

Sequoyah asked himself a bold question. Why couldn't the Cherokee language be written down too? At first he tried drawing a picture for every word. That did not work. There were far too many words to remember. Then he listened closely to how Cherokee sounded. He noticed that every word was built from a limited number of spoken syllables, the small sound pieces inside words. What if each syllable had its own symbol?

Sequoyah worked on his idea for about twelve years. Some neighbors thought he was wasting his time. In the end, he created 86 symbols, one for each Cherokee syllable. This kind of writing system is called a syllabary. Around 1821, he showed Cherokee leaders how it worked. His young daughter, Ayoka, helped him. One of them wrote a message while the other was out of the room. Then the other came back and read it aloud, word for word. The leaders were amazed.

Because each symbol matched a sound people already spoke, a Cherokee speaker could learn to read in a short time, sometimes just days or weeks. Within a few years, thousands of Cherokee people could read and write in their own language. In 1827 the Cherokee Nation wrote its own constitution. In 1828 it began printing a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix, in both English and Cherokee. It was the first newspaper published by a Native American nation.

The Cherokee hoped that schools, laws and a newspaper would show the United States that they were a strong, organized nation that should keep its land. But Georgia wanted that land, and many American leaders supported Georgia. In this chapter, you will see what happened next. Sequoyah's invention outlived those hard years. Today, Cherokee people still use his syllabary in schools, books, signs and even on phones and computers.

Politics of the Common Man

11.1

The Election of 1824

Main ideaIn 1824 no candidate won a majority of electoral votes, so the House chose John Quincy Adams, and Andrew Jackson's supporters cried foul.

By 1824 the old Federalist Party had faded away. Four men from the same party ran for president. They were Andrew Jackson of Tennessee, John Quincy Adams of Massachusetts, William Crawford of Georgia and Henry Clay of Kentucky. Jackson was a famous general who had won the Battle of New Orleans in the War of 1812. Adams was the son of President John Adams and an experienced diplomat.

Jackson won the most popular votes and the most electoral votes. But he did not win a , meaning more than half, of the electoral votes. The Constitution says that when this happens, the House of Representatives picks the president from the top three candidates. Henry Clay came in fourth, so he was out. But Clay was Speaker of the House, and he had great influence there.

Clay backed Adams, and the House chose Adams as president. Soon after, Adams named Clay his secretary of state. Jackson’s supporters were furious. They called the deal a . They claimed Clay had traded his support for a top job. No one ever proved such a deal was made. Still, the charge hurt Adams for four years, and Jackson began planning to run again in 1828.

11.2

Jackson Wins in 1828

Main ideaJackson won in 1828 as a hero of ordinary white men, as more states let white men vote without owning property.

The 1828 rematch between Jackson and Adams was one of the nastiest campaigns in U.S. history. Both sides attacked each other’s character. Jackson’s side called Adams an out-of-touch aristocrat. Adams’s side called Jackson a violent, uneducated brawler. Jackson won easily. His supporters formed the Democratic Party, which still exists today.

Jackson was the first president from west of the Appalachian Mountains. He had been born poor and had lost his parents young. Many farmers, workers and frontier families saw him as one of them. Crowds poured into the White House to celebrate after he took office in 1829. People came to call this new style of politics the rise of the .

Voting changed too. In the early 1800s, many states only let men who owned property vote. By the 1820s and 1830s, most states had dropped that rule for white men. Many more ordinary men could now take part in , government by the people. Parties also began choosing candidates at big meetings called , instead of in small groups of leaders.

But “the common man” meant white men only. Women could not vote anywhere. Enslaved people had no rights at all. Free Black men actually lost voting rights in several states during these years, including North Carolina and Pennsylvania. Most Native Americans were not treated as citizens. The growth of democracy was real, but it was not shared by everyone.

11.3

Jobs for Supporters

Main ideaJackson replaced many government workers with his own supporters, a practice critics called the spoils system.

Jackson believed ordinary citizens could handle most government jobs. He also believed that officials who stayed in office too long became lazy or dishonest. So he removed many federal workers and gave their jobs to people who had helped him win. Giving government jobs to loyal supporters is called the .

The name came from a New York senator, William Marcy, who defended the practice in 1832. He said that the winners of an election had a right to the rewards of winning, the way soldiers took the “spoils” from a defeated army. Critics said the system filled offices with people who were loyal but not skilled. Some of Jackson’s appointees did steal public money.

Jackson also relied on a group of friends and advisers outside his official cabinet. Critics called them the , as if they slipped in through the back door. Supporters said Jackson was simply listening to people he trusted. Either way, it was clear that Jackson meant to run the government his own way, with a strong president at the center.

Section 2

Fights Over Federal Power

11.4

The Nullification Crisis

Main ideaSouth Carolina tried to cancel a federal tariff, but Jackson insisted that no state could break away from federal law.

A is a tax on goods brought in from other countries. In 1828 Congress passed a high tariff to protect factories in the North from foreign competition. The South had few factories. Southern planters sold cotton overseas and bought many manufactured goods. The tariff made those goods cost more. Angry Southerners called it the Tariff of Abominations. An abomination is something hateful.

Jackson’s own vice president, John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, led the fight against it. Calhoun argued for . This was the idea that a state could declare a federal law null and void, meaning it had no force inside that state. He even said a state could leave the Union if pushed too far. In 1830 Senator Daniel Webster of Massachusetts answered in a famous speech defending the Union.

In 1832 South Carolina declared the tariff laws nullified. It threatened to secede, or leave the Union, if the government used force. Calhoun resigned as vice president. Jackson was a Southerner and a slaveholder, but he believed the Union came first. He warned that he would enforce the law. Congress passed the Force Bill, which allowed the president to use the military to collect the tariff.

Henry Clay helped work out a compromise tariff in 1833 that lowered the rates over time. South Carolina backed down. The crisis ended, but the argument over states&#x27; rights did not. Southern leaders would return to Calhoun’s ideas again, especially in the fight over slavery.

11.5

The War on the Bank

Main ideaJackson saw the national bank as a tool of the rich and destroyed it, which helped his popularity but shook the economy.

The Second Bank of the United States held the federal government’s money and made loans. Its president, Nicholas Biddle, used it to keep state banks from printing too much paper money. Many business owners liked the bank because it kept money steady. But Jackson distrusted it. He thought it gave a small group of wealthy people too much power over the country.

The bank’s , its official permission to operate, would run out in 1836. In 1832 Jackson’s opponents, led by Henry Clay, pushed Congress to renew it early. They hoped to make the bank a campaign issue. Congress passed the renewal. Jackson used his , his power to reject a bill, and sent a strong message explaining why.

Jackson won reelection in 1832 in a landslide over Clay. He then moved the government’s money out of the national bank and into state banks. Critics called them “pet banks” because many were run by his friends. With federal money to lend, many of these banks made risky loans and printed large amounts of paper money. That set the stage for trouble a few years later.

Forced From Their Homelands

11.6

The Indian Removal Act

Main ideaThe Indian Removal Act of 1830 let the president push Native nations to trade their eastern homelands for land west of the Mississippi.

In 1830 tens of thousands of Native Americans still lived east of the Mississippi River. In the Southeast, the largest nations were the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee (Creek) and Seminole. They had farms, towns and governments. White settlers, especially cotton planters, wanted their rich land. Many leaders, including Jackson, argued that Native people should be moved west.

That year Congress narrowly passed the Indian Removal Act. It allowed the president to make treaties trading Native lands in the East for land in the West, in what was called . Much of that land later became Oklahoma. The law said nations could choose to move. In practice, the government used heavy pressure, bribes, threats and finally soldiers.

Some Americans spoke out against . Missionaries, some members of Congress such as Davy Crockett of Tennessee, and many Northern church groups called it cruel and unjust. The Choctaw were the first nation forced to go, starting in 1831. They left in winter, with too little food and poor supplies. Many died on the way. Over the next twenty years, tens of thousands of Native people were forced west.

11.7

The Cherokee Go to Court

Main ideaThe Cherokee won their case in the Supreme Court, but President Jackson did not enforce the ruling.

The Cherokee chose to fight removal with laws and courts instead of weapons. They had a written constitution, an elected government and a principal chief, John Ross. When Georgia passed laws claiming power over Cherokee land, the Cherokee said Georgia had no right to rule them. Gold was found on Cherokee land in 1829, and Georgia became even more eager to take it.

Georgia made a law that white people could not live on Cherokee land without a state license. Samuel Worcester, a missionary who lived with the Cherokee, refused to get one. He was arrested and sentenced to hard labor. His case, , reached the U.S. Supreme Court in 1832. Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that the Cherokee Nation was a separate nation and that Georgia’s laws had no force there.

The Cherokee had won. But the Supreme Court has no army. It depends on the president to carry out its rulings. Jackson did not act to protect the Cherokee, and Georgia ignored the decision. A popular story says Jackson dared Marshall to enforce the ruling himself. Historians doubt he said those exact words, but his actions matched the idea. Winning in court was not enough to save Cherokee land.

11.8

The Trail of Tears

Main ideaIn 1838 the U.S. Army forced the Cherokee west, and thousands died on the journey called the Trail of Tears.

In 1835 a small group of Cherokee, not chosen by the nation’s government, signed the . They agreed to give up all Cherokee land in the East. Its signers believed removal could no longer be stopped and hoped to get the best deal possible. Principal Chief John Ross and most Cherokee rejected it. More than 15,000 Cherokee signed a petition protesting the treaty. The U.S. Senate approved it anyway.

In 1838 U.S. soldiers rounded up the Cherokee and held them in crowded camps. Families lost homes, farms and livestock. Many had to leave with only what they could carry. Then they were sent west in large groups, most on foot, some by boat. The journeys took months. Many groups traveled through fall and winter.

Several routes crossed southern Illinois. In the winter of 1838 to 1839, thousands of Cherokee waited near the Mississippi River because ice made it impossible to cross. Many suffered from cold, hunger and disease. Today, Trail of Tears State Forest near Jonesboro, Illinois, marks part of that path.

About 4,000 Cherokee died during the roundup and the removal. The Cherokee called the journey “the trail where we cried.” Today it is known as the . In Indian Territory, the Cherokee rebuilt their government, schools and newspaper. The Cherokee Nation’s capital is still in Tahlequah, Oklahoma.

11.9

Seminole and Sauk Resistance

Main ideaThe Seminole in Florida and Black Hawk's Sauk in Illinois fought back against removal, at great cost.

Not every nation left quietly. In Florida, many Seminole refused to move. A leader named Osceola became famous for his resistance. Among the Seminole lived Black Seminoles, people of African descent, many of whom had escaped slavery. They feared being enslaved again if the Seminole were moved. The Second Seminole War lasted from 1835 to 1842. It was one of the longest and most costly wars the U.S. fought against a Native nation.

Most Seminole were eventually forced west. Osceola was captured while meeting U.S. officers under a white flag of truce, and he died in prison. But a small number of Seminole hid deep in the Florida swamps and were never removed. Their descendants still live in Florida today.

In Illinois, the Sauk and Meskwaki had been pushed west of the Mississippi River by earlier treaties. The Sauk leader said those treaties were unfair. In 1832 he led more than 1,000 Sauk and Meskwaki people, including many women and children, back across the river into northern Illinois. Settlers panicked, and the state called out its militia. A young Abraham Lincoln joined and was elected captain of his company, though he never saw battle.

The lasted about four months. It ended in August 1832 at the Bad Axe River in Wisconsin, where soldiers killed many of Black Hawk’s people as they tried to cross the Mississippi. Black Hawk was captured. Within a few years, the Potawatomi and other nations also gave up their remaining lands in northern Illinois, and most were moved west. Northern Illinois, including Chicago, then filled quickly with settlers.

The Panic of 1837

Main ideaSoon after Martin Van Buren became president, a financial panic started a long depression that hurt millions of Americans.

Jackson chose his vice president, Martin Van Buren of New York, to follow him. Van Buren won the election of 1836. He was a skilled organizer who had helped build the Democratic Party. But he entered office just as the economy was about to crash.

During the mid-1830s, many people borrowed paper money from state banks to buy western land, hoping its price would rise. In 1836 Jackson ordered that public land be paid for only in gold or silver, called . People rushed to trade paper money for coins. Banks did not have enough. In 1837 many banks failed. A sudden wave of fear like this is called a .

The panic turned into a , a long period of economic hardship. Businesses closed. Workers in cities lost their jobs. Farmers could not sell crops for a fair price. Some people went hungry. Van Buren believed the government should not step in to help the economy directly. His opponents blamed him for the hard times and gave him the nickname “Martin Van Ruin.”

11.11

The Whigs Take Over

Main ideaOpponents of Jackson formed the Whig Party and won the White House in 1840 with a lively, image-driven campaign.

Jackson’s enemies came together in the 1830s to form the . They took the name from a British party that opposed the power of kings. They mocked Jackson as “King Andrew the First” for using the veto so often. Leading Whigs included Henry Clay and Daniel Webster. Whigs wanted a national bank, tariffs and federal money for roads and canals.

In 1840 the Whigs chose William Henry Harrison, an old general who had fought at Tippecanoe in 1811. His running mate was John Tyler. Their slogan was “Tippecanoe and Tyler Too!” When a Democratic newspaper sneered that Harrison would be happy sitting in a log cabin drinking cider, the Whigs turned the insult into a campaign theme. They held parades with log cabins on wheels and sold songs and souvenirs.

Harrison actually came from a wealthy Virginia family. But the image worked, and the hard times hurt Van Buren. Harrison won, and a record share of eligible voters turned out. Then, only a month after taking office in 1841, Harrison died. John Tyler became the first vice president to take over after a president died. The age of big, loud aimed at ordinary voters had arrived to stay.

Manifest Destiny

1821–1853
Big questionHow did the United States grow to reach the Pacific, and who gained and who lost as it did?
The story

From Sleepy Village to Gold-Rush City

In about two years, a quiet bay-side village became one of the busiest cities in the world.

Long before any city stood on San Francisco Bay, Ohlone people lived along its shores. They fished, gathered shellfish and acorns, and traded with neighboring peoples. In 1776 the Spanish built a fort and a mission there. After Mexico won its independence from Spain in 1821, a small trading village named Yerba Buena grew up near the water. In 1847, soon after U.S. forces took control of California, the village was renamed San Francisco.

In early 1848 San Francisco was still tiny, with fewer than a thousand people. Then news spread from the foothills to the east. In January 1848, a carpenter named James Marshall had spotted flakes of gold in the stream at Sutter's Mill. At first few people believed it. Then a merchant showed off a bottle of gold dust in the streets of San Francisco. Almost overnight, the town emptied. Shopkeepers, sailors and even soldiers left for the goldfields.

By 1849 the whole world had heard. Hundreds of ships sailed into San Francisco Bay. Many crews deserted to search for gold, leaving ships rocking empty in the harbor. Some of those abandoned ships were dragged ashore and turned into stores, warehouses and even a jail. Tents and wooden shacks spread up the sandy hills. Newcomers arrived from the eastern states, and also from Mexico, Chile, Peru, China, Hawaii, Australia and France.

The Oregon Trail

Main ideaHundreds of thousands of people crossed about 2,000 miles on overland trails, facing hard work, disease and danger.

The Oregon Country was rich farmland in the Pacific Northwest. Both the United States and Britain claimed it. In fact, Native people often traded food and horses with , people leaving one place to settle in another, and helped guide them across rivers. Women and children did much of the work on the trail. They cooked, gathered fuel, cared for the sick and drove the animals. Many kept diaries that tell us about daily life. Between the 1840s and 1860, hundreds of thousands of people traveled the overland trails to Oregon, California and Utah.

The story

Roads to Freedom in the Dark

Thousands of enslaved people risked everything to escape, helped by a secret network that ran through Illinois.

In 1849 a young woman named Araminta Ross, who had taken the name Harriet Tubman, made a dangerous decision. She was enslaved on Maryland's Eastern Shore, and she feared she was about to be sold far away. One night she slipped away. She traveled mostly after dark and followed the North Star. People she could trust gave her food and places to hide. After about ninety miles, she crossed into Pennsylvania, a free state.

Tubman had reached freedom through what people called the Underground Railroad. It was not underground and it was not a railroad. It was a loose network of people, both Black and white, who helped freedom seekers escape slavery. They borrowed railroad words as a code. Guides were “conductors.” Safe houses were “stations.” People who ran them were “stationmasters.” Most of the work was done by African Americans, including freedom seekers themselves.

Tubman did not stay safe in the North. She went back into Maryland about thirteen times over the next ten years. She guided around 70 people to freedom, including members of her own family. Other escapes were just as daring. In 1849 Henry Brown had himself shipped in a wooden crate from Richmond, Virginia, to Philadelphia. The trip took about a day. He became known as Henry “Box” Brown.

Some routes ran through Illinois. Freedom seekers crossed the Mississippi River from Missouri, a slave state, near towns such as Quincy and Alton. From there, helpers moved them north toward Chicago, then across Lake Michigan to Canada. In Princeton, Illinois, minister Owen Lovejoy openly hid freedom seekers in his home. In Chicago, John Jones, a free Black businessman, and his wife, Mary Jones, helped many people on their way.

Helping freedom seekers was against federal law, and many white Illinoisans supported slavery or wanted nothing to do with Black people. Illinois had strict laws against free Black residents. Helpers risked fines and jail. Freedom seekers risked their lives. Their courage was part of a larger wave of Americans who were working, in these years, to change their country.

A Season of Reform

13.4

Revival and the Push for Change

Main ideaA wave of religious revivals inspired many Americans to try to improve society, including the fight against alcohol abuse.

In the early 1800s, a wave of religious excitement swept the country. It is called the . Preachers such as Charles Finney held outdoor camp meetings and revivals that drew huge crowds. They taught that every person could choose to be saved and live a better life. Many listeners came away believing they should also make society better.

Black churches grew strong in these years too. The African Methodist Episcopal Church, founded by Richard Allen in Philadelphia, spread to many cities, including Chicago. Black churches became centers of community life, education and protest against slavery.

People who work to change society for the better are called reformers. Women played a large part in groups, even though they could not vote. One major cause was temperance. Many Americans drank large amounts of alcohol, and drunkenness hurt families and workers. The urged people to drink less or give up alcohol. In 1851 Maine became the first state to ban the sale of alcohol, and several other states followed for a time.

13.5

Schools, Prisons and Hospitals

Main ideaReformers such as Horace Mann and Dorothea Dix worked for free public schools and better care for prisoners and people with mental illness.

In the early 1800s, many children never went to school or went only for a short time. Horace Mann of Massachusetts believed that a democracy needed educated citizens. In 1837 he became the head of the state’s new board of education. He pushed for paid for by taxes, longer school years, better schoolhouses and training for teachers. Other states, including Illinois, set up free public school systems in the following decades.

Most of these schools were for white children. In many places, African American children were kept out or sent to separate schools. In the South, it was often against the law to teach enslaved people to read. Still, Black communities in the North started their own schools.

Dorothea Dix was a teacher in Massachusetts. In 1841 she visited a jail and was shocked to find people with mental illness locked in cold, bare cells alongside criminals. She spent the next two years visiting jails and poorhouses across the state. Then she reported what she saw to lawmakers. Dix traveled through many states urging them to build , hospitals for people with mental illness. Her work led to new hospitals across the country.

13.6

Writers and Artists

Main ideaAmerican writers and painters created a new national culture that celebrated nature, independence and conscience.

For years, many Americans felt their books and art were copies of Europe’s. In the 1830s and 1840s that began to change. A group of New England writers called believed that people could find truth by looking inward and by studying nature. Their leader was Ralph Waldo Emerson, a lecturer and essayist who urged Americans to think for themselves.

Emerson’s friend Henry David Thoreau put these ideas into practice. In 1845 he built a small cabin by Walden Pond in Massachusetts and lived there simply for about two years. His book Walden (1854) describes that life. Thoreau also refused to pay a tax because he opposed slavery and the war with Mexico. He spent a night in jail. His essay on , peacefully refusing to obey an unjust law, later inspired leaders such as Mohandas Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

Painters also found American subjects. Thomas Cole and other artists of the painted huge, glowing scenes of forests, rivers and mountains in New York and beyond. Their paintings showed the American wilderness as grand and beautiful. Writers such as Nathaniel Hawthorne, Herman Melville and Edgar Allan Poe wrote stories and novels still read today.

The Fight to End Slavery

13.7

Calls for Abolition

Main ideaBlack and white abolitionists demanded an immediate end to slavery, even when it put their lives in danger.

By 1830 slavery had ended in the Northern states, but about two million people were enslaved in the South. Some reformers called for , the complete end of slavery. People who worked for this were called . Free Black Americans led the way. In 1829 David Walker, a free Black man in Boston, published a pamphlet called Walker’s Appeal. It called on enslaved people to resist and condemned slavery as a crime against God.

In 1831 William Lloyd Garrison, a white printer in Boston, began a newspaper called The Liberator. He demanded that slavery end immediately, not slowly. Garrison helped found the American Anti-Slavery Society in 1833. Many Northerners disliked abolitionists. They feared trouble with the South, or held racist views themselves. Mobs sometimes attacked abolitionist speakers.

Illinois saw the most famous of these attacks. Elijah Lovejoy, a white minister and newspaper editor, published antislavery articles. After mobs in St. Louis drove him out, he moved across the river to Alton, Illinois. There, mobs destroyed his printing press more than once. In November 1837, a mob attacked the warehouse where he was guarding a new press, and Lovejoy was shot and killed. His death shocked many Northerners and brought new supporters to the cause. His brother Owen Lovejoy became a leading abolitionist in Illinois.

13.8

Voices of Freedom

Main ideaFrederick Douglass, Sojourner Truth and the Grimké sisters used their own experiences to show Americans the truth about slavery.

Frederick Douglass was born into slavery in Maryland. As a boy he secretly learned to read. In 1838 he escaped to the North. He became a powerful speaker for the antislavery cause. In 1845 he published a book about his life, the Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass. In 1847 he started his own newspaper in Rochester, New York, called The North Star.

Sojourner Truth was born enslaved in New York around 1797 with the name Isabella. She gained her freedom in the 1820s and later chose her new name. She traveled widely, speaking against slavery and for women’s rights. Her most famous speech was given in Akron, Ohio, in 1851. Many people know it as “Ain’t I a Woman?” But that version was written down years later by someone else, and it probably does not match her real words.

Sarah and Angelina Grimké grew up in a wealthy slaveholding family in South Carolina. They saw the cruelty of slavery firsthand and moved north. In the 1830s they spoke out against slavery in public, which was unusual for women at the time. Some people attacked them for speaking to mixed crowds of men and women. The sisters answered by defending women&#x27;s rights too.

13.9

The Underground Railroad

Main ideaFreedom seekers and those who helped them built a secret network of routes to freedom, including routes through Illinois.

The was a network of people who helped enslaved people escape to freedom. Most freedom seekers came from the Upper South, closer to free states. Many traveled at night and hid by day. Some made it on their own. Others were helped by free Black communities, churches and white allies. Some went to Northern cities. Many went all the way to Canada, where slavery was illegal.

Harriet Tubman was the most famous , or guide. After escaping from Maryland in 1849, she returned again and again to lead others out. Slaveholders offered rewards for her capture, but she was never caught. Levi Coffin, a Quaker in Indiana and later Ohio, and William Still, a free Black man in Philadelphia, each helped hundreds or more freedom seekers.

Illinois was a free state, but it was not a friendly place for Black people. State laws limited the rights of free Black residents, and an 1853 law tried to ban free Black people from moving into Illinois at all. Still, freedom seekers crossed from Missouri near Quincy and Alton. Helpers passed them north through towns such as Galesburg and Princeton toward Chicago.

In Chicago, free Black residents and churches such as Quinn Chapel AME helped freedom seekers board boats across Lake Michigan to Canada. Because the work was secret and illegal, few records were kept. Historians are careful about legends of hidden tunnels, since many are not supported by evidence. What is certain is that thousands of people escaped, and their courage changed how many Northerners saw slavery.

Section 4

Women Claim Their Rights

13.10

Roots of a Movement

Main ideaWomen's work in reform, especially abolition, led many to see their own lack of rights and to organize.

In the early 1800s, married women had few legal rights. In most states, a married woman’s property and wages belonged to her husband. Women could not vote or serve on juries. Few colleges admitted women. Most people believed a woman’s place was in the home.

Yet women were active in public life. Young women worked in textile mills in Lowell, Massachusetts, and some organized protests over pay and hours. Many women joined reform groups and raised money, wrote petitions and gave speeches. Working against slavery made many of them think about their own position. Like enslaved people, they had no vote and few rights under the law.

In 1840 Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott went to a World Anti-Slavery Convention in London. When they arrived, the men in charge would not let women take part. Women had to sit behind a curtain in a gallery. Stanton and Mott were angry. They agreed that they would one day hold a meeting about the rights of women. It would take eight more years.

13.11

Seneca Falls, 1848

Main ideaAt Seneca Falls in 1848, reformers declared that women and men are equal and called for women's right to vote.

In July 1848, Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Lucretia Mott and a few other women organized a meeting in Seneca Falls, New York. About 300 people came, both women and men. It was the first meeting in the United States called to discuss women’s rights. It became known as the .

Stanton wrote the . She modeled it on the Declaration of Independence. But where Jefferson listed wrongs done by the British king, Stanton listed wrongs done to women. These included being denied the vote, an education and control over their own property and wages.

The most controversial idea was , the right to vote. Some people at the meeting thought demanding the vote went too far. Frederick Douglass spoke strongly in favor of it, and the resolution passed. In the end, about 100 people signed the Declaration. Many newspapers mocked the meeting.

Seneca Falls began an organized women’s rights movement. In 1851 Stanton met Susan B. Anthony, and the two worked together for the next fifty years. Women won some property rights in several states before the Civil War. But the right to vote in all states would not come until 1920, long after most of the Seneca Falls signers had died.
