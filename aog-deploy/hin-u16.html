Comparison and Critique

Comparison

Sacred texts do not only comfort and inspire. They have also been used to rank people, and they have been used to challenge that ranking. In this unit you will study how Hindu texts have been read on caste and gender, and how saints, reformers and scholars inside and outside the tradition have answered.

You will meet Basava, Ravidas, Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule, B.R. Ambedkar, Gargi, Maitreyi and Mirabai. Then you will read the Gita beside other scriptures, the Book of Job, the Dhammapada, the Analects, the Sermon on the Mount, the Psalms and Sufi poetry, on duty, nonviolence and devotion, and trace a chain of readers from Thoreau and Tolstoy to Gandhi and King.

By the end you will be able to describe hard passages historically, name the range of Hindu responses, and compare scriptures carefully: noticing likenesses and differences, citing each text exactly, and never ranking one tradition above another.

The big question

How have readers used sacred texts both to uphold and to challenge injustice, and how can we compare scriptures fairly?

Caste, Gender and the Texts

Critique and Reform
Big questionHow have Hindu texts been used to support and to challenge hierarchies of caste and gender, and how have Hindus themselves responded?
The story

A Fire at Mahad

In 1927 a lawyer holds a public burning of an old law book, and the argument has not stopped.

On December 25, 1927, in the town of Mahad in western India, thousands of people gathered at a conference. Earlier that year many of them had marched to a public water tank, the Chavdar Tale, to drink from it. By law the tank was open to all, but people from communities treated as untouchable had long been kept away. After they drank, some upper-caste residents performed rites to "purify" the water.

The conference was led by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, a lawyer and scholar who had grown up in a Mahar family and faced untouchability all his life. That evening, a copy of the Manusmriti, an ancient Sanskrit law book, was placed on a pyre and burned. Ambedkar's followers explained that they were burning the rules that had denied them dignity, not attacking all of Hinduism.

The act shocked many Hindus. Some were angered that a sacred law book was burned. Others, including some Hindu reformers, agreed that the Manusmriti's rules on caste and on women could not guide a modern society. Many Dalits still mark December 25 as Manusmriti Dahan Din, the day of the burning of the Manusmriti.

Almost a century later, the questions raised at Mahad are still debated in India and among Hindus in the United States. What do the texts actually say about caste and gender? Which texts carry authority, and for whom? How have saints, reformers and scholars answered?

This chapter studies those questions historically. It names the passages, the people who used them, and the many Hindus who pushed back from inside and outside the tradition. It describes each position in terms its holders would recognize, and it does not issue a verdict on Hindus or on Hinduism.

Talk about itBurning a book is a strong symbol. What did it mean to the people at Mahad, and why might other Hindus have seen it differently?

Varna, Jati and the Texts

31.1

Two Different Words

Main ideaVarna names the four classes in the texts; jati names the many birth communities of lived social life.

English uses one word, caste, for two different Sanskrit ideas. means the four classes named in the texts: Brahmins (priests and teachers), Kshatriyas (rulers and warriors), Vaishyas (farmers and merchants) and Shudras (laborers and servants). Varna is a textual and ideal scheme that appears first in Rig Veda 10.90.12.

means "birth" and names the thousands of real communities, often linked to a traditional occupation, region and language, into which people were born and within which they married. Historians note that jatis were numerous and varied, and that how they ranked themselves differed from place to place. Some jatis were placed outside the four varnas and treated as untouchable.

Scholars stress that the relation between the textual scheme and real life changed over time. British colonial censuses from 1871 onward tried to classify every jati in a fixed ranking, which some historians argue made caste more rigid. Understanding the difference between varna and jati helps students read the texts and the history accurately.

31.2

The Manusmriti

Main ideaThe Manusmriti is an ancient law book with strict rules on caste and women, and its authority has always been debated.

The , or Laws of Manu, is a Sanskrit text on dharma credited by tradition to Manu, the first human. Scholars date it roughly to the 2nd century BCE to 3rd century CE. It covers ritual, family life, kingship, law and punishment in about 2,700 verses. It belongs to smriti, texts "remembered" and ranked below shruti.

The Manusmriti sets strict rules by varna, with harsher punishments for Shudras than for others in some crimes, and it bars Shudras from Vedic learning. On women it contains verses that seem to pull in different directions. Verse 3.56 says the gods rejoice where women are honored. Verse 9.3 says a woman should be protected by father, husband and son and is not fit for independence.

Scholars note that the Manusmriti was one of many dharma texts and was not a code applied everywhere. It gained new weight under British rule, when colonial courts used it and similar texts to decide Hindu family law. Today many Hindus reject its caste and gender rules; some honor other parts of it; and critics like Ambedkar rejected it entirely.

31.3

Shambuka and Ekalavya

Main ideaTwo epic episodes, Shambuka in the Ramayana and Ekalavya in the Mahabharata, are central to modern debates about caste in the texts.

In the Uttara Kanda of Valmiki’s Ramayana, a Brahmin’s young son dies, and the sage Narada says it is because a Shudra, Shambuka, is performing austerities, which the text treats as forbidden to his varna. Rama finds Shambuka and kills him, and the boy returns to life. Many scholars note that this episode is in the Uttara Kanda, which they judge to be a later part of the epic.

In the Adi Parva of the Mahabharata, Ekalavya, a tribal prince of the Nishadas, asks the teacher Drona to teach him archery and is refused. He makes a clay image of Drona and teaches himself before it, becoming a superb archer. When Drona learns of this, he demands Ekalavya’s right thumb as his teacher’s fee, so that Arjuna will remain the best archer. Ekalavya gives it.

Readers respond in many ways. Some devotees say these episodes must be understood within their own world, or note that some later retellings, such as Bhavabhuti’s drama, grieve over Shambuka’s death. Dalit and reformist writers read them as clear examples of caste injustice. Many teachers now tell Ekalavya’s story to honor his skill and devotion while naming the wrong done to him.

Saints and Reformers

31.4

Basava and the Vachanas

Main ideaBasava, a 12th-century Shiva devotee in Karnataka, rejected caste distinctions and founded a community of equal devotees.

Basava, also called Basavanna, lived in the 12th century in what is now Karnataka. He was a minister at the court of Kalyana and a devotee of Shiva. With other poet-saints he composed , short prose poems in Kannada, which criticized empty ritual, temple wealth and caste pride.

Basava gathered a community, later called the Lingayats or Virashaivas, in which devotees of Shiva were to be treated as equals regardless of birth. Tradition says he supported a marriage between a family of Brahmin birth and a family of low-caste birth. The marriage provoked a violent backlash in Kalyana, and the community scattered.

One famous vachana contrasts the rich who build temples for Shiva with the poor devotee who has nothing, and says: my legs are pillars, my body the shrine, my head a cupola of gold. Basava’s followers, today many millions in Karnataka and beyond, honor him as a saint and reformer. His vachanas are still sung and studied.

31.5

Ravidas and Begampura

Main ideaRavidas, a bhakti poet from a leatherworking family, imagined a city without sorrow or caste, and his hymns are in the Guru Granth Sahib.

Ravidas, also spelled Raidas, was a bhakti poet of North India, usually dated to the 15th and early 16th centuries, who lived near Varanasi. He was born into a family of leatherworkers, a community treated as untouchable. He worked as a cobbler and became one of the most honored poet-saints of his age.

In one song Ravidas describes , "the city without sorrow," where there is no tax, no fear, no second-class or third-class people, and everyone may go where they please. Scholars read it as one of the earliest visions of an equal society in Indian literature. About forty of his hymns are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh scripture.

Tradition says Mirabai, the Rajput princess-poet, honored Ravidas as her guru. Today the Ravidassia community, many of whom live in Punjab and in the diaspora, honors him as its founder, and temples to him stand in many countries, including the United States. His life shows how bhakti opened space for voices outside the Brahmin tradition.

31.6

Jyotirao Phule

Main ideaJyotirao Phule and Savitribai Phule opened schools for girls and lower castes and challenged Brahmin authority in the 1800s.

Jyotirao Phule (1827 to 1890) was born in Pune into the Mali, or gardener, community. With his wife he opened a school for girls in Pune in 1848, one of the first in India run by Indians. Savitribai became its teacher, facing insults and thrown mud on her way to work. The couple also opened schools for children of castes treated as untouchable.

In 1873 Jyotirao published Gulamgiri, "Slavery," which compared caste to slavery and dedicated the book to the American abolitionists. That year he founded the Satyashodhak Samaj, the "Society of Truth-Seekers," to work for equality. He read the Puranas and Vedic stories critically, arguing that they had been used to justify Brahmin power.

Phule influenced later leaders, including Ambedkar, who called him one of his three gurus, alongside the Buddha and the poet Kabir. Many Hindus admire the Phules as social reformers even when they read the texts differently. They are especially honored in Maharashtra, and Savitribai is remembered as a pioneer of women’s education.

Law and Protest

31.7

Mahad, 1927

Main ideaAt Mahad in 1927, Ambedkar led a march for water rights and a public burning of the Manusmriti.

In March 1927 Ambedkar led thousands to drink from the Chavdar Tale, a public tank in Mahad, to assert a right already granted in law. This is called the . Afterward, some local residents held rites to purify the tank, and a court case over access followed for years.

On December 25, 1927, at a second conference in Mahad, the Manusmriti was burned. Ambedkar explained that the act was a protest against the rules that had denied his community dignity, education and equal rights. The burning, his followers said, was not an attack on all Hindus but on a code of inequality. The act was meant as a symbol, and it was read as one on every side.

Responses varied. Many orthodox Hindus condemned the act. Some reformist Hindus understood it even if they disliked the method. Gandhi, who opposed untouchability, did not endorse the burning. Among Dalits, the day became a symbol of self-respect. The event remains a key moment in the history of caste and scripture.

31.8

The Constitution of 1950

Main ideaIndia's Constitution, drafted under Ambedkar's leadership, abolished untouchability and guaranteed equality before the law.

India’s Constitution came into force on January 26, 1950. Ambedkar chaired its drafting committee. Article 14 guarantees equality before the law. Article 15 forbids discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Article 17 declares that untouchability is abolished and that its practice in any form is forbidden.

The Constitution also created , a system of set-aside places in legislatures, government jobs and universities for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, to repair long exclusion. Laws such as the Protection of Civil Rights Act (1955) and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act (1989) followed.

Many Hindus, including religious leaders, supported these reforms as true to their faith. Others resisted. Scholars and activists note that caste discrimination has not ended, in India or in some diaspora communities. The debate over caste has reached the United States, where some colleges and cities have considered or adopted caste in anti-discrimination policies, and Hindu American groups have disagreed about these steps.

31.9

Gargi and Maitreyi

Main ideaThe Brihadaranyaka Upanishad shows two women, Gargi and Maitreyi, questioning the great teacher Yajnavalkya.

In the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, King Janaka holds a debate among learned Brahmins. The sage Yajnavalkya claims the prize. Among those who question him is , a woman philosopher. In 3.6 she presses him with questions about what the world is woven upon, until he warns her not to ask too much.

In 3.8 Gargi returns and says she will ask two questions, like a warrior with two sharp arrows. Yajnavalkya answers with a teaching about the imperishable, akshara. Gargi then tells the assembly that no one will defeat him in debate. Readers note that she is both a challenger and a fair judge.

In 2.4 and 4.5, Yajnavalkya’s wife asks him, as he prepares to leave home, whether wealth would make her immortal. When he says no, she asks for the knowledge that would. Many Hindu women scholars and teachers today point to Gargi and Maitreyi as evidence that women took part in the highest learning of the Upanishads.

Mirabai and Modern Scholars

Main ideaMirabai defied custom out of devotion to Krishna, and today women scholars and chanters are reshaping the study of the texts.

was a 16th-century Rajput princess and poet devoted to Krishna. Her songs, in Rajasthani and Braj, tell of loving Krishna as her true husband. Tradition says her in-laws tried to stop her from singing and dancing in temples with holy people, and even tried to poison her, but she kept her devotion. Scholars note that many songs credited to her were composed later by others in her name.

Mirabai is loved across India. Her songs are sung in homes, temples and concerts, and she appears in films and comics. Readers see her in different ways: as a model of bhakti, as a woman who chose her own path, or both. Gandhi admired her courage and saw in her a model of gentle resistance.

In modern times many women have become Sanskrit scholars, professors of religion and teachers of Vedic chanting, roles once closed to them in many communities. Some traditional groups resisted this change; others welcomed it. Women scholars have also led new readings of figures such as Sita, Draupadi and Gargi, adding their voices to a very old conversation.

The Gita Beside Other Scriptures

Comparison
Big questionWhat can we learn by reading the Gita beside other scriptures on duty, nonviolence and devotion, without ranking any of them?
The story

Letters Between Two Farms

A letter from Gandhi reaches Tolstoy, but a book of Tolstoy's reached Gandhi first.

In 1894, a young Indian lawyer in South Africa read a book that he later said overwhelmed him. It was The Kingdom of God Is Within You by the Russian novelist Leo Tolstoy, a Christian who had come to believe that the Sermon on the Mount forbids all violence. The lawyer was Mohandas Gandhi.

In 1908 Tolstoy wrote "A Letter to a Hindu," a reply to an Indian nationalist. In it he argued that Indians should resist British rule not with violence but by refusing to cooperate with evil, and he quoted the Hindu scriptures as well as the Gospels. Gandhi read the letter and wrote to Tolstoy in 1909, asking to publish it. Tolstoy agreed.

They wrote to each other until Tolstoy's death in 1910. Gandhi named a community farm near Johannesburg "Tolstoy Farm." There, people of different religions lived and worked together. Gandhi read the Gita every day; he also read the Sermon on the Mount, the Quran and the Buddha's teachings.

Later, in the United States, a young Baptist minister named Martin Luther King Jr. studied Gandhi. In 1959 he traveled to India to learn more. King drew on the Sermon on the Mount, as Tolstoy had, and on Gandhi's nonviolent method, which Gandhi linked to the Gita.

This chain of readers crosses religions and continents. In this chapter you will read the Gita beside other scriptures on three themes: duty and conscience, nonviolence, and devotion. The goal is to understand each text better, not to decide which is best.

Talk about itGandhi, Tolstoy and King each read another religion's scripture closely. What might a reader gain from that, and what might they miss?

How to Compare Well

32.1

Comparison Without Ranking

Main ideaComparing scriptures means placing them side by side to see each more clearly, not deciding which one is true or better.

Comparative religion is an academic field that studies religions side by side. A good comparison places two texts next to each other on a shared question, such as "What should I do when duty and feeling clash?" It then notes how each text answers, in its own terms. Scholars call the shared question the , the third thing that makes comparison possible.

A good comparison notices differences as carefully as likenesses. It is tempting to say that all scriptures teach "the same thing." Many scholars warn that this can erase what makes each tradition itself. The Gita’s karma yoga and the Dhammapada’s teaching on action are related, but they rest on different views of the self.

A good comparison also avoids . In a public school, we do not declare one scripture truer, wiser or more advanced than another. We describe each fairly, attribute each belief to its tradition, and let the comparison show what each text cares about. Students may hold their own views, but essays in this course argue about meaning, not about truth.

Reading Across Traditions

Main ideaEach scripture has its own language, setting and community, so a fair comparison names them for every text.

The texts in this chapter come from very different worlds. The Gita is Sanskrit poetry inside a Hindu epic. The Book of Job is Hebrew poetry in the Jewish and Christian Bibles. The Dhammapada is a Pali collection of the Buddha’s sayings. The Analects are Chinese sayings of Confucius. The Sermon on the Mount is Greek, in the Gospel of Matthew.

Each text also has a living community that reads it. Jews read Job in the Hebrew Bible, Christians in the Old Testament. Theravada Buddhists chant the Dhammapada. Confucian tradition shaped schools and families across East Asia. A fair comparison names the community each time, so that a reader never mistakes one tradition’s belief for another’s.

Translations matter too. When we quote the Gita we name Telang or Arnold. When we paraphrase other scriptures, we still say which text and passage we mean: Job 38, Dhammapada 5, Analects 15.24, Matthew 5:44. lets any reader check our work, and it shows respect for each tradition’s own words.

Section 2

Duty and Conscience

32.3

Arjuna and Job

Main ideaArjuna and Job both face terrible situations and question, and both receive a vast vision of the divine in reply, as their texts tell it.

In the Book of Job, a righteous man loses his children, his wealth and his health. His friends insist he must have sinned. Job refuses to accept this and demands an answer from God. In chapters 38 to 41, God answers out of a whirlwind, asking Job where he was when the foundations of the earth were laid, and showing the vastness of creation.

Readers of the Gita often notice a likeness. Arjuna, too, faces an unbearable situation and questions what is right. He, too, receives a vision of the divine, the universal form in Gita 11, which overwhelms him. Both men respond with awe. Both texts treat human understanding as small beside what they present as the divine view.

The differences are just as important. Job suffers and protests injustice; Arjuna hesitates to act. In Jewish and Christian readings, God speaks as Creator, distinct from creation. In many Hindu readings, the universal form shows all beings existing within Krishna. The two scenes answer different questions in different ways, and each community reads its own text in its own light.

Think it through
Two Visions

Job 38 (paraphrase): God asks Job, from the whirlwind, where he was when the earth's foundations were laid. Gita 11 (paraphrase): Arjuna sees all the worlds within Krishna's form, with the light of a thousand suns.

What does each vision answer, and what does it leave unanswered? How is each hero changed by what he sees?

32.4

Action and Its Fruits in the Dhammapada

Main ideaThe Dhammapada, like the Gita, teaches that the mind shapes action, but it grounds this in the Buddha's teaching of no fixed self.

The Dhammapada opens with a famous pair of verses. The mind comes first, it says; all things are made by mind. If one speaks or acts with a corrupted mind, suffering follows as the wheel follows the ox. If one speaks or acts with a pure mind, happiness follows like a shadow that never leaves.

The Gita also teaches that the state of mind behind an action matters, as in its teaching of action without attachment. Both texts use the word for action and its results. Both urge self-control. Many readers find in both a practical ethics of watching one’s own mind.

But Buddhists and Hindus understand the self very differently. The Gita teaches an eternal self, atman, that is not slain. The Buddha, as Buddhists understand his teaching, taught anatman, "no fixed self." So even where the ethics sound alike, the reasons behind them differ. A careful comparison names that difference rather than hiding it.

Think it through
Mind and Action

Dhammapada 1–2 (paraphrase): mind comes first; act with a corrupt mind and suffering follows like the wheel behind the ox; act with a pure mind and happiness follows like a shadow. Gita 2.48 (paraphrase): act steady in yoga, the same in success and failure.

What do both passages say about the mind behind an action? What different view of the self stands behind each?

32.5

Role and Rightness in the Analects

Main ideaThe Analects and the Gita both link right action to one's role, but Confucius stresses learning, ritual and relationships.

The Analects of Confucius, compiled by his students in China after his death in 479 BCE, teach how to become a , an exemplary person. In Analects 12.11, Confucius says good order comes when the ruler is a ruler, the minister a minister, the father a father and the son a son. Each person should fulfill the role they hold.

The Gita’s teaching of svadharma, one’s own duty, in 3.35 also links right action to one’s role. Readers often compare the two. Both texts value doing one’s part well within a larger order. Both treat family and social duties seriously. Each also warns against people who ignore their duties to others.

Yet the grounds differ. Confucius rarely speaks of gods or the afterlife and says he transmits the ways of the ancients. He stresses learning, ritual propriety (li) and humaneness (ren). The Gita grounds duty in Krishna’s teaching about the self and God. In Analects 15.24 Confucius gives a principle close to the Golden Rule: do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself.

From the source
Analects 15.24
Asked if there is one word to live by, Confucius answers: reciprocity. Do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself.
Analects 15.24, paraphrased from James Legge, The Chinese Classics, vol. 1 (1861) (paraphrased)

How does this principle compare with the Gita's teaching on svadharma? Is it about roles, or about all people equally?

Section 3

Nonviolence

32.6

Ahimsa in Jain and Buddhist Texts

Main ideaJain and Buddhist texts teach ahimsa, non-harming, with great seriousness, and Gandhi drew on these traditions too.

, non-harming, is a key value in Jainism. The Acaranga Sutra, one of the oldest Jain texts, teaches that all breathing, living beings should not be killed, harmed or abused. Jain monks and nuns sweep their path to avoid harming insects. Gandhi grew up in Gujarat among Jain neighbors and counted a Jain teacher, Shrimad Rajchandra, as a guide.

The Dhammapada also teaches non-harming. Verses 129 and 130 say that all tremble at violence and all fear death; putting oneself in another’s place, one should not kill or cause to kill. Verse 5 says that hatred is never ended by hatred, but by non-hatred; this is an eternal law.

The Gita lists ahimsa among good qualities (for example 16.2), yet it is set in a war. Hindus have held many views on how these fit together. Gandhi read the Gita through ahimsa. Others read it as allowing just war. Comparing texts helps readers see where each tradition places non-harming among its values.

From the source
Dhammapada 5
Hatred is never ended by hatred in this world; it is ended by non-hatred. This is an eternal law.
Dhammapada 5, paraphrased from F. Max Müller, The Dhammapada (Sacred Books of the East 10, 1881) (paraphrased)

How might a nonviolent movement use this verse? Compare it with Gita 2.62–2.63 on anger.

32.7

The Sermon on the Mount

Main ideaThe Sermon on the Mount teaches loving enemies and not returning evil for evil, and it shaped Tolstoy, Gandhi and King.

In Matthew 5 to 7, Jesus teaches a crowd on a mountain. He blesses the peacemakers (5:9). He says not to resist an evildoer with force but to turn the other cheek (5:39). He says to love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you (5:44). Christians have read these words in many ways, from strict pacifism to guidance for personal life.

Tolstoy read the Sermon as a strict command of nonviolence. Gandhi said the Sermon went straight to his heart when he first read it in London, and that he saw it as close to the Gita’s teaching. He combined them in his own method, satyagraha. Christians and Hindus have since discussed whether his blend was faithful to each text.

Readers point to likenesses: both texts teach acting without seeking reward (Matthew 6:1 to 6:4; Gita 2.47). They also note differences: the Sermon speaks of a loving Father in heaven; the Gita speaks of Krishna and the eternal self, and it is set on a battlefield. Christians and Hindus each read their own scripture in their own tradition’s terms.

Section 4

Devotion

32.8

Bhakti and the Psalms

Main ideaThe Gita's bhakti and the Psalms both speak of trust and longing for God, in different words and settings.

The Psalms are 150 songs and prayers in the Hebrew Bible, sung in Jewish and Christian worship for centuries. Psalm 23 pictures God as a shepherd who leads, restores and protects. Psalm 42 opens with longing: as a deer longs for streams of water, so my soul longs for you, O God.

The Gita’s chapters on bhakti also speak of trust and closeness. Krishna says he accepts a leaf or flower offered with love (9.26) and that his devotee does not perish (9.31). Later bhakti poets, such as Mirabai and the Tamil Alvars, speak of longing for God in words close in feeling to the Psalms.

Differences matter. The Psalms address the God of Israel, known through covenant and history; they include laments, cries for justice and songs of the whole people. Hindu bhakti is addressed to Krishna, Rama, Shiva or the Goddess, often through a murti in a temple or home. Comparing them shows that devotion is a shared human form with many distinct voices.

Think it through
Two Songs of Trust

Psalm 23 (paraphrase): the Lord is my shepherd; I lack nothing; though I walk through the darkest valley, I will fear no evil. Gita 9.31 (paraphrase): know for certain that my devotee does not perish.

What image does each text use for the divine's care? How does the speaker differ in each (who is speaking to whom)?

32.9

Bhakti and Sufi Poetry

Main ideaSufi poets and Hindu bhakti poets both sang of longing for the divine, and in India they sometimes shared audiences and images.

Sufism is a tradition within Islam that stresses inward love of God. The Persian poet Rumi (1207 to 1273) opens his Masnavi with a reed flute that cries because it was cut from its reed bed; it longs to return to its source. Many readers see this as a picture of the soul longing for God.

Hindu bhakti poets also sing of separation and longing, called viraha. Krishna’s flute is itself a famous image: in the Bhagavata Purana, the cowherd women of Vraj leave everything when they hear it. In India, bhakti and Sufi poets lived side by side for centuries. Kabir (15th century) drew from both and was claimed by Hindus and Muslims alike.

A careful comparison notices that both use the flute and longing, but they mean different things. For Rumi, the reed longs for God as understood in Islam, the One with no partner. For Krishna devotees, the flute is Krishna’s call to the soul. Comparing them shows how shared images carry different meanings in different traditions.

Think it through
Two Flutes

Rumi, Masnavi 1 (paraphrase): listen to the reed; it cries of separation since it was cut from the reed bed. Bhagavata Purana, book 10 (paraphrase): when Krishna plays his flute in the forest, the cowherd women leave their work and run toward the sound.

In one image the flute cries; in the other it calls. What does that difference suggest about each tradition's picture of longing?
