The Satan, Demons and Dark Powers

Dark Powers in the Texts

Many people picture Satan as a horned monster and think of demons as the stuff of scary movies. The ancient texts are quieter and more interesting than that. In the Hebrew Bible, satan is a word for an adversary. In Job, "the satan" stands in God's own court. Much later, the New Testament uses Satan and the devil as names for one enemy of God. This unit follows how that change happened.

This unit reads the documents first. They include the Hebrew Bible, the Greek Septuagint, 1 Enoch and Jubilees, the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Gospels. Early Christian writers such as Justin Martyr and Origen join them. Then you meet the researchers who study these texts. Among them are Peggy Day, Elaine Pagels, Archie T. Wright, Walter Wink and Michael Heiser. You will read each view beside Jewish and Christian readings.

By the end of the unit you will be able to explain what satan means in Hebrew and why the word "the" matters. You will compare readings of Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28. You will trace names like Belial and Mastema, read 1 Enoch 15 and the Gospel stories in Mark, and explain the two goats of the Day of Atonement. You will also practice reading these stories with care.

The big question

How did ancient texts picture the powers that stand against God, and how have readers explained where they came from?

The Adversary
Big questionHow did a Hebrew word for "adversary" become the name of the Bible's great enemy of God?
The story

Who Let Him In?

A student reading Job notices something odd: the satan walks into God's court as if he belongs there.

Maya had a reading assignment for her literature class: the first two chapters of the book of Job. She expected a simple story about a good man who suffers. She opened a Bible at her kitchen table in Joliet and started to read. By the sixth verse she had stopped and read the line again.

The story says the sons of God came to stand before the LORD. It sounds like a royal court, with officials reporting to their king. Then the text says the satan came along with them. Nobody throws him out. Nobody seems surprised. God even speaks to him first and asks where he has been.

Maya had always pictured Satan as a monster with horns, far from God. This figure seemed different. He talked like an official doing a job. He questioned whether Job was good only because life had been easy for him. God set limits on what he could do, and he obeyed them.

She showed her older brother, who was taking a course on the Hebrew Bible. He pointed to a small note at the bottom of the page. It said the Hebrew has "the satan", with the word "the", and that the word means adversary. "So is it a name or a job title?" Maya asked. Her brother smiled. "Scholars have written whole books on that question."

Scholars have followed that question a long way. Peggy L. Day studied every use of the Hebrew word in An Adversary in Heaven (1988). Elaine Pagels traced the later story in The Origin of Satan (1995). This chapter follows the path through the ancient texts themselves. It starts with a word for an opponent. It ends with the names Satan and the devil in the New Testament.

Section 1

A Word for Adversary

21.1

What Satan Means

Main ideaIn Hebrew, satan is an ordinary word that means adversary, someone who opposes or blocks another.

Start with Numbers 22. A foreign prophet named Balaam rides his donkey toward Moab. The text says the angel of the LORD stood in the road "as an adversary" against him. The Hebrew word there is . Here the adversary is God’s own messenger. He blocks the road to stop Balaam from doing wrong.

The same word is used for people. In 1 Samuel 29:4, Philistine commanders worry that David might become a satan against them in battle. In 1 Kings 11:14, God raises up Hadad the Edomite as a satan against King Solomon. In these verses the word just means an enemy or opponent. Nobody in them is a spirit being.

So satan in the Hebrew Bible is first a common noun, like the English word "opponent". It describes a role, not one fixed person. Anyone can be a satan to someone else: a soldier, a king or an angel. Scholars call this the word’s plain , and it is the starting point for everything else in this chapter.

Peggy L. Day made this point in An Adversary in Heaven (1988). She studied each place the Hebrew Bible uses the word. She argued that it does not yet name one single enemy of God there. Many scholars share her starting point. First ask who is opposing whom in each passage.

21.2

The Satan in God's Court

Main ideaIn Job 1–2 the satan is a member of God's heavenly court who questions whether Job serves God for nothing.

Job 1:6 opens a heavenly scene. The sons of God come to present themselves before the LORD, and the satan comes among them. In Hebrew the word has the article: ha-satan, "the satan". Many scholars read this as a title, like "the prosecutor" or "the challenger".

The setting is what scholars call the : God’s heavenly court, where spirit beings stand before him. In Job, the satan reports that he has been roaming the earth. God points to Job as a man without equal. The satan answers that Job is good only because God protects him and his family.

God allows a test, but sets clear limits. In chapter 1 the satan may not touch Job himself. In chapter 2 he may not take Job’s life. The satan can only act inside the bounds God gives. After chapter 2 he never appears in the book again.

Many modern scholars argue that this satan is not the devil of later writings. They see an officer of the court doing a hard job, perhaps with a bad attitude. Many Christian readers have long read Job’s satan as the devil himself. Both readings look closely at the same two chapters.

21.3

An Accuser in Zechariah

Main ideaIn Zechariah 3 the satan accuses the high priest Joshua, and the LORD rebukes the accuser.

Zechariah was a prophet in Jerusalem around 520 BCE. His people had come back from exile in Babylon and were rebuilding the temple. In Zechariah 3 he sees a vision set in a heavenly court. Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the LORD. The satan stands at his right hand to him.

Joshua is wearing filthy clothes. In the vision, that dirt pictures the people’s guilt. The satan’s job seems to be to point it out. But the accuser does not get the last word. The LORD rebukes the satan and calls Joshua a burning stick snatched from the fire.

Then the dirty clothes are taken off. Joshua is given clean robes and a clean turban. The vision says his guilt has been taken away. The scene moves from accusation to cleansing and a fresh start.

Like Job, this text uses the article: ha-satan. Scholars point out that in both books the satan acts inside God’s court, as an accuser. In both, God has the final say. Readers have noticed that the accuser in Zechariah ends up rebuked, a step toward the enemy of God pictured in later writings.

Section 2

Name or Title?

21.4

Why "The" Matters

Main ideaJob and Zechariah say "the satan", which works like a title for a role, not the personal name it becomes in the New Testament.

In Hebrew, as in English, people’s names usually do not take "the". Nobody says "the Moses" or "the Sarah". But Job and Zechariah say "the satan". Heiser argued in Demons (2020) that this small word is a clue. It shows the word is working as a , a description of a role, not a .

Think of "the judge" or "the coach". Those words tell you what a person does, not who the person is. Heiser read ha-satan the same way: the one whose job was to challenge. This is why he said the Old Testament does not use satan as a name the way the New Testament uses Satan.

One verse is harder. In 1 Chronicles 21:1 the word appears without "the": satan stood up against Israel and moved David to count the people. Many translations print it as the name Satan. Other scholars translate "an adversary", which could mean a human enemy or an unnamed spirit. The retelling of the same story in 2 Samuel 24:1 says the LORD’s anger moved David.

Readers handle this in different ways. Some see the Chronicler naming the devil for the first time. Others doubt the verse gives a personal name. The debate shows how much one small word can matter when reading ancient texts.

21.5

From Satan to Devil

Main ideaGreek translators turned satan into diabolos, "slanderer", which gave English the word devil.

Around the 200s BCE, Jewish scholars began to translate the Hebrew Bible into Greek. That translation is called the . In Job, the translators wrote ho diabolos for ha-satan. Diabolos means slanderer, someone who speaks lies or accusations against another. Brenton’s English version of the Septuagint (1844) reads Job 1:6 as "the devil came with them".

The English word comes from diabolos. So does the Spanish diablo. When the New Testament was written in Greek, its writers used both words. Sometimes they wrote Satanas, the Hebrew word in Greek letters. Sometimes they wrote diabolos. By then both had become names for one enemy of God.

Revelation 12:9 pulls the names together in one line. It calls one figure the great dragon, the old serpent, the devil and Satan, who deceives the whole world. Scholars often point to this verse. It links the serpent of Eden with Satan and the devil, something the Old Testament never spells out.

So the path runs like this. A Hebrew word for adversary became a title in God’s court. Greek translators rendered it slanderer. By the time of the New Testament, Satan and the devil were names for one leader of evil.

21.6

The Serpent in Eden

Main ideaGenesis 3 calls the tempter only "the serpent"; later writings link it to the devil, and readers still differ on what it was.

Genesis 3 tells of a serpent in the garden of Eden. It speaks to the woman and questions God’s command. The Hebrew word for serpent is . Genesis 3 never calls it Satan. It is simply the serpent, more crafty than any creature God had made.

Heiser argued in The Unseen Realm (2015) that Eden was more than a garden. He read it as God’s home on earth, where God met with his council. So he saw the serpent as a divine being, not just an animal. He noted that nachash is related to Hebrew words for shining and for divination. To him this hinted at a bright, heavenly figure.

In Heiser’s reading, this being rebelled against God’s plan for humans. His punishment was to be cast down to the ground, below the heavenly realm. Heiser called him the Eden , and he saw him as the one the New Testament names Satan and the devil.

Other readers differ. Many Jewish readers treat the serpent as a clever animal in a story about human choices. An early link to the devil appears in the Wisdom of Solomon, a Jewish book written in Greek before the New Testament. Many Christians, following Revelation 12:9 and Romans 16:20, identify the serpent with Satan, as Heiser did, though not always for his reasons.

Two Kings, Two Poems

21.7

The King of Babylon

Main ideaIsaiah 14 is a taunt song against the king of Babylon, who is pictured as a shining star thrown down from heaven.

Isaiah 14:4 tells Israel to take up a taunt song against the king of Babylon. A mocks a proud enemy who has fallen. The poem imagines the dead in the underworld stirring to greet this king. They say he has become as weak as they are.

Isaiah 14:12 calls the king helel ben shachar in Hebrew. That means "shining one, son of the dawn", likely the morning star we now know as the planet Venus. Modern translations often say Day Star or morning star. The King James Version used , a Latin word for the morning star meaning light-bringer.

In the poem, the king says in his heart that he will climb to heaven. He will set his throne above the stars of God and sit on the mount of assembly in the far north. He wants to be like the Most High. Instead, he is brought down to the pit.

Scholars have noticed that this language fits old stories from Canaan and Ugarit. Those stories told of gods meeting on a mountain in the north. The poem borrows that picture of a heavenly council to show how far the proud king falls.

21.8

The King of Tyre

Main ideaEzekiel 28 speaks to the king of Tyre as if he had been a glorious being in Eden who fell through pride.

Tyre was a rich port city on the coast of what is now Lebanon. Its merchants traded across the Mediterranean Sea. Around the 580s BCE, the prophet Ezekiel spoke against its king. In Ezekiel 28:2 the king claims to be a god, sitting in the seat of the gods. Ezekiel answers that he is only a man.

Then, in 28:12–19, Ezekiel sings a , a sad poem for the dead or the fallen. He says the king was in Eden, the garden of God. He was covered with precious stones. He is called an anointed on the holy mountain of God. He was blameless until wrong was found in him.

His heart grew proud because of his beauty. His trade filled him with violence. So God cast him from the mountain of God to the ground. The poem ends with the king as a warning to other rulers.

The lament mixes two pictures. One is a real king growing rich through trade. The other is a shining being in Eden who is thrown down. Readers have argued for centuries about how these two pictures fit together.

21.9

Kings or a Fallen Spirit?

Main ideaReaders see Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 as poems about human kings, about a fallen spirit, or about kings pictured through an older story of a rebel cast down.

Many Jewish readers and many modern scholars read both poems as being about human kings. Isaiah names Babylon; Ezekiel names Tyre. In their view, the heavenly language is poetry. It shows how high these kings raised themselves and how far they fell.

Many Christian readers, from early church writers such as Origen and Tertullian onward, also saw the fall of a spirit being in these poems. They read them as telling how Satan fell through pride. That is why the name Lucifer, from Isaiah 14, became a popular name for the devil.

Heiser took a middle path in The Unseen Realm (2015). He agreed that the poems are aimed at real human kings. But he argued they borrow the story of a divine rebel who was cast down. For Heiser, the pattern behind both poems was the rebel of : a shining being who wanted to rise, and fell.

All three readings take the words seriously. They differ on how poetry works and on what the poets had in mind. A careful reader can explain each view and point to the verses each one leans on.

New Names, New Testament

21.10

Belial and Mastema

Main ideaJewish writings from the Second Temple period gave the leader of evil spirits new names, such as Belial and Mastema.

The runs from about 516 BCE, when the temple was rebuilt, to 70 CE, when the Romans destroyed it. In these centuries Jewish writers wrote many books outside the Hebrew Bible. Some of them gave names to a leader of evil powers.

One name is . In the Hebrew Bible, beliyya’al is a word meaning worthlessness. The KJV prints phrases like "sons of Belial" for wicked men, as in 1 Samuel 2:12. In the Dead Sea Scrolls, Belial becomes a leader of evil. The War Scroll describes a coming battle of the sons of light against the sons of darkness, who belong to Belial.

Another name is , from a Hebrew word for hostility. The book of Jubilees, written around 160 BCE, calls him the chief of the spirits. In Jubilees 10, Noah prays against the evil spirits troubling his grandchildren. Mastema asks God to leave some spirits under his control. God allows a tenth to stay and binds the rest.

Paul uses the name Belial once. In 2 Corinthians 6:15 he asks what agreement Christ could have with Belial. Elaine Pagels, in The Origin of Satan (1995), is one of many scholars who trace how Jewish thinking about a chief enemy grew in these centuries.

21.11

Satan in the Gospels

Main ideaIn the Gospels, Satan and the devil are names for one enemy who tests Jesus and whose power Jesus says is ending.

The Gospels of Matthew, Mark and Luke all tell of Jesus being tested in the wilderness. In Matthew 4:1–11 the devil offers him bread, a leap from the temple and all the kingdoms of the world. Jesus answers each time with words from Deuteronomy. The devil leaves.

Notice the offer of kingdoms. In Luke 4:6 the devil claims that authority over the kingdoms has been given to him. Some scholars read this against Deuteronomy 32, where the nations were placed under other heavenly powers. Other readers see it as the devil’s boast, not a true claim.

In Luke 10:18, when his followers return from their mission, Jesus says he saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven. In John 12:31 he calls the devil the and says he will be cast out. The New Testament pictures Satan as a real enemy whose power is being broken.

Revelation 12 then shows a war in heaven. Michael and his angels fight the dragon, who is thrown down to the earth. Many readers see the New Testament gathering the threads of the Old: the serpent, the accuser and the fallen king. It gives them one name.

The story

The Man Who Ran to the Shore

On the far side of the Sea of Galilee, a man who has lived alone for a long time runs toward a boat.

The Gospel of Mark tells of a boat crossing the Sea of Galilee at night. Jesus and his followers land on the eastern shore, in the country of the Gerasenes. Most people there were not Jewish. On the hillside above the water, a large herd of pigs is feeding.

Near the shore are tombs cut into the rock. Mark says a man lived there, away from his town. People had tried to hold him with chains, but he broke them. Day and night he cried out among the tombs and the hills. The people of his town had stopped coming near.

Then Mark adds a surprising detail. When the man sees Jesus from far off, he does not run away. He runs toward him and kneels. Mark says an unclean spirit was troubling the man. Jesus speaks to it and asks its name. It answers, Legion, for we are many.

By the end of the story, the man is sitting calmly, dressed, next to Jesus. He asks to get into the boat and go along. Jesus says no. He tells him to go home to his own people and tell them what the Lord has done for him. The man goes through the Ten Cities, telling his story.

Readers have asked many questions about stories like this one. What did people in Jesus's time think these spirits were? Where did they come from? Three Groups of Dark Powers

22.1

Three Stories of Rebellion

Main ideaGenesis tells three stories of rebellion, and some scholars link each one to a different kind of dark power.

Many people use the words Satan, fallen angels and demons as if they meant the same thing. The ancient texts are more varied than that. Start with Genesis. It tells three stories in which the order God set up is broken. Readers often call them the three of Genesis.

The first rebellion is in Eden, Genesis 3, where the serpent turns humans away from God. The second is Genesis 6:1–4, where the sons of God take human wives and the Nephilim, or giants, are born. The third is Babel, Genesis 11, when God scatters the nations.

Michael Heiser, in Demons (2020), linked each story to a group of dark powers. First is the Eden rebel, whom the New Testament calls the devil. Second are the sons of God set over the nations, who became corrupt. Paul’s letters call powers like these , or rulers. Third are , which he read as the spirits of the dead giants.

Not every scholar sorts things this way. Many Christian teachers treat all these as one group of fallen angels led by Satan. Heiser’s map is one way to organize the texts this chapter reads next.

Main ideaDeuteronomy 32 and Paul's letters speak of spirit powers over nations, and scholars differ on who Paul's rulers and powers are.

A few verses later, Deuteronomy 32:17 says Israel sacrificed to , not to God. Shedim is a Hebrew word for spirit beings. It is related to a word in Akkadian, the language of ancient Mesopotamia. The Greek Septuagint translated it daimonia, demons. The KJV says "devils".

Daniel 10 speaks of a prince of Persia and a prince of Greece who resist a heavenly messenger. Paul, writing in Greek, uses words like rulers, authorities and powers. Ephesians 6:12 says the struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against rulers and powers of darkness in the heavenly places.

22.3

Unclean Spirits

Main ideaThe Gospels call demons unclean spirits, using a word from Israel's laws about what may come near God's holy presence.

The Gospel of Mark often uses the phrase . In Greek it is pneuma akatharton. Mark also uses daimonion, , for the same beings. The KJV usually translates daimonion as "devil", so it can say Jesus cast out many devils. Modern translations usually say demons.

Why unclean? In the Law of Moses, being unclean did not mean being dirty or bad. It meant not being ready to come near God’s holy presence. Touching a dead body made a person unclean for a time, as Numbers 19 says. Certain animals, like pigs, were unclean to eat. There were ways to become clean again.

Scholars have noticed how many unclean things fill the story of the man from the tombs. The tombs held the dead. The land was outside Israel. A herd of pigs fed nearby. In that setting, Mark says an unclean spirit troubled the man, and Jesus set him free.

So the name tells readers something. In the Gospels, these spirits stand against God’s holy presence. Jesus, in the stories, has authority over them. That is why Mark tells the story of the man from the tombs the way he does.

The Giants' Spirits in 1 Enoch

22.4

What 1 Enoch 15 Says

Main idea1 Enoch 15 says that when the giants died, their spirits went out as evil spirits on the earth.

is an ancient Jewish book, written in parts between about 300 BCE and the time of Jesus. Pieces of it in Aramaic were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. J. T. Milik published them in 1976. The full book survives in Ge’ez, the old language of Ethiopia. It is part of the Bible only in the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches.

Its first part, the Book of the , retells Genesis 6. In 1 Enoch, the Watchers are heavenly beings who came down to earth, took human wives and fathered giants. The giants brought violence, and God sent judgment. In chapters 12–16, the Watchers ask Enoch to plead for them, and God gives Enoch a reply.

In 1 Enoch 15:8–12, God explains what happens to the giants. They were born from spirits and flesh. So when their bodies die, the spirits that come out of them will be called evil spirits on the earth. The earth will be their home. They will trouble people, because they came from people.

This is one of the earliest explanations of evil spirits that survives. It appears in a Jewish book that many people read in the centuries before the Gospels were written. Many scholars think it helps explain why the Gospels speak of unclean spirits so often.

22.5

From Enoch to the Gospels

Main ideaMany scholars think 1 Enoch's story of the giants' spirits shaped how Jewish readers of Jesus's day understood unclean spirits.

The Hebrew Bible says little about demons like those in the Gospels. Then, in the Gospels, unclean spirits appear often. Where did the idea come from? Many researchers point to the Book of the Watchers and to other books that retell it.

The evidence starts with the documents. Copies of 1 Enoch were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, so the book was read in the period. Jubilees 10 retells the story, with evil spirits troubling Noah’s grandchildren. The New Testament knows 1 Enoch too: Jude 14–15 quotes it by name.

Archie T. Wright, in The Origin of Evil Spirits (2005), studied how early Jewish writers received the Genesis 6 story. He traced how the giants’ spirits became a way to explain evil spirits. Loren Stuckenbruck, in The Myth of Rebellious Angels (2014), also studied these traditions. Michael Heiser made the same link for general readers in Demons (2020).

Scholars offer this link as the best explanation of the , not the only one. The Gospels themselves never say where demons come from. The link rests on reading them alongside the Jewish books their first readers knew.

22.6

Other Answers

Main ideaMany Christians have taught that demons are fallen angels, while other ancient writers held views close to 1 Enoch's.

A common Christian view says demons are themselves. On this view, when Satan rebelled, some angels joined him. Readers point to Revelation 12:9, which says the dragon’s angels were thrown down with him. Matthew 25:41 also speaks of the devil and his angels.

Other early Christians held views close to 1 Enoch’s. Justin Martyr, a Christian writer around 150 CE, wrote about this in his Second Apology. He said angels fathered children with women, and that these children are the ones called demons. So the 1 Enoch reading has ancient company.

Jewish writings give still more accounts. The book of , from about 160 BCE, tells of evil spirits that trouble Noah’s grandchildren. God binds nine-tenths of them and leaves one-tenth under Mastema. Later rabbis wrote about spirits in many different ways.

The Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the 70s CE, gives yet another view. In Jewish War 7.6.3 he says that what people called demons were the spirits of wicked people who had died. The texts do not agree. Readers can weigh each view by the passages it uses.

22.9

Reading These Stories With Care

Main ideaThe Gospels treat healing the sick and freeing people from spirits as different things, and they end these stories with people restored to their communities.

Read Mark 1:32–34 closely. At sunset, people bring to Jesus those who were sick and those troubled by demons. Mark then says he healed many who were sick with various diseases and cast out many demons. Mark lists these as two separate groups. Matthew 4:24 does the same.

This matters for how the stories are read today. Illness, disability and differences in how people’s minds work are a normal part of human life. They are never a sign of an evil spirit, and no one should ever be treated that way. The Gospel writers themselves kept healing and freeing apart.

Look also at how the stories end. The man in Capernaum is freed right in his own town’s synagogue. The man from the tombs goes home to his friends. In Mark 5:34 a woman who is healed is called daughter. The people in these stories have names, homes and families. The stories end with : a person welcomed back.

This course studies how these texts were read. It does not teach any spiritual practice. Readers from many backgrounds notice two things in these stories: Jesus’s authority and his care for people.

Some later rabbis read Azazel as the name of a place: a rough, steep cliff in the wilderness. The Talmud, in tractate Yoma, describes the goat being taken to a high rocky place. On this reading, Azazel names where the goat went.

1 Enoch gives Azazel a different role. In 1 Enoch 8, Azazel is one of the leaders of the Watchers. He taught people to make weapons and jewelry. In 1 Enoch 10:4–5, God tells the angel Raphael to bind Azazel and cast him into darkness in the desert, under rough, jagged rocks.

Many scholars read Azazel as a wilderness , as 1 Enoch does. Even so, they note that the goat was not a gift to Azazel. It carried the people’s sins out of the holy camp. It sent them back to a wild place, far from God’s presence.

9. Which early Christian writer said demons were the children of angels and women?

Why: Justin Martyr, in his Second Apology (c. 150 CE), wrote that the angels' children with women are the ones called demons.

10. What does 1 Enoch 15 say about the giants' spirits?

Why: 1 Enoch 15:8–12 says the spirits that came out of the dead giants became evil spirits on the earth.

11. Which Hebrew word in Deuteronomy 32:17 names spirit beings Israel sacrificed to?

Why: Deuteronomy 32:17 uses shedim, which the Septuagint translated as demons.

12. How does Mark 5 end for the man from the tombs?

Why: In Mark 5:19–20 Jesus sends the man home to his friends, and he tells his story in the Decapolis.

13. How do the Gospels treat healing the sick and freeing people from spirits?

Why: Mark 1:32–34 lists the sick and those troubled by demons as two groups, keeping healing and freeing apart.

14. What happened to the goat for Azazel on the Day of Atonement?

Why: Leviticus 16:21–22 says the high priest confessed the sins over the living goat, and it was led into the wilderness.

15. Which view says demons are fallen angels who followed Satan?

Why: Many Christians teach that demons are angels who joined Satan, pointing to Revelation 12:9. Justin Martyr and 1 Enoch saw them as the giants' spirits.
