Compare like with like

Two traditions. Each one has IDEALS — what its texts and teachers say people should be.

Compare ideals with ideals and practice with practice. Never set your tradition’s best against someone else’s worst — it is the single most common way a comparison stops being honest.

What people get wrong

⚠️ People often think…

All religions are basically saying the same thing.

They agree a great deal about how to treat people and disagree deeply about what is REAL — whether there is a self, whether there is one God, what happens at death. Flattening the differences is a way of not looking, and the traditions themselves reject it.

Agreement on ethics, disagreement on reality.

⚠️ People often think…

Two traditions using the same word mean the same thing by it.

Check the setting. “Salvation”, “soul”, “heaven” and “grace” carry different meanings across traditions and sometimes across schools inside one. Assuming the word does the translating is how a comparison goes wrong quietly.

Same word, check the meaning.

⚠️ People often think…

A good comparison ends by saying which tradition is better.

Ranking is not describing. “More advanced”, “more rational” and “more peaceful” are verdicts, and a description that hands down a verdict has stopped being a description. You can hold a conviction and still write an accurate account.

Describe. Do not rank.

⚠️ People often think…

Civic pluralism means believing all religions are true.

Those are two different things. CIVIC pluralism is about equal rights and real knowledge of your neighbors — you can hold it while being certain your own tradition is right. THEOLOGICAL pluralism is the claim that all are true, which is itself one position among many.

Civic is about rights. Theological is a claim.

Worth knowing cold

Four rules for comparison

The traditions agree a great deal about how to treat people and disagree deeply about what is real. Hold both halves.

  1. Do not flatten, do not separate “They’re all the same” and “they have nothing in common” are both ways of not looking. The interesting answer is always in between.
  2. Match the level Ideals with ideals; practice with practice. A tradition’s scripture against another’s worst century is not a comparison.
  3. Similar words, different meanings Two traditions can both use “salvation” or “soul” and mean genuinely different things. Check the setting before assuming overlap.
  4. Rank nothing “More advanced”, “more rational”, “more peaceful” have no place in a description. Describing is not ranking.

1 Watch one

Two traditions both use the word “soul”. How do you compare them?

  1. Do not assume the word carries the same load in both.
  2. Ask each tradition what it means: is this permanent, is it individual, does it survive death, does it transmigrate?
  3. Write down both answers in that tradition’s own terms first.
  4. Only then compare. Now you are comparing ideas, not a shared translator’s word. ✓

Helpful Hints

🧭 Seven questions

What is ultimately real? · What is a person? · What has gone wrong? · What is the remedy? · How should we treat one another? · What happens at death? · Why do the innocent suffer?

Real, person, problem, remedy, neighbor, death, suffering.

🤝 The one idea

The traditions agree a great deal about how to treat people and disagree deeply about what is real. Hold both halves. Dropping either one is how comparison goes wrong.

⚠️ Traps to avoid

  • ‘They’re all the same’ and ‘they have nothing in common’ are both ways of not looking.
  • Never compare your best with their worst. Ideals with ideals; practice with practice.
  • Similar words ≠ same meaning. Check the setting.
  • Rank nothing. ‘More advanced’, ‘more rational’, ‘more peaceful’ have no place in a description.
  • Civic pluralism (equal rights, real knowledge) ≠ theological pluralism (all are true).
  • You have a lens. Say so.
