Enoch in the New Testament

The Watchers Remembered

The story of the Watchers did not stop with 1 Enoch. Centuries later, New Testament writers still remembered it. Jude quotes 1 Enoch by name. Second Peter speaks of angels put in Tartarus. First Peter tells of spirits in prison from Noah's day. Paul writes again and again about rulers, authorities and powers. This unit reads those passages closely, in translation, with attention to the Greek words behind them.

Michael Heiser argued that these writers read Genesis 6 and Deuteronomy 32 the way many Jews of their time did, through books like 1 Enoch. For him, the powers in Paul's letters are the rebel gods of the nations. Other scholars read the same verses in other ways: as human rulers, as structures of society, or as a rebellion before Eden. You will meet each reading and the evidence behind it.

By the end of the unit, you will be able to compare Jude 14–15 with 1 Enoch 1:9. You will explain three readings of 1 Peter 3:19 and several readings of Paul's powers. You will also explain why Jude's quotation did not bring 1 Enoch into most Bibles, but did not stop one ancient church from keeping it.

The big question

How did New Testament writers remember the Watchers and the gods of the nations, and how have readers understood what they wrote?

Big questionWhat did New Testament writers take from the story of the Watchers, and how have readers made sense of it?
The story

Two Books on One Shelf

An imagined monk in Ethiopia copies the Letter of Jude and knows that the book Jude quotes is sitting right beside him.

Picture a monastery in the highlands of Ethiopia, several hundred years ago. Morning light comes through a small window. A monk sits on a low stool with a sheet of parchment across his knees. He dips a reed pen into black ink and copies the Letter of Jude, one of the shortest books of the New Testament. He writes in Ge'ez, the old church language of Ethiopia.

He reaches verse 14. Jude writes that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, spoke a prophecy about the Lord coming with thousands of his holy ones. The monk pauses. He knows those words. On the shelf behind him is another manuscript, the Book of Enoch, also in Ge'ez. Its very first chapter holds the same prophecy that Jude repeats.

For this monk, there is nothing strange about the match. His church, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, counts the Book of Enoch as part of its Bible. For most churches in Europe, the Middle East and the Americas, the story went another way. They kept Jude and set Enoch aside. For centuries, many Western readers knew Enoch's book only by name.

Jude is not the only New Testament writer who seems to know the Enoch story. Second Peter mentions angels who sinned and were held in chains. First Peter mentions spirits in prison from the days of Noah. In this chapter you will read these passages closely. You will see how Michael Heiser read them in light of 1 Enoch, and how other readers have read them differently.

Section 1

Jude Quotes Enoch

25.1

A Short, Urgent Letter

Main ideaJude is a 25-verse letter that warns a community about false teachers, using examples from old stories of judgment.

The sits just before Revelation at the end of the New Testament. It has only 25 verses. The writer calls himself Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James (Jude 1). Christian tradition has often identified him with Jude, or Judas, named in Mark 6:3 as a brother of Jesus. Some scholars accept that tradition, and others think a later writer used Jude’s name. The date is also debated.

Jude says he meant to write about the salvation his readers shared. Instead, he felt he had to warn them. Certain people had slipped into the community, he says, and were turning grace into an excuse for wrong living (Jude 3–4). To make his point, Jude reaches for well-known stories of judgment. He names Israel in the wilderness, the angels who left their place, and Sodom and Gomorrah (Jude 5–7).

Many of Jude’s examples come from the Hebrew Bible. Some do not. In verse 9 he mentions the archangel Michael arguing with the devil over the body of Moses. That scene is not in the Hebrew Bible. Early church writers said it came from a Jewish book about Moses that survives only in part. In verses 14–15, Jude quotes the Book of Enoch. Jude clearly expected his readers to know these stories too. This makes Jude a window into the wider library of Jewish writings.

25.2

The Seventh from Adam

Main ideaJude calls Enoch 'the seventh from Adam', the same way Genesis 5 and 1 Enoch count his place in the family line.

Jude 14 introduces its quotation with a title: Enoch, the seventh from Adam. That title comes from a count. lists the family line from Adam to Noah. If you count Adam as the first, Enoch is the seventh name: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared and Enoch. Ancient readers often counted this way, and the number seven had a sense of completeness in many biblical texts.

Genesis gives Enoch only a few verses, but they are unusual. Every other man in the list lives, has children and dies. Enoch lives 365 years, and then the text says he walked with God, and he was not, for God took him (Genesis 5:24). The chapter does not say that Enoch died. Readers have wondered about that line for more than two thousand years.

Jewish writers in the Second Temple period filled in the gaps. The book we now call presents itself as Enoch’s own words. It tells of journeys through heaven and of messages he carried. First Enoch also calls him the seventh from Adam (for example, 1 Enoch 60:8). When Jude uses the same title, he is speaking the language of that tradition. Heiser pointed to this as one of several signs that Jude knew 1 Enoch well.

25.3

A Prophecy from 1 Enoch 1:9

Main ideaJude 14–15 repeats a prophecy that closely matches 1 Enoch 1:9, the clearest New Testament quotation of a book outside most Bibles.

Put Jude 14–15 beside 1 Enoch 1:9 and the match is plain. Both say the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones. Both say he comes to carry out judgment on all. Both say he will convict the ungodly of their ungodly deeds and of the harsh words they have spoken against him. The order and the repeated word ’ungodly’ line up closely.

Most scholars agree that Jude is quoting 1 Enoch, not just an idea from the air. The opening chapters of 1 Enoch survive in Aramaic fragments from the , in Greek, and in full in Ge’ez. Jude’s wording is closest to the Greek, though scholars still discuss exactly which form of the text he knew. The key point is simple: Jude introduces the words with ’Enoch prophesied’.

Readers have handled this in different ways. Heiser took it seriously. He argued that Jude and other New Testament writers treated 1 Enoch as a respected guide to Genesis 6, even though Jude does not call it Scripture. Some Christian readers think Jude quoted a true saying preserved in the book, without approving the whole book. Others say Jude simply used a text his readers valued. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church reads the match as support for 1 Enoch’s place in its canon.

Section 2

Angels Held in Chains

25.4

Angels Who Left Their Place

Main ideaJude 6 speaks of angels who left their proper home and are kept in chains until judgment, echoing the Watchers of 1 Enoch.

Jude 6 describes a group of angels. They did not keep their first position, or rule, and they left their own home. So they are kept in everlasting chains under darkness until the judgment of the great day. The verse is short, but every phrase counts. The Greek word for ’first estate’ can mean an original rank or domain. The ’habitation’ is their proper dwelling place.

Genesis 6:1–4 tells of the sons of God taking human wives. It says nothing about chains or darkness. First Enoch fills in that part. In 1 Enoch 10, God tells the angel Raphael to bind Azazel and put him in darkness. He tells Michael to bind Semjaza and his companions under the earth until the day of their judgment. These leaders of the are held, not destroyed.

Heiser argued that Jude 6 points straight to this Enoch story. Jude 7 supports the link, he noted. It says Sodom and Gomorrah sinned ’in like manner’ and went after ’strange flesh’. Heiser read that as a comparison with angels crossing the line between heaven and earth. Other readers see Jude 6 as a general reference to angels who rebelled, perhaps at the beginning, with no link to Genesis 6. Both readings are held by serious scholars.

25.5

Tartarus in 2 Peter

Main ideaSecond Peter 2:4 uses a Greek verb built on Tartarus, the underworld prison where Greek stories said the Titans were held.

Second Peter 2:4 says God did not spare the angels who sinned. The King James Version says God cast them down to hell and handed them over to chains of darkness until judgment. The English word ’hell’ hides something. The Greek verb is , which means ’to put in Tartarus’. It appears only here in the whole New Testament.

was a well-known word to Greek speakers. In the poet Hesiod’s Theogony, written about 700 BCE, Tartarus is a deep, dark place far below the earth. There the Titans, an older generation of gods, were locked up after losing a great war. So when 2 Peter uses this word, Greek-speaking readers would hear the idea of a prison for defeated divine beings, not a place for dead humans.

Heiser argued that the writer chose this word on purpose. He also pointed out that the Greek text of 1 Enoch 20:2 uses Tartarus too, naming the angel Uriel as set over it. For Heiser, 2 Peter is using a word both Greek and Jewish readers would link with imprisoned rebel beings. Other scholars agree the word is borrowed but say it may simply mean ’the lowest depths’, without any intended link to the Titans.

25.6

Angels, Noah, then Sodom

Main ideaSecond Peter 2:4–8 lists judgments in the same order as Genesis, which many readers take as a sign the angels' sin is the one in Genesis 6.

Read 2 Peter 2:4–8 as a list. First come the angels who sinned (verse 4). Next comes the flood in the days of Noah, when God kept Noah and seven others safe (verse 5). Then come Sodom and Gomorrah, and the rescue of Lot (verses 6–8). The writer uses these examples to show that God knows how to rescue the faithful and hold the wrong-doers for judgment (verse 9).

Look at the order. In Genesis, the sons of God story (Genesis 6:1–4) comes right before the flood (Genesis 6–9). Sodom comes later (Genesis 19). Second Peter follows the same sequence. Heiser and many other scholars take this as a clue. If the angels’ sin sits just before Noah in the list, it is most likely the sin of Genesis 6, as 1 Enoch tells it.

Other readers disagree. A long Christian tradition links the fall of angels with a rebellion led by Satan before the creation of humans, or around the time of Eden. In that reading, 2 Peter 2:4 refers to that early fall, and the order of the list is not meant to point to Genesis 6. Readers of both views agree that 2 Peter uses the angels, the flood and Sodom as warnings and as signs of rescue.

The Spirits in Prison

25.7

A Famous Hard Passage

Main ideaFirst Peter 3:18–22 says Christ went and preached to spirits in prison who disobeyed in Noah's day, one of the hardest passages in the New Testament.

First Peter was written to Christians who were being treated badly for their faith. In chapter 3, the writer encourages them with Christ’s example. Christ suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring people to God (3:18). He was put to death in the flesh but made alive in the Spirit. Then comes a surprise in verses 19–20.

In the Spirit, the text says, Christ went and preached to the . These spirits had disobeyed long ago, when God waited patiently in the days of Noah while the ark was being built. Only eight people were saved through water. Verse 21 links that water to baptism. Verse 22 ends with Christ in heaven at God’s right hand, with angels, authorities and powers made subject to him.

Readers have argued over these verses for a very long time. Who are the spirits? Where is the prison? When did Christ go there, and what did he preach? The Greek verb is , which means to announce or proclaim. It does not always mean preaching a message of rescue. Keep these four questions in mind. The next two lessons look at three main answers.

25.8

Heiser's Reading Through Enoch

Main ideaHeiser, with many scholars, read the spirits in prison as the Watchers, and Christ's preaching as an announcement of victory over them.

In 1 Enoch 12–16, the Watchers are already bound. They ask Enoch to carry a written request to God, hoping for forgiveness. Enoch goes, and he returns with God’s answer. They will have no peace, and their request is refused. In that story, a righteous man goes to imprisoned spirits and announces God’s verdict on them.

Heiser argued that 1 Peter 3 draws on this story, as he explains in The Unseen Realm (2015) and Reversing Hermon (2017). The spirits in prison are the Watchers who sinned before the flood. Christ, like a new Enoch, goes to them and announces that he has won. They are not given a second chance. Verse 22 then shows the result: angels, authorities and powers are placed under Christ. This reading was also argued in detail by the Catholic scholar William J. Dalton in a 1965 study.

This reading explains some puzzling details. The New Testament usually uses ’spirits’ for non-human beings rather than for dead people. It explains why Noah is mentioned and why the passage ends with powers made subject. Heiser saw it as a picture of what he called Christ’s mission to reclaim what the rebel powers had taken. Critics of this reading ask why Peter did not name Enoch or the Watchers if he meant them.

25.9

Two Other Main Readings

Main ideaOther readers say Christ preached through Noah to the people of his day, or that Christ went to the realm of the dead after his death.

One reading comes from Augustine, a bishop in North Africa, who wrote about it in a letter in the early 400s. He thought the preaching happened in Noah’s own time. Christ, by his Spirit, spoke through Noah to the people of that day. They did not listen, and so they are now ’in prison’. Some later Protestant scholars have followed this reading, which does not need 1 Enoch at all.

A second reading is often called the . Here, between his death and resurrection, Christ went down to the realm of the dead. There he announced the good news to the people who had died, including those of Noah’s time. Readers often point to 1 Peter 4:6, which says the gospel was preached to the dead. The Apostles’ Creed says Christ ’descended into hell’, which many churches understand in this way. Eastern Orthodox icons of the resurrection often show Christ pulling Adam and Eve from their graves.

So there are three main readings: preaching through Noah, preaching to the dead, and proclaiming victory to the Watchers. Each has strong points and hard questions. Scholars still disagree, and all three are held by careful readers today. Knowing all three helps you see why 1 Peter 3 is called one of the hardest passages in the New Testament.

Section 4

Jude, Enoch and the Canon

25.10

Why Some Doubted Jude

Main ideaSome early Christians hesitated to accept Jude because it quoted 1 Enoch, but churches kept Jude in their Bibles.

A is the list of books a community treats as its Scripture. The New Testament canon took shape over several centuries. Some books were accepted early and almost everywhere. Others were questioned for a time. Jude was one of the questioned books. So were 2 Peter, James, 2 and 3 John and Revelation, for different reasons.

Eusebius, a church historian writing about 325 CE, grouped Jude among the ’disputed’ books. They were known and read by many, but not accepted by everyone. Jerome, the scholar who translated the Bible into Latin, wrote in 393 CE that many rejected Jude because it quoted Enoch, a book not in Scripture. He added that Jude had gained authority by its age and use, and was counted with the holy books.

In the end, Jude stayed in the New Testament of every major church. Most churches did not add 1 Enoch. Heiser pointed out that this history shows a tension. Early readers noticed that Jude valued Enoch’s book, and that bothered some of them. Yet they kept Jude. For Heiser, that makes 1 Enoch important for reading the New Testament, whether or not a church counts it as Scripture.

25.11

Enoch In and Out of the Bible

Main ideaQuoting a book does not make it Scripture for most churches, but the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches include 1 Enoch in their canon.

Quoting a book is not the same as calling it Scripture. Paul quotes Greek poets in Acts 17:28 and Titus 1:12, yet no church put those poets in the Bible. Many readers apply the same rule to Jude and 1 Enoch. Others note that Jude says Enoch ’prophesied’, a word he does not use lightly. Around 200 CE, the North African writer Tertullian argued that the Book of Enoch should be respected, partly because Jude quotes it.

Judaism did not include 1 Enoch in the Hebrew Bible, and most churches followed. The Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Protestant canons all leave it out. The and the Eritrean Orthodox Church are different. Their canon includes 1 Enoch, and Jubilees as well. That is why the full text survived in Ge’ez, copied by hand in monasteries for centuries.

In 1773 the Scottish traveler James Bruce brought Ge’ez copies of 1 Enoch to Europe. English translations followed, including R. H. Charles’s in 1917. Then the Dead Sea Scrolls showed that Jews in the Second Temple period had read Enoch in Aramaic. Heiser used all this evidence. He did not argue that 1 Enoch belongs in every Bible. He argued that readers need it to understand how the New Testament writers read Genesis 6.

The story

A List on the Board

A high school teacher writes Paul's list of powers on the board and asks one simple question that fills the whole hour.

It is a Tuesday afternoon in a high school classroom. Ms. Ortiz picks up a marker and writes a list on the whiteboard: thrones, dominions, principalities, powers. A third wonders if Paul means something like systems, the way people talk about 'the powers that be'.

Ms. Ortiz writes all three answers on the board. 'Good,' she says. 'Scholars have given every one of those answers. Some have given all three at once.' She adds a fourth line: Deuteronomy 32:8. 'Michael Heiser, a scholar of the Hebrew Bible, argued that this verse is the key. He thought Paul's powers are the gods of the nations that the Hebrew Bible talks about.'

The class spends the rest of the hour reading. They look at Ephesians, Colossians, Romans, 1 Corinthians and Galatians. They compare the Greek words behind the English. By the end, no one agrees on everything. But everyone can explain why readers disagree. That is where this chapter will take you, too.

Paul's Vocabulary of Power

26.1

A List of Rulers

Main ideaPaul often lists rulers, authorities, powers, dominions and thrones, words that describe beings by the territory or rank they hold.

Ephesians 1:20–21 says God raised Christ and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places. He is far above all rule, authority, power and dominion, and every name that is named. Main ideaEphesians 6:12 says the struggle is not against flesh and blood but against rulers and authorities in the heavenly places.

Many see the passage as describing both unseen powers and their effects on human life. It is a call to stand firm, not a set of steps or rituals.

26.3

Main ideaRomans 8:38–39 names angels, rulers and powers among the things that cannot separate believers from God's love, as Paul tells it.

Romans 8 ends with one of Paul’s best-known passages. He says he is convinced that nothing can separate believers from the love of God in Christ Jesus. Then he gives a list: death, life, , rulers, things present, things to come, powers, height, depth, and anything else in all creation. The list moves from the biggest human experiences to the whole unseen world.

Some words in the list are easy to read. Death and life, present and future, need little explanation. Others are harder. ’Height’ and ’depth’ were words sometimes used in ancient star-watching for the highest and lowest points of the stars in the sky. Some scholars think Paul had those ideas in mind. Others think he simply meant ’anything above or below’. Either way, Paul’s point is that nothing anywhere is strong enough to break God’s love.

For Heiser, the list shows how Paul saw the unseen world. Angels, rulers and powers were real to Paul, he argued, and some of them opposed God. Yet Paul places them all under the phrase ’any other created thing’. They are creatures, not rivals equal to God. Readers who do not share Heiser’s view still see Romans 8 as a statement of comfort. It is meant to steady people, not to make them afraid.

Section 2

The Gods of the Nations Again

26.4

Back to Deuteronomy 32

Main ideaHeiser argued that Paul's powers are the sons of God set over the nations in Deuteronomy 32:8, now in rebellion.

Deuteronomy 32:8 says that when the Most High divided the nations, he set their borders by a number. In the traditional Hebrew text, the , the number matches the ’sons of Israel’. But a Hebrew manuscript from the Dead Sea Scrolls reads ’sons of God’. The ancient Greek translation, the , has ’angels of God’. Many scholars, Heiser included, think ’sons of God’ was the earlier reading.

Heiser built much of his work on that reading. In his view, Deuteronomy 32:8–9 says God placed the nations under lesser divine beings after Babel, but kept Israel as his own. Deuteronomy 4:19 and 32:17 add that other nations served these beings. Psalm 82 pictures God judging the gods for ruling unjustly. Daniel 10 names a prince of Persia and a prince of Greece who resist a heavenly messenger.

Heiser argued that Paul read his Bible with this picture in mind. The rulers and authorities of Paul’s letters, he said, are these same beings, the gods of the nations. That explains why Paul uses words of territory and rule. It also explains why Paul, sent to the non-Jewish nations, talks so much about the powers. Some scholars accept this link. Others think Paul’s words are more general, or that he mainly meant human and social powers.

Main ideaIn 1 Corinthians 10:20 Paul echoes Deuteronomy 32:17, saying sacrifices to idols are offered to daimonia, not to God.

In Corinth, a Greek city, meat was often sacrificed at temples before it was sold or served. Christians there asked Paul whether they could eat it. In 1 Corinthians 8 and 10, Paul answers carefully. An idol is nothing, he says, just carved wood or stone. But then he adds something in 10:20. Paul is echoing Deuteronomy 32:17. That verse says Israel sacrificed to , not to God, to gods they had not known. The Septuagint translates shedim with the Greek daimonia. Paul uses the same Greek word. The King James Version translates it ’devils’ in both places. Modern translations usually say ’demons’. The idol itself was nothing, but something stood behind it. Other scholars agree Paul is quoting Deuteronomy but read it more simply. They say Paul’s main point is loyalty: believers cannot share in two tables at once (10:21).

The Rulers of This Age

Main ideaFirst Corinthians 2:8 says the rulers of this age would not have crucified the Lord of glory had they understood; readers debate who they are.

The Greek word for rulers here is . The same word can mean human officials or spiritual rulers. Paul adds that these rulers are ’passing away’ or ’coming to nothing’ (2:6).

One reading says the rulers are the humans who took part in the crucifixion: Pilate, Herod and the temple leaders. Acts 4:26–27 uses ’rulers’ this way, naming Herod and Pontius Pilate. Readers who favor the human reading point to how ’rulers’ is used in the Gospels and Acts.

Debated Verses

26.7

Because of the Angels

Main ideaIn 1 Corinthians 11:10 Paul says a woman should have authority on her head 'because of the angels', a phrase readers explain in several ways.

First Corinthians 11:2–16 is about how men and women should appear when they pray or prophesy in the gathered church. The passage deals with head coverings and hair, and it is debated on many points. Verse 10 adds a short, puzzling reason. A woman ought to have authority, or a sign of authority, on her head ’because of the angels’. Paul does not explain what he means.

Readers have offered several answers. Around 200 CE, Tertullian linked the phrase to the angels of Genesis 6 who were drawn to human women. Heiser argued for a reading in the same direction. In Reversing Hermon (2017), he connected the phrase to the Watchers story and its concern about the boundary between heaven and earth. Other scholars think of angels as present in worship. Some Dead Sea Scrolls describe angels as joining the community’s gathering, so good order mattered.

Still other readers focus on the Greek word , ’authority’. They read verse 10 as saying the woman herself has authority over her own head. The whole passage is sensitive. It deals with gender roles, and churches today apply it in very different ways. Some keep head coverings in worship, and many do not. A careful reader can lay out each view of ’because of the angels’ without deciding the wider questions for anyone else.

Main ideaGalatians 4:3, 9 and Colossians 2:8, 20 speak of the stoicheia, a Greek word read as basic principles, physical elements or spiritual beings.

Colossians 2:8 and 2:20 use the same word. The King James Version says ’elements’ or ’rudiments’. Some modern translations say ’elemental spirits’. The translation depends on the reading.

Stoicheia is a Greek word for things in a row, like the letters of the alphabet. It can mean basic principles, the ABCs of a subject. It can mean the physical elements, which Greeks listed as earth, water, air and fire. By the early centuries CE, some writers also used it for spiritual beings linked to the elements or the stars. So there are three main readings: basic principles, physical elements, or spiritual powers.

Galatians 4:8 is often part of the debate. There Paul says the Galatians once served beings that by nature are not gods. Readers who see the stoicheia as spiritual beings, as the divine-council reading tends to, connect 4:8 with 4:9. They see Paul describing the powers behind the nations’ religions. Readers who favor ’basic principles’ note that Paul is talking about the law and religious calendars in 4:10. Scholars have not reached one answer.

26.9

Other Ways to Read the Powers

Main ideaScholars have read Paul's powers as spiritual beings, as structures of human society, or as both, and Heiser's view belongs to this wider debate.

In the twentieth century, scholars argued a great deal about Paul’s powers. The Dutch theologian Hendrikus Berkhof, in Christ and the Powers (1953 in Dutch), read them as the structures that hold society together. These include the state, tradition, morality and religion. They can serve people or trap them. The British scholar G. B. Caird’s Principalities and Powers (1956) also explored how the powers relate to law and government.

In the 1980s and 1990s, the American scholar Walter Wink wrote a series of books starting with Naming the Powers (1984). Wink read the powers as the inner spirit of institutions, such as a company, a nation or an army. In 1992, Clinton E. Arnold’s Powers of Darkness argued the other way. He said Paul’s first readers understood the powers as personal spiritual beings, as many Jewish writings of that time did.

Heiser stood with those who read the powers as personal spiritual beings. His special contribution was the link to Deuteronomy 32 and the divine council. For him, the powers are the rebel gods of the nations. Many readers now combine views. They hold that Paul meant spiritual beings, and that those beings work through human systems. Knowing these options helps you see what is at stake in each reading.

The Powers and the Cross

26.10

Disarmed at the Cross

Main ideaColossians 2:15 says Christ disarmed the rulers and authorities and led them in a public triumph, using an image from Roman victory parades.

Colossians 2:13–15 describes what the writer says God did through the cross. It speaks of forgiveness and of a record of debt canceled and nailed to the cross. Then verse 15 turns to the powers. God disarmed the rulers and authorities, made a public show of them and triumphed over them in it. The King James Version says ’spoiled’, an old word meaning ’stripped’.

The picture comes from Roman life. After a big victory, a Roman general could hold a , a parade through Rome. The defeated leaders were led along, stripped of their weapons, for all to see. The Greek verb in Colossians 2:15, thriambeuō, means to lead in such a triumph. The same verb appears in 2 Corinthians 2:14. The image is surprising. A cross was a sign of shame, but here it becomes a victory parade.

Heiser saw Colossians 2:15 as the center of Paul’s teaching about the powers. The cross, which looked like the powers’ win, was their loss. This fits his reading of 1 Corinthians 2:8. Other readers stress that Colossians places this victory next to forgiveness, so the defeat of the powers and the canceling of debt belong together. Readers of Berkhof’s and Wink’s kind see the cross as exposing unjust human systems. All agree the verse pictures a public reversal.

26.11

Wisdom Shown to the Powers

Main ideaEphesians 3:10 says God's wisdom is made known through the church to the rulers and authorities, which Heiser read as the nations being reclaimed.

Ephesians 3 describes a mystery now revealed. The mystery is that non-Jewish people, the nations, now share fully in God’s promise with Israel (3:6). Then 3:10 says something striking. Through the church, God’s many-sided wisdom is now made known to the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places. The church itself becomes a message to the powers.

Heiser read this against Deuteronomy 32:8–9. If God had placed the nations under lesser beings after Babel, then Jews and non-Jews joining together would be a sign. The nations were being reclaimed. For Heiser, this was part of what he called the forgotten mission of Jesus Christ, the title theme of Reversing Hermon (2017). The powers would see their territories being won back, not by force, but by people from every nation joining one family.

Other readers stress the human side of Ephesians. For them, the key is peace between Jews and non-Jews, who had been divided (2:14–16). The powers are the audience, but the reconciled community is the point. Readers of many views can agree that Ephesians links the unseen realm with a very visible community. It pictures ordinary people from different nations sharing meals, worship and life.

How does Heiser's reading connect these two passages? How would a reader who focuses on the human side explain them?

to bring back together people who were divided

Paul's Powers and Principalities

0 / 8

1. Which Greek word does the King James Version translate as 'principalities'?

Why: Archai means rulers or beginnings, translated 'principalities' in the King James Version.

2. Which verse says the struggle is not against flesh and blood?

Why: Ephesians 6:12 says the struggle is against rulers and powers in the heavenly places.

3. What link did Heiser see between Paul's powers and Deuteronomy 32:8?

Why: Heiser read Paul's powers as the gods of the nations described in Deuteronomy 32:8.

4. Paul's word daimonia in 1 Corinthians 10:20 echoes which verse?

Why: Deuteronomy 32:17 in the Septuagint uses daimonia for the beings Israel sacrificed to.

5. Which statement fits Heiser's reading of 1 Corinthians 2:8?

Why: Heiser argued the rulers of this age were spiritual powers who misread the cross.

6. Which reading of 1 Corinthians 11:10 did Tertullian and Heiser share?

Why: Tertullian and Heiser both connected the phrase to the Genesis 6 story.

7. What image lies behind Colossians 2:15?

Why: The verb thriambeuō pictures leading defeated enemies in a Roman triumph.

8. Which scholar read the powers as the inner spirit of institutions?

Why: Walter Wink, beginning with Naming the Powers (1984), read the powers this way.

Unit test

Fifteen questions across the unit

0 / 15

1. Which passage in 1 Enoch does Jude 14–15 quote?

Why: Jude 14–15 closely matches 1 Enoch 1:9, the prophecy of the Lord coming with his holy ones.

2. In Genesis 5, why is Enoch 'the seventh from Adam'?

Why: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared and Enoch make seven names.

3. Which verse describes angels who kept not their first estate?

Why: Jude 6 speaks of angels who left their proper home and are kept in chains.

4. In Greek stories, who was imprisoned in Tartarus?

Why: Hesiod's Theogony tells of the Titans held in Tartarus.

5. Which order of judgments does 2 Peter 2:4–8 follow?

Why: Second Peter keeps the Genesis order: the angels, Noah's flood, then Sodom.

6. In Heiser's reading of 1 Peter 3:19, what did Christ proclaim to the spirits in prison?

Why: Heiser read the proclamation as an announcement of victory to the imprisoned Watchers.

7. Which early bishop read 1 Peter 3:19 as Christ preaching through Noah?

Why: Augustine held that Christ's Spirit spoke through Noah to the people of his day.

8. Why did Jerome say many people rejected Jude?

Why: Jerome wrote in 393 CE that Jude's quotation of Enoch made many reject it.

9. Which churches keep 1 Enoch in their Bibles?

Why: Only the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo and Eritrean Orthodox churches include 1 Enoch in their canon.

10. What does the Dead Sea Scrolls text of Deuteronomy 32:8 read, instead of 'sons of Israel'?

Why: A Dead Sea Scrolls manuscript reads 'sons of God'; the Septuagint has 'angels of God'.

11. According to Heiser, who are the rulers and authorities in Paul's letters?

Why: Heiser tied Paul's powers to the sons of God placed over the nations.

12. In 1 Corinthians 10:20, Paul says the nations sacrifice to what?

Why: Paul echoes Deuteronomy 32:17: the sacrifices go to daimonia, not to God.

13. Which is one reading of the stoicheia in Galatians 4:3, 9?

Why: Readers take stoicheia as basic principles, physical elements or spiritual beings.

14. What image does Colossians 2:15 use for the defeat of the powers?

Why: The verb thriambeuō pictures leading defeated enemies in a triumph.

15. How did Walter Wink read Paul's powers?

Why: Wink, starting in 1984, read the powers as the inner spirit of institutions such as nations and companies.

1. (Unit 12) Which text says the Watchers swore an oath on Hermon?

Why: 1 Enoch 6 says the two hundred Watchers came down on Hermon and bound themselves with an oath there.

2. (Unit 12) How many followers does Luke 10:1 say Jesus sent out?

Why: The manuscripts of Luke 10:1 differ between seventy and seventy-two, matching the Table of Nations counts.

3. (Unit 12) Which book of the Hebrew Bible tells of the tower of Babel?

Why: The Babel story is in Genesis 11:1–9, right after the Table of Nations in Genesis 10.

Why: Catholic teaching reads the rock as Peter, with his role continued in the bishops of Rome.

5. (Unit 12) Why do Heiser and others place the Transfiguration on Hermon?

Why: The Gospels do not name the mountain, but the event follows the scene at Caesarea Philippi, below Hermon.
