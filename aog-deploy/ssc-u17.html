The Twentieth-Century World

1914–Present

In August 1914 a soldier marched off to war with a rifle, a horse-drawn wagon behind him and a letter home that would take a week to arrive. His great-granddaughter carries in her pocket a device that can reach any person on Earth in a second, and she lives in a world with about eight billion people, nearly two hundred countries, and enough nuclear weapons to end civilization in an afternoon. Between those two lives lie two world wars that killed tens of millions, the rise and fall of dictatorships that claimed the right to control every thought, a forty-year standoff between two superpowers, the end of the European empires, and the wiring together of the whole planet. This unit covers that century.

The three chapters follow a shape. The first, from 1914 to 1945, shows how modern states learned to organize whole societies for war and, in the worst cases, for murder, and how the world tried twice to build something to stop it happening again. The second, from 1945 to 1991, follows a divided world in which the superpowers never fought each other directly but fought through Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan and dozens of other places, while more than half of humanity, in India, Africa, Asia and the Middle East, won independence from empires. The third, from 1991 to today, is about the world those endings made: one connected economy, a rising China, wars that began on a single morning in 2001, and problems such as climate, disease, migration and inequality that no border can hold back.

You will read the evidence: a soldier's letter from a Christmas truce, a president's words after Pearl Harbor, the Nazis' own train schedules, a leader's statement from the dock at his trial, a curve of carbon dioxide measured on a Hawaiian volcano. You will meet people who held total power and people who stood in front of tanks, and you will see how the same event can carry two names for two peoples. By the end you should be able to explain how the twentieth century made the world you live in, to argue with evidence about who gained and who paid, and to bring a historian's habits to the flood of news that reaches your own screen every day.

The big question

From 1914 to today, what did people learn, and fail to learn, about how much power a government should hold over its own people and over the world?

The World Wars

1914–1945
Big questionHow did two world wars in thirty years change what governments could do to people, and what people expected from governments?
The story

The Night the Guns Went Quiet

On Christmas Eve 1914, along a muddy line in Belgium, enemies who had been shooting at each other for months climbed out of their holes and shook hands.

The war was supposed to be over by Christmas. That is what the young men on both sides had been told in August 1914 when they marched off with flowers in their rifle barrels. By December the fighting had frozen into two lines of ditches that ran from the Swiss border to the North Sea. The men lived in them. Rain filled the trenches with cold water. Rats ate the food. Between the two lines lay a strip of mud, wire and unburied bodies that soldiers called no man's land.

On the evening of December 24, in several places along the line near the Belgian town of Ypres, British soldiers heard something strange from the German trenches. Singing. Some German units had put up small Christmas trees with candles on the edge of their trenches. The British answered with carols of their own. A few brave men shouted across the gap. Then a few climbed out, hands raised, and walked into no man's land. No one shot them.

By Christmas morning, in some sectors, hundreds of soldiers were standing in the open. They traded cigarettes, chocolate, buttons and newspapers. They showed each other photographs of wives and children. They helped bury the dead who had lain between the lines for weeks and held joint burial services. Letters home from both sides describe kicking a ball around, though the famous football match may have been several small kickabouts rather than one game. We know this happened because so many soldiers wrote about it, and British newspapers printed their letters in January 1915.

The generals were furious. Orders came down forbidding any contact with the enemy. Within days the shooting started again, and it would not stop for almost four more years. There was no repeat of the truce at Christmas 1915. By then too many men had died, and the bitterness ran too deep. Historians still argue about what the truce means. Some see proof that ordinary people did not want the war their governments had made. Others point out that the same men went back to killing each other within a week.

The truce is a good place to start the story of the twentieth century, because it holds both halves of that story at once. The century brought machines and governments powerful enough to kill millions. It also brought people who kept insisting, in trenches and prison camps and at negotiating tables, that there had to be another way. This chapter follows both.

The Great War

38.1

Why Europe Went to War

Main ideaWorld War I began when a single assassination pulled a web of alliances, rivalries and war plans into a conflict that no single government fully chose.

On June 28, 1914, a nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb named Gavrilo Princip stepped up to an open car in Sarajevo and shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary. The Archduke and his wife died within minutes. Princip belonged to a group that wanted Bosnia freed from Austrian rule and joined to Serbia. Austria-Hungary blamed the Serbian government and, a month later, declared war on Serbia. Five weeks after a killing in a Balkan town, most of Europe was at war.

How did one murder do that? The answer is the system. Over the previous forty years the great powers had signed promises to defend one another. Germany backed Austria-Hungary. Russia backed Serbia and was tied to France. France was tied to Britain. When Austria moved against Serbia, Russia began to gather its army. Germany, fearing a war on two fronts, declared war on Russia and then on France. Its war plan called for knocking out France fast by marching through neutral Belgium. When German troops crossed the Belgian border, Britain declared war on Germany. That was August 4, 1914.

Alliances explain the speed, but not the willingness. For decades the powers had been competing. Germany and Britain raced to build bigger battleships. France wanted back the provinces it had lost to Germany in 1871. Russia and Austria-Hungary both wanted influence in the Balkans as the Ottoman Empire weakened. Each country had taken colonies in Africa and Asia and feared the others would take more. This mix of , which means treating military strength as the highest goal, and , an intense loyalty to one’s own nation, made leaders confident that a short war would settle things in their favor.

Historians still argue over who was most to blame. Some point to Germany’s decision to back Austria without limit. Some point to Russia’s early mobilization, or to Austria’s harsh demands on Serbia, or to the generals in every capital whose plans left no time for talking. What the evidence shows clearly is that nobody in July 1914 was planning a four-year war that would kill about nine million soldiers. They expected the war they remembered, quick and decisive. They got something no one had seen before.

38.2

Life and Death in the Trenches

Main ideaNew weapons made attacking almost suicidal, so the Western Front froze into trenches where millions died to move the line a few miles.

The machine gun changed everything. A single gun crew could fire hundreds of bullets a minute, and a line of them could stop a charging regiment in its tracks. Artillery, the big guns, could now hit targets miles away. Barbed wire slowed attackers while the guns found them. Against all this, the soldier of 1914 still had a rifle, a bayonet and his legs. Both sides quickly learned that the only way to survive was to dig. By the end of 1914 the Western Front was a double line of , deep ditches roofed with sandbags and timber, stretching more than four hundred miles across France and Belgium.

Life in the trenches was misery even when no one was attacking. Water pooled at the bottom, and men who stood in it for days got trench foot, a rotting of the skin that could cost a man his toes. Lice lived in every seam of every uniform. Rats grew fat on the dead. Shells could fall at any moment, and a hit could bury a whole dugout. Soldiers spent days in the front line, then rotated back to rest, then returned. Many wrote that the waiting was worse than the fighting.

When generals did order an attack, the results were terrible. The usual plan was to pound the enemy trench with artillery for days, then send infantry walking across no man&#x27;s land, the strip of ground between the lines. The shelling rarely cut all the wire or killed all the machine gunners. On July 1, 1916, the first day of the Battle of the Somme, the British army suffered about fifty-seven thousand casualties, nearly twenty thousand of them dead, and gained almost nothing. The battle continued until November. At Verdun that same year, French and German armies fought for ten months over a few square miles and lost hundreds of thousands of men between them.

New weapons kept arriving: poison gas in 1915, tanks in 1916, aircraft that grew from scouts into bombers. None broke the deadlock for long. What finally moved the front in 1918 was exhaustion on one side. Germany launched huge spring attacks, gained ground, and then ran out of men and food. Fresh American troops, more than a million of them by the summer, tipped the balance. On November 11, 1918, at eleven in the morning, the guns stopped. Soldiers on both sides could hardly believe the silence.

38.3

The War at Home

Main ideaTotal war pulled whole societies into the fight: factories, farms, women's labor, food rationing and government control of what people could say.

World War I was the first , a war in which a country’s entire economy and population are organized for fighting. Armies needed millions of shells a month, and only whole nations working together could make them. Governments took control of railroads, mines and factories. They told farmers what to grow and fixed the prices of bread and coal. In Germany, where a British naval blockade cut off imported food, the winter of 1916 to 1917 became known as the turnip winter because turnips were about all there was to eat. Hundreds of thousands of German civilians died of hunger and related disease during the war.

With so many men at the front, women filled the jobs they left. In Britain, France, Germany and later the United States, women made shells in munitions factories, drove streetcars, ran farms and worked as nurses close to the fighting. Munitions work was dangerous: the chemicals turned skin yellow, and explosions killed workers. Governments that had refused to let women vote now depended on their labor. In Britain, women over thirty won the vote in 1918. The United States passed the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920. The war did not cause these changes alone, but it made the old arguments against them hard to sustain.

Governments also went to war for people’s minds. Every side produced , posters and films and news stories designed to make people hate the enemy and support the war. Britain and France spread stories of German atrocities in Belgium, some true and some invented. Germany did the same in reverse. Governments censored letters from the front so families would not learn how bad things were. In the United States, which entered the war in April 1917, a new law made it a crime to speak against the draft, and about two thousand people were prosecuted for what they said or wrote.

Total war reached across oceans. Britain and France brought hundreds of thousands of soldiers and laborers from India, Africa, Vietnam and the Caribbean. The United States sent more than two million men to France, and about three hundred and seventy thousand Black Americans served, mostly in labor units, though some fought with distinction beside French troops. In Illinois, factories in Chicago, Rockford and East St. Louis ran around the clock. The war set patterns that would return, larger, in the next one.

38.4

Russia's Two Revolutions

Main ideaWar and hunger toppled the Russian tsar in March 1917, and eight months later Lenin's Bolsheviks seized power and pulled Russia out of the war.

By early 1917 Russia was breaking under the war. Its army had lost millions of men. Its railroads could not carry both soldiers and food. In Petrograd, the capital, women stood in line for bread in the freezing cold and found the shelves empty. On March 8, 1917, thousands of women marched to demand bread. Workers joined them. The , Nicholas II, ordered soldiers to fire on the crowds, but the soldiers refused and joined the marchers instead. Within a week Nicholas gave up his throne. Three centuries of rule by his family were over.

A temporary government of moderate politicians took over and promised elections. It made one fatal choice: it kept Russia in the war. Soldiers kept dying and deserting. Peasants seized land from nobles without waiting for permission. In cities, councils of workers and soldiers called began acting like governments of their own. Into this chaos came Vladimir Lenin, a revolutionary who had spent years in exile. Germany, hoping he would take Russia out of the war, sent him home by train in April. His slogan was simple: peace, land and bread.

Lenin led the , a disciplined party that believed in : the idea that workers should own the factories and land together, with no private owners and, eventually, no classes at all. On the night of November 6 to 7, 1917, armed Bolshevik supporters seized the bridges, telegraph offices and the Winter Palace in Petrograd. There was little fighting. The temporary government simply collapsed. Lenin announced a new government, took Russia out of the war in March 1918 by giving Germany huge stretches of territory, and moved the capital to Moscow.

Then came a civil war, from 1918 to 1921, between the Bolshevik Reds and a loose alliance of Whites who ranged from monarchists to democrats. The Reds won, partly because the Whites could not agree on what they wanted. Millions died in the fighting and in the famine that followed. The Bolsheviks shut down other parties, created a secret police and shot the tsar and his family. In 1922 they named the country the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. For the next seventy years, the existence of a communist great power would shape politics on every continent.

A Broken Peace

38.5

The Treaty That Settled Nothing

Main ideaThe Treaty of Versailles punished Germany, redrew the map of Europe and created a League of Nations, but left grievances that helped bring the next war.

In January 1919 the victors met at Paris to write the peace. President Woodrow Wilson of the United States arrived as a hero. A year earlier he had laid out Fourteen Points for a fair peace: open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, the right of peoples to choose their own governments, and a League of Nations where countries would settle disputes by talking. The French leader, Georges Clemenceau, had other ideas. Germany had invaded France twice in fifty years. He wanted Germany weakened so it could never do so again. Britain’s David Lloyd George stood between them.

The treaty signed at Versailles on June 28, 1919, five years to the day after Sarajevo, was mostly Clemenceau’s. Germany lost about a tenth of its land and all its overseas colonies. Its army was limited to one hundred thousand men, with no air force, no submarines and no troops in the Rhineland along the French border. Article 231 made Germany accept responsibility for the war. On that basis Germany had to pay , payments for the damage, set in 1921 at a sum so large that economists argued it could never be paid. Germans of every political view called it a dictated peace.

Other treaties broke up the losing empires. Austria-Hungary vanished, replaced by Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia and parts of Poland and Yugoslavia. The Ottoman Empire lost its Arab lands, which Britain and France took under a system called mandates. Wilson’s idea of , that each people should rule itself, guided some of the new borders. But every new country contained minorities who had wanted to be somewhere else, and the principle was not applied to Asians or Africans at all. Delegates from Vietnam, Egypt and India who came to Paris asking for it were turned away.

The was created, but without the country whose president had invented it. The United States Senate refused to approve the treaty, mainly over fears that the League could drag America into future wars. Germany and Russia were kept out at first. The League had no army. When strong countries broke the rules in the 1930s, it could only protest. The peace of 1919 has been blamed for too much and too little. It was harsh enough to enrage Germany and too weak to hold Germany down. That combination proved dangerous.

38.6

A Depression Around the World

Main ideaThe crash of 1929 spread through debts, trade and banks until a quarter of workers in some countries had no job, and desperate people looked for anyone with answers.

In October 1929 prices on the New York stock market collapsed. People who had borrowed money to buy shares could not pay it back. Banks that had lent to them failed, taking savings with them. Businesses that could not borrow cut production and fired workers. Workers with no wages stopped buying. By 1933 about one in four American workers had no job. In Chicago, families camped in shacks along the river, and the city could not pay its teachers for months. The was the worst economic collapse the industrial world had seen.

It did not stay in America. During the 1920s American banks had lent heavily to Germany, and Germany had used the money to pay reparations to Britain and France, who used it to pay war debts to the United States. When American lending stopped, the whole circle broke. German banks failed in 1931. By 1932 about six million Germans, roughly a third of the workforce, were unemployed. Countries tried to protect their own workers by raising , taxes on imported goods. The United States raised them sharply in 1930. Other countries answered in kind, and world trade fell by more than half between 1929 and 1933.

The suffering was everywhere but unequal. In Britain the old coal and shipbuilding towns emptied out. In Japan, farmers who depended on selling silk to America watched the price collapse. In Latin America, countries that sold coffee, copper or beef to the world lost their buyers overnight. Peasants in colonies from India to Indonesia saw the prices of their crops fall while their taxes did not. Governments that had promised prosperity now seemed helpless. People asked whether , an economy run by private owners and free markets, could work at all.

Different countries gave different answers. In the United States, Franklin Roosevelt’s New Deal put the government to work hiring the jobless, insuring bank deposits and paying pensions, all within a democracy. Sweden built a similar welfare system. The Soviet Union, cut off from world markets, reported no unemployment at all and drew admirers who did not see the costs. In Germany, Italy and Japan, the Depression helped bring to power men who promised order, jobs and greatness through force. The next lesson looks at them.

38.7

Dictators of the 1930s

Main ideaMussolini, Hitler and Stalin built states that claimed total control over their people, using terror, propaganda and the promise of national greatness.

The first of the new dictators was Benito Mussolini in Italy. Italy had been on the winning side in 1918 but felt cheated by the peace, and strikes and street fighting shook the country. Mussolini, a former socialist turned nationalist, organized squads of black-shirted veterans who beat up his opponents. In 1922 he marched on Rome, and the king made him prime minister. Within a few years he had banned other parties and jailed critics. He called his movement : the nation and its leader above everything, violence as a virtue, democracy as weakness. The word comes from the fasces, a bundle of rods that symbolized authority in ancient Rome.

Adolf Hitler admired Mussolini and copied him. His Nazi party blamed Germany’s defeat and misery on Jews, communists and the politicians who had signed the Versailles treaty. Through the prosperous late 1920s the Nazis were a fringe party. The Depression changed that. In the election of 1932 they became the largest party. On January 30, 1933, President Hindenburg made Hitler chancellor, believing he could be controlled. Within months Hitler had used a fire at the parliament building as an excuse to suspend civil rights, passed a law letting him rule by decree, banned other parties and opened the first concentration camp at Dachau. By 1934 he was the Führer, the leader, with no limits on his power.

Nazism was fascism with race at its center. Hitler taught that Germans were a master race and that Jews were a poison to be removed. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped German Jews of citizenship and banned marriage between Jews and other Germans. Jewish doctors, lawyers and teachers lost their jobs. On the night of November 9, 1938, Nazi mobs burned synagogues, smashed Jewish shops and sent about thirty thousand Jewish men to camps. Newspapers, radio, schools and youth groups all taught the same message. A state that tries to control every part of life this way is called .

Joseph Stalin ruled the Soviet Union in the name of the opposite idea, communism, but his methods were much the same. From 1928 he forced peasants to give up their land to state-run collective farms. Those who resisted were shot or shipped to labor camps. The disruption caused a famine in 1932 and 1933, worst in Ukraine, that killed millions. Between 1936 and 1938 Stalin’s secret police arrested hundreds of thousands of party members, officers, and ordinary people on invented charges; many were shot after staged trials. Fascist and communist states hated each other, but each showed what a modern government could do to its own people when nothing was allowed to stand in its way.

The Second World War

38.8

The Road to War

Main ideaThrough the 1930s Japan, Italy and Germany seized territory step by step while the democracies, hoping to avoid another war, gave way until they could not.

The first move came in Asia. In 1931 Japan’s army seized Manchuria, a resource-rich region of northern China, and set up a puppet state. The League of Nations condemned the seizure. Japan simply walked out of the League. In 1937 Japan launched a full invasion of China. When its army took the capital, Nanjing, in December, soldiers murdered tens of thousands of prisoners and civilians and raped thousands of women over several weeks. Reports reached the outside world through diplomats, missionaries and journalists. The world protested and did nothing.

Mussolini watched and learned. In 1935 Italy invaded Ethiopia, one of only two independent countries left in Africa. Ethiopia’s emperor, Haile Selassie, went to the League in person to ask for help. The League voted for mild trade penalties that did not include oil, and Italy completed its conquest. Then Hitler began. In 1936 he marched troops into the Rhineland, which the Versailles treaty had forbidden. France and Britain protested and did nothing. In March 1938 he took over Austria. Crowds in Vienna cheered.

The crisis came over Czechoslovakia, where about three million Germans lived in a border region called the Sudetenland. Hitler demanded it. In September 1938, at Munich, the British prime minister Neville Chamberlain and the French premier agreed to give it to him in exchange for a promise that Germany wanted nothing more. A War on Every Ocean

Main ideaWorld War II was fought across Europe, Asia, Africa and the Pacific; the turning points came at Stalingrad and Midway, and it ended with the total defeat of Germany and Japan.

Germany’s new way of war was called , lightning war: tanks, trucks and dive-bombers punching through a front and racing ahead before the enemy could react. Poland fell in five weeks. In the spring of 1940 the same method took Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium and, in six weeks, France. Britain stood alone under a new prime minister, Winston Churchill. Through the summer and fall of 1940, German bombers tried to destroy the British air force and then British cities. They failed. Britain survived, and its survival kept a base from which the war could later be carried back to Europe.

In June 1941 Hitler broke his pact and invaded the Soviet Union with more than three million men, the largest invasion in history. German armies reached the edge of Moscow by December. Then winter and Soviet resistance stopped them. The decisive battle came at Stalingrad on the Volga River, where from the fall of 1942 into February 1943 the two armies fought street by street until the Germans were surrounded and about ninety thousand survivors surrendered. The Holocaust

Main ideaNazi Germany and its helpers murdered about six million Jews and millions of others in a planned, industrial killing that we know about through the killers' own records and the survivors' testimony.

The Nazis had persecuted Jews since 1933, but the war turned persecution into murder. When Germany conquered Poland in 1939, more than three million Polish Jews came under its control. They were forced into walled in cities like Warsaw, where hunger and disease killed thousands each month. When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, special killing squads followed the army, rounding up Jewish men, women and children, marching them to pits and shooting them. At Babi Yar, outside Kyiv, they killed more than thirty thousand people in two days in September 1941. By the end of the war these squads had shot well over a million people.

In January 1942 senior Nazi officials met at a villa in Wannsee, near Berlin, to organize what they called the final solution of the Jewish question: the murder of every Jew in Europe. The method was the death camp. At Auschwitz, Treblinka, Sobibor and other camps in occupied Poland, trains arrived from across Europe. Guards sorted the arrivals. Most were sent straight to gas chambers disguised as showers and killed with poison gas within hours. Their bodies were burned. A minority were worked to death. About one million Jews were killed at Auschwitz alone.

The Nazis also murdered Roma people, disabled people, Polish leaders, Soviet prisoners of war, gay men, political prisoners and Jehovah’s Witnesses. The total of Jews murdered in the is about six million, roughly two out of every three Jews in Europe. The killing required thousands of ordinary participants: railway clerks, police, factory managers, neighbors who pointed out hiding places. It also had resisters. Jews rose in the Warsaw ghetto in 1943 and fought for nearly a month. Denmark’s people ferried almost all of the country’s Jews to safety in Sweden. Individuals across Europe hid families at the risk of their own lives.

How do we know? The Nazis kept records: train schedules, camp registers, reports from the killing squads listing the numbers shot. Allied armies photographed and filmed the camps they liberated in 1945. Survivors testified at the Nuremberg trials, where the top Nazi leaders were tried, and have kept testifying since. The word , the deliberate destruction of a people, was coined in 1944 to name what had happened, and in 1948 the new United Nations made it a crime. The Illinois Holocaust Museum in Skokie, founded by survivors who settled near Chicago, is one of many places where that evidence is kept.

38.11

The Bomb

Main ideaScientists working in secret, including at the University of Chicago, built a weapon that destroyed two cities in August 1945 and changed what war could mean.

In 1939 physicists learned that splitting the nucleus of a uranium atom released enormous energy, and that a chain of such splits might make a bomb. Albert Einstein signed a letter warning President Roosevelt that Germany might build one first. The United States began a secret program, the , that eventually employed more than a hundred thousand people. Its first great test happened in Illinois. On December 2, 1942, under the stands of an old football field at the University of Chicago, the physicist Enrico Fermi and his team started the world’s first controlled nuclear . It lasted a few minutes. It proved the bomb was possible.

The first bomb was tested in the New Mexico desert on July 16, 1945. By then Germany had surrendered, and the question was Japan. Japan’s cities were already burning under American firebombing; one raid on Tokyo in March had killed around one hundred thousand people in a night. American planners expected an invasion of Japan to cost huge numbers of American and Japanese lives. President Harry Truman, who had taken office when Roosevelt died in April, decided to use the new weapon. On August 6 a single bomb destroyed the city of Hiroshima. On August 9 a second destroyed Nagasaki. Between them the bombs killed more than one hundred thousand people by the end of 1945, most of them civilians, and many more later from radiation.

On August 8 the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and poured into Manchuria. On August 15 Japan’s emperor announced surrender in a radio broadcast. Most Japanese had never heard his voice. The war was over. Historians still argue about the decision. Some hold that the bombs ended the war quickly and saved lives on both sides. Others argue that Japan was already close to surrender, that the Soviet entry mattered as much as the bombs, or that a demonstration should have been tried first. The evidence supports parts of several views, which is why the debate continues.

What no one disputes is what the bomb meant for the future. A single aircraft could now erase a city. The Soviet Union tested its own bomb in 1949, and within a decade both sides had weapons hundreds of times more powerful than the one dropped on Hiroshima. Scientists who had built the bomb became some of the loudest voices for controlling it. The Chicago team’s research grew into Argonne National Laboratory in the western suburbs, and the clock that Chicago scientists created to warn of nuclear danger, the Doomsday Clock, is still updated every year.

A New Order

Main ideaThe victors of 1945 built the United Nations to prevent another world war, but power now rested with two superpowers that were already drifting apart.

The founders accepted this because the great powers would not join otherwise. The United States Senate approved the charter by a huge majority. This time America was in.

The United Nations did more than debate. It set up agencies to feed refugees, fight disease and help children, and in 1948 it adopted a Universal Declaration of Human Rights listing the rights every person should have. By 1947 the alliance was over. Stalin installed obedient governments across Eastern Europe. The United States pledged to help any country resisting communist takeover. Germany, and Berlin inside it, was split into zones. The next chapter follows the long, strange conflict that resulted, called the Cold War because the two sides never fought each other directly. But it began here, in the ruins of a war that had killed tens of millions and ended with a weapon that could kill everyone. The question of the second half of the century was whether the world had learned enough to avoid a third.

The Note That Opened a Wall

On the evening of November 9, 1989, an East German official read a piece of paper he had not studied, and by midnight the most famous wall in the world was open.

For twenty-eight years the Berlin Wall had cut a city in half. It went up in August 1961 to stop East Germans from escaping to the West, and it worked: a concrete barrier, a strip of raked sand, guard towers, dogs, and orders to shoot. At least one hundred and forty people died trying to cross it. Families waved to each other across it on holidays. Children grew up never having seen the other side of their own street.

By the fall of 1989, East Germany was in trouble. Hungary had opened its border with Austria, and tens of thousands of East Germans were driving to Hungary and simply walking out. Every Monday, crowds in the city of Leipzig marched through the streets chanting that they were the people. The government, terrified of a bloodbath and without the backing of the Soviet leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, decided to ease travel rules to calm things down. A new regulation was drafted allowing citizens to apply for permits to travel abroad. It was supposed to take effect the next day, with paperwork.

That evening a party spokesman named Günter Schabowski held a televised press conference. Near the end, a reporter asked about travel. Schabowski, who had been handed the note just before the broadcast and had not read it carefully, fumbled through it and announced that East Germans could now cross the border. A reporter asked when. Schabowski looked at the paper, shrugged, and said: as far as I know, immediately, without delay. Television carried it live. Within an hour, West German news was reporting that the wall was open.

Thousands of East Berliners walked to the crossing points and demanded to be let through. The border guards had no orders. They phoned their superiors, who had no orders either. The crowds grew. Around eleven-thirty at night, the commander at the Bornholmer Street crossing, facing a crowd of thousands and fearing a stampede, opened the gate. Other crossings followed. East and West Berliners met on top of the wall, hugging strangers and hammering at the concrete with whatever they had.

No one had planned this. Not the East German government, not Gorbachev, not the Western leaders who watched on television with their mouths open. A system built on fear had run out of people willing to shoot. Within a year Germany was reunited. Within two years the Soviet Union itself was gone. This chapter tells the story of how the world came to be divided by that wall, what happened in the rest of the world while it stood, and why it fell.

The Iron Curtain Falls

Main ideaBetween 1945 and 1949 the wartime alliance broke into two hostile blocs, one led by the United States and one by the Soviet Union, divided by what Churchill called an iron curtain.

In February 1945, with Germany nearly beaten, Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin met at Yalta on the Black Sea. They agreed to divide Germany into occupation zones and promised free elections in the countries the Red Army had liberated. Stalin had a different idea of free. Within two years, communist parties backed by Soviet troops and secret police had taken over Poland, Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria. Opposition leaders were jailed or killed. In March 1946 Winston Churchill, speaking in Fulton, Missouri, said that an had come down across Europe from the Baltic Sea to the Adriatic. The phrase stuck.

Each side saw the other as the aggressor. To Americans, Stalin was swallowing Eastern Europe and would take the rest if he could. To Stalin, whose country had been invaded from the west twice in thirty years and had lost twenty-seven million people, a ring of friendly governments was simple self-defense, and the American bomb was the real threat. In 1947, when Britain could no longer afford to support the Greek government against communist rebels, President Truman asked Congress for money and announced that the United States would help any free people resisting takeover. Main ideaBoth superpowers built enough nuclear weapons to destroy each other many times over, and the fear of that outcome kept them talking even as they competed.

China's Revolution and the Korean War

Main ideaCommunist victory in China in 1949 and the invasion of South Korea in 1950 turned the Cold War into shooting wars in Asia.

China had been at war with itself, on and off, since the 1920s. On one side stood the Nationalists under Chiang Kai-shek, backed by the United States. On the other stood the communists under Mao Zedong, who built their strength among peasants in the countryside. The Japanese invasion of 1937 nearly destroyed the Nationalist armies, while the communists grew by fighting behind Japanese lines. When the civil war resumed after 1945, Mao’s forces won. On October 1, 1949, he proclaimed the People’s Republic of China in Beijing. Chiang fled to the island of Taiwan, which the United States protected. Two Chinas claimed to be the real one.

Mao’s new state took land from landlords and gave it to peasants, then took it back into collective farms. In 1958 he launched the Great Leap Forward, ordering villages to make steel in backyard furnaces and report impossible harvests. Main ideaIn October 1962 the discovery of Soviet missiles in Cuba brought the superpowers closer to nuclear war than at any other moment, and both leaders chose to step back.

Gandhi and the Partition of India

Main ideaGandhi's movement of nonviolent resistance helped end British rule in India in 1947, but independence came with a partition that killed hundreds of thousands.

In 1930 a thin man of sixty in a homespun cloth walked from his home in western India toward the sea, about two hundred and forty miles, with a growing crowd behind him. When Mohandas Gandhi reached the beach at Dandi, he picked up a lump of salt. Under British law only the government could make salt, and every Indian paid a tax on it. Gandhi’s act was a crime, and he meant it to be. Across India tens of thousands followed his example, and the British jailed about sixty thousand people. The Salt March showed the world a new kind of weapon, and it showed Indians that the empire could be defied.

Gandhi called his method , which he translated as truth force. It meant refusing to obey unjust laws, accepting arrest and beating without striking back, and treating even the oppressor as a person who could change. It required more discipline than fighting. Gandhi trained in South Africa, where he had spent twenty years defending Indian workers against discrimination. He also believed that India must be one nation of Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs and everyone else. Many Indians disagreed with his tactics, and the Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, came to demand a separate Muslim state.

World War II broke Britain’s grip. Britain had used India’s soldiers and money without asking, and Indian leaders were jailed for demanding independence in return. After the war Britain was bankrupt, and a new government decided to leave. The question was whether to leave one country or two. As violence between Hindus and Muslims spread in 1946 and 1947, Britain agreed to : India for the Hindu majority, Pakistan, in two pieces a thousand miles apart, for Muslims. Independence came at midnight on August 14 to 15, 1947. Gandhi refused to celebrate. He spent the day fasting for peace.

Partition set off one of the largest migrations in history. Something like fifteen million people crossed the new borders in both directions, Muslims to Pakistan and Hindus and Sikhs to India. Trains arrived full of the dead. Estimates of the killed run from several hundred thousand to over a million. In January 1948 a Hindu extremist who thought Gandhi too generous to Muslims shot him dead in Delhi. India and Pakistan have fought several wars since. Yet India became, and has remained, the world’s largest democracy, and Gandhi’s method traveled: Martin Luther King Jr. studied it, and civil rights marchers in Chicago and Selma used it.

Main ideaBetween 1957 and the mid-1970s almost every colony in Africa became independent, some by negotiation and some by war, but colonial borders and economies remained.

The colonial powers had been weakened by World War II, and African soldiers who had fought in it came home asking why they should be ruled by strangers. Apartheid and Its End

Main ideaSouth Africa built a legal system of racial separation in 1948 and dismantled it in 1994 after decades of resistance, world pressure, and a negotiated transfer of power.

Every person was classified by race. Black South Africans, about three-quarters of the population, were assigned to scattered homelands that made up about thirteen percent of the country’s land. They needed passes to enter white cities, where they worked in mines and kitchens but could not live. Marriage across race lines was a crime. Beaches, buses, schools and hospitals were separate and grossly unequal. It was a system for keeping cheap labor and total power in white hands.

Resistance was led by the African National Congress, or ANC, which for years followed Gandhi’s methods of nonviolent protest. In March 1960 police at Sharpeville opened fire on a crowd protesting the pass laws and killed sixty-nine people, most shot in the back. The government banned the ANC. One of its young leaders, a lawyer named Nelson Mandela, concluded that nonviolence alone would not work and helped form an armed wing that sabotaged power lines and government buildings. He was captured, and in 1964 he was sentenced to life in prison. He spent twenty-seven years there, much of it breaking rocks on Robben Island.

Inside South Africa, protest never stopped. In 1976 schoolchildren in the township of Soweto marched against being taught in Afrikaans, and police killed hundreds in the months that followed. Outside, the world slowly turned against apartheid. Athletes were banned from international sports. Students in the United States, including at Illinois campuses, demanded that their universities sell shares in companies doing business in South Africa. In 1986 Congress passed economic sanctions over President Reagan’s veto. The Cold War had given South Africa’s government cover, since it called every opponent a communist. As the Cold War ended, the cover disappeared.

Mandela insisted that the goal was not revenge but a country that belonged to everyone. In April 1994 South Africans of every race lined up, some for hours, in the first election in which all adults could vote. Mandela became president. A commission then heard testimony from victims and perpetrators of apartheid crimes, offering amnesty for full confession. Apartheid’s legal structure was gone. Its economic legacy, and the argument over how to repair it, remains.

Globalization and Today

1991–Present
Big questionIn a world where money, goods, people, diseases and ideas cross borders in hours, who decides anything, and how can a citizen tell what is true?
The story

Three Journeys Around One World

A ship the length of four football fields, a phone in your pocket and the shirt on your back each tell the same story: nothing you own comes from one place anymore.

Start with the ship. It leaves the port of Shanghai carrying about twenty thousand steel boxes, each the size of a truck trailer. Nobody on board knows what is in most of them. That is the point. Before the 1960s, dockworkers loaded ships by hand, sack by sack and crate by crate, and a ship could sit in port for a week. In 1956 an American trucking owner named Malcom McLean put fifty-eight truck bodies on a tanker and sailed them from New Jersey to Texas. The standard *container* was born. A crane can now move a box from ship to train to truck in minutes, and the cost of shipping a T-shirt across the Pacific fell to a few cents. That box, more than any treaty, built the world economy you live in.

Now the phone. Its screen glass may come from Kentucky. The processor was designed in California and made in Taiwan. The memory chips came from South Korea, the camera lens from Japan, the battery from China. The cobalt in that battery was probably dug in Congo, some of it by people, including children, working in dangerous pits. The whole thing was assembled in a Chinese factory city where hundreds of thousands of workers live in dormitories, and shipped in a container. It runs software written by people in a dozen countries. No single person on Earth knows how to make the phone from scratch.

Finally the shirt. The cotton may have grown in Texas or in India. It was spun into thread and woven into cloth, perhaps in China, and cut and sewn in Bangladesh, where a garment worker might earn a few dollars a day. In April 2013 a factory building called Rana Plaza collapsed near Dhaka, killing more than eleven hundred workers who had been ordered back inside after cracks appeared in the walls. Many of them were sewing clothes for Western brands. The shirt came to Illinois through a warehouse near Joliet, one of the largest inland freight hubs in North America, and reached you by truck.

Each journey shows the same pattern. Work is split into pieces and sent wherever it can be done cheapest. Goods, money and information move almost freely; people, mostly, do not. Prices fall, and more people than ever before can afford phones and shirts. At the same time, a factory closes in Rockford or Decatur, a worker in Bangladesh dies under a concrete slab, and a child in Congo digs cobalt. The word for all of this is *globalization*, and this chapter is about the world it has made: richer and more connected than any before, and full of problems that no one country can solve alone.

Wired Together

Main ideaIn one generation the internet went from a scientists' tool to the nervous system of the world, changing how people work, learn, shop and argue.

Main ideaFalling shipping costs and new trade agreements tied the world's economies together, creating enormous wealth and sharp losers at the same time.

In 1994 the United States, Canada and Mexico joined in the North American Free Trade Agreement, removing most tariffs among them. The gains were real and huge. Main ideaAfter 1978 China combined one-party rule with market reforms and became the world's factory and its second-largest economy, changing the balance of power.

The results were the fastest sustained growth any large country has recorded. For three decades China’s economy grew close to ten percent a year, doubling roughly every seven years. It joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, and its exports flooded the world. By 2010 it had passed Japan to become the second-largest economy on Earth. About eight hundred million Chinese people escaped extreme poverty, by the World Bank’s count, the largest such change in history. Cities of skyscrapers rose where rice fields had been. China built more high-speed rail in fifteen years than the rest of the world combined.

It censors the internet, jails critics, and has held perhaps a million Uyghur Muslims in camps in the western region of Xinjiang, which the government calls vocational training and human rights groups and several governments call mass detention. The party’s bargain with its people was prosperity in exchange for obedience. For many years, with incomes rising, most accepted it. China’s rise changed the world’s map of power. It is the largest trading partner of most countries on Earth, including many in Africa and Latin America, where it builds ports, railroads and stadiums in exchange for resources and influence. It has built a large navy and claims most of the South China Sea. The United States, which welcomed China into the world economy hoping trade would make it more open, now treats it as its chief rival. Whether the two will compete, cooperate or collide is a question American and Chinese students will inherit. Illinois soybeans, Boeing planes and Caterpillar machines, all sold to China, are one measure of what is at stake.

Main ideaMore people live outside the country of their birth than ever before, and the world's response to migrants and refugees divides nations and neighbors.

Rights Beyond Borders

Main ideaSince 1945 the world has written rules that say governments may not do anything they like to their own people, and has struggled to enforce them.

For most of history, what a ruler did inside his own borders was nobody else’s business. The Holocaust broke that idea. At Nuremberg in 1945 and 1946, the Allies put Nazi leaders on trial for crimes against humanity and established that following orders was no defense. In December 1948 the United Nations adopted the , drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt. It listed rights every person holds simply by being human: life, liberty, fair trials, free speech, free worship, education, and freedom from torture and slavery. It was a statement of principle, not a law, but it gave every movement for rights since a text to point to.

Treaties followed. The 1948 Genocide Convention made the deliberate destruction of a people a crime under international law. The Geneva Conventions of 1949 set rules for war: prisoners must be fed and protected, the wounded cared for, civilians spared. Since then, treaties have banned torture, protected children, and asserted the rights of women. By signing, governments accepted that others could judge them. Whether they lived up to their signatures was another matter. The Cold War superpowers each pointed at the other’s abuses and shielded their own allies.

The 1990s tested the system and it mostly failed. In Rwanda in 1994, extremists among the Hutu majority murdered about eight hundred thousand Tutsi and moderate Hutu in about one hundred days, with machetes and radio broadcasts, while UN peacekeepers on the ground were ordered not to intervene and the world argued over the word genocide. In Bosnia in 1995, Serb forces killed about eight thousand Muslim men and boys at Srebrenica, a town the UN had declared a safe area. Afterward, courts were created to try the killers, and in 2002 a permanent opened at The Hague to prosecute genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity when national courts will not.

The court has convicted a handful of warlords and indicted heads of state. But it has no police force, and the United States, China, Russia and India have not joined it, fearing that their own soldiers or leaders could be charged. Human rights law thus lives in tension: it claims that some acts are crimes everywhere, but it depends on governments to enforce it against themselves. Still, the words matter. A prisoner in a cell, a woman denied school, a worker in a locked factory can all say that the world has agreed their treatment is wrong. In 1900 they could not.

A Warming Planet

Main ideaBurning coal, oil and gas has raised the amount of carbon dioxide in the air and warmed the Earth, and no country can fix it alone.

In 1958 a scientist named Charles Keeling began measuring carbon dioxide in the air on top of a volcano in Hawaii, far from any city. The line he drew has climbed every year since. In 1960 the air held about three hundred and seventeen parts of carbon dioxide per million. By 2020 it held about four hundred and fourteen, higher than at any time in at least eight hundred thousand years, as air bubbles trapped in ancient ice show. The cause is not in doubt: burning coal, oil and gas releases carbon that had been buried for millions of years. traps heat, as scientists have known since the 1800s. More of it means a warmer planet.

The Earth has warmed about 1.2 degrees Celsius, roughly 2 degrees Fahrenheit, since the late 1800s. That sounds small. It is enough to melt glaciers, raise sea levels, and make heat waves, droughts, wildfires and heavy rains more common. Illinois has seen it: the state’s winters have warmed faster than its summers, and the very heavy rains that flood Chicago basements come more often than they did fifty years ago. Farmers in the state plant earlier and face wetter springs. Effects fall hardest on the poor, in Illinois and in the world, because they have the fewest ways to move, rebuild or cool off.

The problem is global in a way that makes it fiendishly hard. Carbon dioxide from a power plant in Ohio or a factory in China mixes into the same air. A country that cuts its own emissions pays the cost while everyone shares the benefit, so each has a reason to wait for the others. Countries that industrialized early, led by the United States, put most of the carbon now in the air there; countries industrializing now, led by China and India, emit the most today and argue that they deserve the same chance to grow. In 2015, in Paris, nearly every country agreed to set its own targets and try to keep warming well below two degrees. The targets so far are not enough to meet that goal.

What to do about it is a political argument, and the evidence bears on it without settling it. Solar and wind power have become the cheapest new electricity in much of the world, and electric cars are spreading, which means cutting emissions costs less than it once did. But coal still powers much of Asia, and oil fuels nearly every ship and plane. People disagree about how fast to change, who should pay, and how much to trust governments to manage it. Those are questions about values as well as facts. The facts, though, are the starting point, and the Keeling curve is still climbing.

40.9

Disease in a Connected World

Main ideaDiseases travel as fast as people do, and pandemics from 1918 to COVID-19 show both what global cooperation can do and how easily it fails.

In the fall of 1918, as World War I ended, a new strain of influenza spread along the routes soldiers traveled. It killed about fifty million people worldwide, more than the war itself, most of them young adults. In Chicago, theaters and dance halls were closed and funerals were limited to ten people. There were no vaccines, no antibiotics for the pneumonia that followed, and no way to see the virus, which was too small for the microscopes of the day. Cities that closed schools and gatherings early and kept them closed had fewer deaths, a lesson public health officials remembered a century later.

The twentieth century then won a series of victories. Vaccines defeated polio, which had paralyzed thousands of American children each summer. A worldwide campaign led by the World Health Organization tracked down every case of smallpox, a disease that had killed hundreds of millions, and in 1980 declared it gone from the Earth, the only human disease ever wiped out. Then in 1981 doctors in Los Angeles and New York reported a new illness that destroyed the body’s defenses. HIV, the virus that causes AIDS, has since killed about forty million people, most in Africa. Drugs that keep it in check exist, and the fight to get them to everyone who needs them continues.

In late 2019 a new virus appeared in the Chinese city of Wuhan. By March 2020 it was on every continent, and the World Health Organization declared a . Air travel had carried it around the world in weeks. Governments closed borders, schools and businesses. Streets in Chicago emptied. Scientists sequenced the virus’s genes within days of its discovery and shared them online, and vaccines were developed and tested in under a year, a speed never before achieved. The reported death toll passed seven million; most experts believe the true number is far higher because many deaths were never counted.

The pandemic revealed the connected world at its best and worst. Data flowed freely among scientists, and manufacturers on three continents made billions of vaccine doses. But rich countries bought most of them first, and poor countries waited. Supply chains for masks and medicines snapped. False claims about the virus and the vaccines spread online faster than corrections. Trust in governments and experts fell in many countries. The next lesson turns to the question this raised for everyone: in a world of instant information, how do you decide what to believe?

40.10

The Widening Gap

Main ideaThe world has grown richer and less poor at the same time that wealth within many countries has concentrated at the top, and both trends shape politics.

Two things are true at once. Between countries, the gap has narrowed: China, India and much of Asia have grown far faster than Europe and the United States, so the average person in a poor country is closer to the average person in a rich one than in 1990. Within countries, in many places, the gap has widened. In the United States the share of income going to the top one percent of earners roughly doubled between 1980 and the 2010s, according to standard studies, while the wages of workers without a college degree barely rose after adjusting for prices. A similar story, in different degrees, played out in Britain, China, India and Russia.

Why? Economists point to several causes working together. Technology rewarded people with skills that computers made more valuable and replaced people whose work computers could do. Trade moved factory jobs to lower-wage countries. Unions, which had once pushed wages up for millions, shrank. Tax rates on high incomes fell in many countries after 1980. The rewards for owning things, such as stocks, companies and buildings, grew faster than the rewards for working. Which of these matters most is argued, and the answer differs by country. What is not argued is the pattern.

The gap is visible in Illinois. Chicago’s Loop and North Side have some of the highest incomes in the nation, while neighborhoods a few miles south and west have lost factories, stores and population for fifty years. Life expectancy in the wealthiest Chicago neighborhoods runs about thirty years longer than in the poorest, one of the largest such gaps in any American city. Downstate towns that lost a plant to Mexico or to automation have seen young people leave. These are not abstractions to the people living them, and they shape how people vote.

Inequality raises questions that evidence can inform but not settle. Does a large gap slow growth, or does the chance of great wealth drive it? Does it undermine democracy when the rich can fund campaigns and the poor drop out of voting? What should be done: higher taxes on wealth, stronger unions, better schools, a minimum income, or nothing beyond letting markets work? Reasonable people disagree, and every one of these positions has serious economists and voters behind it. A citizen’s job is to know the facts, weigh the trade-offs, and decide, rather than accept the first slogan that fits.

40.11

Where the News Comes From

Main ideaEvery story you see was chosen, shaped and delivered by someone with reasons, and knowing how news is made is the first step to judging it.

In 1980 most Americans got their news from one of three television networks and a local newspaper, and those outlets decided what counted as news. Today a story reaches you through a feed built by software that chooses what to show you based on what you clicked before. The software, called an , is not trying to inform you. It is trying to keep you watching, because time on the screen is what the company sells to advertisers. Stories that make you angry, afraid or amused keep you watching longer than stories that are merely true and important. That is not a conspiracy. It is a business model, and it shapes what billions of people see every day.

Behind every story is a : the person or record where the information came from. A good reporter names sources, quotes documents and lets you check. A weak story says experts believe or people are saying. Ask who is speaking and how they know. A doctor describing a disease she treats, a government office publishing its own count, and a stranger on a video repeating something a friend told him are not equal, even if the video is more exciting. Ask also who benefits. A story about a miracle cure posted by the company selling it is not the same as one from a hospital.

Every news source has a point of view, and that is not the same as lying. A newspaper owned by a business group, a broadcaster funded by a government, and a site run by an activist will choose different stories and different words for the same event. A refugee crisis in one outlet is a migrant invasion in another. The word bias describes this tilt. The cure is not to find the one unbiased source, which does not exist, but to read across sources with different tilts and notice where they agree. When a right-leaning and a left-leaning outlet report the same facts, those facts are probably solid. Where they differ is where you need to look harder.

The historians’ habits taught in this course are the same habits a citizen needs. Who made this? When? Why? What did they leave out? Who else was there, and what would they say? A photograph from a war may be real but from a different war. A quote may be real but cut off before the sentence that changes its meaning. A number may be real but chosen from a range to make one case. None of that is new. What is new is the speed and the volume, and the fact that you, not an editor, are now the one deciding what to pass on.

40.12

Checking What You See

Main ideaA few habits, such as pausing, reading laterally, tracing to the original, and asking what would change your mind, turn a reader into a citizen who is hard to fool.

is false information spread by people who believe it. is false information spread on purpose to deceive, often by governments or groups with a goal. Both spread faster than truth: a study of millions of posts found that false stories traveled farther and faster than true ones, mostly because they were more surprising. Governments including Russia’s have run operations that create fake accounts posing as ordinary Americans to inflame arguments on both sides of an issue. The goal is not always to make you believe something false. Sometimes it is to make you believe nothing at all.

The first habit is to pause. Strong feeling is the signal to slow down. If a story makes you furious or thrilled, that is exactly when you are easiest to fool, and exactly when a false story is designed to be shared. The second habit is : instead of studying the page in front of you, open new tabs and find out what other sources say about the site and the claim. Professional fact-checkers do this and outperform professors and students who stay on the page. A site’s own About page tells you what it wants you to think of it. Other sites tell you what it is.

The third habit is to trace a claim to its origin. A statistic in a post came from somewhere: a study, a report, a count. Find it. Often the original says something narrower than the post, or says the opposite, or does not exist. A photograph can be checked by searching for the image to see where else it has appeared and when. A quote can be searched to see whether the person said it, and what came before and after. This takes minutes, and it is the difference between knowing and repeating.

The last habit is the hardest. Ask yourself what evidence would change your mind, and go look for it. People are very good at finding reasons to believe what they already believe, and the feed is very good at supplying them. A citizen who cannot name what would change his or her mind is not reasoning but rooting. This course has shown you how historians argue from evidence about slavery, war, revolution and empire. You now live in a world that generates more evidence, and more noise, in a day than those historians saw in a lifetime. The habits are the same. The stakes are yours.
