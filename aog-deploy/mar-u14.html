What PE Flattens

School

Fighting arts have long been taught inside larger institutions. Swedish and German gymnastics halls put fencing beside other exercise. Armies taught boxing to recruits in a few weeks. Colleges built fencing and wrestling teams, and Japanese universities built sumo clubs. Each institution kept what served its goals and set the rest aside.

Schools also faced a question the old masters rarely wrote down: who is responsible when a student is hurt? Courts, lawmakers and sports bodies answered with laws, rulings and new rules. In this unit you will trace those answers with dates and names. You will be able to explain how a school unit, an Army program or a college team changed an art, and why safety rules were rewritten.

The big question

When schools, armies and colleges take in a fighting art, what do they keep, what do they drop, and who writes the rules that protect students?

A Unit Called Combatives

School PE
Big questionWhat happens to the long history of a fighting art when a school or an army packs it into a few weeks of physical training?
The story

Six Weeks, Six Centuries

A gym teacher's plan book gives six weeks to arts that took centuries to grow.

A plan book lies open on a desk in a high school gym office. One page is headed Combatives, Weeks 1 to 6. Down the left side the teacher has written six names: wrestling, boxing, judo, karate, fencing and taekwondo. Each name gets one week.

Each of those names carries a long past. Wrestling appears in the art of ancient Egypt and Greece. Fencing schools were chartered by rulers in Europe in the 1400s. Judo was founded in Tokyo in 1882, and taekwondo was named in Korea in the 1950s.

The plan book has no room for most of that. It lists dates for a skills test, a grade and a permission slip. It does not list the masters, rule books, clubs and nations that shaped each art. A school unit is built for a semester, not for a family tree of teachers.

Schools did not invent this habit. Armies, gymnastics halls and college gyms packed fighting arts into short courses for more than two hundred years. They did it for fitness, for war and for school credit. Their records show what each short course kept and what it left out.

27.1

Ling's Institute in Stockholm

Main ideaPer Henrik Ling built a Swedish school system of gymnastics in 1813 that placed fencing inside a larger plan for the body.

Per Henrik Ling was born in Sweden in 1776. He worked as a fencing master at Lund University in the early 1800s. In 1813 he founded the Royal Central Institute of Gymnastics in Stockholm. The Swedish state paid for it and trained teachers there. Its graduates taught in schools and army units across Sweden.

Ling divided his into four branches: educational, military, medical and aesthetic. included fencing with swords and training with bayonets. Fencing was no longer a separate art with its own masters. It became one branch of a national program for soldiers and students.

Ling died in 1839, and his students carried the system abroad. In 1889 the Boston Normal School of Gymnastics opened to train American women as teachers of the Swedish system. A is a college that trains teachers. Swedish gymnastics then spread into many American public schools.

The Turners Cross the Ocean

Main ideaGerman Turner clubs began in Berlin in 1811, came to American cities with immigrants after 1848, and pushed gymnastics into public schools.

In 1811 Friedrich Ludwig Jahn opened an outdoor exercise ground at the Hasenheide in Berlin. It was called a , a place for Turnen, the German word for gymnastics. Jahn linked exercise to German national feeling after Napoleon’s armies had defeated Prussia. Prussia banned Turnen in 1820 because rulers feared its politics, and the ban lasted until 1842.

The revolutions of 1848 in the German states failed. Many who took part fled to the United States, and Americans called them . They founded Turner clubs, called a . The first in the United States opened in Cincinnati in 1848, and the Chicago Turngemeinde followed in 1852.

A Turner hall held gymnastics, fencing, singing, lectures and a library. Turners in Midwestern cities, including Chicago, pressed school boards to add gymnastics to public schools. They also trained many of the first gym teachers. Their clubs treated exercise as one part of a free citizen’s education.

Harvard Measures Its Students

Main ideaIn the late 1800s American colleges turned exercise into a measured school subject, and teachers formed a national association in 1885.

In 1879 Dudley Allen Sargent became director of the new Hemenway Gymnasium at Harvard College. He measured each student’s height, weight and strength when they arrived. Then he wrote a personal exercise plan for each one. Measuring the body in this way is called .

Sargent designed his own exercise machines with weights and pulleys, and other colleges copied them. His records turned exercise into something a college could chart, grade and compare. That idea of a graded school subject shaped for the next century.

In 1885 William G. Anderson gathered teachers in Brooklyn to found the American Association for the Advancement of Physical Education. The group grew into today’s . A national association could write standards, hold meetings and publish journals. Fencing and wrestling in schools would now be judged by those standards, not by the old masters of each art.

Soldiers Learn to Box

27.4

Training Camps of 1917

Main ideaWhen the United States entered World War I in 1917, the Army taught boxing to huge groups of recruits as fitness and morale training.

The United States entered World War I in April 1917. The Army had to train hundreds of thousands of new soldiers in months. The War Department formed the that same year. Raymond Fosdick led it. Congress passed a draft law, the Selective Service Act, in May 1917.

The commission ran sports, games and entertainment in the camps. Boxing got special attention because officers believed it built fitness and courage. Boxing coaches, including some professional fighters, taught large groups of recruits at the same time. Camp newspapers reported bouts between units.

Army boxing looked very different from a boxing club. A club let a boxer learn under one trainer for years. A camp gave thousands of men a short course before they shipped to France. After the war, many of those men came home as boxing fans, and the sport grew in American cities in the 1920s.

27.5

Tunney and the AEF Title

Main ideaGene Tunney won a boxing title in the American Expeditionary Forces in 1919 and became world heavyweight champion in 1926.

The , or AEF, were the U.S. troops sent to Europe in World War I. After the fighting ended in November 1918, many soldiers waited months in France to go home. The Army held sports tournaments to keep them busy. Boxing was one of the largest.

Gene Tunney was a young Marine from New York. In 1919 he won the light heavyweight championship of the AEF in Paris. The title made his name known to soldiers and sportswriters. He turned his wartime fame into a professional career. He fought as a professional for the rest of the 1920s.

In 1926 Tunney defeated Jack Dempsey in Philadelphia to win the world title. In 1927 they fought again at Soldier Field in Chicago. That rematch is remembered for the long count, when a referee’s delay in counting became a famous argument. Tunney retired as champion in 1928.

27.6

Pre-Flight Schools, 1942

Main ideaIn World War II the Navy trained future pilots at college campuses, where boxing and wrestling were required parts of the course.

In 1942 the U.S. Navy opened four on college campuses. They were at the University of Iowa, the University of North Carolina, the University of Georgia and Saint Mary’s College in California. Cadets spent months there before they began flight training.

Navy officer Tom Hamilton, a former football coach, shaped the program. Cadets took swimming, football, boxing, wrestling and hand-to-hand combat. The Navy hired college coaches as officers to teach them. Each school also fielded a football team that played college teams, such as the Iowa Pre-Flight Seahawks.

The pre-flight schools used college gyms, pools and coaches for a war goal. A cadet might box and wrestle for a few months and never step on a mat again. Yet thousands of young men left these schools with a short, graded course in combat sports on their records.

Where the Word Combatives Comes From

Main ideaCombatives is a military word; the U.S. Army used it for its field manuals and built a new program around it in the early 2000s.

is a word from military training. It means fighting skills taught to soldiers as one package, not as separate arts with their own lineages. The U.S. Army printed a called FM 21-150, Combatives, in several editions in the twentieth century. A field manual is an official Army handbook.

In the late 1990s Matt Larsen, a soldier in the 75th Ranger Regiment, began building a new program. It drew on Brazilian jiu-jitsu, wrestling, boxing, judo and Muay Thai. The Army set up a combatives school at Fort Benning, Georgia. In 2002 it published a new manual, FM 3-25.150, Combatives.

The Army program became the . It borrowed pieces from arts with long histories in Brazil, Japan, Thailand and elsewhere. It kept what served soldiers and left out the rooms, rituals and teachers of each art. School PE units later borrowed the word combatives from this military use.

Law in the Gym Class

27.8

Title IX and the Mixed PE Class

Main ideaRules written under Title IX in 1975 made most PE classes coeducational, while letting schools group students by sex during contact sports.

Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 bans sex discrimination in schools that receive federal money. In 1975 the federal government issued the that explained how schools must follow it. Regulations are detailed rules written by a government agency to carry out a law.

One regulation covered physical education classes. Schools could no longer run separate PE classes for boys and girls. A class had to be , meaning open to students of both sexes. Before this, many schools taught wrestling only to boys and dance only to girls.

The rule had an exception. During wrestling, boxing, rugby, ice hockey, football, basketball and other , a school could group students by sex inside the class. Teams for school sports follow a different regulation. The PE class rule is about who may enroll, not who may join a varsity team.

From the source
The PE Class Rule
A school may not run separate physical education classes for boys and girls. It may group students by ability. It may also separate students by sex during wrestling, boxing, rugby, ice hockey, football, basketball and other sports whose main activity involves bodily contact.

A Waiver a Court Refused

Main ideaIn 1988 the Washington Supreme Court ruled that a public school could not make students sign away their right to sue before joining school sports.

A , also called a release, is a paper in which a person gives up a right to sue if they are hurt. In the 1980s, school districts in Washington State asked students and parents to sign waivers before sports. Families who refused could not play. Two families challenged the practice.

In 1988 the Washington Supreme Court decided the case . The court ruled that the school waivers were not enforceable. It said school sports are an important public service and families have little choice but to sign. The court used factors from a 1963 California case, Tunkl v. Regents of the University of California.

The ruling did not ban school sports or change how teachers ran practice. It said a school still owes its students a , the legal duty to act with reasonable safety. A signed paper could not erase that duty. Courts in other states have looked to the case in later disputes.

Illinois Answers Molitor

Main ideaAfter a 1959 court case ended blanket school immunity in Illinois, the state passed the Tort Immunity Act of 1965 to set new limits on lawsuits.

For many years, Illinois courts held that school districts could not be sued for injuries. This idea is called . In 1959 the Illinois Supreme Court decided Molitor v. Kaneland Community Unit District No. 302. A student hurt in a school bus crash in Kane County had sued the district.

The court ended the old immunity for school districts in that case. School districts across Illinois now faced lawsuits over injuries on buses, playgrounds and in gyms. The General Assembly responded. In 1965 it passed the Local Governmental and Governmental Employees .

A is a harm one person causes another that a court can remedy with money. The 1965 act gave schools and their workers protection in many cases, but not all. It left them open to suits for conduct that was willful and wanton, meaning reckless disregard for safety. Illinois gyms and wrestling rooms still work under this law.

28.1

Guilds, Masters and the Mask

Main ideaFencing was first taught by licensed masters in guilds, and the wire mask of the late 1700s made practice safer.

In 1487 the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick III granted a charter to a fencing called the Marxbrüder, based in Frankfurt. A guild was an organization of skilled workers that controlled training and licenses. Only guild members could call themselves masters and open schools. Other European cities had their own fencing guilds and licensed masters.

A fencing school was called a , from the French word for room. Masters taught paying pupils there with blunted swords. For centuries fencers practiced without face protection. Eye injuries and deaths in practice were a real risk. The master decided when a pupil was ready and signed the pupil’s papers.

In the late 1700s French masters began using a made of wire mesh. The French master La Boëssière is often credited with it. The mask let fencers practice at full speed with far less danger. It helped fencing move from a training for duels toward a sport.

Clubs and Colleges in America

Main ideaAmerican fencing gained a national league in 1891, a college association in 1894 and an NCAA championship in 1941.

In 1891 American fencers founded the , or AFLA. It held national championships and set rules for amateur fencing. In 1981 it became the United States Fencing Association. Today the body is called USA Fencing. It later joined the FIE, the world fencing federation founded in Paris in 1913.

Colleges formed their own group in 1894, the . Eastern universities held team meets under its rules. Fencing grew as a college sport before many campuses had wrestling or basketball teams. College fencers competed with the three weapons: foil, épée and sabre.

The , the National Collegiate Athletic Association, held its first fencing championships in 1941. Fencing now had a national college title, a rule book and a place on the athletic budget. The old master and pupil bond moved into a system of coaches, teams and seasons.

Women Take the Strip

Main ideaWomen fenced in the Olympics from 1924 and in their own college association from 1929, but waited until 1982 for an NCAA title.

Women first fenced in the Olympic Games in 1924 in Paris, in a single event with the foil. Other weapons stayed closed to women at the Olympics for decades. Women’s épée joined the Games in 1996, and women’s sabre in 2004. Each opening came from a vote of the governing bodies.

In American colleges, women formed the National Intercollegiate Women&#x27;s Fencing Association in 1929. It ran women’s college championships for more than fifty years. Women’s colleges and coeducational universities in the East sent teams to its meets. The group kept women’s fencing alive while men’s college sport grew under the NCAA.

The NCAA added a women’s fencing championship in 1982, ten years after Title IX. In 1990 the NCAA combined the men’s and women’s events into one team championship. Men and women now score for the same . Fencing is one of the few NCAA sports that does this.

28.4

A Federation Born in Chicago

Main ideaIn 1920 five Midwestern state associations, including Illinois, met in Chicago to form the group that now writes national high school rules.

On May 14, 1920, leaders of five state high school associations met in Chicago. They came from Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Michigan and Wisconsin. They formed the Midwest Federation of State High School Athletic Associations. Within a few years it became a national body.

Today it is the , or NFHS. It writes playing rules for many high school sports, including wrestling. A state , like the IHSA in Illinois, runs the tournaments and follows or adapts those rules. Colleges follow separate NCAA rules.

The federation was formed partly to stop colleges and private promoters from running high school contests. The founders wanted school leaders to control school sports. That choice shaped wrestling in America. It became a school sport run by educators, with rules about grades, age and enrollment.

28.5

Gable's Iowa Room

Main ideaDan Gable's career as a college wrestler, Olympic champion and Iowa coach shows how American wrestling grew inside universities.

Dan Gable grew up in Waterloo, Iowa, and wrestled for Iowa State University. He lost only one college match, the 1970 NCAA final, to Larry Owings of Washington. At the 1972 Olympics in Munich he won a gold medal in freestyle wrestling. Reports from the Games note that no opponent scored a point on him.

In 1976 Gable became head at the University of Iowa. His teams won 15 NCAA team titles before he retired from coaching in 1997. Iowa City became a center of college wrestling. Crowds filled the university arena for home meets. Iowa’s teams also won 21 Big Ten titles under Gable.

Gable’s story shows how the United States built wrestling through schools and colleges, not through private clubs. College programs produced many American Olympic wrestlers. American colleges use their own rules, which differ from the Olympic freestyle rules. Athletes switch between the two rule books.

A Judo Teacher at the White House

Main ideaYamashita Yoshitsugu of the Kodokan taught judo to President Theodore Roosevelt in 1904 and then at the U.S. Naval Academy.

Yamashita Yoshitsugu was one of the first students of Kanō Jigorō at the , the judo school in Tokyo. He came to the United States in 1903. In 1904 President Theodore Roosevelt took judo lessons from him at the White House. Roosevelt wrote about the lessons in letters.

Yamashita then taught judo at the in Annapolis, Maryland, for a time around 1905. It was one of the first times an American school gave judo a place in its training. His wife, Yamashita Fude, is recorded as teaching judo to American women during their stay. The couple returned to Japan in 1907.

In 1935, after his death, the Kodokan made Yamashita its first tenth , its highest rank. His years in America show judo arriving on campus through one teacher, not through a national program. The art came with a Kodokan lineage, but the Naval Academy used it as military training.

Main ideaIn Japan, school and university sumo clubs became a path into professional sumo, as the career of Wajima shows.

Professional sumo in Japan is run by the . Wrestlers live and train in stables, called heya, under a stablemaster. Many enter as teenagers straight from middle school. Others first wrestle in high school and university clubs, which run their own amateur tournaments.

Wajima Hiroshi wrestled for Nihon University in Tokyo and won major student titles. He entered professional sumo in 1970. Because of his record, he was allowed to start in a higher division instead of the lowest. He reached the top rank of in 1973.

Wajima won 14 top-division championships. His path showed that a university club could feed the professional sport. Today many top wrestlers come from college sumo. A school room and a professional stable now form two stages of one career. Nihon University and other schools are known for sending wrestlers into the top division.

28.8

Three Deaths in 1997

Main ideaAfter three college wrestlers died while cutting weight in late 1997, the NCAA rewrote its weight rules in 1998.

Wrestlers compete in , so each athlete faces someone of about the same weight. Many wrestlers tried to lose weight fast before weigh-ins to compete in a lower class. This practice is called . It often meant going without water in hot rooms.

Between November and December 1997, three college wrestlers died while cutting weight. They were Billy Saylor of Campbell University in North Carolina, Joseph LaRosa of the University of Wisconsin-La Crosse and Jeff Reese of the University of Michigan. Each died within weeks of the others. Their deaths shocked schools and families.

In 1998 the NCAA changed its rules. It banned rubber suits and saunas for losing weight and moved closer to the start of competition. Later it set a lowest allowed weight for each wrestler, based on a body-fat test. High school associations adopted similar weight plans in the years that followed.

Think it through
Before and After 1998

The Lystedt Law

Main ideaWashington State's 2009 Lystedt Law set the model for school concussion laws that every state later passed.

In 2006 Zackery Lystedt, a 13-year-old football player in Maple Valley, Washington, suffered a brain injury. He had returned to a game after a blow to the head. The injury left him with lasting disabilities. His family worked with doctors and lawmakers to change the rules.

In 2009 Washington passed the . It had three main parts. Schools must teach athletes, parents and coaches about , a brain injury caused by a blow or jolt. An athlete who may have one must be removed from play, and may return only with written from a licensed health care provider.

Other states copied the model, and by 2014 every state had a youth concussion law. The laws cover wrestling, judo clubs in schools and other contact sports, not only football. Illinois passed its own school concussion laws. A single injury in one state changed rules in gyms across the country.

28.10

Headgear Comes Off

Main ideaIn 2013 amateur boxing's world body removed headgear for elite men, showing that safety rules can change direction.

Olympic boxers wore , padded head guards, from 1984 on. Many people assumed headgear made the sport safer. In 2013 the world amateur boxing body, then called AIBA, removed headgear for elite men’s bouts. Women and youth boxers kept it. The Olympic rule for men had lasted nearly thirty years.

AIBA said its own medical data supported the change. Some doctors disagreed and asked for more research. The same year AIBA adopted the ten-point must , the system used in professional boxing. Men boxed without headgear at the 2016 Olympics in Rio de Janeiro.

The change shows that safety rules are claims that can be tested and argued. A school, a college and a world may reach different answers about the same equipment. USA Boxing kept headgear for youth events. Each body writes rules for its own athletes.

Think it through
Same Ring, Different Rules

At the 2016 Olympics, an elite men's bout has no headgear. The same week, a women's Olympic bout and a youth tournament in Chicago both require it. All three follow rules written by the bodies in charge of them.

Why might different bodies reach different decisions about the same piece of equipment?
