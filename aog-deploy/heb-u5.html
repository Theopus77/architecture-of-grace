Into the Land

Nevi'im: The Former Prophets

The Torah ends with Moses looking at the land from a mountain. This unit begins on the other side of the river. The books of Joshua and Judges tell how the people came into the land and how they lived there, tribe by tribe, with no king. Leaders called judges stepped up when trouble came. Some were wise, like Deborah. Some were brave, like Gideon. Some were strong but made big mistakes, like Samson.

Then the unit turns to two families. In the book of Ruth, a young woman from Moab chooses to stay with her mother-in-law. In the first book of Samuel, a woman named Hannah prays for a son. That son, Samuel, grows up to hear a voice in the night and to lead the people. At the end, the people ask him for a king.

In this unit you will learn where these books sit in the Tanakh, what they say, and how Jewish readers have read them. You will meet a Hebrew word, chesed, that means loyal kindness. And you will practice saying "the text says" and "Jewish tradition teaches" when you tell what you found.

What do the books of Joshua, Judges, Ruth and 1 Samuel say about courage, loyalty and the kind of leader a people needs?

The story

The Judge Under the Palm Tree

People walk for miles up a hill road, because a woman under a tree will tell them what is right.

The sun is hot on the hill road. A farmer and his neighbor walk side by side, but they do not talk. They have been arguing for weeks about a goat. Whose goat is it? They cannot agree. So they are walking to the palm tree.

Everyone in the hills of Ephraim knows the tree. The book of Judges says a woman named Deborah sat under it. She was a prophetess, a person the text says spoke for God. She was also a judge. People came from all over to ask her what was right.

The two men reach the top of the hill. Under the wide palm leaves sits Deborah. She does not hurry. She listens to the farmer. She listens to the neighbor. She asks questions. Then she gives her answer, and both men nod. The argument is over.

The book of Judges says Deborah did more than settle arguments. When an enemy army came, she called a leader named Barak. She told him to gather ten thousand men. He said he would go only if she went too. She went.

This chapter is about leaders like Deborah. The Tanakh calls them judges. They were not kings. They were people who stepped up in a crisis. Some were wise. Some were brave. Some made big mistakes. Their stories are in the book of Judges, right after the book of Joshua.

Crossing into the Land

9.1

A New Leader Named Joshua

Main ideaThe book of Joshua says Joshua led the people into the land after Moses died, and was told to be strong and brave.

The Torah ends with the death of Moses. The next book in the Tanakh is . It is the first book of the Nevi&#x27;im, the Prophets. Jewish tradition calls Joshua and Judges the Former Prophets. They tell the story of the people after Moses.

The book of Joshua says Joshua had been Moses’ helper for many years. Now he became the leader. The text says God spoke to him. God told him to cross the Jordan River into the land. God told him three times to be strong and have courage.

The people crossed the Jordan. The book says the water stopped, like at the sea in Exodus. They set up twelve stones to remember it, one for each tribe. Then they were in the land the Torah had promised to Abraham.

9.2

The Walls of Jericho

Main ideaThe book of Joshua says the people marched around Jericho for seven days, and the walls fell.

The first city in the land was . It had a high wall all around it. Before the people crossed the river, Joshua sent two spies. A woman in Jericho named Rahab hid them on her roof. She asked them to keep her family safe. They promised.

The book of Joshua tells what happened next. The people marched around the city once a day for six days. Priests carried the ark of the covenant. Seven priests blew ram’s horns, called . No one spoke. On the seventh day they marched seven times.

Then the priests blew, and all the people shouted. The text says the wall fell down flat. The people took the city. Rahab and her family were kept safe, as promised. Jewish readers have long told this story. The Talmud even remembers Rahab as a person who joined the people of Israel.

9.3

Land for the Tribes

Main ideaThe book of Joshua says the land was divided among the tribes of Israel, and Joshua gave a farewell speech.

The people of Israel were made of twelve . A tribe is a big family group. Each tribe came from one of Jacob’s sons. The book of Joshua says the land was divided among them. Each tribe got its own part to live in and farm.

One tribe was different. The tribe of Levi did not get a big piece of land. The text says the Levites helped with worship instead. They got towns spread all over the land. Joseph’s two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh, each got a part of their own.

At the end of the book, Joshua is old. He gathers the people at a place called Shechem. He tells the story of Abraham, Egypt and the crossing. He asks the people to choose to serve God. The text says the people answer that they will.

Section 2

Leaders Called Judges

9.4

What a Judge Did

Main ideaIn the book of Judges, a judge was a leader who stepped up in a crisis, not a king.

After Joshua died, there was no one leader. The book of says the people lived tribe by tribe. Again and again, trouble came. An enemy would attack. The text says the people would cry out. Then a leader would step up. That leader was called a judge.

A judge in this book is not a person in a courtroom. A judge was a rescuer. Some led armies. Some settled arguments. Some did both. When the crisis was over, the judge did not become king. There was no palace and no crown.

The book of Judges has a pattern. It says the people turned away from God. Trouble came. The people cried out. A judge saved them. There was peace for a while. Then it happened again. Readers call this the of Judges.

9.5

Deborah Under the Palm Tree

Main ideaThe book of Judges says Deborah was a prophetess and judge who led the people with Barak against an enemy army.

The book of Judges says was a prophetess. That means the text says she spoke for God. She was also a judge. She sat under a palm tree in the hills of Ephraim. People came to her to settle their arguments.

An enemy king named Jabin ruled over the people. His general was Sisera, who had nine hundred iron chariots. Deborah sent for Barak. She told him to gather ten thousand men. said he would go only if Deborah went with him. She said she would.

The text says a big rain came and the chariots got stuck in the mud. Barak’s men won. Sisera ran away on foot to a tent. A woman named Jael was there, and the text says she killed him. Judges 5 is a long song of Deborah. Many scholars think it is one of the oldest poems in the Tanakh.

Gideon and Samson

9.6

Gideon's Small Army

Main ideaThe book of Judges says Gideon won with only three hundred men, so the people would trust God and not their numbers.

The book of Judges says a people called the Midianites came every year. They took the crops. A man named was hiding wheat from them. The text says a messenger from God called him a mighty man of valor. Gideon did not feel mighty. He asked for signs first.

Gideon gathered an army of thirty-two thousand men. The text says God told him the army was too big. If they won, they would say they did it by themselves. Gideon sent home everyone who was afraid. Twenty-two thousand left. Ten thousand stayed.

That was still too many. The text says God had Gideon watch the men drink from a stream. Only three hundred lapped water from their hands. Gideon kept those three hundred. At night they blew horns, broke jars and held up torches. The Midianite camp panicked and ran.

9.7

Samson's Strength

Main ideaThe book of Judges says Samson was set apart as a Nazirite from birth and was very strong.

The book of Judges tells about . Before he was born, the text says a messenger came to his mother. Her child would be a . A Nazirite made special promises. He did not drink wine. He did not cut his hair. Samson was set apart from birth.

Samson grew up very strong. The text says he tore a lion apart with his hands. He fought the , a people who lived by the sea. Once he caught three hundred foxes and tied torches to their tails to burn the Philistines’ fields.

The Philistines tried to tie him up. The text says he broke the ropes like burned string. He fought a thousand men with the jawbone of a donkey. The book says Samson judged Israel for twenty years. But he was not wise like Deborah. He acted first and thought later.

Section 4

What the Stories Teach

9.9

No King in Those Days

Main ideaThe book of Judges ends with a line that says there was no king, and everyone did what seemed right to them.

The last line of the book of Judges is famous. It says that in those days there was no in Israel. Every person did what was right in their own eyes. The book says this line four times near the end. It is a kind of sad sigh.

Readers have long asked what the line means. Some say it is a wish for a king who would bring order. The next books, Samuel and Kings, are about kings. Other readers say the line is a warning. When everyone decides alone, things can go wrong.

The stories at the end of Judges are hard. There is fighting between tribes. People do cruel things. The text does not cheer for them. It tells them plainly. Jewish tradition reads these chapters as a lesson about what happens when a people loses its way.

9.10

Courage, Trust and Choices

Main ideaJewish readers have found lessons about courage, trust and good choices in the stories of Joshua and Judges.

The stories in this chapter show different kinds of . Joshua is told to be strong and brave. Rahab risks her life to hide the spies. Deborah goes to battle when Barak asks. Gideon is afraid at first, but he goes. Courage in these books does not mean having no fear.

The stories also show . Gideon’s army is made small so the people will trust God, not their numbers. The book of Judges says trouble came when the people forgot. Jewish tradition reads these stories on some Shabbat mornings as a , a reading from the Prophets after the Torah portion.

The rabbis had a saying about strength. In Pirkei Avot, a book of rabbis’ sayings, Ben Zoma asks who is strong. His answer is the person who controls their own urges. Many Jewish readers think of Samson here. He could lift a city gate, but he could not control himself. That is the choice the stories ask about.

The story

A Promise on a Dusty Road

Three women stand at a crossroads. One is going home. The other two must choose.

The road is dry and dusty. It runs from the land of Moab toward Bethlehem, a town in the hills of Judah. An old woman named Naomi stands on the road. Two young women stand with her. They are her sons' wives. Her sons have died. Her husband has died. She has nothing left in Moab.

Naomi turns to the two young women. Their names are Orpah and Ruth. Go back, she tells them. Go home to your mothers. You are young. You can marry again. I am old and I have nothing to give you. She kisses them. All three of them cry.

Orpah kisses Naomi and turns back toward Moab. She has made her choice, and the book of Ruth does not blame her. But Ruth does not move. She holds on to Naomi. Naomi tells her again to go. Ruth answers with words that people still say today.

Do not ask me to leave you, Ruth says. Where you go, I will go. Where you sleep, I will sleep. Your people will be my people. Your God will be my God. Where you die, I will die. Naomi sees that Ruth means it. She stops arguing. The two women walk on together.

This chapter begins with Ruth's promise. It is the story of a stranger who chose to stay. Then it turns to another woman, Hannah, who prayed for a child. Her son Samuel would grow up to lead Israel. And the chapter ends with the people asking for something new: a king.

Section 1

Ruth and Naomi

10.1

A Famine and a Journey

Main ideaThe book of Ruth says a family left Bethlehem in a famine, and Naomi came back with only Ruth beside her.

The book of is short. It has only four chapters. It begins in the days when the judges judged. There was a famine, a time with no food. A man from Bethlehem took his wife and two sons to the land of Moab. Moab was across the Dead Sea, to the east.

In Moab, Naomi’s husband died. Her sons married Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. Then both sons died too. Naomi heard that there was food again in Bethlehem. She decided to go home. Her sons’ wives started out with her.

On the road, Naomi told them to go back. Orpah went back. Ruth stayed. When they reached Bethlehem, the women of the town were surprised. Naomi told them not to call her Naomi, which means pleasant. She said to call her Mara, which means bitter. Her life had been very hard.

Think it through
Pleasant or Bitter?

In Hebrew, the name Naomi is close to a word meaning pleasant. When Naomi comes home, she says: Do not call me Naomi. Call me Mara, which means bitter. Picture the women of Bethlehem hearing this. They knew her years ago, when she left with a husband and two sons. Now she is back with one young woman from Moab.

Why does Naomi want a new name? What has changed for her? What has she not lost?

10.2

Where You Go, I Will Go

Main ideaRuth's promise to Naomi is one of the best-known lines in the Tanakh, and Jewish tradition reads it as a choice to join the people of Israel.

On the road, Ruth made a . She said she would go where Naomi went. She would live where Naomi lived. Naomi’s people would be her people. Naomi’s God would be her God. She would die where Naomi died. Only death would part them.

Ruth was a Moabite. She was not born into the people of Israel. Jewish tradition reads her words as a choice to join them. The rabbis of the Talmud and the midrash saw Ruth as a model of a person who becomes Jewish by choice. In Hebrew, such a person is called a .

Many readers notice what Ruth puts first. She does not say she will get a better life. She says she will stay with Naomi. Loyalty comes before anything else. The book of Ruth never uses the word love for the two women. It shows it instead.

10.3

Gleaning in the Fields

Main ideaThe book of Ruth says Ruth gathered leftover grain, a practice the Torah set up to feed the poor, in the field of a man named Boaz.

Ruth and Naomi came to Bethlehem at the start of the barley harvest. They had no food. Ruth said she would go . To glean is to pick up grain that the harvesters drop or leave behind. The Torah says farmers must leave some grain for the poor and the stranger.

Ruth went to a field. It belonged to a man named . He was a relative of Naomi’s husband. Boaz noticed Ruth. He had heard how she stayed with Naomi. He told his workers to let her glean safely. He told them to drop extra grain on purpose.

Ruth worked all day. She came home with a big load of barley. Naomi asked whose field it was. When she heard the name Boaz, she was glad. Boaz was family. In the book, gleaning is not just about food. It shows how the Torah’s laws about the poor worked in a real field.

From the source
Leave Some for the Poor
Leviticus 19:9–10 tells farmers not to harvest all the way to the corners of their fields, and not to go back for what was dropped. The corners and the fallen grain are to be left for the poor and for the stranger.
Leviticus (Vayikra) 19:9–10, paraphrased from the Jewish Publication Society translation, 1917 (paraphrased)

Ruth was both poor and a stranger. How does this law from the Torah help explain what she does in Boaz's field?

Section 2

Boaz and a New Family

10.4

Boaz the Redeemer

Main ideaThe book of Ruth says Boaz acted as a redeemer, married Ruth, and their great-grandson was King David.

In the Torah, a close relative could help a family that lost its land or its men. Such a helper was called a go&#x27;el, a redeemer. Naomi knew Boaz was a relative. She sent Ruth to ask him to be their redeemer. Ruth went at night to the threshing floor, where grain was beaten out.

Boaz said he was willing. But there was a closer relative. Boaz went to the town gate, where business was done. He asked the other man in front of ten elders. The man said no. So Boaz took off his sandal, an old sign of a deal, and agreed to redeem the family.

Boaz married Ruth. They had a son named . The women of the town said a son had been born to Naomi. The book ends with a family list. Obed was the father of Jesse. Jesse was the father of David. So Ruth, the stranger from Moab, was the great-grandmother of Israel’s most famous king.

Think it through
From Ruth to David

Read the chain at the end of the book of Ruth like a set of steps. Ruth and Boaz have a son, Obed. Obed has a son, Jesse. Jesse has a son, David. David becomes the king remembered in the Psalms. Now go back to the dusty road. Ruth, a Moabite, was told to go home.

Why might the book end with this family list? What does it say about who can belong to the people of Israel?

Words to know
go'el

Obed

Check yourself

1. What is a go'el?

Why: A go'el is a redeemer. Boaz took on that role for Naomi and Ruth.

2. Where did Boaz settle the matter with the closer relative?

Why: Ruth 4 says Boaz went to the town gate, where business was done, and spoke before ten elders.

3. How is Ruth related to King David, according to the book?

Why: The book ends: Obed, son of Ruth, was the father of Jesse, who was the father of David.
10.5

Ruth and Shavuot

Main ideaJewish tradition reads the book of Ruth on Shavuot, the spring harvest holiday that also remembers the giving of the Torah.

In the Tanakh, the book of Ruth is not next to Judges. It is in , the Writings, the third part. It is one of five short books called the , or scrolls. Each of the five is read on a different holiday. Ruth is read on .

Shavuot comes seven weeks after Passover. Its name means weeks. It was a harvest holiday for wheat and the first fruits. Jewish tradition also says the Torah was given at Mount Sinai on Shavuot. Many Jewish families eat dairy foods, like cheese blintzes, on this day.

Why read Ruth on Shavuot? Readers give a few reasons. The story happens during the harvest. Ruth chose to accept the Torah, just as the people did at Sinai. And Jewish tradition says King David, Ruth’s great-grandson, was born and died on Shavuot. In Chicago and around the world, synagogues read this scroll each spring.

Read the numbers
Counting to Shavuot

Leviticus 23:15–16, Jewish Publication Society translation, 1917; common Jewish practice

Seven weeks is forty-nine days. What number day is Shavuot? Why might a harvest holiday be a good day to read a story about a field?

Section 3

Hannah and Samuel

10.6

Hannah's Prayer

Main ideaThe first book of Samuel says Hannah prayed quietly for a child, and the rabbis made her prayer a model for how to pray.

The book of begins with a woman named . She was married to Elkanah. She had no children. This made her very sad. Every year the family went to the holy place at Shiloh. One year, Hannah went to the door of the sanctuary and prayed.

Hannah made a promise. If God gave her a son, she would give him back to serve God all his life. She prayed with her lips moving, but no sound came out. The priest saw her. He thought she was drunk. Hannah said no. She was pouring out her heart.

Eli blessed her. The text says Hannah had a son. She named him . When he was a small boy, she brought him to Shiloh to live with Eli, as she had promised. The rabbis of the Talmud pointed to Hannah. They said prayer should be like hers: quiet, from the heart, with the lips moving.

From the source
How Hannah Prayed
In the Talmud, tractate Berakhot 31a, the rabbis learn rules for prayer from Hannah in 1 Samuel 1:13. She spoke in her heart, so a person praying should mean it inside. Her lips moved, so a person should form the words. Her voice was not heard, so prayer should be quiet.
Talmud, Berakhot 31a, paraphrased from Rodkinson's translation, 1896–1903; 1 Samuel 1:13, JPS 1917 (paraphrased)

The rabbis turned a story into rules. Find each rule and the part of Hannah's story it comes from.

10.7

A Voice in the Night

Main ideaThe text says young Samuel heard a voice calling his name at night, and Eli taught him how to answer.

Samuel grew up at Shiloh. He helped Eli, who was old and could not see well. One night Samuel was lying down near the , the holy box that held the tablets. He heard a voice call his name. He ran to Eli and said, here I am. Eli said he had not called.

This happened three times. The third time, Eli understood. He told Samuel to lie down again. If the voice came, Samuel should say: speak, for your servant is listening. The voice came a fourth time. Samuel answered as Eli had said.

The text says the message was hard. Eli’s sons had done wrong, and Eli had not stopped them. Samuel was afraid to tell Eli. In the morning, Eli made him tell everything. Eli accepted it. From then on, the book says, all Israel knew Samuel was a , a person who speaks for God.

From the source
Speak, I Am Listening
And the LORD came, and stood, and called as at other times: 'Samuel, Samuel.' Then Samuel said: 'Speak; for Thy servant heareth.'
1 Samuel 3:10, Jewish Publication Society translation, 1917

"Heareth" means hears. Who taught Samuel to answer this way? Why might it matter that he says he is listening, not talking?

10.8

Samuel the Judge

Main ideaThe text says Samuel was the last of the judges, traveling from town to town to lead the people.

Samuel grew up to be a judge, like Deborah and Gideon. But he was also a prophet. The book says he traveled in a circle each year. He went to Bethel, Gilgal and Mizpah. In each place he judged the people. Then he went home to Ramah.

The Philistines were still a danger. The text says they once captured the ark in battle. Eli died when he heard the news. Later, Samuel gathered the people at . He prayed for them. The text says the Philistines were driven back.

Samuel set up a stone to remember that day. He called it Eben-ezer, which means stone of help. Readers often call Samuel the last judge. After him, the book turns to kings. Jewish tradition counts 1 and 2 Samuel as two parts of one book, named for him.

Section 4

The People Ask for a King

10.9

Give Us a King

Main ideaThe book of 1 Samuel says the people asked Samuel for a king like other nations, and Samuel warned them what a king would take.

Samuel grew old. His sons were judges, but the text says they were not honest. The elders of Israel came to Samuel at Ramah. They said: give us a king to judge us, like all the . Samuel did not like this. He prayed about it.

The text says God told Samuel to listen to the people. But Samuel should warn them first. So Samuel told them what a king would do. A king would take their sons for his army. He would take their daughters as workers. He would take the best of their fields and a tenth of their crops.

The people still said they wanted a king. They wanted someone to lead them in battle. Samuel agreed. Later, the text says, he poured oil on the head of a tall young man named . This was called anointing. Saul became the first king. The next chapter tells his story.

From the source
What a King Will Take
In 1 Samuel 8:11–17, Samuel warns the people. A king will take their sons to drive his chariots and run before him. He will take their daughters to cook and bake. He will take the best fields and vineyards and give them to his servants. He will take a tenth of their grain and flocks. And the people, Samuel says, will be his servants.
1 Samuel 8:11–17, paraphrased from the Jewish Publication Society translation, 1917 (paraphrased)

The people heard this warning and still wanted a king. Why do you think they did? Was Samuel right to warn them?

10.10

Chesed, the Thread

Main ideaThe Hebrew word chesed, loyal kindness, runs through the stories of Ruth, Hannah and Samuel.

There is a Hebrew word that runs through this chapter. The word is . It is hard to say in one English word. It means kindness, but more than that. It means kindness that keeps a promise. It means loyalty. The JPS 1917 translation often says kindness or mercy.

The book of Ruth uses the word three times. Naomi hopes God will show chesed to Ruth and Orpah. Naomi says Boaz has shown chesed to the living and the dead. Boaz tells Ruth her chesed is great. Ruth stayed when she did not have to. That is chesed.

Hannah kept her promise and gave up her son. Eli accepted hard news with grace. Samuel served the people all his life. Jewish tradition calls chesed one of the things the world stands on, in Pirkei Avot 1:2. Readers say the book of Ruth is a story of chesed from start to end.

From the source
What the World Stands On
In Pirkei Avot 1:2, Simeon the Just is quoted as saying the world stands on three things: on the Torah, on worship, and on acts of loving-kindness, in Hebrew gemilut chasadim.
Pirkei Avot 1:2, paraphrased from Charles Taylor, Sayings of the Jewish Fathers, 1877 (paraphrased)

Pick one person from this chapter. Which of the three things in this saying did they do? Give one example from the text.

loyalty
