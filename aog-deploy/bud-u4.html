What the Buddhist Texts Are

The Library

Walk into a Buddhist temple and you may see shelves of books, bundles of palm leaves or long scrolls. These are Buddhist scriptures. There are so many that people call them a library, not one book.

In this unit you will learn how the oldest collection, the Tipitaka, is sorted into three baskets. You will see where the Dhammapada and the Jatakas fit. You will also learn about the Mahayana sutras kept in Chinese and Tibetan.

Then you will follow the teachings on their long trip. They went from memory and chanting to palm leaves, printed scrolls and English books. By the end, you will be able to explain how the Buddhist library is built and how it traveled.

The big question

What are the Buddhist scriptures, and how did they reach us?

The Three Baskets

The Tipitaka
Big questionHow are the Buddhist scriptures organized, and why is there no single Buddhist Bible?
The story

Three Baskets on a Shelf

A monk explains why the old books were once stored in three baskets.

A fourth-grade class visits a Theravada Buddhist temple near Chicago. A monk in an orange robe greets them at the door. He bows slightly with his palms together. The students bow back.

He leads them to a wooden shelf. On it sit rows of books with gold letters. "These are our scriptures," he says. "We call them the Tipitaka. That means three baskets."

A boy named Omar raises his hand. "Why baskets?" The monk smiles. "Long ago, in India, workers passed dirt and bricks along in baskets. Some teachers say the name came from that. Others say the palm-leaf books were kept in baskets. Either way, the teachings were sorted into three groups."

He holds up three fingers. "One basket is for rules for monks and nuns. One is for the Buddha's talks. One is for careful lists about the mind." The students count along with him.

Omar looks at the long shelf. "That is a lot of books," he says. The monk nods. "And this is only one part of the Buddhist library." The students look around the room with new eyes.

Section 1

The Tipitaka

7.1

Three Baskets in Pali

Main ideaThe Tipitaka, or three baskets, is the collection of scriptures Theravada Buddhists use, written in the Pali language.

Theravada Buddhists in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Cambodia and Laos share one big set of scriptures. It is called the . The word means "three baskets." Tipitaka is the Pali form. The Sanskrit form is Tripitaka.

The Tipitaka is written in . Pali is an old language from India. Few people speak it at home today. Monks learn it to read and chant the texts. English readers often call this collection the Pali Canon.

Buddhists believe the Tipitaka holds the Buddha’s teachings as his students remembered them. Scholars say it grew over several centuries. Both groups agree it is very old and very large. The English translation fills dozens of books.

7.2

The Vinaya Basket

Main ideaThe Vinaya holds the rules for Buddhist monks and nuns and stories of why each rule was made.

The first basket is the . It holds the rules for monks and nuns. The rules cover eating, clothing, sleeping and living together. They also explain how to settle arguments.

Each rule comes with a story. The Vinaya tells what happened, and then how the Buddha made a rule about it. For example, it tells of monks traveling in the rainy season. Farmers worried about their crops being stepped on. So monks were asked to stay in one place during the rains.

Theravada monks recite the main list of rules together twice a month. This list is called the . For fully ordained monks it has 227 rules. Nuns have a longer list.

7.3

The Sutta and Abhidhamma Baskets

Main ideaThe Sutta basket holds the Buddha's talks, and the Abhidhamma basket holds careful lists about the mind.

The second basket is the Sutta basket. A is a talk or teaching. The Sanskrit word is sutra. Most suttas show the Buddha or a student teaching someone. There are thousands of them.

The suttas are grouped into five collections called Nikayas. Some hold long talks. Some hold middle-length talks. One, the Khuddaka Nikaya, holds a mix of shorter books.

The third basket is the . It sorts ideas about the mind into careful lists. It names kinds of thoughts and feelings. Theravada teachers value it highly. Many scholars think it was put together later than the suttas.

Section 2

Books Inside the Baskets

7.4

The Dhammapada

Main ideaThe Dhammapada is a short book of 423 verses inside the Sutta basket.

The sits in the Khuddaka Nikaya, the collection of shorter books. Its name means something like "path of the teaching." It has 423 short verses in 26 chapters.

Many Buddhists memorize its verses. They are short and easy to remember, like proverbs. Chapters have names like "The Mind," "Flowers" and "Anger." Readers often quote them.

The Dhammapada is one of the best-known Buddhist books in the world. It has been translated into many languages. One of the first English translations was by F. Max Muller in 1881.

7.5

The Jatakas

Main ideaThe Jatakas, stories of the Buddha's earlier lives, are also in the Sutta basket.

The Khuddaka Nikaya also holds the . These are stories Buddhists tell about the Buddha’s earlier lives. There are about 547 of them. The stories are often long.

In the Pali Canon itself, each Jataka is a set of verses. A later commentary tells the full story around the verses. Most people know the stories from that commentary.

The last ten Jatakas are long and famous. The very last is the story of Prince Vessantara, who gives away everything he has. Buddhists in Thailand and Laos still hear it told at festivals.

7.6

"Thus Have I Heard"

Main ideaSuttas usually begin "Thus have I heard," words tradition gives to the Buddha's student Ananda.

Many suttas begin the same way: "Thus have I heard." In Pali it is . Then the sutta names a place. It might say the Buddha was staying in a park near a city.

Buddhist tradition says the "I" is Ananda. He was the Buddha’s cousin and helper. He was known for his memory. Tradition says he recited the suttas after the Buddha died.

The opening tells listeners where a teaching came from. It says, in effect, "I did not make this up. I heard it." Mahayana sutras start the same way. It also marks the start.

A Library, Not One Book

7.7

Mahayana Sutras

Main ideaMahayana Buddhists also treasure many other sutras, first written in Sanskrit and kept in Chinese and Tibetan.

Buddhism is followed in China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam and Tibet. Mahayana means "great vehicle." Mahayana Buddhists honor many early texts too. They also treasure many other sutras.

Famous ones include the Lotus Sutra, the Heart Sutra and the Diamond Sutra. Many were first written in Sanskrit. Many Sanskrit copies were later lost. They survive in Chinese and Tibetan translations.

Mahayana Buddhists believe these sutras teach the Buddha’s deepest message. Scholars date most of them from about the 1st century BCE onward. Theravada Buddhists do not include them in their canon.

7.8

No Single Buddhist Bible

Main ideaBuddhism has no one book that every Buddhist uses; different traditions keep different collections.

Some religions have one main book. Buddhism is different. Buddhists have a large library of scriptures. Different traditions choose different parts. The library is very big.

A Theravada monk in Thailand reads the Pali Canon. A Zen teacher in Japan may chant the Heart Sutra. A Tibetan nun studies texts in the Kangyur. Each is reading Buddhist scripture.

Many families also have favorite texts. A Pure Land Buddhist may love a sutra about Amitabha Buddha. A Nichiren Buddhist chants the name of the Lotus Sutra. No one book holds it all.

7.9

Where the Branches Live

Main ideaTheravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism grew strong in different parts of Asia and now live in America too.

Theravada Buddhism is strongest in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Mahayana Buddhism grew in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam. Buddhism is strongest in Tibet, Bhutan and Mongolia.

Each branch has its own favorite scriptures. Each has its own ways of practice. Buddhists in each branch see themselves as following the Buddha. None is ranked above the others here.

All three branches are found in Illinois today. Families from Thailand, Japan, Vietnam, Tibet and many other places have temples here. The Buddhist Temple of Chicago was founded in 1944.

From Memory to Palm Leaves

How the Texts Traveled
Big questionHow did the Buddha's teachings travel from spoken words to the books we have today?
The story

Five Hundred in a Cave

Five hundred monks sit in a cave, and one of them begins, "Thus have I heard."

Buddhist tradition tells this story. A few months after the Buddha died, his students gathered near the city of Rajagaha. They met in a large cave in the hills. Five hundred senior monks came.

They had a big worry. The Buddha had taught for many years. But he had written nothing down. How would they keep his words safe? Some of the monks looked at one another in silence.

First a monk named Upali stood. He recited the rules for monks and nuns. The others listened closely. When he finished, they agreed that his words were correct.

Then Ananda stood. He had been the Buddha's helper for many years. He had heard more teachings than anyone. The cave was quiet. Ananda began: "Thus have I heard."

The monks chanted the teachings back together. Buddhists call this meeting the First Council. For hundreds of years after, the teachings lived in memory and chanting.

Remembering the Teachings

8.1

The First Council

Main ideaTradition says the Buddha's students met after his death to recite and agree on his teachings.

Buddhist tradition says a meeting was held soon after the Buddha died. It is called the First . A council is a meeting to decide something important. Tradition places it at Rajagaha, in northern India.

The Vinaya tells the story. It says 500 monks attended. Upali recited the rules. Ananda recited the talks. The group agreed on what they heard. Then they chanted it together.

Scholars cannot check every detail of this story. Many think the texts grew and changed over time. Buddhists and scholars both agree the teachings were first passed on by voice, not writing.

was the Buddha’s cousin. For about 25 years he was the Buddha’s personal helper. He walked beside him and brought him water. He heard many talks.

8.3

Chanting Together

Main ideaFor centuries, monks kept the teachings by chanting them together.

After the council, groups of monks memorized different parts of the texts. They chanted them together, again and again. This is called oral .

Chanting together helps catch mistakes. If one monk says a word wrong, the others notice. Repeated lists and phrases made the texts easier to remember.

Scholars say the suttas show signs of this. They repeat whole lines many times. They use numbered lists, like the Four Noble Truths. These help memory.

Section 2

Written Down at Last

8.4

Palm Leaves in Sri Lanka

Main ideaTradition records that the Pali Canon was first written down on palm leaves in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE.

Buddhism reached Sri Lanka around the 3rd century BCE. Monks there kept chanting the texts. Then came hard times, with war and famine. Many monks died.

The Sri Lankan chronicles say monks worried the teachings might be lost. So they wrote them down. Tradition places this at Aluvihara in the 1st century BCE.

They wrote on palm leaves. Writers scratched letters into dried leaves with a metal pen. Then they rubbed in ink or soot to make the letters dark. The leaves were tied into bundles.

8.5

Making a Palm-Leaf Book

Main ideaPalm-leaf books were made by hand, and copies had to be made again and again.

A palm-leaf book is long and thin. Each leaf has holes. A string runs through the holes. Wooden covers protect the pages. The covers are often painted or carved with care.

Palm leaves slowly wear out. Heat, bugs and damp harm them. So monks copied the texts onto new leaves every so often. Copying was careful, slow work. A copy could take months.

Many Buddhist temples in Sri Lanka, Thailand and Myanmar still keep palm-leaf books. Some libraries study them today. Very old leaves are rare, because most wore out.

Across Asia and the World

8.6

The Diamond Sutra of 868

Main ideaA printed Diamond Sutra from 868 CE is the oldest dated printed book known.

In 1900 a monk in Dunhuang, China, found a hidden cave room. It was full of old scrolls. One was a copy of the , a Mahayana text. The cave had been sealed for centuries.

It was printed with carved wooden blocks. At the end it gives a date that matches 868 CE. It says a man named Wang Jie had it made for his parents. It is the oldest dated printed book known.

Today it is kept at the British Library in London. The scroll is about 16 feet long. It shows how Buddhists in China used printing to share their texts.

8.7

Into Chinese and Tibetan

Main ideaTranslators carried Buddhist texts into Chinese and Tibetan over many centuries.

Buddhism reached China by about the 1st century CE. Translators worked for centuries to turn texts into Chinese. One famous translator was Kumarajiva, around 400 CE.

Buddhism reached Tibet in the 7th and 8th centuries CE. Tibetan scholars made careful from Sanskrit. They made word lists so each term was translated the same way.

Some texts now survive only in Chinese or Tibetan. Without these translators, many sutras would be lost. Buddhists in East Asia and Tibet read them in their own languages.

8.8

Into English

Main ideaScholars began translating Buddhist texts into English in the 1800s, and Chicago hosted Buddhist speakers in 1893.

In the 1800s, European scholars began to study Pali and Sanskrit. F. Max Muller translated the Dhammapada in 1881. T.W. Rhys Davids translated suttas that same year. He also founded the Pali Text Society in 1881.

In 1893 the World’s of Religions met in Chicago. Two Buddhist speakers came. Anagarika Dharmapala came from Sri Lanka. Soyen Shaku, a Zen teacher, came from Japan.

Today many translations exist. Buddhist monks, nuns and scholars all translate. Some translations are free online. Readers can compare them side by side.

1. What does Tipitaka mean?

Why: Tipitaka means three baskets.

2. Which basket holds rules for monks and nuns?

Why: The Vinaya holds the monastic rules.

3. Which basket holds the Buddha's talks?

Why: The Sutta basket holds the talks.

4. Where is the Dhammapada found?

Why: The Dhammapada is one of the shorter books of the Khuddaka Nikaya.

5. How many verses does the Dhammapada have?

Why: It has 423 verses. 547 is about the number of Jatakas.

6. What are the Jatakas?

Why: Buddhists tell the Jatakas as the Buddha's earlier lives.

7. Which is a Mahayana sutra?

Why: The Heart Sutra is Mahayana. The others are in the Pali Canon.

8. How do suttas usually begin?

Why: Suttas open with evam me sutam, "Thus have I heard."

9. Who does tradition say recited the suttas at the First Council?

Why: Tradition says Ananda recited the suttas.

10. How were the teachings kept for centuries?

Why: The teachings were kept by oral tradition.

11. Where does tradition say the Pali Canon was first written?

Why: The Sri Lankan chronicles name Aluvihara.

12. What is the oldest dated printed book known?

Why: The Dunhuang Diamond Sutra is dated 868 CE.

13. Who translated many sutras into Chinese around 400 CE?

Why: Kumarajiva was a famous translator into Chinese.

14. Why do people say Buddhism has no single Bible?

Why: Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhists treasure different collections.

15. Who spoke for Buddhism in Chicago in 1893?

Why: Dharmapala and Soyen Shaku spoke at the World's Parliament of Religions.
