Genres and How to Read Them

Method

The Bible is not one kind of book. Open it at random and you might land on a story, a list of laws, a family tree, a poem, a warning from a prophet, a letter to a small church or a vision of beasts rising from the sea. Each of these is a different kind of writing, and each one asks to be read in its own way. This unit teaches the first tool of careful reading: noticing the kind, or genre, before anything else.

In a public school, we read the Bible as literature and history. We ask what the text says, how it is built and how Jewish, Christian and other readers have understood it. We do not ask you to believe it or to reject it. That is why you will hear phrases like 'the text says,' 'tradition names' and 'many scholars think.' Those words keep us accurate about who is speaking.

By the end of the unit you will be able to pick up a passage and tell whether it is narrative, law, genealogy, poetry, prophecy, gospel, letter or apocalypse. You will know what each genre does, what clues give it away and what questions to bring to it. You will also be able to compare a biblical text with an older text from its world, such as the laws of Hammurabi, and say what is alike and what is different.

The story

One Page, Two Books

A student reads the same page as a story, then as a law code, and finds two different books.

Maya's class was given one photocopied page from the book of Exodus. Her teacher, Mr. Ortiz, said the class would read it twice. The first time, they should read it the way they would read a novel. The second time, they should read it the way a judge reads a set of rules. Maya thought that sounded like the same job done twice. She was wrong.

On the first read, the page felt like a scene. Moses is on a mountain. The people wait below. There is smoke and the sound of a horn. Maya noticed who was speaking and who was afraid. She wanted to know what happened next. She read fast, looking for the plot.

On the second read, the plot stopped mattering. The same page had a list. Do not do this. If a person does that, then this follows. Maya slowed down. She started to ask different questions. Who does this rule protect? What happens if two rules seem to bump into each other? Why does one line say 'do not' and the next line say 'if… then'?

When the class compared notes, a student named Devin said the page felt like two books stapled together. Mr. Ortiz said that was close to right. The Bible is not one kind of writing. It holds stories, laws, lists of names, poems, letters and visions. Each kind asks to be read in its own way. The word for a kind of writing is *genre*, and learning to spot it is the first tool a careful reader needs.

Maya wrote one sentence at the bottom of her page: 'Same words, different questions.' Mr. Ortiz read it over her shoulder and asked if he could put it on the board. By the end of the week the whole class was using it. That sentence is the idea behind this whole chapter, and it is the habit every careful reader of the Bible learns first.

Talk about itThink of a recipe and a poem about the same meal. What would you look for in each one that you would not look for in the other?
Section 1

What Genre Means

17.1

Kinds of Writing

Main ideaGenre is the kind of writing a passage is, and it tells a reader which questions to ask.

Pick up a newspaper, a comic book and a birthday card. You already know that you read them differently. You do not ask a comic book to be accurate about the weather. You do not ask a birthday card for a plot. Each kind of writing comes with its own set of expectations. That kind is its .

The Bible is a collection of many genres. Genesis is mostly , which means it tells a story with people, places and events in order. Exodus starts as narrative and then turns into , a set of rules for a community. Chapters of Genesis and Numbers are lists of names, called genealogies. Later books hold poems, speeches, letters and visions.

Readers in every tradition have known this for a long time. Jewish teachers wrote about the difference between halakhah, the parts of the Torah that give rules, and aggadah, the parts that tell stories. Christian scholars sorted the books of the Bible into history, poetry, prophecy and letters. Modern scholars use the word genre for the same idea.

Knowing the genre does not tell you what a passage means. It tells you what to look for. In a story you look for plot and character. In a law you look for the case and the outcome. In a list of names you look for the shape of a family or a people. This chapter practices the three prose genres: narrative, law and genealogy.

17.2

A Library, Not One Book

Main ideaThe Bible is a library of books in different genres, so a careful reader checks the shelf before reading.

The word Bible comes from a Greek word that means ’books.’ That is a good reminder. Jews call the Hebrew Bible the Tanakh; Christians call it the Old Testament. It holds law, history, prophecy, poetry and wisdom. The New Testament, which Christians add, holds four Gospels, a history called Acts, twenty-one letters and one vision.

The first five books are called the in Jewish tradition and the Pentateuch by many scholars. The word Torah is often translated ’law,’ but it means something closer to ’teaching.’ That fits, because these five books mix story and law together. Genesis is almost all story. Leviticus is almost all law. Exodus, Numbers and Deuteronomy hold both.

Even one book can change genre from chapter to chapter. Exodus 1 through 19 is narrative: Egypt, Moses, the plagues, the sea. Exodus 20 through 23 is law. Exodus 24 goes back to narrative. Exodus 25 through 31 gives building instructions for a tent shrine. A reader who expects one genre all the way through will get lost.

So the first habit of a careful reader is to ask, before anything else, what kind of writing is this? The answer changes the questions you bring. It also helps you be fair to the text. A story is not failing when it does not give a rule. A law is not failing when it does not tell a story.

17.3

Studying a Sacred Text at School

Main ideaIn a public school the Bible is studied as literature and history, and every belief about it is described, not taught.

This course treats the Bible the way a literature class treats a novel and a history class treats a document. We ask what the text says, how it is put together and how people have read it. We do not ask you to believe it or to reject it. That is the job of families and communities, not the job of a public school.

The rule comes from American law. In 1963 the United States Supreme Court decided a case called Abington School District v. Schempp. The Court ruled that public schools may not lead students in Bible readings as worship. In the same decision, the Court said that studying the Bible for its literary and historic qualities, as part of a secular program of education, is allowed.

That is why this course uses careful words. We say ’Genesis tells,’ ’the text says,’ ’Jews read this as’ and ’Christians believe.’ Those phrases are not a way of doubting anyone. They are a way of being accurate about who says what. A Jewish reader, a Christian reader, a Muslim reader and a reader with no religion can all study the same page together.

It also means we read the Bible in translation, mostly from older English versions that are free to copy, like the King James Version of 1611. Where translations differ, we say so. Where traditions differ, we describe both. Your job is to read closely and to explain what you find, not to decide whose faith is right.

Section 2

Narrative

17.4

Plot in a Few Words

Main ideaBiblical narrative moves fast, gives few details and leaves gaps the reader must notice.

Open Genesis 22. In two verses the story sets up a test. God speaks to Abraham and tells him to take his son Isaac to a mountain and offer him there. There is no description of Abraham’s face. There is no account of the night before. The text says he rose early, saddled his donkey and went.

This is how biblical narrative usually works. It gives a plot in a few words. Where a modern novel would spend a page on feelings, the Bible often spends nothing. The literary scholar Erich Auerbach, writing in the 1940s, compared this style to Homer’s. Homer lights up every detail. Genesis leaves most of the scene in shadow.

That shadow is not a mistake. It is a , a place where the text does not say. Readers have filled the gap for thousands of years. Jewish tradition asks what Abraham thought during the three-day walk. Christian readers have seen the scene as a picture of a later story. Modern readers ask what Isaac knew. The text lets all these questions stand, because it does not answer them.

So when you read a Bible story, do two things. First, track the plot: who does what, in what order. Second, mark the gaps: what does the story refuse to tell? The second task is where the reading gets interesting.

17.5

The Narrator Who Stays Quiet

Main ideaThe biblical narrator rarely judges or explains, so readers must weigh the characters for themselves.

In most modern stories, the narrator tells you what to think. A character is ’kind’ or ’cruel.’ In the Bible, the almost never does this. The narrator reports what people say and do and then moves on. Judgments are rare, and when they come, they land hard.

Take the story of David and Bathsheba in 2 Samuel 11. David sees a woman bathing, sends for her, and later arranges for her husband Uriah to die in battle. For twenty-six verses the narrator says nothing about right or wrong. Then comes one short sentence: the thing David had done displeased the LORD. That one line is the narrator’s whole verdict.

This quiet style means is shown, not told. You learn who Jacob is by watching him trade a bowl of stew for his brother’s birthright. You learn who Ruth is by hearing her refuse to leave Naomi. Readers have to do the work a modern narrator would do for them. That is why the same character can look different to different readers.

Jewish, Christian and academic readers all use this feature of the text. The rabbis of the Midrash asked why the narrator withheld a judgment and what that silence teaches. Christian preachers filled the silence with sermons. Literary scholars such as Robert Alter, in The Art of Biblical Narrative (1981), described the quiet narrator as one of the Bible’s main tools.

17.6

Repetition and Pattern

Main ideaBiblical stories repeat words and patterns on purpose, and a change in the pattern is a signal to the reader.

Genesis 1 is built on repetition. Each day begins with ’And God said.’ Most days end with ’And the evening and the morning were’ a numbered day. Six times the text says God saw that it was good. Then on the sixth day the phrase changes: God saw everything that he had made, and it was very good. The pattern sets up the change, and the change carries the point.

Repetition is a tool in a text that was meant to be read aloud. Listeners could not flip back a page. A repeated phrase told them where they were. A broken phrase told them to pay attention. Scholars call a repeated key word in a passage a , a German term meaning leading word.

Patterns also work across whole stories. Scenes of a man meeting a woman at a well repeat in Genesis 24, Genesis 29 and Exodus 2. Each time the setting is the same, but the details differ. Rebekah runs to draw water; Rachel waits while Jacob rolls the stone; Moses defends the daughters of a priest. Readers compare the scenes and learn something about each person from the difference.

So watch for the pattern and then watch for the break. When a phrase appears three times and then changes, ask why. When a scene looks like an earlier scene, ask what is different. In a text that explains so little, repetition is one of the main ways the writer speaks to you.

Section 3

Law

17.7

Commands and Cases

Main ideaBiblical law comes in two shapes: direct commands and 'if… then' cases, and each shape does a different job.

Read Exodus 20 and then Exodus 21. The sound changes. Exodus 20 says ’Thou shalt not steal.’ It is short and absolute, spoken to ’you.’ Exodus 21 says, in effect, if a man opens a pit and an ox falls in, the owner of the pit shall pay. It sets up a case and states the outcome.

Scholars have names for both shapes. A direct command with no conditions is called law. A rule that begins with a condition, ’if this happens, then this follows,’ is called law, from a word meaning case. The Ten Commandments are apodictic. Most of the chapters that follow them are casuistic.

Each shape has a job. Apodictic commands draw the outer lines of a community: do not murder, do not steal, honor your parents. Casuistic laws handle the messy middle: what if the ox was known to be dangerous, what if the fire spread to a neighbor’s grain, what if the injured person recovers? Cases fill in the space that commands leave open.

This mix is not unique to the Bible. Law codes from Mesopotamia, written centuries earlier, are almost all casuistic. Many scholars think the Bible’s use of direct second-person commands, spoken by God to a people, is one of its distinctive features. Jewish tradition counts 613 commandments in the Torah, a number given in the Talmud, and reads both shapes as binding.

17.8

Ten Words, Three Numberings

Main ideaThe Ten Commandments appear twice in the Torah, and Jews, Catholics and Protestants number them differently.

The Ten Commandments appear in Exodus 20 and again in Deuteronomy 5. The Hebrew text calls them the ’ten words,’ which is why scholars also say , from Greek for ten words. The two versions are close but not identical. Exodus says to remember the sabbath because God rested on the seventh day of creation. Deuteronomy says to keep the sabbath because God brought Israel out of slavery in Egypt.

The text does not number the commandments, so traditions count them differently. Jewish tradition treats ’I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt’ as the first word. Catholics and Lutherans join the ban on other gods with the ban on images. They then split the last command about coveting into two. Most Protestants and Orthodox Christians keep the ban on images as a separate second commandment.

None of these numberings changes the words. Each one reflects how a community reads the passage. For Jewish readers, the opening line about the exodus is the base of everything that follows. For Catholic readers, joining the first two bans keeps the focus on one God. For many Protestants, a separate command about images mattered in the arguments of the 1500s.

When you compare, be exact. Say ’the sixth commandment in the Protestant numbering’ or ’the fifth word in the Jewish count.’ A careful reader knows that a number in one tradition may point to a different line in another.

17.9

The Covenant Code and Hammurabi

Main ideaBiblical case law shares a world with older Mesopotamian codes, and the differences show what each valued.

In 1901 French archaeologists in the Persian city of Susa found a tall black stone covered in writing. It was the law code of , a king of Babylon who ruled around 1750 BCE. The stone holds about 282 laws, most of them in ’if… then’ form. It was carved roughly five centuries before the earliest date anyone gives for Moses.

Some of its cases match cases in Exodus 21 through 23, a section scholars call the . Both codes deal with an ox that gores a person. Both distinguish a first-time accident from an ox already known to be dangerous. Both hold the owner responsible for a known danger. Many scholars think the two codes share a common legal culture across the ancient Near East.

The differences are just as telling. Hammurabi’s code makes the owner of a known goring ox pay a fine in silver when the victim is a free man, and a smaller fine for a slave. Exodus 21:29 says the ox shall be stoned and its owner shall also be put to death, though the next verse allows a ransom to be set. Exodus makes no price difference by class in this case. Readers debate what that shows about how each code valued a human life.

Comparing codes does not decide which is better or which is true. It shows what each community wrote down and why it might have mattered. It also helps readers see that biblical law spoke into a real world of farms, oxen, wells and neighbors.

Section 4

Genealogy and the Reader's Questions

17.10

Lists of Names as Maps

Main ideaA genealogy is a map of a people drawn in names, and it tells readers who belongs to whom.

Many readers skip the lists of names. That is a mistake, because a is doing work. Genesis 5 traces ten generations from Adam to Noah. Genesis 10, which scholars call the Table of Nations, sorts the peoples of the known world into the families of Noah’s three sons. Genesis 11 runs from Noah’s son Shem down to Abraham. Together the lists carry the story from creation to the ancestors.

A genealogy answers the question ’who belongs to whom?’ In the ancient world, that question decided land, marriage, priesthood and kingship. The book of Ruth ends with a short list that makes Ruth, a foreigner from Moab, the great-grandmother of King David. The list is the point of the story. It says that David’s line includes an outsider.

The New Testament opens with a genealogy too. Matthew 1 traces Jesus from Abraham through David in three sets of fourteen generations. Luke 3 runs the list in the other direction, from Jesus back to Adam. The two lists differ in places, and Christian readers have offered several explanations since ancient times. Many scholars think each Gospel shaped its list to make a point about who Jesus is.

So when you meet a list of names, ask three questions. Where does it start and where does it end? Who is included that you might not expect? What does the list connect? A genealogy is a map, and every map was drawn for a reason.

17.11

Who, for Whom, When and Why

Main ideaCareful readers ask who wrote a passage, for whom, when and why, and hold the answers as tradition or as scholars' views.

Every text was written by someone, for someone, at some time, for some reason. Those four questions, who, for whom, when and why, are the base of reading a text as history. The Bible rarely answers them directly. Most books do not name a writer. So readers have answered the questions in two ways: through tradition and through scholarship.

Tradition assigns names. Jewish and Christian tradition long held that Moses wrote the Torah, that David wrote many psalms and that Matthew, Mark, Luke and John wrote the Gospels. These names carry meaning in each community. Modern scholars often reach different answers. Many think the Torah was shaped from several sources over centuries and that the Gospels were written by early Christians whose names we do not know for certain.

The right habit for this course is to hold both. Say ’tradition names Moses as the author’ and ’many scholars think the book took shape over time.’ Neither sentence claims to settle the question, and both are accurate about who says what. Section 1

Hebrew Poetry

18.1

Say It, Then Say It Again

Main ideaHebrew poetry is built on parallelism: a line says something, and the next line echoes, sharpens or turns it.

English poems often rhyme. Hebrew poems almost never do. Instead, a Hebrew poem works in pairs of lines. The first line says something. The second line says it again in different words, or pushes it further, or flips it around. Scholars call this . About a third of the Hebrew Bible is written this way, including the Psalms, Proverbs, Job and most of the prophets.

Psalm 19 begins with a clear example. The heavens declare the glory of God; the sky shows the work of God’s hands. Both lines say that the sky points to its maker. The second line is not filler. It makes the first line more concrete: glory becomes handiwork, heavens become the sky you can see. Readers hear the idea twice and understand it better the second time.

There are several kinds. When the second line repeats the idea, scholars call it synonymous parallelism. When the second line gives the opposite, as in Proverbs 15:1, where a soft answer turns away wrath but harsh words stir up anger, it is called antithetic. When the second line completes or builds on the first, it is often called synthetic. Robert Lowth, an English scholar, described these patterns in lectures in 1753.

Parallelism survives translation, which rhyme does not. That is one reason the Psalms have been sung in so many languages. When you see two lines that echo each other, you are looking at a poem. Read the second line as a comment on the first and ask what it adds.

18.2

Pictures in Words

Main ideaBiblical poets teach through images drawn from daily life, such as shepherds, trees and water.

A poem does not explain; it shows. Psalm 23 never says ’God takes care of people.’ It says the LORD is my shepherd. A reader in ancient Israel knew what a shepherd did: found grass, found water, walked ahead, carried a rod against wild animals. The single word does the work of a paragraph. That is an , a picture in words that carries an idea.

Psalm 1 compares a person who studies the Torah day and night to a tree planted by streams of water. The tree gives fruit in its season. Its leaves do not wither. The opposite person is compared to chaff, the dry husks that the wind blows away at harvest. Two pictures from farming, and the whole psalm’s argument is inside them.

When one thing is said to be another, as in ’the LORD is my shepherd,’ the figure is a . When the comparison uses ’like’ or ’as,’ as in ’like a tree planted by the water,’ it is a simile. Both ask the reader to bring what they know about the picture and apply it to the idea. Different readers bring different things, which is why poetry stays open.

Images also link the two Testaments. The Gospel of John has Jesus say ’I am the good shepherd.’ Christians read this as picking up Psalm 23 and Ezekiel 34. Jewish readers hear the shepherd image in the Psalms and the prophets as a picture of God and of a good king. Noticing the image lets you see how later writers reused it.

18.3

Poems with a Shape

Main ideaSome biblical poems follow the Hebrew alphabet letter by letter, and their shape is part of their meaning.

Some Hebrew poems have a hidden frame. Each line or section begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from aleph to tav. This is called an . The Hebrew alphabet has twenty-two letters, so a full acrostic poem has twenty-two parts. You cannot see the pattern in English, but it is there in the original.

Psalm 119 is the largest example. It has twenty-two sections of eight verses each, 176 verses in all, and every verse in a section begins with the same letter. The whole poem is about the Torah, which it calls by many names: law, word, statutes, commandments. Readers have seen the alphabet frame as a way of saying that the subject covers everything from A to Z.

The book of Lamentations, a set of poems about the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BCE, uses the same frame. Chapters 1, 2 and 4 have twenty-two verses, one per letter. Chapter 3 has sixty-six, three verses per letter. Chapter 5 has twenty-two verses but no acrostic. Some readers think the broken pattern at the end mirrors a broken city.

Proverbs 31:10 through 31, the poem about a capable woman, is also an acrostic of twenty-two verses. Psalm 145 is an acrostic too, but the standard Hebrew text has only twenty-one verses; one letter’s line is missing. A shape like this shows that these poems were planned and crafted, not spoken on the spot.

Prophecy and Wisdom

18.4

The Messenger's Words

Main ideaBiblical prophecy is a message delivered to people in the present, not a prediction of the far future.

Many people think a is someone who tells the future. In the Bible, a prophet is mainly a messenger. The prophet’s job is to deliver a message from God to a king, a city or a people. The message is about what they are doing right now. The most common opening is ’Thus saith the LORD.’ Scholars call this the . It is how a royal messenger in the ancient Near East announced the words of a king.

Listen to Amos. He spoke in the northern kingdom of Israel around 760 BCE. His message was about the present: the rich were selling the poor for a pair of sandals and cheating with false weights. He told them their worship meant nothing while this went on. Then came the famous line: let judgment roll down like water, and righteousness like a strong stream.

Prophets did speak of what was coming, but usually as a result of the present. If the city keeps crushing the poor, then it will fall. If the people turn back, then it will be spared. The future in prophecy is tied to a choice being made now. That is why the prophets sound like speeches and warnings, not like fortune-telling.

Jewish tradition reads the prophets as calling Israel back to the covenant. Christians also read some prophecies as pointing to Jesus, and readers in both traditions have argued about which passages do what. Amos 5:24 was quoted by Martin Luther King Jr. in his ’I Have a Dream’ speech in 1963, which shows that a message to the present can find a new present centuries later.

18.5

Acted-Out Messages

Main ideaProphets used forms like the oracle, the woe and the sign-act, and each form shaped how a message landed.

Prophets did not only speak. Sometimes they acted out the message. Scholars call this a . Isaiah 20 says the prophet walked barefoot and stripped for three years as a sign of what would happen to Egypt. Jeremiah 19 says he took a clay jar to the valley outside Jerusalem, smashed it in front of the elders and said the city would be broken like that. Ezekiel 4 says he built a model of Jerusalem under siege on a brick.

Sign-acts worked because they were hard to forget. A speech can be ignored. A man walking barefoot for three years cannot. Hosea’s book turns his own marriage into a sign, reading his wife’s unfaithfulness as a picture of Israel’s. Readers have found that story painful, and scholars debate how much of it is event and how much is literary figure.

The spoken forms have shapes too. An oracle is a single message, often introduced by the messenger formula. A woe begins with the word ’woe’ and lists what a group has done wrong. A lawsuit oracle puts a people on trial, calling the mountains as witnesses, as Micah 6 does. A vision report tells what the prophet saw, as in Isaiah 6 and Ezekiel 1.

Knowing the form helps you read fairly. A woe is not a curse thrown in anger; it is a set form, like a legal charge. A sign-act is not a strange stunt; it is a message in a medium people could see. The prophets used the tools of their time to make a present-tense message stick.

18.6

Short Wise Sayings

Main ideaWisdom sayings pack a lesson about daily life into two balanced lines that are easy to remember.

The book of Proverbs is a collection of short sayings about how to live. Each one is a , a compact line or pair of lines that states a truth about daily life. A soft answer turns away wrath, but harsh words stir up anger. Pride goes before a fall. The sayings are about work, speech, friends, money, anger and patience. They are the kind of thing a parent says to a child, and the book often frames them that way.

Wisdom sayings use parallelism, like the psalms. Many are antithetic, setting the wise person against the foolish one. The form makes them easy to carry in the memory. That was the point. Wisdom writing was a school for living, and in the ancient Near East it crossed borders. An Egyptian text called the Instruction of Amenemope, from around 1100 BCE, has close parallels to Proverbs 22 and 23, and many scholars think the biblical writer knew it.

Not all wisdom books agree. Proverbs is confident: do right and things will go well. Job tells of a good man who suffers anyway and asks why. Ecclesiastes says that much of life is ’vanity,’ a Hebrew word meaning breath or vapor, and that the wise and the foolish both die. Together the three books hold a conversation about whether the world makes sense.

For reading, the key is the pair of lines. Find the two halves. Ask whether they echo or clash. Then ask what situation in daily life the saying is about. A proverb is not a law or a promise. It is a general observation, and Proverbs itself says that a saying in the mouth of a fool is useless.

Gospel and Letter

18.7

An Ancient Life with a Purpose

Main ideaA gospel is an ancient kind of life story, written to tell readers who Jesus was and to move them to a response.

The New Testament opens with four books called Gospels. The word translates a Greek word meaning good news. The books are not diaries or modern biographies. Many scholars compare them to an ancient Greek and Roman form called a bios, a ’life,’ which told about a famous person to show the person’s character and to teach the reader.

A gospel has features of its own. It tells events from the life of Jesus, mostly his teaching, healing and travels, and then gives most of its space to the last week of his life. Mark, the shortest, uses about a third of its sixteen chapters on that final week. Each gospel says why it was written, or shows it. John 20:31 says its stories were written so that readers may believe. Luke 1 opens by saying the writer set out to give an orderly account for a reader named Theophilus.

So a gospel is a life with a purpose. It selects and arranges. This is why the four gospels differ. They agree on much, but each tells the story in its own order and with its own emphasis. Matthew often quotes the Hebrew Bible to show Jesus fulfilling it. Luke pays attention to the poor and to women. John has long speeches found nowhere else.

Reading a gospel as a genre means asking what the writer is doing with each scene. Why put this story here? Why repeat that phrase? Christians read the gospels as the center of their scripture. Historians read them as first-century sources about Jesus and his followers. Both kinds of readers benefit from noticing how each book is built.

18.8

Four Tellings of One Life

Main ideaMatthew, Mark and Luke share much material, John stands apart, and the differences are clues to each writer's aim.

Lay Matthew, Mark and Luke side by side and you will see the same stories in nearly the same words, often in the same order. Scholars call these three the Gospels, from a Greek word meaning seeing together. Most scholars think Mark was written first, around 70 CE, and that Matthew and Luke used Mark along with other material. Tradition ordered them differently, with Matthew first.

John is different. It has no parables of the kind found in the Synoptics, no birth story and no Last Supper bread and cup. It has long conversations, seven ’I am’ sayings and a set of signs. Many scholars date it around 90 to 100 CE. Christian tradition from the second century said John wrote last and knew the others.

The four books also have different lengths and shapes. Matthew has twenty-eight chapters and is built around five big blocks of teaching, which some readers compare to the five books of the Torah. Mark has sixteen chapters and moves quickly, using the word ’immediately’ again and again. Luke has twenty-four chapters and continues into a second volume, the book of Acts. John has twenty-one.

Reading four tellings does not require deciding which one is right. It means asking what each writer chose to include, to leave out and to put first. The same scene, such as the feeding of a crowd, can carry a different accent in each book. Christians have read the four together since the second century, and scholars study them apart to hear each voice.

18.9

How a Letter Is Built

Main ideaNew Testament letters follow the shape of ancient letters: greeting, thanks, body and farewell.

Twenty-one books of the New Testament are letters, also called . Thirteen carry Paul’s name. Letters were the everyday writing of the Roman world, and thousands of ordinary ones survive on papyrus from Egypt. They followed a set pattern, and the New Testament letters follow it too.

First comes the greeting: the sender, the receiver and a wish for their well-being. 1 Thessalonians begins: Paul, Silvanus and Timothy, to the church of the Thessalonians, grace and peace. Next comes a thanksgiving, where the writer thanks God for the readers. Then the body, which carries the letter’s business: teaching, answers to questions, correction. Last comes the farewell, with greetings to named people and a blessing.

Knowing the pattern helps you notice changes. Galatians skips the thanksgiving and goes straight to a rebuke, which readers since ancient times have taken as a sign of how upset the writer was. Romans has a long greeting because Paul had never visited that church. Paul’s letters often end with a list of names, which shows how the early churches were connected.

A letter is one side of a conversation. The readers knew things we do not: what they had asked, what had gone wrong, who the people named were. So reading a letter means reconstructing the situation from clues. Scholars call that the letter’s occasion. 1 Thessalonians, written around 50 CE, is likely the earliest book in the New Testament.

18.10

Reading a Letter Whole

Main ideaA letter should be read from start to finish, in its situation, before any single verse is lifted out.

People often quote a single verse from a letter. But the writer did not write verses; verse numbers were added in the 1500s. The writer wrote a whole letter to real people about a real situation. Reading one line alone is like hearing one sentence of a phone call. To understand it, you need the whole call.

Philemon is a good practice letter because it is only twenty-five verses long. Paul writes from prison to a man named Philemon about Onesimus, who is described as Philemon’s slave and who is now with Paul. Paul sends him back and asks Philemon to receive him no longer as a slave but as a brother. Paul offers to pay any debt. He never gives a direct order, and he says so.

Read whole, the letter shows its strategy. The greeting includes the whole house church, so the request is public. The thanksgiving praises Philemon’s love, setting up the appeal. The body makes the request with careful pressure. The farewell says Paul hopes to visit, which means he will see what happened. Readers have long debated whether Paul is asking for Onesimus to be freed, and the letter does not say plainly.

The letters have been read many ways. Christians read them as scripture and as guides for church life. Historians read them as the earliest window into the first followers of Jesus. In the 1800s, both defenders and opponents of American slavery cited Philemon, which shows how much depends on reading a letter whole and in its world.

Apocalypse

18.11

Visions in Hard Times

Main ideaAn apocalypse is a vision report written in a time of persecution to tell readers that history has a hidden end.

The word comes from a Greek word meaning unveiling or revelation. As a genre, an apocalypse is a report of visions in which a person is shown hidden things about heaven and about how history will end. Two books in the Bible are largely apocalypse: the second half of Daniel, and Revelation. Many other Jewish and Christian writings of the same kind exist outside the Bible.

Apocalypses were written in hard times. Many scholars date the visions in Daniel 7 through 12 to around 165 BCE. At that time the Greek king Antiochus IV was persecuting Jews in Judea and had set up an altar to a foreign god in the Temple. Jewish tradition places Daniel himself centuries earlier, in Babylon, and reads the book as his. Revelation, most scholars think, was written around 95 CE. Christians in the Roman province of Asia then faced pressure to honor the emperor. Its writer, John, says he was on the island of Patmos.

The genre has clear marks. A vision is shown to a seer, often by an angel who explains it. Strange creatures stand for kingdoms. Numbers carry meaning. History is shown as a sequence of empires that will be judged. The message is that the powers now in charge are not the last word.

Readers have handled apocalypse in very different ways. Some Christians read Revelation as a map of future events. Others, along with most scholars, read it first as a message to seven real churches in the first century, using pictures its readers knew. Jewish readers of Daniel have read the visions as encouragement under persecution. For a student, the first task is the same in every case: learn how the symbols worked for the first readers.

Symbols and Numbers

Main ideaApocalyptic writing uses symbols and numbers as a shared code, and readers decode them by learning what they meant then.

Revelation is full of numbers. Seven churches, seven seals, seven trumpets, seven bowls. Twelve tribes, twelve gates, twelve foundations. A crowd of 144,000, which is twelve times twelve times a thousand. In the Hebrew Bible, seven marks completeness, as in the seven days of creation, and twelve marks Israel’s tribes. The numbers are not counts. They are a system that the first readers knew.

The images work the same way. A beast with seven heads and ten horns rises from the sea in Revelation 13, echoing Daniel 7. A woman named Babylon sits on seven hills in Revelation 17. Rome was famous as the city on seven hills, and Babylon was the empire that had destroyed the first Temple. Most scholars read Babylon here as a code name for Rome, which readers under Roman rule would have caught at once.

The most famous number is 666, the number of the beast in Revelation 13:18. The text says it is the number of a man and invites the reader to work it out. Greek and Hebrew letters also served as numbers, and many scholars note that ’Nero Caesar’ written in Hebrew letters adds up to 666. A few ancient manuscripts read 616, which fits a different spelling of the same name. Other readers, ancient and modern, have proposed other answers.

Reading apocalypse well means resisting two shortcuts. One is to treat every symbol as a literal prediction of our own time. The other is to dismiss the book as nonsense. The first readers were not confused. They were people in trouble who were handed a picture of hope in a code they shared. Learning the code is the reader’s job.

Why: Hebrew poetry rarely rhymes. It works by parallelism, pairing lines so the second comments on the first.

Why: Psalm 119 follows the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet, eight verses per letter.

Why: The messenger formula marks a prophet's words as a message from God, like a royal messenger's announcement.

4. Which is a sign-act?

Why: A sign-act is a message acted out. Jeremiah 19 tells of the broken jar as a picture of the city's fall.

5. Which ancient form do many scholars compare a gospel to?

Why: The Gospels select and arrange events from Jesus's life to show his character and move readers, like an ancient bios.

6. Which part of an ancient letter does Galatians leave out?

Why: Galatians moves from greeting straight to rebuke. Readers have long seen the missing thanksgiving as a sign of alarm.

7. What situation do many scholars think lies behind the visions in Daniel 7 through 12?

Why: The visions fit the crisis under Antiochus IV. Jewish tradition places Daniel earlier, in Babylon, and reads the book as his.

Why: Twelve stands for Israel's tribes and a thousand for a great number. The figure is a symbol of completeness, not a census.

1. What is the first question a careful reader of the Bible should ask about a passage?

Why: Genre comes first because it tells the reader which questions to bring to the text.

2. Which book is mostly law rather than narrative?

Why: Leviticus is almost all law. Genesis, Ruth and Mark are narrative.

3. What did the narrator of 2 Samuel 11 do for most of the chapter about David and Bathsheba?

Why: The narrator reports and stays quiet. The single verdict comes at verse 27.

4. In Genesis 1, the phrase 'it was good' changes to 'very good' after the sixth day. What reading tool does that illustrate?

Why: Repeated phrases guide listeners, and the break in the pattern signals the chapter's climax.

5. 'If an ox gore a man and the owner was warned' is an example of what?

Why: It begins with a condition and states an outcome, which is the shape of case law.

6. Why does the Jewish count of the Ten Commandments begin differently from the Protestant count?

Why: The text gives no numbers. Jewish tradition counts the opening statement about the exodus as the first of the ten.

7. What did the Code of Hammurabi and Exodus 21 both address?

Why: Both codes rule on a goring ox and treat a warned owner as responsible. Their penalties differ.

8. What does a genealogy such as Genesis 10 or Matthew 1 do?

Why: A genealogy is a map of a people or a family, drawn to make a point about belonging.

9. Which sentence uses the habit this unit calls attributing?

Why: Attributing says whose view a statement is, without asserting or denying it.

10. Psalm 19:1 says the heavens declare God's glory, and the sky shows God's handiwork. What is this an example of?

Why: The second line echoes and sharpens the first, which is the basic pattern of Hebrew poetry.

11. In the Bible, a prophet is best described as what?

Why: Prophets like Amos delivered messages about what people were doing now and what would follow.

12. Which book is a collection of short wise sayings about daily life?

Why: Proverbs holds two-line sayings about speech, work, anger and friendship. Lamentations mourns Jerusalem.

13. What do the Synoptic Gospels have in common?

Why: Matthew, Mark and Luke can be seen together. John stands apart with its own material.

14. Which part of an ancient letter names the sender, the receiver and a wish for well-being?

Why: The greeting opens the letter, as in 1 Thessalonians 1:1. Thanks, body and farewell follow.

15. How do most scholars read the symbols in Revelation?

Why: Most scholars read Revelation first as a message to seven real churches under Roman rule, in symbols they knew.
