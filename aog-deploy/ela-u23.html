Advanced Grammar and Usage

Language

A newspaper drops one comma from a writer's thank-you note and turns her parents into a professor and a library. A hiring manager reads a cover letter that promises attention to detail and stops at the word you're. Neither reader needed a grammar book to react. Each one read a small mark or a small word as a signal about the person behind the page, and each decided, in about two seconds, how much to trust what came next.

This unit is about those signals at full strength. The first chapter takes the marks most students avoid and shows what each one does: the semicolon that joins equals, the colon that points forward, the dash that raises its voice and the parentheses that lower it, the comma that follows structure rather than breath, the hyphen that keeps a small-business owner from being a short one, and the brackets and ellipses that keep a quotation honest. You will also see how professional editors decide when two rulebooks disagree.

The second chapter steps back and asks where rules come from and what they are for. You will learn the difference between describing a language and prescribing it, meet rules that quietly died and one pronoun that the major guides accepted within three years, and practice the registers of email, applications and cover letters. You will build a ten-minute editing routine, learn which reference settles which question, and think carefully about the many Englishes of Chicago and about the plain respect of calling people what they call themselves.

The big question

When the rules of punctuation and usage disagree with each other, or change under our feet, how does a writer decide what a reader needs?

The story

The Same Sentence, Two Ways

A writer sends one acknowledgments page to two publishers, and a single comma turns her parents into a punchline.

Dana Whitfield spent four years writing a history of the freight tunnels under downtown Chicago. When the book was done, two places wanted it. A Sunday newspaper magazine would print one chapter as an excerpt. A university press would publish the whole book. She sent the same file to both, and on the last page she thanked the people who had kept her going: "I owe this book to my parents, Professor Adeyemi, and the Harold Washington Library."

The newspaper's copy editor worked in the style most American newspapers use, which drops the comma before the last item in a list. She cut it by habit, the way she had cut ten thousand others. The excerpt ran on a Sunday morning. By noon, Dana's phone was full of messages from friends who found it hilarious that her parents were apparently a professor and a public library. Without that comma, the list could be read as three items or as one item with a description attached.

The university press followed a different guide, one that requires the comma before the final item. Its editor left Dana's sentence exactly as she wrote it. Then she did something more: she wrote a note in the margin asking whether Dana might want to reorder the list anyway, since some readers would still stumble. Dana moved the library to the front of the sentence, and the problem disappeared.

When Dana called the newspaper, the editor was apologetic but not surprised. Her own guide, she admitted, told her to keep the comma whenever dropping it would confuse a reader. The rule had been applied without the judgment the rule assumed. That is the lesson behind every mark in this chapter. Punctuation has rules, and the rules differ from one guide to the next, but the purpose behind all of them is the same: a reader should never have to read a sentence twice to know what it means.

Talk about itBoth editors followed their rulebooks, and only one of them produced a sentence that meant what the writer meant. What should a rule-follower do when the rule and the meaning pull in different directions?
Section 1

Semicolons and Colons

52.1

The Semicolon Joins Equals

Main ideaA semicolon joins two complete sentences that belong together, and it separates list items that already contain commas.

Read this: "The train was late; nobody on the platform looked surprised." A period would be correct there. So would the word "and." The writer chose a because the two halves are really one thought: the lateness and the lack of surprise explain each other. Each half is an , a group of words with a subject and a verb that could stand alone as a sentence. That is the semicolon’s first and most important rule. Whatever sits on either side of it must be able to stand by itself.

The rule fails in a sentence like "The train was late; which surprised no one." The second half cannot stand alone, so it needs a comma, not a semicolon. The rule also matters with words like however, therefore, meanwhile and for example. These are , and they do not have the joining power of and or but. "The train was late, however, nobody looked surprised" is a , two sentences stuck together with only a comma. Fix it with a semicolon before however and a comma after it: "The train was late; however, nobody looked surprised."

The semicolon has a second job that has nothing to do with joining sentences. When items in a list already contain commas, a reader cannot tell where one item ends and the next begins. Semicolons become the stronger divider: "The tour stops in Springfield, Illinois; Madison, Wisconsin; and Des Moines, Iowa." Without those semicolons, the sentence looks like a list of six places instead of three.

Some writers avoid the semicolon and get along fine with periods. Others use it in every paragraph. The mark is worth keeping for the moment when a period would break a connection you want the reader to feel. Overuse makes writing feel stiff; a single well-placed semicolon can make two sentences click together like a latch.

52.2

The Colon Points Forward

Main ideaA colon announces that what follows explains or completes what came before, and the words before the colon must form a complete sentence.

Here is a colon doing its job: "She packed three things: a map, a flashlight and her grandfather’s compass." Everything before the is a complete sentence. Everything after it delivers what that sentence promised. That is the shape of nearly every correct colon. The mark points forward, like an arrow, and says: here it comes. The most common colon error is putting one after words that cannot stand alone, as in "She packed: a map, a flashlight and a compass." Since "She packed" is not a complete thought about three things, no colon belongs there.

A colon can introduce a single word or a whole explanation, not just a list. "There was one problem: the bridge was gone." The colon makes the reader wait a half second and then lands the answer. Compare it with the semicolon. "The bridge was gone; we turned back" balances two ideas of equal weight. "There was one problem: the bridge was gone" sets up and then delivers. The semicolon is a hinge. The colon is a drumroll.

Colons also have fixed jobs. They follow the greeting in a formal letter: "Dear Dr. Ruiz:" (a comma is for personal letters). They separate hours and minutes, as in 7:45, and the parts of a ratio, as in 3:1. In a research paper, a colon usually introduces a , a long quotation set off from the paragraph. Should the first word after a colon be capitalized? Style guides disagree. Some capitalize when a full sentence follows; others do not. Pick one rule and hold to it through the whole paper.

A quick test before you type a colon: cover everything after it and read what is left. If those words make a sentence on their own, the colon is legal. If they trail off, delete the colon or rewrite. "The reasons are: cost and time" fails the test. "There are two reasons: cost and time" passes it.

Interrupters

52.3

Dashes for Interruption and Emphasis

Main ideaThe em dash interrupts a sentence or throws weight on what follows it; the en dash joins ranges; the hyphen joins words.

"The bus—the one I had waited forty minutes for—drove past without slowing." The pair of long dashes cuts into the sentence, drops in a bitter detail, and lets the sentence continue. That mark is the , named because it is about as wide as a capital M. Used in a pair, em dashes set off an . Used alone near the end of a sentence, one em dash throws all the weight onto whatever comes after it: "He had one rule—never be late."

Three marks on the keyboard look alike, and editors care about the difference. The (-) is the shortest and joins words: well-known, twenty-one. The (–), about as wide as a letter N, joins ranges and pairs: pages 12–18, the years 1871–1893, the Chicago–Detroit train. The em dash (—) is the longest and interrupts. In most word processors, typing two hyphens between words turns into an em dash. Book publishers usually close the em dash up tight with no spaces; newspapers usually put a space on each side. Either is acceptable if you do it the same way every time.

Dashes are loud. A comma pair around an interruption is quiet; a pair of parentheses whispers; a pair of dashes raises its voice. That makes the dash powerful and dangerous. A paragraph with a dash in every sentence sounds breathless, as if the writer cannot finish a thought. Save the dash for the interruption that matters or the ending that deserves a spotlight. Emily Dickinson built poems out of dashes, and her lines feel like someone catching her breath; in an essay, that effect wears out fast.

52.4

Parentheses Lower the Voice

Main ideaParentheses hold an aside the sentence can live without, and the sentence must still read correctly with the parentheses and their contents removed.

"The cafeteria (which had been closed since March) reopened on Monday." Take out the and everything inside them, and the sentence still stands: "The cafeteria reopened on Monday." That is the test. Whatever you put inside parentheses is an , a remark the reader could skip. If removing it breaks the sentence, the words were not an aside and do not belong in parentheses.

The end punctuation follows one rule. If the whole sentence is inside the parentheses, the period goes inside too: "We finished early. (No one complained.)" If the parenthetical sits inside a larger sentence, the period goes outside: "We finished early (no one complained)." Never put a comma before an opening parenthesis. If the sentence needs a comma at that point, it goes after the closing one: "After the vote (which took an hour), the meeting ended."

Academic writing uses parentheses for jobs that are not really asides: a page citation (Smith 42), a date, or the short form of a long name the first time it appears, as in "the Chicago Transit Authority (CTA)." After that first time, CTA alone is enough. These uses are fixed by the style guide, not chosen for tone.

Now compare the three ways to interrupt a sentence. "The cafeteria, which had been closed since March, reopened on Monday" is neutral; the commas treat the fact as ordinary. "The cafeteria—which had been closed since March—reopened on Monday" is emphatic; the dashes make the closure a big deal. "The cafeteria (which had been closed since March) reopened on Monday" is quiet; the parentheses tell the reader this is background. Same words, three volumes.

52.5

Brackets Inside Quotations

Main ideaSquare brackets mark words a writer has changed or added inside someone else's words, so the reader always knows which words belong to the source.

Suppose a source says, "She refused the offer twice." You want to quote it, but your reader will not know who "she" is. You may not silently change the quotation to "Jane Addams refused the offer twice," because those are no longer the source’s exact words. Instead you write: "[Jane Addams] refused the offer twice." The square are a promise. Everything inside them is yours; everything outside them is the source’s. A quotation is a kind of testimony, and brackets keep the testimony honest.

Brackets have several standard uses. They hold a word that clarifies: "The council voted against it [the stadium plan]." They hold , a Latin word meaning "thus," placed after an error in the original to show that the mistake is the source’s, not yours: "the state capitol in Springfeild [sic]." They hold a note that you changed the look of the words: "[emphasis added]" after a word you italicized. And when one parenthesis must go inside another, most style guides switch the inner pair to brackets.

One small case divides the guides. If a quotation begins mid-sentence and you need a capital letter, some styles write "[T]he" to show the change, while others let you change the first letter without comment. Legal writing uses the brackets; most book and magazine editing does not. Your instructor’s guide decides. What no guide permits is using brackets to make a source say something it did not say. "The plan is [not] affordable" would be a lie in brackets.

A good habit: after you bracket, read the sentence as your reader will, and then read the original again. The bracketed version should change nothing except clarity. If the source’s meaning has shifted even slightly, drop the brackets and paraphrase in your own sentence with a citation instead.

Commas and Hyphens

52.6

Commas in Complex Sentences

Main ideaIn long sentences, commas mark structure: after an introductory clause, around a nonrestrictive clause, and before a conjunction that joins two complete clauses.

Try reading this in one breath: "When the ice on the lake finally broke the ferry ran again and the island’s eleven families came ashore for the first time since January." Somewhere around "broke the ferry" a reader stumbles, because for a moment the ice seems to be breaking the ferry. Commas exist to prevent that stumble. The old advice to "put a comma where you breathe" is unreliable; readers breathe in different places. Commas follow the structure of the sentence, and the structure can be learned.

First rule: a that opens a sentence gets a comma after it. "When the ice finally broke, the ferry ran again." Reverse the order and the comma usually disappears: "The ferry ran again when the ice finally broke." Second rule: a clause, one that adds information without narrowing the meaning, is set off by commas; a clause, which narrows the meaning, is not. "The students who finished early left" means only the early finishers left. "The students, who finished early, left" means all of them left, and by the way they were early. Many American editors reserve "that" for restrictive clauses and "which" for nonrestrictive ones, though careful writers have long used "which" both ways.

Third rule: when a (and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor) joins two independent clauses, put a comma before it: "The ferry ran again, and the families came ashore." When the conjunction joins only two verbs with one subject, leave the comma out: "She read the report and signed it." Two other commas cause trouble by showing up where nothing needs them. Never split a subject from its verb ("The ferry that runs in winter, is small" is wrong), and never put a comma right after a conjunction that opens a sentence.

Here is the same scene punctuated by structure. When the ice on the lake finally broke, the ferry ran again, and the island’s eleven families came ashore for the first time since January. The mail, which had piled up in a canvas sack at the post office, filled the back of the truck. Nobody complained about the wait. The ferry captain, a woman named Ruth who had run the route for thirty years, said that the ice had been thicker than any she remembered, and she had records to prove it. By noon the island was empty again.

52.7

The Serial Comma Debate

Main ideaThe serial comma before the last item of a list is required by some style guides and dropped by others, but every guide agrees that a list must not be misreadable.

"We invited the mayors, a poet and a juggler." How many groups is that? Three, probably. But the sentence can also mean two mayors who happen to be a poet and a juggler. The comma that would settle it, placed before the "and" in a list of three or more, is the , also called the Oxford comma. "We invited the mayors, a poet, and a juggler" can only mean three groups. Whether that comma is required is the most argued-about question in American punctuation.

The dispute is between , the rulebooks that publishers adopt. The Chicago Manual of Style, used by most book publishers, requires the serial comma, and so do the MLA and APA guides that most schools follow. The Associated Press Stylebook, used by most newspapers, leaves it out, partly to save space, and tells editors to add it only when a list would otherwise confuse. Both camps claim clarity. The Declaration of Independence lists "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" with no serial comma, and no one has ever misread it.

The stakes are not always small. In 2017 a federal appeals court decided a case brought by delivery drivers in Maine over overtime pay. The state law listed the kinds of work that did not earn overtime, ending with "packing for shipment or distribution." Without a comma before "or," the court found the phrase could mean one activity, packing, rather than two, and it ruled for the drivers. Reports at the time put the settlement at about five million dollars. One missing comma, read closely, cost a company a fortune.

So what should you do? Use whatever guide your teacher or employer names, and use it every time. Then read each list for , the possibility of two meanings. If a list could be misread with or without the comma, punctuation alone will not save it. Rewrite: move the item that looks like an , a renaming phrase, to the front or end of the list. "We invited a juggler, a poet and the mayors" is safe under either rule.

52.8

Hyphens and Compound Modifiers

Main ideaHyphenate two or more words that act as one adjective before a noun, but not after the noun and not after an adverb ending in -ly.

"A small business owner" could be a short person who owns a business. "A small-business owner" is someone who owns a small business. The hyphen turns "small business" into one unit that describes "owner." Two or more words working together as a single adjective before a noun form a , and the hyphen is what tells the reader to read them together: a well-known author, a twelve-year-old cousin, a first-place finish, an out-of-state license.

Two limits keep the rule from spreading everywhere. When the same words come after the noun, they usually go back to being separate: "The author is well known." "My cousin is twelve years old." And when the first word is an adverb ending in -ly, no hyphen is needed, because the -ly already announces that the word modifies the next one: a highly paid consultant, a badly damaged roof. Numbers from twenty-one to ninety-nine are hyphenated when spelled out, and fractions used as adjectives usually are too: a two-thirds majority.

A is a word part attached to the front of a word, and most prefixes attach with no hyphen at all: nonprofit, preheat, coworker, misread. The hyphen returns in three situations. Before a capital letter: pre-Columbian, un-American, mid-July. When the same word without the hyphen already means something else: re-sign a contract is not resign from a job, and re-cover a chair is not recover from an illness. And sometimes when two identical vowels collide, as in anti-inflammatory, though many guides now close up re-enter as reenter. Dictionaries change on these; "e-mail" became "email" in most guides within a decade.

One more tool: the . When two compounds share a final word, write the word once and leave the hyphens hanging: "first- and second-year students," "pre- and post-test scores." It looks odd the first time and saves the reader a repeated word every time after.

Punctuation for Scholars

52.9

Quotations and Ellipses

Main ideaIn scholarly writing, quotation marks, ellipses and citations work together so a reader knows exactly which words are the source's and exactly what was cut.

American style has a fixed rule that surprises many students: periods and commas go inside the closing quotation mark, even when they were not part of the quotation. "The plan failed," she said. He called it "a disaster." Colons and semicolons go outside: He called it "a disaster"; nobody argued. Question marks and exclamation points go inside only if they belong to the quoted words. The one exception matters in research papers: when a parenthetical citation follows a quotation, the period moves to after the citation: He called it "a disaster" (Ortiz 42).

Sometimes a quotation is too long, and you want only part of it. An , three spaced periods, marks the words you removed from the middle: "The committee . . . voted to close the branch library." If the cut comes at the end of a sentence, most guides add the sentence’s period first, making four dots. You usually do not need an ellipsis at the start of a quotation; the reader assumes the source said things before. What you may never do is cut words in a way that changes the meaning. Turning "the plan is anything but affordable" into "the plan is . . . affordable" is not editing. It is lying.

Length changes the format. A prose quotation that runs more than four typed lines in MLA style, or roughly a hundred words in Chicago style, becomes a block quotation: indented from the left margin, with no quotation marks around it, introduced by a sentence ending in a colon. Inside any quotation, a quotation of someone else’s words takes : "The witness said, ’I saw nothing,’ and sat down."

Where Rules Come From

53.1

Prescriptive and Descriptive

Main ideaPrescriptive rules tell writers what they should do; descriptive study records what speakers and writers actually do; a skilled writer uses both.

Two people look at the sentence "Me and him went to the store." The first says, "That is wrong; it should be He and I went." The second says, "Millions of English speakers say it that way every day, so it is part of English." Both are right about something. The first is speaking , stating a rule about what writers should do. The second is speaking , reporting what speakers actually do. Grammar books are mostly prescriptive. Linguists, the scientists who study language, are mostly descriptive. The two groups have argued for two hundred years, and the argument is worth understanding because you will live inside it.

The descriptive view rests on a fact: language changes, and no rulebook has ever stopped it. English once had the word thou, and every dictionary lost it. Rules that were once fiercely enforced, such as never using the word contact as a verb, are now forgotten. A descriptivist notices that "Me and him went" follows a perfectly regular pattern that many speakers share, and that calling it wrong is really calling it nonstandard, which means outside the variety of English that schools and employers expect. That variety, , is not more logical than the others. It is the one with power behind it.

The prescriptive view rests on a different fact: readers judge. Priya did not care whether you’re for your was a natural variation. She cared that the letter was for a job. When you write for school, for work, for a grant or for a court, your reader expects Standard English, and a rule that says "use it here" is not snobbery but advice about the room you are in. The best writers hold both views at once. They know the rules, they know which rules are really just habits, and they know that following the expected rules is a courtesy to the reader that costs the writer nothing.

A question, then, is rarely "is this correct?" It is "is this what this reader, in this setting, expects?" That reframing will get you through most of the disputes in this chapter. It also tells you what to do when a rule seems silly: follow it where a reader expects it, drop it where no one does, and be able to say why.

53.2

Rules That Changed

Main ideaSeveral famous rules, including the bans on split infinitives, sentence-ending prepositions and singular they, were never solid, and today's major guides have loosened or dropped them.

"She decided to quietly leave." For more than a century, teachers marked that sentence wrong because an adverb sits between to and leave, splitting the . The rule appeared in the 1800s, when some grammarians modeled English on Latin, where an infinitive is one word and cannot be split. English is not Latin. Writers had been splitting infinitives since the 1300s, and every major American style guide now says the split is fine when it reads more naturally. "She decided quietly to leave" is stiff and slightly unclear; the split version is better.

The ban on ending a sentence with a has the same history and the same fate. "Where are you from?" is normal English; "From where are you?" is nobody’s speech. A related rule said never to begin a sentence with and or but. Good writers have always done it, and doing it is often the clearest way to mark a turn. None of these was ever a rule of English grammar. Each was a rule of taste that some people mistook for a law.

The newest change is . English has no common pronoun for one person of unknown or unstated gender, and for centuries writers filled the gap with they: "Someone left their umbrella." Shakespeare did it, and so did Jane Austen. Grammarians of the 1800s pushed "he" as the generic pronoun, and schools taught it until the late twentieth century. The guides have since reversed. The Associated Press Stylebook permitted limited singular they in 2017, the Chicago Manual of Style’s seventeenth edition (2017) accepted it in speech and informal writing, and the APA manual’s seventh edition (2019) endorsed it in all writing. Merriam-Webster named "they" its Word of the Year for 2019. Singular they also serves people who use it as their personal pronoun, and using a person’s stated pronoun is now standard courtesy in most workplaces.

The lesson is not that rules do not matter. It is that a rule has a history, and knowing the history lets you follow it or set it aside on purpose. If you split an infinitive in a college paper, no careful reader will blink. If you write "Each student must bring their own laptop," the APA will nod. But an older reader, or a very formal setting, may still expect the old forms, and you can choose to give them what they expect. That choice, made knowingly, is what fluency looks like.

Main ideaSome usage questions, such as less versus fewer, who versus whom, and literally as an intensifier, remain genuinely contested, and the writer's best move is to know the dispute and choose for the reader.

Dictionaries note that less with countable nouns has been in use for a thousand years, and "10 items or less" bothers almost no shopper. Still, in a college paper or a job application, "fewer" is the safe choice, because the reader who winces might be the one grading. This is the pattern of a : both forms are common, one is safer with careful readers.

Who and whom follow the same pattern. Whom is the object form ("To whom did you speak?"), and it is fading from speech. Many editors now accept who in most positions except right after a preposition. Hopefully as a sentence adverb ("Hopefully, the bus is on time") was fought over for decades and is now accepted by nearly every guide. Data was once treated only as a plural ("the data are"), and many scientific journals still require it, while most general writing treats it as a mass noun ("the data is"). Literally has come to mean "figuratively" in casual speech ("I literally died"), and dictionaries record that sense, but in writing it still makes careful readers laugh at you, not with you.

Then there are the pairs that are not contested at all, only confused: affect (usually a verb) and effect (usually a noun); lie (to recline) and lay (to place); imply (the speaker hints) and infer (the listener concludes); , which dictionaries list as nonstandard and which no guide accepts in formal writing. 53.4

Matching the Room

Main ideaRegister is the level of formality a writer chooses to fit the reader and the occasion, and shifting it on purpose is a skill, not a betrayal of your voice.

Here is the same news three ways. To a friend: "got the job!!! start monday." To a teacher who wrote a recommendation: "I wanted to let you know I got the internship. Nothing in the first version is a mistake. It is the right for a friend, and the third version would be strange sent to one. Register is the level of formality and the set of word choices a writer picks to fit the reader and the occasion.

Registers differ in predictable ways. writing uses contractions, sentence fragments, slang, first names and whatever spelling the keyboard gives. writing spells everything out, uses complete sentences, avoids slang, uses titles and last names until invited otherwise, and follows Standard English closely. In between sits most of college and work writing: complete sentences and standard usage, but a plain, human tone, with contractions allowed in many settings. The mistake students make most is not writing too casually. It is swinging so far toward formal that the writing goes stiff: "I am reaching out to inquire as to whether" instead of "I am writing to ask whether."

Most people already switch registers all day, between a coach, a grandparent and a group chat, without thinking about it. 53.7

Errors That Cost Credibility

Main ideaA short list of errors, mostly involving apostrophes, homophones and agreement, does most of the damage to a writer's credibility, and each one can be checked with a simple test.

Read this paragraph from a company’s proposal, written for this lesson with its errors left in. "Our team is proud of it’s record this year. There were three project’s finished ahead of schedule, and the client’s said they were more satisfied then last year. Each of the managers have signed off. We hope you will choose us for you’re next project, its the right move." Four sentences, seven errors. A reader who catches even two of them stops trusting the numbers in the rest of the proposal. That is what means: the reader’s willingness to believe you, and it is spent one small error at a time.

Four of the seven are apostrophe errors, and apostrophes cause more trouble than any other mark. The does two jobs: it shows possession (the client’s office) and it marks missing letters in a contraction (it’s for it is). It never makes a plural. "Three projects" and "the clients" take no apostrophe. The test for its and it’s is to say "it is" out loud in the sentence: "proud of it is record" fails, so the word is its. The same test works for you’re (you are), they’re (they are) and who’s (who is).

The come next: then and than, there, their and they’re, to, too and two, affect and effect, loose and lose. Than compares (more satisfied than last year); then tells time. "Could of" is not English; the phrase is could have, which sounds like could’ve in speech. errors hide when a phrase comes between the subject and the verb: "Each of the managers have signed" should be "has," because the subject is each, not managers. Cover the phrase between subject and verb and read what is left.

Here is the same paragraph repaired. "Our team is proud of its record this year. Three projects finished ahead of schedule, and the clients said they were more satisfied than last year. Each of the managers has signed off. We hope you will choose us for your next project; it’s the right move." Nothing about the content changed. Everything about the writer changed.

53.8

The Ten-Minute Edit

Main ideaWhen time is short, edit in passes with a fixed order: meaning first, then the personal error list, then the mechanical checks, then a final read aloud.

The essay is due at midnight and it is 11:40. Or the form must be submitted before the office closes. Editing under pressure is a skill of its own, and it is mostly a matter of order. Most people, given ten minutes, read from the top and fix whatever they happen to notice. That catches the errors in the first paragraph and almost none in the last. A better plan is to make several fast , each one looking for one thing, so your eye does not have to hold everything at once.

Pass one, three minutes: meaning. Read only the first sentence of every paragraph. Does the piece still make the argument you meant, in the order you meant? Fix any paragraph whose first sentence does not match its job. Pass two, three minutes: your own . Every writer has three or four errors they make over and over; yours might be its/it’s, run-on sentences and dropped words. Keep the list in your notes and search for those errors only. A word processor’s find function can hunt for "it’s" and " then " in seconds.

Pass three, two minutes: the checks that do not need judgment. Names spelled the same everywhere. Titles marked consistently. Every quotation with a citation. Every citation in the works cited. Numbers that match between the text and any table. The spell-checker run once, with each flag actually read, since it will not catch a correct word in the wrong place. Pass four, two minutes: read the last two paragraphs aloud, slowly, because that is where tired writers leave the most mistakes. Then stop.

Under real pressure, the last skill is knowing what to skip. A sentence that is clumsy but clear can stay. A sentence whose meaning a reader could miss cannot. When you must choose between polishing a phrase and checking whether your citation page matches your quotations, check the citations; the are higher. Editing is triage. Fix what would cost you the most, in the order it would cost you, and let the rest wait for a draft you have time for.

53.9

Settling a Usage Question

Main ideaA usage question is settled by consulting the right reference in the right order: the dictionary for words, the style guide for format, a usage guide for disputes, and real examples for what writers actually do.

You are writing a lab report and you cannot remember whether it is "comprised of" or "composed of," whether "data" takes "is" or "are," and whether the abbreviation for milliliters takes a period. Three questions, three different tools. Guessing is the fourth option, and it is the one most students take. Learning which answers which kind of question takes an hour and pays off for life.

For the meaning, spelling and standard forms of a word, the tool is a , and for American writers the one most publishers use is Merriam-Webster, which is free online. Its entries also carry usage notes on disputed words; look up "comprise" and you will find the whole argument summarized in a paragraph. For format questions, such as how to abbreviate units, how to cite or when to spell out numbers, the tool is your style guide: MLA for most English classes, APA for psychology and the sciences, Chicago for books, AP for journalism. Most school libraries have them, and the guides’ own websites answer common questions for free.

For a genuine dispute, when the dictionary says "some object" and you need to know how much they object, the tool is a , a book organized by the disputed words. Garner’s Modern English Usage and Merriam-Webster’s Dictionary of English Usage are the two most respected American ones, and they often disagree with each other, which is itself informative. The last tool is real evidence: a , a large searchable collection of actual published writing. The Corpus of Contemporary American English, built by a linguist at Brigham Young University and free online, lets you see how often "the data is" appears in academic writing versus magazines. That is the descriptive view made practical.

The order matters. Dictionary, then style guide, then usage guide, then corpus. Stop as soon as you have an answer that satisfies your reader; most questions end at the first or second step. And write down what you decided in your style sheet so that you never look it up twice for the same paper. A writer who knows where to look is never stuck for more than two minutes.

53.10

Language Variety

Main ideaEvery variety of English, including African American English and the Englishes of bilingual communities, has consistent grammar; Standard English is one variety among many, expected in certain settings.

Chicago speaks in many Englishes. A grandmother on the South Side may say "She be working late," using a form that African American English uses to mark a habit, something that happens regularly, which Standard English cannot say in a single verb. A student in Pilsen may say "I have twelve years" the first month after arriving from Mexico, carrying the Spanish way of stating age into English. A teenager on the Northwest Side says "gym shoes" where a New Yorker says "sneakers." Each of these is a or variety of English: a full system with its own rules, sounds and words, shared by a community.

Linguists established decades ago that nonstandard varieties are not broken Standard English. , sometimes called African American Vernacular English, has consistent grammar. "She be working" and "She working" mean different things, habitual and right now, and a speaker never confuses them. That grammar has been studied in universities since the 1960s and is the subject of hundreds of books. Calling a variety "bad English" is a social judgment, not a linguistic one, in exactly the way that calling one accent "no accent" is. Everyone has an accent. Everyone speaks a dialect.

None of this makes Standard English unnecessary. It is the variety of the classroom, the courtroom, the cover letter and most published writing, and a student who cannot produce it on demand is shut out of rooms they deserve to enter. The point is what kind of thing it is. Standard English is a register and a variety, not the language itself, and learning it is , not correction. A student who speaks African American English at home and writes Standard English in a lab report is doing what a bilingual person does. That is more skill, not less.

For the writer, two practical rules follow. First, in a setting that expects Standard English, use it, and use it well; the reader is not wrong to expect it. Second, never treat another person’s variety as a sign of intelligence or effort. In dialogue, in a quotation or in a profile, render a person’s speech accurately and respectfully, and do not "correct" a quotation into a dialect the speaker did not use. A writer who understands variety is harder to fool and kinder on the page.

53.11

Respect on the Page

Main ideaRespectful usage means calling people and groups what they call themselves, getting names and titles right, and choosing words that describe rather than judge.

A reporter spells a source’s name wrong, and the source never trusts the newspaper again. A form lists a student as "Jonathan" when he has gone by "Jon" his whole life. A letter opens "Dear Sirs" to an office run by three women. None of these is a grammar error, yet each one costs the writer more credibility than a comma splice, because each says the writer did not look. Respect in usage starts with : their names, spelled as they spell them; their titles, as they hold them; their pronouns, as they state them.

Names for groups follow the same principle. Use the term a group uses for itself, and know that these terms change. Most American style guides now capitalize Black when referring to people; they are divided on White. Native American, American Indian and Indigenous are all in use, and the most respectful choice is often the specific nation, such as Potawatomi, whose people lived where Chicago now stands. Latino, Latina, Hispanic and the newer Latinx are used by different people for different reasons; the AP Stylebook notes that Latinx is not widely used by the people it describes. When in doubt, ask, or follow the usage of the community’s own institutions.

("a student with a disability") was the standard for decades; some communities, including many autistic and Deaf people, prefer identity-first language ("an autistic student"). Neither is wrong. The respectful move is to use what the person or community prefers, and, when writing about individuals, to mention a disability, a religion or an origin only when it is relevant to the point. That last rule, , does most of the work. A sentence that mentions a person’s background when it has nothing to do with the story tells the reader more about the writer than about the subject.

Finally, the , using he and man to mean everyone, was standard until the 1970s and is now avoided by every major guide. Write "a nurse should wash her hands" only if the nurse is a specific woman; otherwise recast in the plural ("nurses should wash their hands"), use singular they or repeat the noun. None of this is about fear of offending. It is the same discipline as every other lesson in this unit: knowing who the reader is, and choosing the words that let the reader see the subject clearly instead of seeing the writer’s assumptions.
