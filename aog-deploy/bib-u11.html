The History Books and the Ancient Near East

Former Prophets and Writings

The books from Joshua to 2 Kings tell one long story: a people enters a land, lives under judges, asks for kings, splits into two kingdoms, and loses both to empires. The books that follow, Ezra, Nehemiah, Chronicles, Esther and Daniel, tell what came next: exile in Babylon, return under Persia, a smaller Temple, and life under Greek and then Roman rule. This unit reads that story in the order the Bible tells it.

It also puts the Bible next to things dug out of the ground. A stone at Tel Dan names the House of David. A Moabite king tells his side of a war. Sennacherib's clay prism, with a copy in Chicago, gives Assyria's account of the attack on Jerusalem. Cyrus's cylinder shows the policy that Ezra says sent the exiles home. Each of these lets you compare two records of one event and ask what each writer wanted readers to believe.

By the end you will be able to place the main events from about 1200 BCE to the first century CE in order, explain how Jews and Christians read key passages, and use a stone or a tablet alongside a chapter of the Bible the way a historian does: not to prove or disprove, but to understand.

The story

A Broken Stone at Tel Dan

In the summer of 1993, a surveyor at a dig in northern Israel noticed letters on a stone in a wall.

Tel Dan sits at the far north of Israel, where springs feed the Jordan River. In July 1993, a team led by the archaeologist Avraham Biran was clearing an old city gate there. A member of the team, Gila Cook, saw the afternoon light catch something odd. A flat basalt stone in a wall had lines cut into it. The lines were letters.

The stone was a piece of a stele, a stone slab that a king sets up to brag about a victory. The words were in Aramaic, the language of Israel's neighbors to the north. The king who wrote it was not from Israel. He was telling how he had beaten two enemy kings. Two more pieces turned up the next year.

One line stopped the scholars cold. It named the enemy kingdoms. One was Israel. The other was written as a single word, bytdwd, which most scholars read as "House of David." Until that summer, David's name had never been found on anything outside the Bible. Here it was, cut into stone by an enemy about a hundred and fifty years after the Bible says David lived.

The stone did not prove the Bible's stories about David. It did not say who David was or what he did. It showed something narrower and still important: a kingdom in the south was known to its neighbors, in the 800s BCE, as the house of a founder named David. Some scholars questioned the reading at first. Most now accept it.

This chapter walks through the Bible's own story from Joshua to the fall of Jerusalem. Along the way it stops at three stones and one dig. Each one lets you hold the Bible's account next to a record made by someone else, and ask what fits, what does not, and what neither one says.

Into the Land

21.1

Joshua and the Walls of Jericho

Main ideaThe book of Joshua tells how Israel entered Canaan, took Jericho, and divided the land among twelve tribes.

The book of Joshua opens where Deuteronomy ends. Moses has died. The text says God tells Joshua, his helper, to lead the people across the Jordan River into Canaan. In the Jewish Bible, Joshua is the first of the , the books that tell Israel’s story in the land. Christians count it as the first of the historical books of the Old Testament.

The most famous scene is Jericho (Joshua 6). The story says the people march around the walled city once a day for six days. Priests carry the ark and blow rams’ horns. On the seventh day they march seven times, the horns sound, the people shout, and the wall falls flat. Only Rahab, a woman of Jericho who hid Israel’s spies, is saved with her family.

The text then describes a hard practice. Jericho and other cities are put under the , a rule that the city and its people are destroyed as an offering. Readers have wrestled with these chapters for centuries. Jewish and Christian teachers have read them as history, as a warning about idol worship, or as a story shaped to teach later readers. Many scholars note that the book itself says the land was not fully taken (Joshua 13:1).

The second half of Joshua is mostly a map in words. The land is divided by lot among the twelve , the large family groups named for Jacob’s sons. The book ends at Shechem, where Joshua asks the people to choose whom they will serve, and they renew the covenant.

21.2

What the Ground Says

Main ideaArchaeology gives a picture of early Israel that partly matches Joshua and partly does not, and scholars explain the gaps in different ways.

The word means the study of the past through what people left in the ground. That means walls, pots, bones, and writing. It cannot tell us what people believed or who was right. It can tell us when a city was lived in and when it burned. For the time of Joshua and the judges, the and early Iron Age, the ground gives a mixed answer.

At Jericho, an English dig in the 1930s found fallen walls and said they were Joshua’s. Kathleen Kenyon dug again in the 1950s. She dated those walls many centuries earlier. In her view, Jericho was small or empty around 1200 BCE, the time many readers place Joshua. Hazor is different. Its upper city burned around 1230 BCE, which fits Joshua 11:11. Other cities in the list show no sign of a burning at that time.

Two finds matter most. The is an Egyptian stone from about 1208 BCE. It names "Israel" as a people in Canaan, the oldest mention outside the Bible. And surveys of the hill country show that hundreds of small farming villages appeared after 1200 BCE where few had been before. These villages had simple houses, no temples, and almost no pig bones.

Scholars read the evidence in different ways. Some hold that a quick conquest happened much as Joshua tells it. Others think tribes moved in slowly. Others think many early Israelites were Canaanites who left the cities for the hills. Many today combine these ideas. The Bible’s own books leave room for this: Joshua describes a sweeping victory, while Judges 1 lists many cities the tribes did not take.

21.3

Judges in a Crisis

Main ideaJudges tells a repeating story of trouble and rescue, with leaders like Deborah, Gideon and Samson, and ends by saying there was no king.

The book of Judges covers the years between Joshua and the first kings. A here is not a person in a courtroom. A judge is a leader who rises up in a crisis, usually to fight off an enemy. The book has a pattern that repeats like a wheel. The people turn from God, an enemy oppresses them, they cry out, God raises a judge, and the land rests. Then it starts again (Judges 2:11-19). Scholars call this the of Judges.

Deborah is a prophet who judges under a palm tree (Judges 4-5). She sends the general Barak against Sisera, whose army has iron chariots. The text says a storm and flood defeat the chariots, and a woman named Jael kills Sisera in her tent. Judges 5 retells the battle as a song. Many scholars think this poem is among the oldest writing in the Bible.

Gideon hides from the Midianites until a messenger calls him a mighty warrior (Judges 6-8). The story says God cuts his army from thousands to three hundred men. They win at night with torches, jars and horns. Samson is the strongest and the strangest judge (Judges 13-16). He is set apart from birth with uncut hair. He fights the Philistines alone, is betrayed by Delilah, and dies pulling down a temple.

The judges get worse as the book goes on. The last chapters tell of civil war. The book’s final verse gives its verdict: in those days there was no king, and everyone did what seemed right to them. Readers disagree about that line. Some hear it asking for a king. Others hear it asking for people to keep the covenant. Either way, it sets up the books of Samuel.

The First Kings

21.4

The People Ask for a King

Main ideaIn 1 Samuel the elders ask Samuel for a king, and the text gives both a warning against kings and the start of kingship.

The books of Samuel begin with a woman who cannot have children. Hannah prays at the shrine at Shiloh, and the story says her son Samuel is born and given to serve there (1 Samuel 1-3). Samuel grows up to be a , a person who speaks a message from God, and the last of the judges. Meanwhile the Philistines press in from the coast. They even capture the ark, the gold-covered chest that held the tablets of the law (1 Samuel 4).

When Samuel is old, the elders come to him with a demand (1 Samuel 8). They want a king like the other nations. Samuel is not pleased. The text says God tells him the people have rejected God as their king, but to give them what they ask. Samuel warns them what a king will do. He will take their sons for his army, their daughters for his kitchens, and a tenth of their crops. The people insist.

Samuel then finds Saul, a tall young man from the tribe of Benjamin. He pours oil on his head to him, that is, to mark him as chosen (1 Samuel 10). The Hebrew word for an anointed one is mashiach, which gives English the word messiah. Saul wins a battle, and the people confirm him as king.

Readers have long noticed that 1 Samuel speaks with two voices. Some chapters treat the king as a gift from God. Others treat kingship as a mistake. Jewish and Christian teachers have read both as true at once: a king can protect the people and also fail them. Many scholars think the two voices come from different sources that an editor joined.

21.5

Saul, David and One Kingdom

Main ideaThe books of Samuel tell how kingship passed from Saul to David, who made Jerusalem his capital and received a promise about his house.

Saul’s reign goes wrong in the text. Twice he disobeys Samuel’s instructions, and Samuel tells him God has rejected him (1 Samuel 13 and 15). Samuel then secretly anoints a shepherd boy from Bethlehem named David. David enters Saul’s court as a harp player, kills the giant Goliath, and becomes close friends with Saul’s son Jonathan. Saul grows jealous and tries to kill him. David flees and lives as an outlaw. Saul dies fighting the Philistines on Mount Gilboa (1 Samuel 31).

Second Samuel tells of David’s rule, which the text places around 1000 BCE. He is king of Judah first, then of all the tribes. Historians call this the . David captures Jerusalem from the Jebusites and makes it his capital (2 Samuel 5). He brings the ark there. He does not build a temple, but in 2 Samuel 7 the prophet Nathan brings him a promise: his house, meaning his family line, will last.

That promise is read in more than one way. Jewish tradition sees in it the hope for a future king from David’s line, the , who will rule in a time of peace. Christians believe Jesus, whom the Gospels call the son of David, is that king. Historians read 2 Samuel 7 as the founding charter of the Davidic dynasty that ruled Judah for four centuries.

The text does not hide David’s faults. He takes Bathsheba, another man’s wife, and has her husband Uriah killed in battle (2 Samuel 11). Nathan confronts him with a story about a rich man who steals a poor man’s one lamb. David condemns the rich man and then hears the words, You are the man. His later years bring a revolt by his son Absalom. The Bible’s picture of its greatest king is a mixed one.

21.6

Solomon and the Temple

Main ideaFirst Kings presents Solomon as a wise builder who raised the Temple in Jerusalem, and also as a king whose burdens and shrines led to trouble.

David’s son Solomon takes the throne after a struggle among his brothers. Early in his reign the text says God offers him anything, and Solomon asks for wisdom to judge the people (1 Kings 3). The story of two women who each claim the same baby shows that wisdom in action. Solomon offers to cut the child in half, and the true mother gives it up to save it. Later tradition tied the book of Proverbs to his name.

Solomon’s great work is the (1 Kings 6-8). The text says it took seven years to build, with cedar from Lebanon sent by King Hiram of Tyre. It had a courtyard, a main hall, and an inner room called the , where the ark was placed. Many scholars date its completion to the 950s BCE. No part of it has been excavated, because the site in Jerusalem is holy to Muslims and Jews today and is not dug.

The text also records the price. Solomon used forced labor gangs and heavy taxes to build his palace, cities and fleet (1 Kings 5 and 9). He married many foreign wives, some for treaties, and 1 Kings 11 says he built shrines for their gods. The book blames these shrines for the troubles that follow. The queen of Sheba’s visit (1 Kings 10) shows how far his fame had spread, in the text’s telling.

Archaeologists debate how large Solomon’s kingdom really was. Some see gates at Hazor, Megiddo and Gezer as his work, matching 1 Kings 9:15. Others date those gates a century later. What is not in doubt is the Temple’s place in the story. For the rest of Kings, the Temple in Jerusalem is the center that every king is measured against.

Section 3

Two Kingdoms

21.7

The Kingdom Splits

Main ideaAfter Solomon, the text says ten northern tribes broke away as Israel, leaving Judah under David's line, and Kings judges every ruler by one test.

When Solomon died, his son Rehoboam went to Shechem to be made king (1 Kings 12). The northern tribes asked him to lighten the heavy burden of labor and taxes. His older advisers told him to say yes. His young friends told him to answer harshly, and he did. The text says the north replied with a cry: What share do we have in David? To your tents, Israel! Most scholars date this break to about 930 BCE.

From then on there were two kingdoms. The northern one, with ten tribes, kept the name . Its first king was Jeroboam, and its capital moved until King Omri built in the 870s BCE. The southern kingdom was , with Jerusalem, the Temple, and kings from David’s line. To keep his people from going to Jerusalem, the text says Jeroboam set up golden calves at Dan and Bethel. Kings calls this the sin of Jeroboam and never lets it go.

The books of Kings grade every ruler with one sentence: he did what was right, or what was evil, in the eyes of the LORD. The test is loyalty to God and to the Temple in Jerusalem, not wealth or victories. By that test every king of Israel fails, even strong ones like Omri and Ahab. In Judah, a few kings pass. Many scholars think the books were written or finished in Judah, which explains the grading.

The two kingdoms were often at war with each other and sometimes allied. Israel was larger and richer, with better farmland and trade routes. Judah was smaller and poorer but had the Temple and the Davidic line. Prophets such as Elijah and Elisha worked in Israel; Isaiah and Micah worked in Judah. The story of both kingdoms runs side by side through 1 and 2 Kings.

21.8

Assyria Takes Samaria

Main ideaThe Assyrian empire pressed Israel for tribute for a century and then, in 722 BCE, captured Samaria and deported its people.

was an empire in the north of what is now Iraq, with great cities at Ashur and Nineveh. From the 800s BCE its armies marched west almost every year. Smaller kingdoms could pay , that is, yearly gifts of silver, gold and goods, or be crushed. A carved black stone in the British Museum, the Black Obelisk of about 825 BCE, shows Israel’s King Jehu bowing before the Assyrian king. It is the only known picture of an Israelite king.

Under Tiglath-pileser III, from 745 BCE, Assyria began taking land, not just tribute. Second Kings 15 says he carried off people from Israel’s north. When Israel’s last king, Hoshea, stopped paying and looked to Egypt for help, Assyria besieged Samaria. The city fell in 722 BCE after about three years (2 Kings 17:5-6). Assyrian records agree, and King Sargon II claims the victory and says he carried off about 27,000 people.

The practice of was Assyria’s policy. Leaders, craftsmen and soldiers were moved to distant parts of the empire, and other peoples were settled in their place. Second Kings 17 says foreigners were brought to Samaria’s towns. Israel’s northern tribes never returned as a group. Later legends spoke of ten lost tribes. Jewish tradition connects the mixed population of the region with the Samaritans of later centuries.

The text explains the fall in its own way. Second Kings 17 gives a long sermon: Israel fell because it worshiped other gods and ignored the prophets. A historian would add Assyria’s strength, Hoshea’s gamble on Egypt, and Samaria’s position on the road to Egypt. Both explanations are in the chapter, one stated and one visible in the events.

21.9

Hezekiah, Josiah and the Fall of Jerusalem

Main ideaJudah survived Assyria under Hezekiah, reformed under Josiah, and then fell to Babylon, which burned the Temple in 586 BCE.

Judah watched Samaria fall and lasted another 136 years. King Hezekiah, who ruled from about 715 BCE, cut a tunnel through rock to bring water inside Jerusalem’s walls. Visitors can still walk it. Then he rebelled against Assyria. In 701 BCE King Sennacherib invaded, destroyed the city of Lachish, and camped around Jerusalem. Second Kings 18-19 says the prophet Isaiah told Hezekiah the city would stand, and that an angel struck the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib’s own record says Hezekiah paid heavy tribute. Both agree Jerusalem was not taken.

Two generations later King Josiah, who ruled from about 640 BCE, led a . Second Kings 22 says workers repairing the Temple found a book of the law, and Josiah tore his clothes when it was read. He closed shrines outside Jerusalem and kept a great Passover. Many scholars think the book was an early form of Deuteronomy. Josiah died in battle at Megiddo in 609 BCE, and his reform faded.

By then Assyria was collapsing. Its capital Nineveh fell to and its allies in 612 BCE. Babylon’s King Nebuchadnezzar became the new master of the region. Judah’s kings switched sides between Babylon and Egypt. In 597 BCE Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem and carried off King Jehoiachin with thousands of others. Clay tablets found in Babylon list food rations for Jehoiachin, king of Judah, by name.

The last king, Zedekiah, rebelled again. Babylon’s army besieged Jerusalem for about a year and a half. In 586 BCE the walls were breached, the Temple was burned, and more people were deported (2 Kings 25). Some scholars date the fall to 587 BCE. The book of Kings ends in Babylon, with Jehoiachin released from prison and eating at the king’s table. It is a quiet ending that leaves a door open.

Stones That Speak

21.10

The House of David Stone

Main ideaThe Tel Dan stele, found in 1993, is an enemy king's record that names the House of David, the first mention of David outside the Bible.

A is a standing stone with words or pictures cut into it. Ancient kings set them up to record victories and to warn enemies. Most steles from Israel’s neighbors have been lost or broken. A Clay Cylinder in a Glass Case

In 1879 a digger working for the British Museum pulled a barrel of baked clay from the ruins of Babylon.

Hormuzd Rassam grew up in Mosul, in what is now Iraq, and spent his life digging for the British Museum. In March 1879 his workers were clearing a temple wall in the ruins of Babylon. They found a clay object shaped like a small barrel, about nine inches long, covered in tiny wedge-shaped marks. It was broken but mostly whole. Rassam packed it and shipped it to London.

The marks were cuneiform, the script of ancient Mesopotamia. When scholars read them, they found the voice of a king. The cylinder was made for Cyrus, ruler of Persia, soon after his army took Babylon in 539 BCE. In it Cyrus says that Babylon's own god, Marduk, chose him to rule. He says he entered the city in peace and let captive peoples and their gods go home to their shrines.

The cylinder never mentions Judah, Jerusalem, or the exiles from the kingdom of Judah. But the book of Ezra opens with a decree from this same Cyrus. Ezra 1 says he told the exiles to go up to Jerusalem and rebuild the house of their God. Readers have set the clay and the scroll side by side ever since. The stone shows a policy; the book shows what one people said that policy meant for them.

Today the Cyrus Cylinder sits in a glass case in the British Museum. It has traveled to Tehran, to Washington, and in 2013 to five American cities. Some call it the first charter of human rights, though most historians say that goes too far. It is a king's announcement, written to make Babylon's priests accept a new ruler.

This chapter begins with a people carried off to Babylon and ends with the world Jesus was born into, about five centuries later. Between those points, the Bible's own books changed shape. Stories were written down and gathered. Prayer found a home outside the Temple. And a small province under one empire after another decided what it would keep.

Main ideaBabylon deported Judah's leaders in 597 and 586 BCE, and the exiles settled in villages where they could keep together.

Main ideaWithout land, king or Temple, the exiles held on through text, prayer and practices that marked them as a people.

Daniel in Babylon's Court

Main ideaDaniel tells stories of Jewish young men staying faithful in a foreign court, and then visions of empires rising and falling.

The book of Daniel is set in Babylon during the exile. Daniel and three friends, young men from Judah, are trained for the king’s service. The first six chapters are , stories about serving a foreign king without giving up one’s faith. Daniel refuses the king’s food and thrives on vegetables. He explains dreams that no Babylonian wise man can read. Later the writing on a wall spells out the fall of King Belshazzar.

Two stories are famous. In Daniel 3, the three friends refuse to bow to a golden statue and are thrown into a furnace. The text says they walk out unharmed. In Daniel 6, Daniel keeps praying toward Jerusalem against a royal ban and is thrown to the lions, and the text says he survives the night. In both, the men say they will obey God whether or not they are rescued.

Chapters 7 to 12 change form. They are visions. Beasts rise from the sea. A figure like a son of man comes with the clouds. Empires rise and fall in coded numbers. Part of the book, from 2:4 to 7:28, is written in rather than Hebrew. The visions follow the history of the Persian and Greek empires closely, down to a king who attacks the Temple.

Readers date the book differently. Jewish and Christian tradition has read it as Daniel’s own work from the 500s BCE. Many scholars think the visions were written or completed around 165 BCE, during the crisis under the Greek king Antiochus IV, to encourage people suffering then. Both readings agree on the book’s message: empires pass, and faithfulness under pressure matters.

22.9

Alexander and the Greek World

Main ideaAlexander's conquest in 332 BCE brought Greek language and culture to Judea, and the Torah was translated into Greek.

Persian rule ended fast. Alexander the Great, king of Macedon, beat the Persian army and marched down the coast of Judea in 332 BCE. He died in Babylon nine years later, and his generals divided his empire. Judea was ruled first from Egypt, by the Ptolemies, and after 200 BCE from Syria, by the Seleucids. Greek became the language of trade, government and learning. Historians call this mix of Greek and local ways .

Hellenism reached Jewish life everywhere. Cities got Greek names, theaters and gyms. Some Jews took Greek names and studied Greek philosophy. Others held tighter to Torah and worried about losing themselves. Both attitudes appear in the books written in these years. The tension between them helped bring on the revolt described in the next lesson.

The most lasting product of this period was a translation. In Alexandria, Egypt, the largest Jewish community outside Judea spoke Greek, not Hebrew. Beginning around 250 BCE, the Torah and then the other books were translated into Greek. This translation is called the , from the Latin for seventy, after a legend that seventy-two scholars produced it.

The Septuagint mattered far beyond Egypt. It was the Bible of Greek-speaking Jews, and later of the first Christians. Most Old Testament quotations in the New Testament follow its wording. It also included books written in this period, such as the Maccabees, which is one reason Catholic and Orthodox Bibles contain books that the Hebrew Bible does not.

The Maccabees and Hanukkah

Main ideaA revolt against the Greek king Antiochus IV won back the Temple in 164 BCE, a story told in the books of Maccabees and remembered at Hanukkah.

In 167 BCE the Seleucid king banned the practice of Judaism. First Maccabees says he outlawed Sabbath and circumcision, burned Torah scrolls, and set up a foreign altar in the Temple. A priest named Mattathias refused to obey and fled to the hills with his five sons. His son Judah, nicknamed Maccabee, which may mean the hammer, led a guerrilla war against far larger armies.

In 164 BCE Judah’s forces retook Jerusalem. They cleaned the Temple, built a new altar and it, that is, set it apart for worship again. First Maccabees 4 says they celebrated for eight days. The holiday of , which means dedication, remembers this. The story of a single day’s oil burning for eight days is not in Maccabees. It appears later, in the Talmud (Shabbat 21b). Judah’s family, the Hasmoneans, ruled Judea as priests and kings for about a century.

The story is told in 1 and 2 Maccabees, written in the 100s BCE, one in Hebrew and one in Greek. These books are part of the . Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include them in the Old Testament and call them deuterocanonical. The Hebrew Bible does not include them, so most Protestant Bibles leave them out or print them in a separate section, as the King James Bible of 1611 did. Jewish tradition knows the story mainly through later writings.

Second Maccabees also tells of a mother and her seven sons who die rather than break the law. These are among the earliest stories of , people killed for their faith. The same book contains the earliest clear statement in Jewish writing of hope that the dead will rise. Both ideas became important to Jews and Christians in the centuries that followed.

22.11

The World Jesus Was Born Into

Main ideaBy the first century Judea was under Rome and Herod, with a rebuilt Temple, synagogues, and several Jewish groups reading the same scriptures.

The Hasmonean kingdom ended in 63 BCE, when the Roman general Pompey marched into Jerusalem. From then on Rome ruled, sometimes through local kings and sometimes through governors. In 37 BCE Rome made king of Judea. He ruled until 4 BCE. He was a great builder and a feared ruler. Around 20 BCE he began enlarging the Second Temple into one of the largest sanctuaries in the Roman world. Its outer walls still stand in Jerusalem.

Jewish life in this period had several centers. The Temple in Jerusalem, with its priests and sacrifices, drew pilgrims at festivals. Synagogues in towns and villages gathered people for Torah reading and prayer every Sabbath. People spoke Aramaic at home, Greek in trade, and heard Hebrew in scripture. Jewish communities lived from Rome to Babylon.

Jews also disagreed about how to live by the Torah. The ancient historian Josephus describes several groups. The taught in synagogues and valued oral traditions alongside the written law. The , many of them priests, held to the written Torah and the Temple. The Essenes withdrew to live in strict communities; many scholars connect them to the Dead Sea Scrolls found near Qumran in 1947. Others hoped for a messiah who would end Roman rule.

This is the world of the Gospels. When the New Testament mentions Herod, the Temple, synagogues, Pharisees and Sadducees, it is describing this period. Jewish tradition traces rabbinic Judaism to the Pharisees. Christians trace their church to a Jewish teacher from Galilee in this same world. The Second Temple period ended in 70 CE, when Rome destroyed the Temple. Both traditions took shape in the years after.
